7.1 The new star of 1572

Tycho measured the new star’s distances from the chief stars of Cassiopeia with the sextant; they showed no daily parallax, and he placed the star among the fixed stars.

The reading made in 2026 from a copy of the print in a private collection gave the latitude as 54°35′; longitude agrees (6°54′ ♉); latitude read as 54°35′ where the print gives 53°45′ — the comparison tables on p. 369 (the star's place by Copernican and Alfonsine star places, 54°05′ and 54°0′) are the likely source of the misread; declination 61°47′ agrees.

Brightness and colour

Tycho set down a month-by-month decline (Progymnasmata, p. 301). In November 1572 the star equalled Venus in size; through December it nearly rivalled Jupiter; in January 1573 it was a little less than Jupiter but still brighter than the first-magnitude stars. By February and March it was of the first magnitude, by April and May of the second, and through the summer it sank to the third — the size of the larger stars of Cassiopeia. By October it was of the fourth order, by the end of that year and into January barely above the fifth, in February 1574 of the sixth and smallest sort; and in March 1574 it grew so faint that it ceased altogether to be seen. Its colour changed as it faded. At its brightest, when it equalled Venus and Jupiter, it shone with a whitish (albicanti), clear and pleasant light; and Tycho affirms (p. 466) that it varied its colour, unlike the constancy of the other stars — which he takes as further proof that it belonged to the celestial and not the elementary world.

The narrative gives no explicit date of discovery; it begins simply 'In Novembri'. The observation span Tycho gives runs from November 1572, when the star equalled Venus, through to March 1574, when it faded below visibility — about sixteen months of continuous monitoring.

Constitisse autem hanc Peregrinam Stellam toto suae durationis Tempore, in eodem prorsus Caeli loco, instar Affixarum Stellarum... Neque enim vllum proprium motum interea obtinere cognoscebatur, sed solummodo Reuolutioni vniuersali diurnae... obtemperauit.
That this strange star stood, through the whole time of its duration, in exactly the same place of the sky, like the fixed stars... For it was found to have no proper motion of its own, but obeyed only the universal daily revolution. (p. 303)
In Nouembri (uti dixi) Veneris Stella quantitate visebatur. Per Decembrem Iouis Sidus ferme aemulabatur... donec vltimo Mense Martio adeo exilis reddita sit, vt conspici celerius prorsus desineret.
In November, as I said, it appeared the size of the star Venus. Through December it nearly rivalled the star Jupiter... until at last, in the month of March, it became so faint that it presently ceased to be seen at all. (p. 301)
...omnium aperissime ostendebat illam [inter Affixa]rum Stellarum supremam... sedes... delegisse, omniumq; Planetarum gyratio[nes] [&] infimarum elementarium Mundano exuperasse.
...it showed most plainly of all that it had chosen its seat among the highest of the fixed stars, surpassing all the revolutions of the planets and the lowest elementary world. (p. 171, Liber V)
...obseruando videlicet easdem binas Stellas, siue Cassiopeæ, siue alterius Asterismi per vtrumq; Instrumentum, tam Vetus illud, quam Nouum Sextantem nulli Parallaxi obnoxium.
...observing, that is, the same pairs of stars, whether of Cassiopeia or of another asterism, through each instrument — both that old one and the new sextant, which is subject to no parallax. (p. 343). The freedom from parallax here is the sextant's, not the star's: Tycho is comparing his two instruments, having found the older one's star-distances a few minutes too large; the passage does not bear on the star's own parallax.

7.2 The comet of 1577

Tycho fixed the comet’s place on each night of observation by its distances from fixed stars and tested it for daily parallax; the parallax placed it many times the Moon’s distance away, moving through the region assigned to the orbs of Venus and Mercury.

The tail

On 16 November Tycho measured the tail: 'Longitudo caudæ 17 Grad. Latitudo 6 Grad. 30 min. vbi erat latissima' — some 17 degrees long, and 6° 30′ across at its widest point. On the later nights the log fixes its endpoints by azimuth and altitude ('Initii caudæ' and 'Finis caudæ' on 30 November; 'Finis caudæ' and a second, doubtfully read 'Rursum caudæ' (?) on 1 December), from which the tail's direction across the sky follows. The leaves read here give only that — length, breadth and endpoints. Tycho's celebrated claim about the direction itself, that the tail pointed away not from the Sun but from Venus (a coincidence he later suspected of being an optical illusion, since a tail averted from the Sun would be the more natural thing), belongs to the argument of De mundi, not to these pages.

Read from a copy of the 1588 print in a private collection; the passage in Opera Omnia IV is not yet located (§11).

Longitudo caudæ 17 Grad. Latitudo 6 Grad. 30 min. vbi erat latissima.
The tail's length 17 degrees, its breadth 6 degrees 30 minutes, where it was widest (observation of 16 Nov 1577).
quod Luna Horizonti vicina, non loco apparebat, in quo reuera erat (prout Optica ratio po[stulat])
...that the Moon, near the horizon, did not appear in the place where it really was (as optical/parallax reasoning requires) — Tycho reasoning about parallactic displacement, the same effect whose near-absence in the comet proved it lay far above the Moon.
Ideòq; longitudo Cometæ in P. 1. M. 17. latitudo P. 27. M. 46.
And thus the comet's longitude is at 1 deg 17 min, latitude 27 deg 46 min (north) — the computed position for 15 December 1577, a representative result of the parallax-triangle method.
