Observationes — The observation archive of Tycho Brahe, and the printed catalogue of stars

The 38 manuscript volumes in which Tycho Brahe’s observations survive — where each is, what it contains, in whose hand, what it was copied from, how it descended, and on which pages of Dreyer’s edition it is printed — with the catalogue of 777 fixed stars he printed from the record in 1602, star by star, and the new star of 1572 and the comet of 1577 as he tabled them.

The record of Tycho Brahe's observing survives in 38 manuscript volumes. Seventeen are at the Royal Danish Library in Copenhagen: eight original nightly protocols (Dreyer's Codices B–I), two books of comet observations, a working volume of theory and observations, Elias Olai's quadrant book of 1589, four contemporary transcripts (Codices A, K, L and P) and Erasmus Bartholin's compilation of the 1660s. Twenty are at the Austrian National Library in Vienna: the nineteen annual fair copies, arranged by subject, that Tycho had made from the protocols (Dreyer's Codex V), and a working catalogue of 296 zodiacal stars. One octavo of the earliest observations is lost. J. L. E. Dreyer printed the whole record as volumes X–XIII of the Opera Omnia (1923–26), and this chapter indexes those printed pages back to the codices they were set from.

From the record Tycho printed one thing directly: the catalogue of 777 fixed stars for the year 1600, in the Progymnasmata of 1602 — given here star by star in his own coordinates, with the modern identification of each and the distance between his place and ours. The new star of 1572 and the comet of 1577 are given as they stand in these books and as he printed their result.

How to read an entry

The catalogue (§2) is one row per codex and sorts on any column; the register (§3) gives each codex in full, in the order of Dreyer's letters, with his description of the hand quoted, the scribe attribution and its basis, what the volume was copied from, the pages of the Opera Omnia set from it, the library's catalogue record and, where one exists, the facsimile. Every codex keeps a stable key printed in its record; a link of the form ?volume=O_GKS311 opens the page at that record. A star of the catalogue is cited by its number in the 1602 print (st-307 is Polaris), a page of the printed edition by volume and page (p-XI-394).

Two grades are used and kept apart. The record grade (A–D) states how securely a volume's identity, extent and dates are established: A for every extant codex here, B for the lost octavo. The hand grade (B–D) on the fair copies and transcripts states how securely the engrossing scribe is identified by the documentary prior — who was on Hven in that year — and not by palaeography: B one primary candidate, C several, D unattributed or the one named candidate excluded by date. A star's identification grade (1–6) is Verbunt & van Gent's: 1 secure and the nearest modern star, 2 secure though not the nearest, 3 probable, 4 possible, 5 not identified, 6 a double entry.

How this chapter was made

The codices are described from Dreyer's Prolegomena editoris to Opera Omnia X (pp. I–XXVII), read in the Latin and in an English working translation made for this chapter, against the two libraries' own catalogue records and the leaf counts of the Vienna Tabulae codicum (1873). The pages of the printed edition are assigned to their codices by Dreyer's stated rule (p. XXV) and the volumes' tables of contents; the page-by-page summaries of §5 come from a reading of 976 of the 1,850 pages of volumes X–XIII made in 2026 from copies of the print, an irregular sample that omits pp. 1–18 of vol. X, and the other pages are indexed only by volume and year.

The star catalogue is founded on the keyed text of the three editions published by Verbunt & van Gent (2010) from Dreyer's reprint, in which the 777 stars of the 1602 print are selected by their sequence number and the readings peculiar to 1602 are restored from their variant table; each star carries their Hipparcos identification and its grade. A reading of the same catalogue made in 2026 from a copy of the 1602 print (759 rows) is collated against that text star by star and its verdict recorded: 647 readings agree, 19 differ in a detail, 44 were misread by more than three minutes of arc, 12 were filed under the wrong constellation, 37 could be placed against no star, and 55 stars of the print — Ursa Minor entire among them — were never read. The accuracy figures of §6 are computed twice: by this chapter's own method (nearest neighbour in the Yale Bright Star Catalogue within 40′ of Tycho's place rotated to the modern frame) and from Verbunt & van Gent's graded identifications. The nova's and the comet's figures are read from the page images of Opera Omnia II and IV; the readings made earlier from copies of the 1602 and 1588 prints are kept beside them as witnesses.

Facsimiles were probed on 5 September 2026: the Austrian National Library's catalogue records no digital copy of any of its twenty volumes; the Royal Danish Library's records show none for fifteen of its seventeen, and the two known viewers resolve.

What the figures are not

The count of codices is Dreyer's list plus his second Vienna codex and the two Copenhagen volumes he did not letter; it is not a count of everything at the two libraries that carries Tycho's astronomy (§11). The genre of a codex states what it is, its institution where it is, and its originality its relation to the observing; a "protocol" is an original nightly record, and only eight survive. The hand grade measures who could have written a fair copy, not who did. No page of the printed edition is called autograph here: Codex G, from which vol. XIII pp. 1–115 are set, is a scribal codex in which Dreyer sets Tycho's own entries in italic type. The star count is the print's, not the archive's: no star of §6 is joined to a page of §5, because the catalogue is Tycho's reduction of many nights and the print does not say which. The accuracy medians are over the stars named beside each figure and, for this chapter's method, still mix Tycho's error with the identification's. The page index is a sample of the printed edition until the remaining pages are read; every count in §5 states its denominator.

What this chapter does not contain

The 1,004 stars of the 1598 manuscript catalogue are not tabled; its witnesses are named in §6 and the text is Opera Omnia III pp. 333–373. The reductions made from the observations — Tycho's own theories, Kepler's Rudolphine Tables (1627), Curtz's Historia Coelestis (1666) — belong to the afterlife told in Receptio. The weather diary kept on Hven beside the observations is Diarium's. The texts of De nova stella and the farewell elegy in Codex G are read in Scripta; their figures and the codex stay here. The hand of Tycho himself is the Graphonomicon's subject; this chapter bridges to its specimens and does not repeat them.
