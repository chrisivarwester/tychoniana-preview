Observationes — the record, and the sky it produced

Tycho's life was the measurement of the heavens, and its record fills 37 manuscript volumes — the archive this pillar begins with. From its numbers the other views draw the result Tycho himself distilled from them and printed: 759 of his fixed stars in the coordinates he assigned them for the year 1600, and the two appearances — a new star and a comet — by which he proved the sky is neither unchanging nor walled with solid spheres. The numbers were recovered in 2026 by a page-by-page re-transcription of the Progymnasmata (1602) and De mundi (1588); the method, and what it failed to recover, are set out under The Sky and The Two Anomalies.

The archive itself

Everything in this pillar is distilled from one thing: the record of Tycho's nightly observing, preserved in 37 manuscript volumes spanning 1563–1601 — from the student observations begun at Leipzig to the last entries of the Prague exile. 17 volumes rest at the Royal Danish Library in Copenhagen — chiefly the original protocols, bought for Frederik III in 1655 — and 19 at the Austrian National Library in Vienna, chiefly the fair copies made for working use, later the basis of the first (unreliable) printed edition of the observations (Augsburg 1666). One further Copenhagen volume — an octavo of the earliest observations (1563–74) — is lost. These are the books Kepler inherited and worked from: the data of the Astronomia Nova.

The record is a dated log: night by night, the positions of Sun, Moon, planets, comets and stars, measured against reference stars and entered mostly by the assistants on duty, with Tycho's own hand intervening — in the printed record indexed under The Record, 81 of the 187 indexed pages of the final observation volume (Opera Omnia XIII — the last Hven and the exile years) are flagged autograph. The archive has two wounds: an octavo of the earliest observations (1563–74) — the dashed block in the Copenhagen row below — recorded on Bartholin’s 1664 receipt but probably lost in Paris during a loan of c.1706–07; and the Vienna fair copy for 1593, whose year survives on the Copenhagen side — chiefly in Codex K (GKS 1828), a volume of later transcripts, with four leaves appended to Codex E (GKS 313).

How to read this sky. Horizontal = ecliptic longitude through the twelve zodiac signs (Aries at left); vertical = ecliptic latitude, north above the central ecliptic line, south below. Dot size = Tycho's magnitude (largest = 1st magnitude); a dot with no recorded value is drawn small and faint, since there is nothing to size it by. No magnitude limit can bind 354 of the dots: for 353 of them the binding gutter took the magnitude column, and one — Praesepe — carries Tycho's word nebulosa in place of a number. They stay on the chart at every setting. Coordinates are Tycho's, for the 1600 epoch — his places hold to a median of about 3′ against the modern sky; that is the two-dimensional separation; each coordinate on its own runs smaller — a median of 1.9′ in longitude and 1.7′ in latitude. What "the sky" is made of

The 759 plotted stars come from the fixed-star catalogue printed in the Progymnasmata (1602), Tycho's "1600 epoch" restitution of the eighth sphere. Its pages were garbled by ordinary OCR; a 2026 vision re-OCR recovered 349 stars at citation grade and supplementary high-resolution passes recovered 410 more from the gutter-bound facing pages and the Cassiopeia reduction worksheets. The catalogue holds 777 stars in all (Tycho's manuscript catalogue extends the list to 1,004); the 18 not plotted here are the far-southern figures his Hven latitude barely reached and that were not in the re-OCR'd pages (Centaurus, Lupus, Ara, Corona Australis, Piscis Austrinus). Magnitudes are a separate loss: they fall away wherever the binding gutter cut the rightmost column.

The 1572 nova comes from the same Progymnasmata (its Liber I, De Nova Stella Anni 1572); the 1577 comet from the other book named above, De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis (1588). Both are read at the legibility floor of their scans: the nova's position figures are approximate, and the comet's zodiac signs are reconstructed from the continuity of Tycho's own ephemeris rather than printed row by row. Both caveats stand on the cards themselves, under The Two Anomalies.

The plotted sky is the distilled result; this is the nightly record behind it — 1,026 re-transcribed pages of the observation logs (Opera Omnia X–XIII, with the vol. XV addenda, 1569–1599), indexed by what Tycho watched and when. Filter by body; the manuscript volumes these pages were printed from are set out in the concordance below. Volume XIII prints the last Hven and the exile years (1596–1601) from Codices G–I; its Codex G stretch is written largely in Tycho's own hand — 81 of its 187 indexed pages here are flagged autograph (✍), the join to the Manus — the Graphonomicon →.

Observed body

The 37 volumes

Of them, 17 are at the Royal Library, Copenhagen — chiefly the original protocols, in Tycho's and his assistants' hands; 19 are the yearly fair copies (apographa) at the Austrian National Library, Vienna; and one Copenhagen-series protocol — an octavo of 1563–74, on Bartholin’s 1664 receipt — is now lost. They are presented as a concordance, a timeline and a gallery.
