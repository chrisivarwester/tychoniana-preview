Observationes — The Observation Volumes

The 37 volumes of Tycho Brahe’s astronomical observation archive — 17 volumes at the Royal Library, Copenhagen (the original protocols, a few contemporary transcripts, and Bartholin’s later compilation) and 19 yearly fair copies (apographa) at the Austrian National Library, Vienna, and one volume now lost.

The making of the archive — origins, structure, and assistants

A systematic programme

When Tycho Brahe observed the close conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn in August 1563, he was sixteen years old and using borrowed instruments. The mismatch between his measurements and the published tables — the Alphonsine tables a month in error, the Copernican off by several days — convinced him to build something new: a continuous, high-precision record of the sky from which corrected theories of the planets and fixed stars could be derived. He spent the rest of his life fulfilling that resolution, first at Hven (1576–1597) and then during the exile years through Wandsbek, Wittenberg, and Benátky to Prague. Each night’s work — solar altitudes, meridian transits, planetary positions measured against reference stars, eclipse timings, comet paths — was measured on instruments of unmatched accuracy and recorded in the moment. Tycho called them “a most rare and precious treasure” (Mechanica, 1598). A year and a half before Tycho’s death, Kepler — newly arrived at Benátky — wrote in a private deliberation that Tycho would not allow him to copy the observations — “and rightly so, for this is his treasure, in which he has spent his entire life.”

Two layers of record

The archive had two physical layers. At Hven the nightly work went into large folio journals — the protocols — in which each measurement was entered as it was made, hands shifting mid-page as different assistants took over the instrument. As the Hven years progressed, these were fair-copied into a second tier: annual quarto volumes organised by subject — solar, lunar, the planets from Saturn down to Mercury, the fixed stars — so that theorists could work from a single subject sequence. Tycho describes the arrangement in the Mechanica (fol. G2): the observations “had been entered in large volumes, and afterwards, for each year, copied into separate volumes and sorted according to subject.” The copying was done by Hven’s assistants. The engrossing hands are rarely signed, and the attributions below rest mostly on who was on the island in a given year rather than on paleographic proof: Peder Flemløse, Gellius Sascerides, Elias Olai Morsing and Hans Crol in the early volumes, Christen Longomontanus from 1589/90, and Johannes Eriksen from 1596. Dreyer prints a roster of the pupils and assistants from a loose sheet written by Crol in 1588/89 — before Longomontanus reached the island.

Three centuries of dispersal — from Prague to Copenhagen, 1601–1923

Kepler, Tengnagel, and the instruments

Tycho died in Prague on 24 October 1601. His instruments were sold to Emperor Rudolf II for 20,000 thaler, of which the family ever received only some 4,000. Frans Tengnagel, Tycho’s son-in-law, returned to Prague in 1602, assumed the role of scientific heir, and had the instruments stored in a vault under the house of the imperial vice-chancellor Jakob Kurtz von Senftenau — Tycho’s former lodging, the house in which he died — blocking Kepler’s access. During the Bohemian rising of 1619 the instruments were destroyed. The manuscripts were a different matter: by a 1604 agreement, Kepler received both the folio protocols and the quarto fair copies, retaining them as pledges for unpaid salary. He intended to publish them throughout his life — in 1625 he still wrote of “printing the books of observations, truly a treasure of ancient value” — but died in November 1630 with the edition unbegun. The manuscripts passed to his son Ludwig, a physician in Königsberg.

The Vienna copies: Curtz and the Historia Coelestis

Nineteen annual quarto volumes — covering 1582–1592 and 1594–1601 — never reached Ludwig Kepler in Königsberg. By an uncertain route — perhaps a gift from Kepler in 1621, perhaps via Kepler’s daughter Susanna after her husband Jakob Bartsch’s death in 1633 — they came instead to Albert Curtz (Albertus Curtius), a Jesuit who had taught mathematics at Dillingen on the Danube and served as rector at Neuburg, Eichstätt, and Lucerne. Curtz believed he had the originals. In fact he had the fair copies — the apographa. As a specimen he printed the 1582 observations in 1656 under the anagrammatic pseudonym “Lucius Barrettus” as the Sylloge Ferdinandea, dedicated to Emperor Ferdinand III. The complete edition, Historia Coelestis (Augsburg, 1666), was a magnificent folio of 977 pages with engraved views of Uraniborg, Wandsbek, Benátky, and the instruments. But Curtz’s copies had never been collated against the originals: pages were skipped, the year 1593 was missing entirely, zodiacal signs were misread as numerals (Cancer as “69”), and directional terms were transposed throughout. Dreyer called the edition “well-nigh useless for any scientific purpose.” The nineteen volumes remain today in the Austrian National Library.

The originals reach Copenhagen

In 1655 Ludwig Kepler sold the original folio protocols to King Frederik III of Denmark, who deposited them in the newly founded Royal Library in Copenhagen. Frederik III commissioned the mathematician Erasmus Bartholin to prepare a correct edition, providing generous funding and six student copyists; his principal assistant was Ole Rømer, future discoverer of the finite speed of light. A complete copy was prepared and read against the originals. When Bartholin obtained the Historia Coelestis in 1666 and found it “extremely defective and erroneous,” he published his critique — Specimen recognitionis (Copenhagen, 1668), 48 quarto pages demonstrating the scale of Curtz’s errors from the 1582 observations alone — and announced a correct edition.

The Paris episode: Picard, Rømer, and the long return

In 1671 Jean Picard came to Copenhagen to fix the latitude of Uraniborg. Learning of Bartholin’s project, he proposed taking the copy to Paris for printing at Louis XIV’s expense. On 2 April 1672 Picard gave Bartholin a formal receipt for the observations “written out fair in five folio volumes, from 1563 to 1601, with the comet observations” — Bartholin’s copy. Ole Rømer went along with the originals themselves. Ninety-six pages were set before Picard (1682) and Colbert (1683) died and the project halted. The manuscripts remained at the Paris Observatory with Philippe de La Hire. In 1697 the Danish scholar Friderich Rostgaard located them and wrote home: “I found seven folio volumes, seven quarto volumes or bundles, and one octavo volume.” They were reclaimed by the Danish envoy and returned to Copenhagen in 1707. Deposited in the Royal Library, they survived the great fire of 1728 that destroyed the University Library, the Round Tower observatory, and Rømer’s own unpublished observations. Bartholin’s copy, left behind in Paris, was transcribed in full by Joseph-Nicolas Delisle into six volumes and worked from by Pingré, Lalande, and Jeaurat; Delisle’s transcript remains at the Paris Observatory today — what finally became of Bartholin’s own copy is unknown.

The modern edition

A complete critical edition of the observations was finally published by J. L. E. Dreyer as volumes X–XIII of Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia (Copenhagen, 1923–1926). After the First World War, the nineteen Vienna apographa were lent to Copenhagen for the purpose. Dreyer’s editorial preface describes the Copenhagen originals codex by codex — Codices A to P, skipping J — and collates the fair-copied years against the Vienna apographa, his Codex V. Three of them carry the comets (N, O and P). All 15 appear in the inventory below — Codex L only as a probable identification, the volume’s own identity being disputed — which follows Dreyer’s codex designations throughout; where a volume below carries no letter, Dreyer letters none.
