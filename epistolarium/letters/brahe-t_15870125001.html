The original

CLARISSIMO VIRTUTE ET ERUDITIONE PRÆEMINENTI VIRO D. DOCTORI THADDÆO HAGECIO AB HAYK, MATHEMATICO ET MEDICO INPRIMIS EXCELLENTI, AMICO SUO PERPETUO COLENDISSIMO.
ZU PRAG.
S.

REMITTO tibi nunc tandem, eruditiss. amantissimeque D. THADÆE, doctas et ingeniosas illas tuas de Nova Stella et Cometis aliquot lucubrationes, quas Francofurtum ad hasce proxime instantes vernales Nundinas (ut illinc per eundem Bibliopolam Gallum, de quo mihi significasti, ad te procurentur, vel, si is officium hac in re detrectârit, ut per hunc ipsum, cui eas tradidi, negotium hoc apud aliquem certum hominem expediatur) perferendas curavi. Quod vero eas nunc tua expectatione proprioque meo proposito tardius hunc tuum foetum recipias, nuntiorum penuria in causa fuit; rarissime enim aliquis hinc ad vos proficiscitur, et, cum nulla commodior daretur occasio, volebam praeteritis autumnalibus nundinis Francofordensibus idem praestitisse, et ob id cum quodam nostratium Bibliopolarum illuc ad dictas nundinas quotannis proficiscentium egeram, ut is hîc ante suum discessum me conveniret secumque librum tuum unâ cum meis literis Francofurto ad te mittendum asportaret; nam et ipsi etiam literas ad Illustriss. Principem Landgravium Hassiae ipsiusque Cels. Mathematicum ROTHMANNUM, ubi Cassellas pertransiret, expediendas unâ committere volebam; ille vero, mihi suam operam hac in re non gravatim promittens, omnino me ante discessum suum accedere constituerat. Verum accidit ijs ipsis diebus, quibus illi huc veniendum erat, ut Illustribus hospitibus, utpote Regina ipsa denuo me invisente et secum utrumque parentem adducente unâ cum duce SIGISMUNDO, patrui filio, magnoque nobilium virorum comitatu, excipiendis occuparer, quod ille ubi cognoverat, et iam iam dies illi instarent, quibus sine mora abeundum erat, me non accedens itineri se unâ cum socijs commisit. Hinc itaque eveniebat, ut, hac commoditate librum tuum remittendi mihi tunc praerepta, usque in hoc tempus illum mecum retinere coactus fuerim, quemadmodum etiam Illustrissimo Principi Landtgravio hanc meam iustam excusationem, quod etiam ipsius Celsitudinis votis in rescribendo tardius satisfaciam, proxime elapsis diebus per literas indicavi. Perlegi autem hunc tuum librum non sine voluptate et diligentia, tuumque in hac materia indefessum studium pari labore et industria coniunctum commendatione dignissimum censeo. Essent quidem nonnulla, quae paulo enucleatius forent constituenda, si observationes exactae unâ cum otio tibi suppeterent, sed sunt ea utplurimum eiuscemodi, quae non facile cuivis obvia occurrant, nec usque adeo magni momenti exsistunt. Quod nullae ex omnibus illis eruditis et ingeniosis alias satis, quibus Parallaxes indagare doces, rationibus motui proprio Cometarum (quo rarissime carent) satisfaciant, existimo, me in ultimis ad te datis literis admonuisse; quare alia erit Parallaxium a motu Cometarum proprio separatarum instituenda ratiocinatio, si non solum speculative, sed etiam in ipsa praxi veritatis scopum collimatius attingere intendamus; quod tibiipsi ulterius expendendum considerandumque relinquo. Stellarum Cassiopeae tum a se invicem, tum etiam a Nova illa Stella remotiones paulo aliter se habent, quam tui etiam correctiores et reiterati numeri exhibent; sic etiam distantia polaris stellae ab 11a Cassiopeae, pro qua tantum laborasti, 9 scrupulis in tua annotatione abundat. Nequaquam omnino per Radium Astronomicum exquisitae et ipsi coelo congruae stellarum distantiae ulla adhibita diligentia capi possunt, quacunque tandem longitudine et divisionum subtilitate Radius ille praeditus sit, ut alio loco certis rationibus confirmabo, ubi, favente Numine, opus nostrum de prima et Mechanica Astronomiae parte absolutum fuerit et publicae utilitati communicatum. Ut enim ipsissima stellarum intercapedo obtineatur, necesse est utramque stellam bifariam per rimulas, quae lineas visuales a superiori pinnacidio ubique paralelas efficiunt, centraliter intueri. Quod nequaquam Radio praestari potest, ideoque nova quaedam organa, quae huic incertitudini praeveniant et stellarum exactas infallibilesque remotiones exhibeant, iam dudum excogitavi, quorum fabricam et usum in praenominato opere aliquando (Deo conatus nostros secundante) perspicies. Declinatio Novi Sideris a te constituta 4 ad minus scrupulis iusto maior est, et ratio inquirendi eius longitudinem e tempore transitus per Meridianum non caret evidentibus obstaculis; difficillime enim exactissima illa temporis momenta cognita veniunt, et licet hoc magna adhibita industria possibile concedamus, nihilominus Solis locus vel ex ipso COPERNIANO calculo depromtus huic negotio non sufficit, siquidem Solari in coelo cursui nequaquam congruus existit, ut ex ea, quae in Diario isto, quod a nobilibus Gallis accepisti, reperitur, restitutione colligere potes. Oportuit etiam affixarum stellarum loca, quorum usus in Stella illa Nova et Cometis requirebatur, prius per certas observationes emendata habere, ut ea, quae superstruerentur, rectius constarent; quod dum non solum a te omnibusque fere alijs hactenus neglectum est, sed etiam a me in tractatulo nostro de Stella illa Nova dudum edito praetermissum, factum est, ut scopum ipsum in certis et exactis longitudinibus latitudinibusque et ijs, quae hinc derivari possunt, cognoscendis non satis praecise in Stella illa Nova attigerimus. Horum verum a me emendationem in opere, quod nunc de tota hac materia impressioni adorno, brevi, Deo volente, invenies. Si itaque tua omnino edere decrevisti (in quo proposito ut perseveres, potius autor quam dissuasor esse velim), percuperem, ne quid ex meo illo libello adiungeres, cum omnia correctiora et elimatiora sim propediem in lucem emissurus. In Cometa anni 1577 videris non eandem quam in Nova Stella adhibuisse diligentiam, licet teipsum postea in alio quodam scripto oportune correxeris. Nam plane exigua et fere insensibilis illi Cometae aderat Parallaxis, et rectissime omnium de eo scripsit MÆSTLINUS, ut ob id immerito illum in quibusdam taxes. Ratio illa observandi per filum in directum ad stellas extensum non quidem exactae certitudini satisfacit, nihilominus si exquisita et nulli fallaciae obnoxia organa in promtu non sunt, dubijs et incertis imo ipsi Radio quamoptime elaborato hanc filarem observandi rationem anteferrem. Sed de his suo loco et tempore copiosius. TRAPEZUNTIJ in PTOLOMÆUM introductionem an quispiam sit Typographorum Francofurtum venientium, qui eam typis edere velit, expiscari curo. Interea librum illum mecum conservo, quod bona tua venia fieri non diffido. Et quia constitui (Deo favente) proxima instante aestate in Germaniam excurrere (modo veniam a Sereniss. Rege nostro, uti spero, impetravero), cumque tunc quamprimum Cassellis (ubi Principem Landtgravium ob commune hoc studium convenire decrevi) discessero, Francofurtum me conferre in votis habeo idque circa tempus autumnalium Nundinarum. Ideo, si TRAPEZUNTINA illa editio prius non successerit, inter alia librum illum mecum accipiam eiusque publicationem omni conatu tunc procurabo, quod eam etiam ob causam tibi nunc indicare volui, ut, si forte res tuae ita ferrent, quod in illa mea excursione alicubi in vobis vicina Germania vel Lipsiae aut Dresdae simul convenire possimus, interea tecum de hoc deliberare possis; nihil enim mihi gratius eveniret, quam si facultas daretur, ut quemadmodum Ratisbonae olim nunc iterum alicubi vel pauculis diebus nos invicem gratis sermonibus coram complecti possemus. Tuum itaque erit mature aliquid certi hac in re statuere et mihi tuam voluntatem prima quaque occasione significare. Pragam in illo itinere accedere vix mihi commodum erit; etiamsi enim omnia votis meis successerint, non tamen ante initia Iulij hinc discedere statui, et ante hyemem in patriam mihi necessario ad mea revertendum erit. Tu itaque da operam, ut medium aliquod inveniatur, quo votis nostris utrinque (nam et te idipsum exoptare non dubito) satisfiat, mecumque tuam mentem in his quam primum (ut prius etiam rogavi) communica. Existimo, me tibi ultimo misisse exemplar quoddam Literarum Illustriss. Principis WILHELMI, Landtgravij Hassiae, quibus meis respondebat, unâ etiam cum Mathematici ipsius Celsitudinis CHRISTOPHORI ROTHMANNI literis. Et quia semel atque iterum ab eodem Principe de responsione sollicitatus sum, eo quod easdem ob causas, quibus tibi etiam hactenus scribere non licuit, hanc multo tempore differre coactus fuerim, proxime idcirco elapsis diebus, nactus iam tandem commodam occasionem, ipsius Celsitudini rescripsi; quare, ut meum etiam hoc responsum cum prioribus Principis huius literis conferre possis, adiunxi hîc illius exemplar, praesertim cum eam inprimis materiam contineat, qua te plurimum affici compertum habeo. Addidissem quoque exemplar meae ad ROTHMANNI literas responsionis, verum, cum is, per quem nunc hasce mitto, abitum festinet, eo quod Cimbriam, patriam suam, ante Nundinarum illarum Francofordensium accessum invisere constituerit, non relinquebatur nobis tantum temporis, ut descriptionem eius fieri curare concederetur, ipsa etiam prolixitate, qua Landtgravianas literas fere in duplo excedunt, pro hac tanta otij penuria idipsum non admittente; sed haec et alia suo tempore commodius fortassis obtinebis. Cumque nunc charta deficiens scribendi finem facere admoneat, pluribus supersedeo, teque, charissime THADÆE, cum tota familia Deo omnium rerum autori commendo. Is te quam diutissime conservet animo corporeque incolumem. Vale et prima quaque oblata occasione rescribe. Iterum quam felicissime vale. Datae Uraniburgi die 25. Ianuarij Anno 1587.

Tuus
TYCHO BRAHE.

Boni consule has raptim exaratas et incertas literas, quas recognoscere, nedum rescribere nuncij festinatio non admittebat.

To the most distinguished man, pre-eminent in virtue and learning, Master Doctor Thaddaeus Hagecius ab Hayk, mathematician and physician especially excellent, his perpetual and most honoured friend.
At Prague.
Greeting.

I now at last send back to you, most learned and most beloved Master Thaddaeus, those learned and ingenious lucubrations of yours on the New Star and on several comets, which I have arranged to have conveyed to Frankfurt for this spring fair now next at hand (so that from there they may be procured for you by the same French bookseller of whom you informed me; or, should he decline the office in this matter, so that this business may be dispatched through this very man to whom I have handed them, by way of some trustworthy person). That you now receive back this offspring of yours later than your own expectation and my own intention, the scarcity of messengers was the cause; for very rarely does anyone set out from here to your parts, and, since no more convenient occasion offered, I had wished to have done the same at the past autumn fair at Frankfurt, and on that account I had dealt with a certain one of our [Danish] booksellers who travel there to the said fair every year, that he should call on me here before his departure and carry off with him your book together with my letters, to be sent from Frankfurt to you; for I wished at the same time to entrust to him also letters to be dispatched to the Most Illustrious Prince, the Landgrave of Hesse, and to his Highness's mathematician Rothmann, when he should pass through Kassel; and he indeed, promising me his service in this matter not grudgingly, had quite resolved to come to me before his departure. But it happened in those very days on which he was to come here that I was occupied in receiving illustrious guests — namely, the Queen herself visiting me again and bringing both her parents with her, along with Duke Sigismund, her uncle's son, and a great retinue of noblemen — and when he learned this, and the days were now pressing upon him on which he had to leave without delay, he, not calling on me, committed himself to the journey along with his companions. Hence, then, it came about that, this convenience of sending your book back having then been snatched from me, I was forced to keep it with me right up to this present time — just as also I made known this just excuse of mine to the Most Illustrious Prince the Landgrave, in letters a few days ago now, [namely] that I am satisfying his Highness's wishes too somewhat tardily in replying. I have, however, read this book of yours through, not without pleasure and diligence, and I judge your tireless zeal in this subject, joined with equal labour and industry, most worthy of commendation. There would indeed be some points which would need to be established a little more clearly, if exact observations together with leisure were available to you; but these are for the most part of such a kind as do not easily come to anyone's notice, nor are they of such great moment. That none of all those learned and ingenious methods otherwise sufficient, by which you teach how to track down parallaxes, satisfy the proper motion of comets (which they very rarely lack), I think I pointed out in my last letter sent to you; wherefore another reckoning of parallaxes separated from the proper motion of comets will have to be instituted, if we mean to attain the mark of truth more pointedly not only speculatively but also in the very practice [of it] — which I leave to you yourself for further weighing and consideration. The distances of the stars of Cassiopeia, both from one another and also from that New Star, stand somewhat otherwise than even your corrected and repeated figures present them; so too the distance of the pole star from the 11th [star] of Cassiopeia, over which you laboured so much, is in your annotation in excess by 9 minutes [scruples]. By no means at all can the exact distances of stars, congruent with the heaven itself, be taken by the cross-staff (radius astronomicus) with any diligence applied, with whatever length and fineness of divisions that staff be at last endowed — as I shall confirm in another place with sure reasons, when, by the favour of the Deity, our work on the first and Mechanical part of astronomy shall have been completed and communicated to the public good. For in order that the very interval of the stars be obtained, it is necessary to look at each star centrally, in two parts, through little slits which everywhere make the lines of sight parallel from the upper sight (pinnule); and this can by no means be furnished by the cross-staff. And therefore I devised long ago certain new instruments, which forestall this uncertainty and exhibit the exact and infallible distances of the stars — whose construction and use you will at some time perceive in the aforesaid work (God seconding our efforts). The declination of the New Star, as established by you, is greater than is right by at least 4 minutes [scruples], and the method of inquiring after its longitude from the time of its transit across the meridian is not free of evident obstacles; for those most exact moments of time become known only with the greatest difficulty, and although we grant this possible with great industry applied, nonetheless the Sun's place, even when drawn from the Copernican calculation itself, does not suffice for this business, since it is by no means congruent with the Sun's course in the heaven — as you can gather from that restoration which is found in that Diary which you received from the noble Frenchmen. It was also necessary to have the places of the fixed stars — whose use was required in that New Star and in the comets — first emended through sure observations, so that the things to be built upon them might stand more correctly; and while this has hitherto been neglected not only by you and by almost all others, but was also passed over by me in our little treatise on that New Star published long ago, it came about that we did not attain the mark itself precisely enough, in the case of that New Star, in coming to know the sure and exact longitudes and latitudes and the things that can be derived from them. But you will shortly find, God willing, the emendation of these things by me, in the work which I am now dressing for the press concerning this whole subject. If therefore you have quite resolved to publish your [writings] (and in that purpose I would rather be your encourager than your dissuader, that you persevere in it), I would greatly wish that you add nothing from that little book of mine, since I am about very soon to send into the light everything in more corrected and more polished form. In the comet of the year 1577 you seem not to have applied the same diligence as in the New Star, although you afterwards opportunely corrected yourself in some other writing. For that comet had a plainly minute and almost imperceptible parallax, and of all men Maestlin wrote about it most correctly, so that on that account you undeservedly censure him in certain points. That method of observing by a thread stretched straight out toward the stars does not indeed satisfy exact certainty; nonetheless, if exquisite instruments not liable to any deception are not at hand, I would prefer this thread-method of observing to dubious and uncertain ones — indeed even to the cross-staff however excellently wrought. But of these things more copiously in their place and time. Whether there be any of the printers coming to Frankfurt who may be willing to publish it in type, I am taking care to fish out — [I mean] Trebizond's introduction to Ptolemy. Meanwhile I keep that book with me, which I do not doubt may be done by your good leave. And since I have resolved (God favouring) to make an excursion into Germany this coming summer (provided I obtain leave, as I hope, from our Most Serene King), and since then, as soon as I shall have departed from Kassel (where I have determined to meet the Prince Landgrave on account of this common study), it is among my wishes to betake myself to Frankfurt, and that around the time of the autumn fair: therefore, if that Trebizond edition shall not have succeeded beforehand, I shall among other things take that book with me and then procure its publication with every effort — which I wished to indicate to you now for this reason too, that, if perhaps your affairs should so bear it, [namely] that in that excursion of mine we might be able to meet together somewhere in the Germany neighbouring you, or at Leipzig or Dresden, you might in the meantime be able to deliberate with me about this; for nothing more welcome could befall me than if the opportunity were given that, just as once at Regensburg, so now again we might somewhere, even for a very few days, embrace one another in person with pleasing discourse. It will therefore be for you to settle something certain in this matter in good time, and to signify your will to me at the very first occasion. To approach Prague on that journey will scarcely be convenient for me; for even though everything succeed according to my wishes, I have nonetheless resolved not to depart from here before the beginning of July, and before winter I shall necessarily have to return home to my affairs. Do you therefore see to it that some middle way be found by which our wishes on both sides may be satisfied (for I do not doubt that you yourself long for the very same thing), and communicate your mind to me on these matters as soon as possible (as I asked before too). I think I sent you last time a certain copy of the letter of the Most Illustrious Prince Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, by which he was replying to mine, together also with the letter of his Highness's mathematician Christoph Rothmann. And because I have been solicited once and again by the same Prince concerning a reply — since, for the same reasons by which I have been hitherto unable to write to you also, I was compelled to defer this for a long time — therefore a few days ago now, having at last got a convenient occasion, I wrote back to his Highness; wherefore, so that you may be able to compare this reply of mine too with the earlier letters of this Prince, I have here appended a copy of it, especially since it contains in particular that subject by which I have found that you are very much affected. I would have added too a copy of my reply to Rothmann's letter; but, since the man through whom I now send these things hastens his departure — because he has resolved to visit Cimbria, his homeland, before the approach of that Frankfurt fair — there was not left to us so much time that the making of a transcription of it could be allowed, the very length too (by which they exceed the Landgrave's letters by nearly double) not admitting of this, given so great a scarcity of leisure; but these and other things you will perhaps obtain more conveniently in their time. And since now the failing paper warns [me] to make an end of writing, I forbear from more, and commend you, dearest Thaddaeus, with your whole household, to God the author of all things. May He keep you as long as possible unharmed in mind and body. Farewell, and write back at the very first occasion offered. Again, most happily, farewell. Given at Uraniborg, the 25th day of January, the year 1587.

Yours,
Tycho Brahe.

Take in good part this letter, hastily scrawled and uncertain, which the messenger's haste did not permit me to revise, let alone to rewrite.

Apparatus

  • Sender / recipient. Tycho Brahe, writing from Uraniborg on Hven. The addressee, Thaddaeus Hagecius ab Hayk = Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku (Thaddaeus Hagecius, 1525–1600), the Bohemian physician, astronomer, and personal physician at the imperial court in Prague; like Tycho he had observed and written on the 1572 New Star and on comets, and the two had met at the Regensburg imperial diet of 1575 (the Ratisbonae olim of the letter). The superscription's ZU PRAG ("at Prague") is a German code-switch within an otherwise Latin address; preserved by the edition's convention
  • Salutation. S. = salutem ("greeting"), the edition's convention
  • Persons named. Illustriss. Princeps Wilhelmus, Landgravius Hassiae = Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel; his court Mathematicus Christophorus Rothmannus = Christoph Rothmann. Maestlinus = Michael Maestlin (1550–1631), the Tübingen astronomer (later Kepler's teacher), whose treatment of the 1577 comet Tycho here defends against Hagecius's criticism. Trapezuntius = George of Trebizond (Giorgio da Trebisonda, 1396–1486), whose Latin commentary/"introduction" to Ptolemy's Almagest Hagecius wished to see printed at Frankfurt. Dux Sigismundus, patrui filius = a Duke Sigismund, named as the cousin ("son of [the Queen's] paternal uncle") in the royal visiting party; Regina ipsa … utrumque parentem adducente refers to Queen Sophie of Denmark (of Mecklenburg) bringing both her parents (Duke Ulrich III of Mecklenburg and Duchess Elisabeth) — the Mecklenburg ducal house, to which Sigismund belonged.
  • Places. Francofurtum / Francofordenses Nundinae = Frankfurt and its book fair (Nundinae, "the fair," held spring and autumn). Cassellae = Kassel. Lipsia = Leipzig; Dresda = Dresden; Ratisbona = Regensburg. Cimbria = Cimbria, the Jutland/Holstein region of the messenger's homeland (rendered as the Latin name).
  • Technical terms (per glossary–5.2). Parallaxis → parallax; motus proprius (Cometarum) → proper motion (of comets); Declinatio → declination; longitudo / latitudo → longitude / latitude; transitus per Meridianum → transit across the meridian; affixae stellae → fixed stars; organa / nova organa → instruments / new instruments; Radius Astronomicus → cross-staff (radius astronomicus); pinnacidium → sight (pinnule). Stella Nova / Novum Sidus → the New Star (the supernova of 1572 in Cassiopeia). Cometa anni 1577 → the comet of 1577.
  • scrupulis — scrupulum here = minute of arc; "9 scruples / 4 scruples" rendered "9 minutes / at least 4 minutes [scruples]," keeping the period term in brackets. The discrepancies Tycho cites (the pole-star/11th-Cassiopeiae distance "in excess by 9 minutes"; the New Star's declination "greater than is right by at least 4 minutes") are his own corrections to Hagecius's figures and are reported, not adjudicated.
  • opus nostrum de prima et Mechanica Astronomiae parte — Tycho's projected great work on the "first and Mechanical part of astronomy"; Mechanica here is the planned instrument-and-method volume (cf. the later Astronomiae instauratae Mechanica, 1598). opere, quod nunc … impressioni adorno = the work "now dressing for the press" on the New Star — the Astronomiae instauratae Progymnasmata, then in preparation. tractatulus noster de Stella illa Nova dudum editus = Tycho's "little treatise on that New Star published long ago," i.e. De nova stella (1573). Per the edition's convention, restitutio (of the Sun's place) → restoration.
  • Diario isto, quod a nobilibus Gallis accepisti — "that Diary which you received from the noble Frenchmen": an ephemeris/diary containing a solar restoration; the "noble Frenchmen" are not further identified in the letter and are left unidentified here (no silent expansion).
  • ratio observandi per filum — Hagecius's "thread-method" of observation (sighting along a thread stretched toward the stars); kept as described. Tycho's qualified judgment — that he would prefer it to a poorly made cross-staff if better instruments are unavailable — is preserved with its conditional intact.
  • Epistemic hedges preserved. Tycho's recurrent qualifiers are kept verbatim in force: Deo favente / Deo volente / favente Numine / Deo conatus nostros secundante ("God favouring / God willing / by the favour of the Deity / God seconding our efforts"); uti spero ("as I hope"); fortassis ("perhaps"); vix mihi commodum erit ("will scarcely be convenient"). None is flattened to plain assertion.
  • Closing formulae. Vale … Iterum quam felicissime vale → "Farewell … Again, most happily, farewell". Datae Uraniburgi → "Given at Uraniborg." The postscript Boni consule … is a self-deprecating apology for a "hastily scrawled" (raptim exaratas) letter the messenger's haste left unrevised.
  • Calendar. Dateline die 25. Ianuarij Anno 1587 — 25 January 1587, Old Style (Denmark used the Julian calendar until 1700; the edition's convention). The catalogue label ("1587. 25. januar. Uraniborg") agrees with the dateline; no discrepancy. No date is converted.
  • Language. Latin throughout (the heuristic tag la is correct on reading), with two non-Latin tokens: the German address ZU PRAG and the place-name Cimbria. No Greek occurs in this letter.
  • Correction. Latin text-error fixed: 'nuntiorum penuriam causa fuit' restored to Dreyer's 'nuntiorum penuria in causa fuit' (TBOO VII, p.111); the English translation was already correct and unaffected.
