The original

TYCHO BRAHE AD IOSEPHUM SCALIGERUM.
ILLUSTRIS ET GENEROSE VIR DOMINE ET AMICE HONORANDISSIME.

ELAPSAe sunt septimanae, ni fallor, sex, ex quo in arce Caesarea Bennaticha, ubi nunc sedes est habitationis meae, Illustris Dominationis tuae literas acceperim VI Eid. Martias Lugduni Batavorum scriptas, easque non saltem eruditionis eximiae, quâ praepolles, sed et humanitatis et benevolentiae erga me (pro quibus etiam officiosissime gratias habeo) refertissimas. Cum vero nuper a Scra Caesa Mate, Domino nostro Clementissimo, postquam Pilsena, ubi per tres anni quadrantes ob pestem abfuit, in arcem consuetae residentiae huc nuper redijt, evocatus sum, siquidem ipsius Matas de quibusdam ad studia quae tracto pertinentibus clementissime mecum loqui dignabatur (prout semel atque iterum affatim factum est) accidit, quod ante biduum Nobilis et eruditus hic Adolescens NICOLAUS SUCKERBACK de Myssen noviter ex Italia veniens me in aula inter alios ambulantem, Barone quodam indicante, agnosceret et compellaret, seque a Batavia oriundum, atque eo brevi cogitare indicaret, rogans, an quidpiam literarum ad amicos et isthic notos mittere vellem, cumque prae caeteris, an ad te meas dirigere possit, ab eo percontarer, id officij in se recepit. Existimavi autem eum tamdiu hîc mansurum, donec allatis tuis ultimis (de quibus dixi) literis e Bennaticha, ijs secundum singula earundem contenta, quatenus in hac urbe strepitus et interpellationes aulicae ferunt, respondere possem; at is hodie me in hospitio quaerens se circa meridiem hinc abiturum in ijs hominibus significavit, quare ne plane mearum vacuus ad vos rediret, has ieiunas et breviusculas, potius quam nullas ad te exarandas duxi. Quoniam vero literae tuae ad manus non sunt, nonnulla saltem, quae ex ijs recordor, attingam.

Conceptiones tuas de aequinoctijs et motu octavae Sphaerae aliter salvando, quas promittis, avide exspecto. Stellam Polarem ante plurima secula, quemadmodum reliqua omnia octavae Sphaerae (uti vocant) affixa sidera in eodem coeli loco haerere, facile tibi concessero, modo declinationem et ascensionem rectam, tum quoque longitudinem et nonnihil etiam latitudinem, tam in hac quam in alijs alteratas fatearis. Quod autem id ipsum evenerit, non tam octavae Sphaerae promotione quam aequinoctiorum anticipatione, prout magnus ille COPERNICUS pariter animadvertisse videtur, etiam tibi astipulor. Sed in hoc cardo negotij vertitur, quomodo anticipatio illa intelligenda et exsequenda sit. Per motum axis terreni, prout speculatus est COPERNICUS, reciprocum et liberatum annuaeque restitutioni non plane coincidentem, id evenire frustranea, imo prorsus absurda est assumtio nec apparentijs vel hoc seculo, nedum caeteris omnibus satisfacit. Ego ipsum Solem hanc varietatem efficere statuo, dum aliam atque aliam diversis seculis designat Eclipticam, aliasque eiusdem cum Aequatore intersectiones retrorsum et quidem non admodum aequaliter inducit, prout suo loco latius Deo volente ostensurus sum. Nam et infimam Lunam non multum dissimili ratione suam orbitam singulis mensibus variare depraehendi, ita ut non saltem latitudo eius maxima ad tertiam gradus partem (quanta etiam omnino in Eclipticae obliquitate hactenus comperta est differentia) mutetur, sed et nodi et intersectiones cum Ecliptica, licet antrorsum motu aequali, reciproce tamen et inaequali ratione id fieri satisque notabili discrimine, quod grad. 1¾ attingere queat, cognovi, prout lunaris curriculi restitutio propediem, favente Numine, in Progymnasmatis nostris publicanda plenius patefaciet. Si tibi aliqua ratio a nostra forte diversa innotuit, et ex historijs antiquissimis ratisque observationibus comperta est, quâ haec commode explicari queant, vellem eam mecum communicari. Interim ut dicam id quod res est, assequi non possum, id quod tam in his literis, quam alibi innuisti: Polum nimirum mundi alium esse a Polo aequinoctialis et lineas meridianas moveri. Videtur id enim minus convenire, nisi forte tuam sententiam, quae paulo obscurior est, necdum percipiam, quare hac de re uberius a te instrui cupio.

Pro MANILIO in integrum a te restituto mihique transmisso plurimas habeo gratias. Perlustravi eundem successivis horis, prout otium tulit, mihique tua industria et perspicacitas in hoc authore a mendis vindicando et expoliendo, quae etiam in plurimis alijs satis explorata est, summopere placuit.

Plura scriberem, si literae tuae in promptu essent, ulteriorem procul dubio materiam suppeditaturae, modo etiam id ferret huius loci irrequietudo, et is, qui hasce allaturus est, abitum adeo festinanter non acceleraret; quare haec subito effusa Illustris Dominatio tua boni consulat, meque favore suo prosequi pergat, ac per internuncij oportunitatem suis cultissimis literis invisere non intermittat, quaeso.

Vale.
Dabantur Pragae Bohemorum die 23 Iulij stylo novo Anno Epochae Christianae Dionysiacae et vulgatae 1600.
Illustris Dominationis tuae
studiosissimus
TYCHO BRAHE,
manu propria scripsi.

[Postscript — restored from Dreyer, Opera Omnia VIII, p. 330; dropped by the working transcription. See Apparatus.]
Cum has literas ante 6 fere septimanas ad te exarassem, discesserat Batavus ille, qui eas tibi traditurus erat, una vel altera hora citius quam existimassem, ideoque eas huc usque mecum reservare oportuit, donec alia sese offerret occasio eas ad te dirigendi.

TYCHO BRAHE TO JOSEPH SCALIGER.
Illustrious and noble Man, most honoured Lord and Friend.

Six weeks have elapsed, unless I am mistaken, since at the imperial castle of Benátky, where the seat of my dwelling now is, I received Your illustrious Lordship's letter, written at Leiden on the 6th day before the Ides of March [10 March] — most full not only of the eminent erudition in which you excel, but also of humanity and benevolence toward me (for which too I most dutifully give thanks). But since I was lately summoned by the Sacred Imperial Majesty, our most clement Lord — after he had lately returned hither, from Pilsen (where for three quarters of a year he was absent on account of the plague), to the castle of his accustomed residence — for His Majesty deigned most clemently to speak with me of certain things pertaining to the studies I pursue (as has been done amply, once and again): it happened that, two days ago, a noble and learned young man here, Nicolaus Suckerback of Meissen, newly come from Italy, recognised and accosted me as I walked among others at court (a certain Baron pointing me out), and indicated that he was a native of Holland and meant to go thither shortly, asking whether I wished to send any letters to friends and acquaintances there; and when I inquired of him whether, above all, he could direct mine to you, he took the office upon himself. I had thought he would stay here long enough for me, when your last letter (of which I spoke) was brought from Benátky, to answer it point by point according to its contents, so far as the din and the courtly interruptions of this city allow; but today, seeking me at my lodging, he signified, in the presence of those men, that he would depart hence about midday — wherefore, lest he return to you quite empty of anything of mine, I judged these meagre and rather brief lines should be written to you, rather than none. And since your letter is not at hand, I will touch at least on a few things I remember from it.

Your conceptions about the equinoxes, and about saving the motion of the eighth Sphere otherwise, which you promise, I eagerly await. That the Pole Star, many ages ago — like all the other fixed stars affixed to the eighth Sphere (as they call it) — stuck in the same place of the heaven, I will readily grant you, provided you confess that its declination and right ascension, and also its longitude, and even somewhat its latitude, were altered, both in this [star] and in the others. And that this very thing came about not so much by an advance of the eighth Sphere as by the anticipation of the equinoxes — as that great man Copernicus likewise seems to have observed — I agree with you too. But on this the hinge of the matter turns: how that anticipation is to be understood and carried out. That it comes about by a motion of the earth's axis (as Copernicus speculated) — reciprocal and librating, and not quite coinciding with the annual restitution — is a vain, nay wholly absurd assumption, and does not satisfy the appearances even of this age, let alone all the rest. I hold that the Sun itself effects this variation, in that in different ages it designates a different and different Ecliptic, and induces different intersections of the same with the Equator, backward, and indeed not quite uniformly — as in its place, God willing, I shall show more fully. For I have detected that the lowest [planet], the Moon, varies its orbit in not very dissimilar fashion every month, so that not only is its greatest latitude changed by a third part of a degree (as much difference as has hitherto been found in the obliquity of the Ecliptic), but I have also learned that the nodes and intersections with the Ecliptic — though by a forward, uniform motion — come about nevertheless reciprocally and by an unequal ratio, and with a notable enough difference, which can reach 1¾° — as the restitution of the lunar course, soon (Heaven favouring) to be published in our Progymnasmata, will more fully disclose. If some reasoning, perhaps different from ours, has become known to you — and has been ascertained from the most ancient histories and from reliable observations — by which these things can conveniently be explained, I should wish it communicated to me. Meanwhile, to say the thing as it is, I cannot follow what you hinted both in this letter and elsewhere: namely that the Pole of the world is other than the Pole of the equinoctial, and that the meridian lines move. For that seems less fitting — unless perhaps I do not yet grasp your opinion, which is a little obscure; wherefore I desire to be more amply instructed by you on this matter.

For the Manilius, restored entire by you and transmitted to me, I give many thanks. I have scanned it through in successive hours, as leisure allowed; and your industry and perspicacity in vindicating this author from corruptions and polishing him — well proved in very many others too — pleased me in the highest degree.

I would write more, were your letter at hand (which would doubtless supply further matter), if only the restlessness of this place allowed it, and if he who is to bring these did not hasten his departure so hurriedly. Wherefore may Your illustrious Lordship take in good part these things suddenly poured out, and continue to pursue me with your favour, and not fail, when a messenger offers, to visit me with your most cultivated letters, I pray.

Farewell.
Given at Prague of the Bohemians, the 23rd day of July, New Style, in the year of the Christian, Dionysian, and common Era 1600.
Of Your illustrious Lordship
the most zealous,
Tycho Brahe, written in my own hand.

[Postscript:] When I had written this letter to you nearly six weeks ago, that Hollander who was to deliver it to you had departed one or two hours sooner than I had supposed; and so I was obliged to keep it by me until now, until another occasion should offer itself of directing it to you.

Apparatus

  • Recipient. Joseph Justus Scaliger (1540–1609), then at Leiden — the supreme chronologist and classical philologist of the age (cf. the chronology jest at brahe-t_16000327002). One of two giants meeting on contested astronomical ground.
  • Significance — a substantive astronomical letter (precession theory + Tycho's lunar discoveries).
  • Precession / the eighth sphere. Tycho concedes the fixed stars hold their place but insists their declination, right ascension, longitude, and even latitude are altered; agrees with Scaliger (and Copernicus) that the cause is the anticipation of the equinoxes (precession), not an advance of the eighth sphere — the crux being how.
  • Rejection of Copernican axis-libration. Tycho dismisses precession by a reciprocal, librating motion of the earth's axis as "a vain, nay wholly absurd assumption."
  • Tycho's solar theory of precession. "I hold that the Sun itself effects this variation," designating a different ecliptic in different ages and shifting its equator-intersections backward, non-uniformly — to be expounded in the Progymnasmata.
  • Tycho's lunar discoveries. The Moon's orbit varies monthly: greatest latitude changes by ⅓° (variation of the inclination) and the nodes move reciprocally and unequally by up to 1¾° — to be published in the Progymnasmata. A precise, datable statement of Tycho's lunar theory.
  • The disputed point. Tycho cannot follow Scaliger's hint that "the Pole of the world is other than the Pole of the equinoctial, and the meridian lines move," and asks for fuller instruction.
  • The Manilius. Thanks for Scaliger's edition of Manilius (the Roman Astronomica), praising his text-critical skill.
  • The bearer. A chance courier, Nicolaus Suckerback of Meissen, lately back from Italy, a native of Holland, who recognised Tycho at court and offered to carry letters to Leiden — hence the apologetic haste. Not further identified.
  • Setting. Written from Prague (residence at Benátky), the Emperor lately returned from Pilsen (absent three quarters of a year for the plague) and consulting Tycho.
  • Date. die 23 Iulij stylo novo … 1600 → 23 July 1600 [N.S.] (identifier, label, and dateline all agree). Note the precise era formula "the Christian, Dionysian, and common Era" — a courtesy to the chronologist. (See the duplicate-record note above: the June-dated 16000623001 is the same letter mis-dated.)
  • Persons & works named. Joseph Justus Scaliger; Copernicus; Manilius (Scaliger's edition); Nicolaus Suckerback of Meissen (courier); Rudolf II; the Progymnasmata. Places: Prague, Benátky, Pilsen, Leiden, Meissen, Italy, Holland.
  • §1 Dreyer collation. Delivery-delay postscript (the Hollander courier left early; the letter held six weeks), restored from Dreyer VIII p.330 (dropped by the working transcription).
  • Correction. Corrected the English rendering of 'in ijs hominibus significavit': Suckerback himself signified his midday departure 'in the presence of those men' (unnamed bystanders), not 'by his servants' as previously translated, which had invented an agent not present in the Latin (Dreyer, TBOO VIII p.328).
