The original

CLARISSIMO VARIOQUE DOCTRINARUM GENERE ERUDITISSIMO EXCELLENTISSIMOQUE VIRO, DOMINO THADDÆO HAGGECIO AB HAYCK, PHILOSOPHIÆ ET MEDICINÆ DOCTORI EXIMIO, AMICO SUO INPRIMIS DILECTO.

MIRATUS equidem non parum sum, clarissime et præstantissime Domine THADDÆE, amice summopere dilecte, quod tota hac elapsa æstate nihil a te literarum receperim. Verum idipsum, quod ex tuis iamprimum circa initia Octobris redditis cognovi, te minus prospera usum fuisse valetudine, alijsque occupationibus distentum, iam antea suspicabar. Video te responsioni meæ ad tua dubia et obiecta, circa disputationem prioribus literis motam, iam plane acquiescere. Quæ tua ingenuitas admodum mihi grata est. Facis hac in parte multo syncerius, quam plerique, qui solo contradicendi studio, sua, licet sibiipsis suspecta, mordicus tueri solent. Quæ res Philosophiam (ut de Religionis negotio nihil dicam) non parum hactenus turbavit, et nondum finis.

Facis quidem mentionem effigierum tibi transmissarum, quodque Magnificus Dominus CURTIUS de Epigrammate, eo nomine ab arguto quodam Poeta concinnando, prospexerit. Pro qua erga me benevolentia gratias habeo. Attamen hoc non tanti referebat, atque librum transmissum (qui ea quæ Tomo primo de Nova Stella tunc typis mandata fuêre, continebat) excutere, vestramque de his eruditam censuram perscribere, quemadmodum etiam de Idæis quorundam ex meis instrumentis in parva forma expressis et capsulæ cuidam inclusis, quæ omnia Tabellario isti seniori ad vos redeunti perferenda tradidi. Quare cum de illis, quæ maioris erant momenti, quam chartaceæ istæ effigies obiter saltem communicatæ, ne verbo quidem in hac Epistola memineris, vereor, ut ista vobis allata sint, forte, quod senex ille onus portandi detrectârit, quod tamen non invite hîc in se recepit, aut quod illi in via quidpiam humanitus acciderit. Quicquid id est, rogo peramanter, velis prima quaque commoditate me certiorem de his reddere, et si liber is, de quo loquor, de Nova Stella affatim tractans, ad tuas manus pervenit, amplissimi Domini CURTII et tuam, quemadmodum tum quoque petebam, de eius contentis sententiam indicare ne graveris, tum etiam, quid vobis de instrumentorum conformatione, parvis modulis repræsentatâ, videatur. Fui et ego totâ hac æstate plurimis distractionibus et avocamentis impeditus, quo minus susceptam telam laborum meorum pertexere commodum fuerit. Quamprimum opportuniore otio eorum, quæ dudum suscepta sunt, quidpiam typis absolvere licuerit, vos eius participes reddere non intermittam.

Intelligo ex scheda literis tuis inclusa senem illum M. PETRUM JACHENAW nomine, qui semel atque iterum mecum aliquandiu fuit, Pragam, Cæsareæ Maiestati in artificijs quibusdam mechanicis suam operam offerendi gratiâ accessisse. Et certe quantum ad currum attinet (qui itineris confecti spatia scite et iucunde, instar horologij cuiusdam, denotat, eadem sonitu campanularum, prout libuerit, auribus ingerit) confectionem eius artificiosam, quæ et usui commoda sit et constans permaneat, egregie callet, diuque in ista machinatione varie efformanda versatus est. Nam et aliquot Germaniæ Principibus eiuscemodi currus confecit, tum quoque quendam, ab Illustrissimo Duce Megapolitano, Serenissimo Regi nostro laudabilis memoriæ dono missum. Quin et meo currui, quo subinde in hac Insula cum amicis circumvehor, tale quoddam Automatum, cum ultimo hîc esset, adaptavit; quod tum integra miliaria, tum etiam eorundem diversas subdivisiones, adeoque in minuta sexagenaria distributionem tam indicibus aliquot ostendit, quam pulsu sonitum duarum campanularum discriminatim edendo manifestat. Ut ob id indubia fide testari possim, nullam sub hoc opere, quod ille profitetur, subesse imposturam, sed omnia rite et constanter non sine delectabili atque utili spectaculo succedere. Si itaque virum hunc, qui per se est integer et probatæ vitæ atque morum, prænominato Domino CURTIO ita commendaveris, ut eius intercessione apud Cæsaream Maiestatem vel alios illustres et opulentos Dominos, suam in curribus huiusmodi parandis operam elocare possit, atque aliquid lucelli hinc percipere, feceris mihi rem inprimis acceptam. Illum enim ob perspectam eius integritatem et fidem diligo, quodque in hoc senio pro sustentatione sui ipsius et familiæ hincinde peregrinari necesse habeat, ipsi condoleo. Solet is, rebus suis domi in angustiam redactis, nonnunquam huc Vismariâ transmarina, ubi habitat, ad me in Daniam traijcere. Et quoniam totam hyemem præteritam hîc mecum consumpsit, fieri facile potuit, ut me aliquoties de te verba facientem (prout retulit) audierit. Est enim alias satis veridicus. Tu eius conatibus, quod te vel meo nomine facturum non diffido, ne defueris, quo aliquando huc reversus meam hanc qualemcunque commendationem sibi non infrugiferam fuisse deprædicet. Sed de hoc forte paulo prolixior sum, quod in altero, cuius etiam in literis meministi, brevitate compensabo, de eo vix verculum additurus, neque enim dignus est. Ipsemet brevi apud vos, quemadmodum et antea ubique alibi, qualis sit, prodet, nisi forte religiosa illa (si Dijs placet) metamorphosis eum a seipso difformem reddiderit. Vale et magnificum Dominum CURTIUM a me officiose saluta, et si literas, quas semel atque iterum ad illum postmodum dedi, accepit, ut per oblatam a gravioribus Reipubl. curis vacationem respondere et de rebus Mathematicis iucunde mecum conferre non gravetur, rogatum habe.

Iterum diu multumque vale.

To the most renowned and, in every varied kind of learning, most erudite and most excellent man, Master Thaddaeus Hagecius of Hájek, distinguished Doctor of Philosophy and Medicine, his especially beloved friend.

I have indeed wondered not a little, most renowned and most outstanding Master Thaddaeus, my exceedingly beloved friend, that throughout this whole past summer I received no letter from you. Yet the very thing which I learned only now from yours, delivered around the beginning of October — that you had been enjoying less than prosperous health, and were taken up with other occupations — I had already suspected beforehand. I see that you now fully rest content with my reply to your doubts and objections, concerning the dispute raised in my earlier letters. This candour of yours is very welcome to me. In this respect you act much more sincerely than most, who, from the mere zeal of contradiction, are wont to defend tooth and nail their own positions, suspect though [these be] even to themselves. This thing has hitherto troubled Philosophy not a little (to say nothing of the business of Religion), and there is as yet no end.

You do indeed make mention of the portraits sent to you, and that the Magnificent Master Curtius has made provision regarding an epigram, to be composed under that head by some clever poet. For which goodwill towards me I give thanks. Nevertheless this did not matter so much as to examine the book sent [to you] (which contained those things about the New Star that were then committed to print in the first volume), and to write out your learned judgement of these — just as also [your judgement] of the designs of certain of my instruments, rendered in small format and enclosed in a certain little case, all of which I handed over to that elder courier returning to you, to be carried [there]. Wherefore, since of those things which were of greater moment than those paper portraits — communicated only in passing — you make mention not even with a word in this letter, I fear that these may [not] have been brought to you, perhaps because that old man declined the burden of carrying [them] — which, however, he undertook here not unwillingly — or because something befell him on the way, as happens to men. Whatever it is, I beg most lovingly, [that] you be willing at the very first convenience to inform me about these matters; and if that book of which I speak, treating fully of the New Star, has reached your hands, do not be reluctant to indicate the most ample Master Curtius's opinion and your own of its contents — just as I requested then too — and likewise what seems [good] to you regarding the configuration of the instruments, represented in small models. I too throughout this whole summer have been hindered by very many distractions and interruptions, so that it has not been convenient to weave through to the end the web of my labours that I had undertaken. As soon as, with more suitable leisure, I am permitted to complete in print some part of those things that were long since undertaken, I shall not fail to make you a sharer in it.

I understand from the note enclosed in your letter that the old man named Master Peter Jachenaw — who once and again was with me for some while — has gone to Prague, for the sake of offering his service to His Imperial Majesty in certain mechanical contrivances. And certainly, as far as concerns the coach (which marks the stretches of road accomplished skilfully and pleasantly, after the manner of a kind of clock, and the same it brings to the ears, as one wishes, by the sound of little bells), he is excellently skilled in its ingenious construction, [such] as is both convenient for use and remains constant, and he has long been engaged in fashioning this device in various ways. For he has built coaches of this kind for several Princes of Germany, and also a certain one [that was] sent as a gift by the Most Illustrious Duke of Mecklenburg to our Most Serene King of laudable memory. Indeed to my own coach, in which from time to time I ride about this Island with friends, he fitted such a self-acting mechanism [Automatum], when he was last here; which shows both whole miles, and also their several subdivisions, and indeed their division into sexagesimal minutes, both by several indicators, and likewise reveals [them] by striking, by giving out the sound of two little bells distinctly. So that on this account I can testify with undoubted credit that there underlies this work, which he professes, no imposture, but that all things proceed duly and constantly, not without a delightful and useful spectacle. If, therefore, you will so commend this man — who is of himself upright and of approved life and character — to the aforenamed Master Curtius, that by his intercession with His Imperial Majesty or with other illustrious and wealthy Lords he may be able to place out his service in preparing coaches of this kind, and to gain some little profit thereby, you will do me an especially welcome thing. For I esteem him on account of his manifest integrity and fidelity, and I feel for him, since in this old age he must travel here and there for the support of himself and his family. He is wont, when his affairs at home are reduced to straits, sometimes to cross over to me into Denmark from Wismar across the sea, where he lives. And since he spent this whole past winter here with me, it could easily have happened that he heard me several times speaking words about you (as he reported). For he is otherwise truthful enough. Do not fail his endeavours — which I do not doubt you will do even in my name — so that, returned here at some time, he may proclaim that this commendation of mine, such as it is, was not unfruitful to him. But on this I am perhaps a little too long-winded, which I shall make up for by brevity in the other [man], of whom too you made mention in your letter, scarcely going to add a little word about him — for he is not worthy of it. He himself will shortly reveal among you, just as before everywhere else, what manner of man he is, unless perhaps that pious (God save the mark) metamorphosis has rendered him unlike himself. Farewell, and greet the magnificent Master Curtius dutifully on my behalf; and if he received the letters which I sent to him once and again afterwards, hold him entreated that, by such leisure as is afforded by [respite from] the weightier cares of the Commonwealth, he would not be reluctant to reply and to confer pleasantly with me about Mathematical matters.

Again, long and much, farewell.
Given at Uraniborg, on the [—] day, the year 1591.

POSTSCRIPT. I had indeed resolved to breathe not a word about that Plagiarist and Thief, holding him unworthy even of mention. But while this letter, for a day or two, waits here for a wind and a ship to carry it across to Copenhagen, I have meanwhile come upon another, your most recent letter, in which you make mention of that Ursus of Dithmarschen. And since I see that in both letters you speak of that man in the same way — the man I had accused of plagiarism — indeed, that in the later one you even tacitly excuse him somewhat, and go so far as to praise his character, as though you hesitated whether he ought rightly and simply be called a Plagiarist, perhaps because with his crafty and cunning words he has so cleared himself in your eyes: I am therefore compelled, willing or not, to write to you more openly and at greater length about this, so that you may know more certainly how the matter stands.

This Ursus of Dithmarschen was here, seven or eight years ago unless I am mistaken, in the retinue of my intimate friend Erik Lange, whom he was then serving. And when for fourteen days that same Erik, together with certain other Barons and noblemen whom he had brought with him, had passed the time genially with me, as happens, and, once they had been dismissed, had settled himself to philosophize with me (for he is not unacquainted with the liberal arts, and loves them, above all indeed our Urania — and if under this name you also understand my sister, otherwise called Sophia, now a young widow, all as one, since he too is accustomed to call her by that name, the matter itself will show that I have said nothing far from the truth; for he loved her, and is eager to take her to wife) — he, I say, Erik, being by then weary of the Bacchic drink and desiring to be refreshed by our Urania, began, among other things, to ask whether some scheme might not be found by which the absurdity that must guard against the motion of the Earth introduced by Copernicus, and likewise the superfluity and inelegance of the Ptolemaic system, might nonetheless be done away with — and yet in such a way that the celestial appearances might be satisfied in every respect, and elegantly. For he had sensed that I was contriving something of the kind, having heard me let slip from my own mouth more than once that neither the one scheme nor the other agreed with the truth, but that a far more convenient and more probable one could be given. And since it is not my custom to deprive my Erik willingly of his wishes, so far as lies in my power — but, glancing back over my shoulder, I noticed that among the other attendants this Ditmarsian too was standing by the table, and that he, impudent as he was, was hanging on our conversation more eagerly than the rest — I signalled to Erik with a nod that he should send him off somewhere; and so that he should not grasp the matter, and should not return too soon, we at once gave him a subject which he was to compose separately in verse, for he also professed himself a would-be poet.

Meanwhile, having snatched up a piece of chalk, I sketched out on the table for my aforesaid Erik both the Ptolemaic and the Copernican speculations concerning the celestial motions, and laid open the absurdities of each. And when he further inquired about some third, more fitting form, I sketched out for him, in general terms and succinctly, our own discovery in this matter also, and explained it — a sketch which we afterwards, so far as it could be done, rubbed out again (for it had been drawn upon the green cloth that covered the table). But that sharp-nosed Ursus, sniffing out that we had kept this hidden from him — whether from those traces which perhaps still remained on the cloth, or from a certain paper which lay tossed among other rubbish in my Museum, where he used to be, or from one of my Students, to whom this had perhaps become known, or from wherever else in the end — conceived in his mind some such idea of our Hypothesis.

And not very long after he had been dismissed by Erik on account of his tasteless, arrogant, and foolish manners, passing through Holstein and coming to Magdeburg, he boasted a great many astonishing things to that most learned man Georg Rollenhagen, chief Rector of the School there, about this invention of the Hypothesis, which he falsely claimed for himself — to such a degree that, when he was questioned by that man, no novice in Mathematics, about certain points concerning the saving of the retrogradations and stations of the Planets, he could not extricate himself; and from this Rollenhagen perceived clearly enough that he was not the first author of this invention. And this, not very long after, he made known by letter both to the most noble Lord Heinrich Rantzau and to myself, and was the very first of all to expose this plagiarism — long before that feeble and insufficient Fundamentum, scraped together, or rather filched, from various authors, was too presumptuously thrown out into the world by that Ursus.

The same man also went prowling about and secretly searched out many other things of mine here, while for so many days I was indulging, as I have said, my good spirits rather than my wits, with Erik and my other noble companions; and he copied out most of them, and kept some in memory, which afterwards — being possessed, as he is, of a brazen face — he did not blush to peddle as his own. Such are the things he brings forward concerning the abbreviated computation of Triangles carried out by prosthaphaeresis, concerning instruments, the Sextant and the Quadrant, concerning the restitution of the fixed stars by means of the star Venus and the Sun as intermediaries, and other similar matters.

For as far as the abbreviated methods for Triangles are concerned, I long ago, when Wittich was here, discussed them with him freely and openly, and we shared with one another what had been discovered in this matter both by him and by me. But Wittich himself, afterwards returning to his own country, and thence proceeding to the most illustrious Prince Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, and having stayed there some while, disclosed to the Prince's Automaton-maker, Jost Bürgi — an unlettered man indeed, but a most industrious mechanical craftsman — all the mysteries of the Triangles that we had worked out together; and likewise made public there the construction of certain instruments of mine, and the method of their graduation and of the sights upon them, so that the Prince soon had both a Sextant and a Quadrant made in imitation of mine (though Wittich did not admit even this), with which it was afterwards granted them to attend to observations more precisely and more successfully than before, as you will learn from the Astronomical letters written back and forth between us.

But after Wittich's departure, this Ursus, not lingering long in any one station or den — perhaps in imitation of the bears of the forest — likewise hastened to Kassel, and offered my hypotheses to the Prince as his own. And when they were eager to reduce these into an Automaton, although they had got hold of a sufficiently competent mechanical craftsman, they could not manage it until Rothmann arrived on the scene, who wove in certain particulars there in imitation of Copernicus; for this Ursus had at least snatched away the more general points, as commonly happens with thieves, who out of fear and the wavering of a guilty conscience often leave certain not unuseful things untouched. Indeed, he also drank in more fully there the method of observing and restoring the positions of the stars — a method he had here seen in part, but which was disclosed there by Wittich, and afterwards more fully explained in the exchange of letters between the Landgrave and his Mathematician and myself — and, which was the chief thing (though it turned out very badly), he obtained from the said Jost Bürgi the abbreviated demonstration of the Triangles and their resolution into numbers, which Wittich had granted to him; and also certain other things, in payment for which he translated the whole of Copernicus into the German tongue — as Christoph Rothmann informed me of these and other matters, not so much by letter as in person, two years ago, when he had been sent here by the Prince for the purpose of viewing my instruments.

But this Ursus, not lingering long there either, soon crept off to Strasbourg, and after he had for some time set forth privately to Students, for the sake of gain, the things he had seen, learned, and pilfered here and at Kassel, at length — maimed and mutilated as they were in most respects — he was not afraid to publish them abroad under his own name, with too arrogant and showy a title; wherein, having by some new and tasteless precedent dedicated some part of these scrapings gathered from here and there, in name only, to every Mathematician he knew, he passed over me alone in silence (in which I even congratulate myself, that he did not befoul my name with his thefts), lest, if it had become known to others from this that he had some acquaintance with me, his fraud and plagiarism should the more quickly be detected — although the greater part of what he sets forth there, except in so far as he corrupts it or misuses it, issued from my own workshop.

But so that you may be certain the matter stands thus, especially as regards the shaping of the Hypothesis, I have enclosed the very discarded sheet itself, which I mentioned before, from which, beyond doubt, he drew this speculation while he was here — a sheet which, as soon as I had been alerted to this plagiarism by the said Rollenhagen, one of my Students, having searched it out among the other papers, brought to me, reporting that this sheet, while this Ursus was here, had lain openly among the other papers in my Museum, so that he could easily have conceived from it the notions which he published as his own. That he had these things, which he falsely claimed as his own, from this source, is proved sufficiently, if by nothing else, by the fact that in this very old sketch the orb of Mars, through some carelessness, wholly encloses that of the Sun — and it was for this reason, perhaps, that this sheet had been thrown away by me as useless — just as it is likewise in his own diagram, in imitation of which these things are wrongly drawn, the rest that he has added on being stitched in at his own pleasure and contributing nothing solid to the matter. I desire, however, that this very sheet be surely returned to me.

Moreover, the aforenamed Gellius Sascerides, who was a member of my household for eight years, when, on his way from here to Italy, he stayed for some time in Germany and then passed through Strasbourg, and there found this Ursus boasting of his Fundamentum, scraped together from the stones of others, told him plainly to his face that he falsely attributed to himself the invention of that Hypothesis: for it was entirely mine, and he himself had known it in my company several years before; and that this crude error concerning the orb of Mars, which in his diagram wholly encompasses that of the Sun, sufficiently betrays that he does not understand these foundations, still less that he could be the author of this invention. And he charged him with many other things stolen from me besides. But he, overspread as he is with shameless cunning, did not scruple to swear falsely that he had not had this Hypothesis from me; nonetheless, he was compelled by that same Gellius to confess that he did not rightly understand it, and had wrongly drawn the orb of Mars in it. How then, I ask, could he himself have discovered things which, once discovered by another, he did not rightly understand — nay, rather corrupted? Whereas otherwise it is easy enough to add something to what has already been discovered. From the very letters of this same Gellius, which he wrote to me at length on this matter, both from Strasbourg and afterwards from Italy, and which I have enclosed here — to be returned to me, however, surely and safely — you will learn beyond all doubt that the matter stands thus.

And that young man, who has now passed his thirtieth year, is indeed not only thoroughly learned in various sciences, but is also endowed with integrity of character and a singular gravity, and above all, if anyone is, most scrupulously observant of truth, the love of which he has often heard most earnestly commended by me. Indeed, even if there were nothing else to prove that he obtained this Sketch, by whatever trick in the end, from my invention, and that I did not borrow it from his rag-bag scraped together here and there, this will become plain enough from the fact that my own second book, On the More Recent Phenomena of the Aetherial World, had long since been printed before his foundational rhapsodies ever came to light. And when they [my pages] did come out, [and] I had already devised these hypotheses before, the celestial appearances themselves — [if] examined closely — impelling me to this, although up to that time I had resolved to withhold this hypothesis for the whole work on the Restoration of Astronomy; but fearing this very thing — that it would happen just as with this Ursus, and with a certain other Scot too, who, having learned this here in the course of conversation, afterwards privately proposed it to his own students at Rostock, yet who, unlike this Ursus, did not deny with a candid mind that it was my invention, but testified that he had conceived this scheme here in my company — fearing, I say, this very thing and things like it, I judged it more advisable, for a certain particular reason, to insert this hypothesis, setting it ahead of the rest, in that second Volume.

As far as the Triangles are concerned, you will see in its proper place, in one or another of my Volumes, certain teachings on these matters, which I prescribed to my Students many years ago already, so that they might thereby have an easier method of Astronomical calculation. And since a copy of these lay open to view on each of the tables of our Museum (so that almost every one of my Students has his own particular copy at hand), it was very easy for this Ursus, playing the sly Parasite for so many days, to lick these up — although he could scarcely have acquired their Geometrical demonstrations here, for I had kept those apart among certain of my own writings of greater importance, which he later obtained at Kassel from the Landgrave's Automaton-maker, on the occasion I have mentioned.

Indeed, I also have many other things, written in his own hand, which he secretly copied out here, and which, along with the rest, he had set his mind on carrying off. Although he always used to say openly that everything he saw here was superfluous and of no consequence, he did not blush to boast that with three pieces of wood he would accomplish more, more correctly and more quickly, than I with all my instruments and machines — an art which he called 'Tridendronic.' I do not know whether he understood this to mean the three pieces of gallows-wood, which — together with the epigram written below, in his likeness — I had had painted, for that very purpose. If that is so, let him keep this method of observing the stars for himself; indeed no one will envy him it, but will rather pity him for it. The epigram, moreover, was of this kind:

He who first founded the Tridendronic art
was to be known by this face and this beard.
No wonder that his features come to us in so dim a likeness —
he who agrees so well with three pieces of wood.
But bound he must be: whether one day joined to them himself,
or made to sustain the office of the one who binds.

Indeed, so that he may not, on any just pretext, deny that he copied out here, without my knowledge, a great many things of mine, I still have to this day a whole bundle of the things which he had secretly written out here in his own hand, and was eager to carry off with him. For I had arranged with Erik, his master, that these should be secretly carried off from him again by night, once he was buried in wine and sleep — and this on one day or another before he left here — although the one who was to render him this like for like was not clever enough, and did not even find these purloined things; just as he himself likewise did not manage to snatch up entire everything he wished to carry off — whether you understand this of his wit or of his hand.

But if he persists in his malevolence, and does not confess and beg pardon for the plagiarism, but perversely and mendaciously excuses himself, I shall see to it that he is publicly accused there, convicted, and — unless I spare him on account of the certain acquaintance with Mathematics he has acquired — punished according to the laws, as he deserves. For indeed I have quite just grounds, should I wish, for exercising this vengeance, since he has not only treacherously snatched away certain things of mine, won by many nights' watching, labours, and expenses, and mendaciously ascribed them to himself, but has also, with his petulant and malicious lies, attempted — so far as it lay in him — to belittle my reputation and honour at Kassel, in the court of the Landgrave, and perhaps elsewhere too, as you may see from the letters of Christoph Rothmann, in the portion of the volume of Astronomical Letters sent to you, page 33, near the end of them.

Although I have not deigned to answer these trifles with so much as a single word, because calumny, lies, and scurrility lead themselves in the end into absurdity. Nor should you allow him to write me anything at all, for I already have his crafty and futile pretexts sufficiently seen through and suspected on other grounds; and I have come to know the man more than is needful — yet not, as you still suppose, humane, straightforward, free of envy, frugal, and pure through and through (if you understand these words by antithesis, you will perhaps judge more rightly: a snake lies hidden in the grass). Nor do I doubt that this Shrew will soon betray himself by his own telltale sign, so that it will be left in no obscurity for you too to know him for what he is. I will add no more, although other things too regarding his crafty and treacherous character have come to my notice; for he is not worthy that even these things should be written of him, as I said before too, although they are true enough and more than enough. Nor do I wish you to bring these things out into the open, but rather to keep them known only to Master Curtius and yourself, unless some necessity should press. For he is already, by himself, sufficiently infamous, and will make himself more infamous still hereafter, unless he takes on other manners, so that there is no need for him to be made more notorious through others.

Written at Uraniborg, on the fourteenth day of March, in the year 1592.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Thaddaeus Haggecius ab Hayck = Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku / Thaddaeus Hagecius (1525–1600), the Bohemian court physician, astronomer, and mathematician to Emperors Maximilian II and Rudolf II in Prague; like Tycho an observer of the 1572 nova (his Dialexis de novae … stellae of 1574), and Tycho's principal Prague correspondent and the man who later (1599) helped open the imperial court to him.
  • Sender / place. Tycho Brahe, writing from Uraniborg on Hven (Uraniburgum, the edition's convention). hac Insula = "this Island" = Hven.
  • Magnificus Dominus Curtius = Jakob Kurtz von Senftenau (Jacobus Curtius), Imperial Vice-Chancellor at Rudolf II's court in Prague and himself a mathematical amateur; Tycho's hoped-for court patron, to whom the portraits, the De nova stella volume, and the instrument-models were being routed via Hagecius. Named again at the close (magnificum Dominum Curtium … saluta).
  • librum … qui ea quae Tomo primo de Nova Stella … continebat — the first volume of the Astronomiae instauratae progymnasmata (Tycho's projected great work on the 1572 nova, the "Tomus primus de Nova Stella"), which was being printed at Uraniborg in this period and was not formally published until 1602. Rendered "the first volume … about the New Star"; Nova Stella = the new star / nova of 1572.
  • effigierum … chartaceae istae effigies — "the portraits … those paper portraits": engraved likenesses (of Tycho), distinguished by Tycho as of lesser moment than the book and the instrument-models. Epigramma … ab arguto … Poeta concinnando — an epigram to accompany such a portrait, to be composed by a poet under Curtius's patronage.
  • Idaeis … ex meis instrumentis in parva forma expressis — "the designs of certain of my instruments, rendered in small format": small-scale drawings or models of Tycho's instruments (instrumentorum conformatione, parvis modulis repraesentatâ = "the configuration of the instruments, represented in small models"). instrumenta / organa → instruments; the spelling Idaeis (= ideis, "ideas/designs") is kept as printed and glossed.
  • Tabellario isti seniori — "that elder courier": the same aged man identified two sentences later as Petrus Jachenaw, who had carried the consignment toward Prague; Tycho fears the package miscarried because Hagecius's letter mentions only the portraits.
  • M. Petrus Jachenaw — Peter Jachenaw (form as printed; M. = Magister), an elderly mechanician resident at Wismar (Vismaria transmarina, the Mecklenburg Hanseatic port across the Baltic), maker of odometer-coaches, who had wintered with Tycho on Hven and was now seeking imperial employment at Prague.
  • currus … qui itineris confecti spatia … instar horologij … denotat … sonitu campanularum — the hodometer- or way-wiser coach: a carriage with a geared mechanism that measures and displays distance travelled (whole miles, their subdivisions, down to minuta sexagenaria = sexagesimal/sixtieth parts) and announces it by striking small bells. Automatum = a self-acting mechanism; horologium = clock (the analogy). miliaria = miles; minuta sexagenaria = "sexagesimal minutes" (sixtieths). Such measuring-coaches were a recognized Renaissance court novelty; the description is kept concrete, not modernised to "odometer."
  • Illustrissimo Duce Megapolitano — "the Most Illustrious Duke of Mecklenburg" (Megapolis = Mecklenburg, the latinised form). Serenissimo Regi nostro laudabilis memoriae — "our Most Serene King of laudable memory" = the late Frederik II of Denmark (d. 1588), the formula laudabilis memoriae marking him as deceased.
  • in altero … de eo vix verculum additurus … religiosa illa (si Dijs placet) metamorphosis — the contrasted "other [man]" whom Hagecius had also mentioned and whom Tycho dismisses with contempt ("not worthy" of more than "scarcely a little word [verculum]"). The barbed religiosa illa (si Dijs placet) metamorphosis — "that pious (God save the mark) metamorphosis" — implies a recent religious conversion Tycho disdains; the man is not named in the letter and is left unidentified here rather than guessed. si Dijs placet ("if it please the gods") is the standard sarcastic aside, rendered "God save the mark."
  • Greek / symbols. None in the source (no Greek interpolations, no planet or zodiac glyphs).
  • Negation crux (flagged). In vereor, ut ista vobis allata sint the verb of fearing vereor governs ut + subjunctive, which in the classical idiom of verbs of fearing means a fear that the thing did not happen ("I fear that these have not been brought to you") — the sense the following clauses (the courier may have declined the burden, or met mishap on the way) require. The English supplies the negation in brackets — "I fear that these may [not] have been brought to you" — and it is flagged here because the Latin carries no overt non: the negative is idiomatic, not lexical, and is marked editorially rather than smuggled in.
  • Dating crux (flagged; do not reconcile). The dateline is printed "Datae Uraniburgi die Anno 1591" — die ("on the … day") with the day-number left blank and no month. The header therefore leads with the as-written date "[day blank] 1591" and does not assert a day or month the text does not supply. The edition label reads "1591 (uden dato) og 14. marts 1592" ("1591 [undated] and 14 March 1592"): the two ÖNB witnesses (A = cod. Vindob. lat. 10686.59; B = cod. Vindob. lat. 10686.56) evidently transmit, respectively, this undated 1591 letter and a related/continued piece bearing 14 March 1592; the tekstnet locator carries a notional …15910203001 identifier-date (3 Feb 1591) that the printed dateline does not substantiate. Per the edition's convention the discrepancy is recorded, not resolved: the source text translated here is the undated 1591 letter; no day or month is asserted, and the 14 March 1592 date is not imported into this letter's dateline. O.S. (Denmark kept the Julian calendar; Tycho on Hven wrote Old Style).
  • disputatio … prioribus literis mota — the running scholarly dispute in Tycho's prior letters to Hagecius (their exchange over cometary and astronomical matters); Tycho commends Hagecius for now conceding rather than contending "tooth and nail."
  • telam laborum meorum pertexere — "to weave through to the end the web of my labours," the Ciceronian tela ("web/warp") metaphor for an unfinished literary-scientific work (here the Progymnasmata); kept as metaphor.
  • Closing formula. Iterum diu multumque vale = "Again, long and much, farewell" (an emphatic doubled valediction; cf. Iterum vale, the edition's convention).
  • What the recovered text is. A 2,218-word POSTSCRIPTA appended to Tycho's letter to Thaddaeus Hagecius, dated at the close 14 March 1592 — a year or more after the body of the letter (Uraniborg, 1591), which had evidently sat waiting for a ship to Copenhagen while Tycho received further letters from Hagecius. It is Tycho's own first-person narrative of how NICOLAUS REYMERS BAER, called URSUS ("of Dithmarschen"), came to know the Tychonic geo-heliocentric world-system, written because Hagecius's own letters had begun, in Tycho's view, to go soft on Ursus.
  • The origin scene, told nowhere else in this detail. Tycho gives an eyewitness account of Erik Lange's fortnight-long visit to Hven in September 1584 ("seven or eight years ago") — confirmed independently by Christianson, On Tycho's Island — and Ursus's presence in Lange's retinue. Tycho describes sketching the Ptolemaic, Copernican, and his own third (Tychonic) system in chalk on the green cloth covering the table for Erik Lange, then rubbing it out — and Ursus, hovering at table as a servant, apparently reconstructing the idea from chalk traces, a discarded sketch-sheet left in Tycho's Museum, or a loose-lipped student. This tablecloth episode does not appear in the published letter proper and is a genuinely new primary account of the moment Tycho believed Ursus stole the system.
  • The Erik Lange / "Urania" wordplay identifies Sophie Brahe. Tycho glosses "Urania" (the muse he and Lange discussed astronomy under) as also Erik Lange's pet name for Tycho's sister SOPHIE BRAHE, "now a young widow" (she was widowed in 1588, per Christianson) — matching the courtship that Zeeberg's Urania Titani (1994) takes its very title from. This is a contemporary, Tycho's-own-hand confirmation of that pun, predating Zeeberg's study by four centuries.
  • Names two corroborating witnesses by name, both cited as enclosures. (1) GEORG ROLLENHAGEN, Rector at Magdeburg, who — per Tycho — first caught Ursus out when Ursus could not answer basic questions on planetary stations and retrogradation, and reported this by letter to both Tycho and HEINRICH RANTZAU (matches Oestmann's 2004 edition of the Rollenhagen–Rantzau correspondence). (2) GELLIUS SASCERIDES, Tycho's disciple of eight years, who confronted Ursus face-to-face in Strasbourg and extracted a confession that he did not understand the Mars-orbit error in his own diagram. Tycho states he has physically enclosed with this letter both the original discarded sketch-sheet Ursus allegedly copied from, and Sascerides's own letters recounting the Strasbourg and Italy testimony — explicitly asking Hagecius to return both. These are named physical exhibits in an active evidentiary dossier, not general assertions.
  • The Kassel transmission chain: Wittich → Bürgi → Ursus, with Rothmann completing it. Tycho traces a second leak: PAUL WITTICH, after leaving Hven, disclosed Tycho's compendious triangle-methods and instrument designs to JOST BÜRGI ("the Prince's Automaton-maker," unlettered but highly skilled) at the court of WILHELM IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel; Ursus then visited Kassel himself and obtained further material from Bürgi, though the Kassel automaton (mechanical model) of the system could not be completed until CHRISTOPH ROTHMANN arrived and supplied Copernican particulars. Tycho attributes this account to Rothmann's own report, given in person at Hven roughly two years before writing (i.e. c.1590) — which matches Rothmann's documented visit to Hven, summer 1590.
  • The unnamed "Scot" is almost certainly Duncan Liddel, though not named in the text: "a certain other Scot... who privately proposed [the system] to his own students at Rostock," but — unlike Ursus — openly credited it to Tycho. Dreyer (1890) and Thoren (Lord of Uraniborg) independently record Duncan Liddel lecturing on the Tychonic system at Rostock c.1589/90 while still crediting Tycho, only later drawing a plagiarism accusation himself at Helmstedt. This postscript, datable to March 1592, is an early data point in Liddel's reputational trajectory, and explains — in Tycho's own words — why he rushed the system into print in the second book of De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis (1588) ahead of the full Astronomiae Instauratae: fear that Ursus or Liddel's students would claim priority first.
  • Two authentic cruxes flagged, not smoothed over. (1) The sentence beginning "Cum et prodierint..." (on why Tycho pre-empted publication) is transmitted with two words already bracketed as editorial supplements in the source text itself ("[et]", "[si]") — a genuine textual gap or corruption at a historically important juncture; I have preserved the bracket convention in the English at the corresponding point rather than silently resolving it. (2) The postscript cites "the letters of Christoph Rothmann, in the portion of the volume of Epistolae Astronomicae sent to you, page 33" — but Tycho's Epistolae astronomicae was not printed at Uraniborg until 1596, four years after this postscript's own dateline of 14 March 1592. Either an installment of the Uraniborg press's typesetting was already circulating to Hagecius years before formal publication, or this line was added/updated after the postscript's ostensible date — the record's own catalogue note ("1591 [undated] and 14 March 1592") already signals a composite, multi-layered document. This is flagged, not resolved.
  • The Ursus caricature-and-epigram. Tycho reports commissioning a painted image ("effigiem") of Ursus together with a six-line Latin epigram, built on Ursus's own boast that he could out-observe Tycho's whole instrument-park with "three pieces of wood" (an art he called, in his own coinage, "Tridendronic"). Tycho puns venomously on "tribus lignis" (three sticks) as "tribus lignis patibuli" (the three timbers of a gallows), and the epigram's closing couplet threatens Ursus with being either bound to that wood himself or made to play the executioner's role — a real piece of hostile visual/literary propaganda, distinct from anything in the printed Ursus-Tycho pamphlet war. The running animal wordplay (Ursus = 'bear'; "in imitation of the bears of the forest"; "nasutulus" sniffing him out; and finally, near the close, Ursus recast as "Sorex," a shrew) is a consistent authorial signature across the postscript and is worth noting as a stylistic fingerprint.
