The original

CLARISSIMO ET EXCELLENTISSIMO VIRO, DOMINO IOHANNI ANTONIO MAGINO MATHEMATUM IN CELEBRI BONONIENSIUM GYMNASIO PROFESSORI EXIMIO ET AMICO SUO INPRIMIS DILECTO.

Clarissime et Excellentissime Vir.

CUM plures iam elapsi sint anni, atque uti existimo ad minimum quinque, ex quo quidam inter meos domesticos studiosos eruditum tuum librum quem Tabulam Tetragonicam vocas, mihique dedicasti, Hafnia e nundinis Francofurtensibus istuc per bibliopolam nostratem allatum, Uraniburgum secum attulerit, idque uno vel altero anno postquam a te evulgatus esset (neque enim ullum a teipso accepi exemplar, sive nullum miseris, sive quod prae nimia viarum intercapedine interciderit) ego vero postquam illud exemplum, quod studiosus meus asportavit, inspexissem et diligentius perlustrassem, mihi admodum placuit, utut nonnulla in eo sint, in quibus tibiipsi forte non satis pro hac prima conceptione satisfeceris, nec in magna numerorum copia processus sit sufficienter expeditus, praeterquam quod id de quo eius usum pro me praecipue infers, circa aequationum nimirum angulos in Planetis facilius, quam per Tangentium et Secantium Tabulas inde colligendos, mihi qua ratione id fieri posset, necdum perspectum, siquidem angulos illos per solas numerorum προσθαφαιρέσεις, nempe additionem et subtractionem absque omni multiplicatione aut divisione minimo negocio investigare liceat; quam πραγματείαν non saltem in omni genere triangulorum sphaericorum, sed et planorum magno Astronomici calculi compendio locum mereri nobis constat; laude nihilominus dignissimum censui et etiamnum censeo hunc tuum laborem in componenda illa tabula cum suo usu, qui sane late patet et multiplex esse potest. Quod autem non antea tibi de his rescripserim, factum est, quod diu a teipso expectarem exemplar aliquod mihi destinatum, quod alicubi in itinere haerere putavi, ut rescribendo gratus vicissim esse possem, partim quod certum et fidelem internuncium non repererim, qui tibi tuto quippiam a me perferret.

Quae vero et tuo et aliorum nomine in epistola illic praefixa rogas, videlicet ut Astronomiae instaurationem, quam diu multumque molior, mature absolvam et publici iuris faciam, id equidem ut secundum tua et multorum vota praestarem, nihil magis ipsemet in votis habui: sed obstiterunt hactenus quaedam, partim proprie ad artem redintegrandam stabiliendamque facientia, partim ex huius inferioris et terrestris mundi tumultuaria scena prodeuntia, et politicis fatis obnoxia.

Prius illud tarditas circuituum superiorum Planetarum, praesertim ♄i causatur, quo minus sufficientes acronychias observationes per totum circuli ambitum citius adipisci concederetur; neque enim tres sufficiunt ad eorum Apogaea et Eccentricitates explicandas, uti imitatione PTOLEMÆI et COPERNICI hactenus frustra creditum est, inaequalitate adhuc alia, quam eorum ferunt hypotheses, coincidente, et Eccentricitate illa, quae Aequanti tribuitur, non retinente eandem ad alteram proportionem, quam illi assumunt et pro cognito praesupponunt potius quam demonstrant; aliter enim ea se habet et in singulis variat. Taceo nunc, quantum difficultatis ingerant, quantumque temporis requirant reliqui duo inferiores Planetae, inprimis vero vagabundus et varijs ambagibus implicitus ☿, rarissimeque apparens, cum alias circa horizontem, ubi hi duo observandi ut plurimum veniunt, pauca admodum concedatur aëris satis pura serenitas, ob vapores istic obvolantes, decliviore etiam Sphaera aliquid obstaculi suggerente. Quin et ☾, utut singulis mensibus recurrat, non parum suis anfractuosis et pluribus, quam hactenus creditum est, inaequalitatibus obnoxia nos torsit, donec non ex tribus solummodo Eclipsibus haud sufficientibus, uti antecessores, eius motiones scrutaremur, sed sexies ternis aut pluribus eosdem eruere, ut alia praetereantur, necesse habuerimus.

Alterum obstaculum, quod politicum dixi, quodque per se ingratum, meam intentionem studiumque in hac arte redintegranda alias indefessum multifariam exagitavit turbavitque inde a defuncto laudatissimae memoriae Rege Danorum FRIDERICO Domino et fautore meo clementissimo, qui me meaque studia, quoad vixit, clementer et benigne iuvit et promovit: in interregno octennali frigidius ijs, quae requirebantur, prospectis, donec tandem creato hoc novo Rege adolescente, qui, sive ob aetatem iuniorem, sive dissuadentibus, aut minus adhortantibus dynastis, utut alias heroico sit animo et ingenio acri praeditus, ac iudicio pro hac aetate plus quam credi posset valens, huius sublimis scientiae in suo regno promotioni attendere non duxit operaepretium, sed per Cancellarium literis a me, quod subsecuturum esset, discrete praemonitum, mihi responderi permiserit, sibi non esse integrum in Astronomici exercitij Instrumentorumque eo pertinentium sustentationem quippiam impendere, subtractis etiam successive, statim ac coronatus est, sumptibus ab optimo Parente et Regni consiliarijs mihi concessis et eo deputatis, non curantibus ijs qui Regi sunt ab intimis, Senatus Consultum mox ab obitu FRIDERICI Regis mihi in membrana impertitum, et a Primario eorum qui nunc Curiae Magister est, suapte manu subscriptum et sigillo munitum, sic quod alias insolitum est, exauthorari fecit. Unde factum est, supervenientibus etiam alijs molestationibus, ut priusquam Astronomiae, cuius tam diu tamque impense reaedificationem in Patria molitus sum, ruinam ibidem indecoram hisce oculis spectarem, multo potius maluerim ipsam Patriam cum omnibus in ea consanguineis et amicis derelinquere atque in Germaniam me cum Coniuge et liberis totaque mea familia, assumpto mecum ingenti illo apparatu Astronomico, concedere, quod ante sesquialterum annum, si forte adhuc inscius es, praesenti et forti animo a me factum est. Substiti tamen interea cum meis iuxta fines Daniae et Germaniae in arce quadam Ranzoviana prope Hamburgum sita, quo nostratibus rem rectius dispicienda Patriae, cui multum debemus, adhuc studens, satis temporis relinquerem, atque eo forem excusabilior, si alio me prorsus converterem. Verum cum id nihilominus incassum fieret, et Augustissimus Romanorum Imperator RUDOLPHUS resciscens me e Dania emigrasse atque ad Holsatiae australiorem oram habitare, me pro ea qua est erga Mathematicas artes, quas ipsemet etiam laudabiliter exercet, Clementissimo favore praeditus, ad se in Bohemiam evocari mandavit, spe facta quod non frustra venturus essem. Sum itaque iam nunc in itinere, et hic Dresdae aliquantisper cunctor, donec missis in aulam Caesaris literis edocear, ubi et quando eius Maiestas adeunda sit, hoc praesertim tempore, cum ob luem epidemicam passim istic grassantem turbata sint omnia, et Caesar Praga in alium salubriorem locum, ubi solitarie agit, discesserit. Responsum est Caesarem accessum meum libenter intellexisse; sed cum ipsemet in Bohemia vix locum inveniat, ubi a periculo contagij increbrescentis tutus sit, suadere et cupere, ut aliquamdiu hîc aut alibi in vicinia me contineam, donec remiserint istic morbi populariter grassantes; interea velle se de commoda et opportuna sede cogitare atque prospicere, ubi me meaque collocet. Huic clementissimo Caesaris responso, uti par est, acquiescens, constitui, volente supremo Numine, in vicinam aliquam superioris Saxoniae vel Misniae Academiam hisce diebus me conferre, ut aliqua in Progymnasmatis meis alijsque operibus typographico labore necdum plane ob eas, quae mihi in Patria evenerunt, conturbationes absoluta, ibi compleri procurem, donec pacatiore et saniore in Bohemia restituta degendi commoditate a Caesare eo accedere iubear. Quod tibi indicandum duxi, optime MAGINE, ut de statu mearum rerum conscius esses, utque simul ex iam commemoratis aliquatenus intelligeres, quae et quanta Astronomiae, quam intendo, instaurationem remorata sint. Plura et particulariora exhibitor harum FRANCISCUS TIGNAGLIUS nomine tibi seorsim aperiet. Est ille adolescens genere nobilis, natione Germanus, qui mihi usque in tertium annum fuit domesticus, meaeque e Dania migrationis comes, estque artibus liberalibus egregie imbutus, et Mathematicis etiam, interea dum mecum fuit, operam non contemnendam navavit, observationibus simul assuefactus et numerorum tractationi, alias per se apprime ingeniosus et disciplinis quibusvis capessendis idoneus, moribus insuper integris et virtute praeditus, ideoque ob tam praeclaras animi dotes mihi semper carissimus fuit, et filij loco habitus. Hunc igitur de meliori, uti dicunt, nota tibi commendatum habeas, te etiam atque etiam rogo, quicquid in illum contuleris officij et beneficii, eum insuper apud Illustres et praestantes istic viros commendatione tua promovendo, id totum non tam ipsi quam mihi te praestitisse scias, inveniesque me ad compensandum perpetuo pro virili quam paratissimum. Tradidi illi librum quendam tibi ex me donandum, qui duos in se commodioris vectionis causa complectitur: prior Mechanicam Astronomiae artem, prout a nobis in usu hactenus fuit, exhibet: posterior stellas fixas millenas accuratissime restitutas labore diutino atque ingenti, impendioque haud mediocri. Hoc habe librarium vicissim munus pro libro mihi prius inscripto, donec plura ex meis quae desideras chalcographico labore penitus absoluta fuerint. Sunt illi quaedam etiam alia ad manus, quatenus in hac mea peregrinatione in promptu erant, quae simul monstrabit, et de pluribus me meaque studia concernentibus te edocebit. Spero me ubi commodius et durabilius ocium in Germania adeptus fuero, tuis et aliorum votis in Astronomiae illustratione et locupletatione consumanda uberius satisfacturum.

Inserui priori libro circa calcem epistolas tuas ad me datas, cum spacium pro ijs et Curtianis vacaret, idque certis de causis, praesertim ut alios ad haec studia amanda excolendaque excitarem: et licet te inconsulto hoc fecerim, tamen haud ingratum fore confido, siquidem tibi, ut existimo, id non sit inhonorificum. Observationes quas polliceris in altera Epistola, si quas interea peregistis, obtinere averem. Sin vero id hactenus neglectum est, ut in posterum sedulo resarciatur et praestetur, rogo; cedet id in artis nostrae, veluti spero, emolumentum, eritque et tibi et alijs, qui hoc factitarint, laudabile. Instrumenta si conficere libuerit, liber is formam et rationem luculenter suppeditabit.

Plura nunc non addo, forsan in iam exaratis epistolae metas excessi, quod tamen libenter amicitiae nostrae et studiorum communium causa admisi, locis etiam quibus disiungimur crebras interlocutiones non admittentibus, ideoque, ubi fieri potest, non abbreviandis. Sique plura hîc forte omissa scire desideras, is, quem tibi nunc enixe commendavi adolescens, cum rerum mearum haud sit ignarus, plura expediet, atque votis tuis hac in parte, uti mihi polliceor, satisfaciet. Eum itaque iterum atque iterum tibi acceptum habe, et ad meliora quaeque, quantum in te est, istic iuva et promove, quo aliquando, favente Numine, ad me reversus, tuam in se benevolentiam merito praedicare possit, idque erit mihi longe gratissimum.

Vale.
Dabantur Dresdae Misniae, ubi est aula Electorum Saxoniae, Novembris 28 stilo veteri. Anno etc. 1598.

To the most renowned and excellent man, Lord Giovanni Antonio Magini, eminent Professor of Mathematics in the celebrated Gymnasium of Bologna, and his especially beloved friend.

Most renowned and excellent Man.

Since several years have now elapsed — at the least five, as I reckon — from when one of my household students brought to Uraniborg your erudite book, which you call the Tabula Tetragonica, and dedicated to me (carried from Copenhagen, from the Frankfurt fair, thither by a bookseller of ours), and that one or two years after it had been published by you — for I received no copy from you yourself, whether you sent none, or whether it perished through the over-great stretch of the roads — I, when I had inspected and more diligently perused that copy which my student carried off, found it much to my liking; although there are some things in it in which you perhaps did not fully satisfy yourself, for this first conception, and the process is not sufficiently expedited in a great supply of numbers. Besides, that for which you chiefly infer its use for me — namely, the deriving thence of the angles of the equations in the Planets more easily than through Tables of Tangents and Secants — I have not yet seen by what method that could be done, since those angles may be investigated, with least trouble, by the sole prosthaphaereses [προσθαφαιρέσεις] of numbers — namely addition and subtraction, without any multiplication or division; which method [πραγματεία] we are sure merits a place, not only in every kind of spherical triangles, but of plane ones too, with great compendium of astronomical calculation. Nonetheless I judged, and still judge, this labour of yours, in composing that table, with its use (which is indeed broadly open and can be manifold), most worthy of praise. That I did not write back to you about these before, came about because I long expected from you yourself some copy destined for me (which I thought stuck somewhere on the road), that I might be grateful in return by writing back; partly because I did not find a sure and faithful messenger to carry anything from me to you safely.

As to what you ask, both in your own name and others', in the epistle prefixed there — namely, that I mature and make public the restoration of Astronomy which I long and much labour at — I myself wished nothing more than to perform this, according to your wishes and those of many; but certain things have hitherto stood in the way — partly things properly making to the restoring and establishing of the art, partly things proceeding from the tumultuous scene of this lower and earthly world, and subject to political fates.

The first [obstacle] is caused by the slowness of the circuits of the superior Planets, especially Saturn [♄], whereby less leave is granted of getting sufficient acronychal [opposition] observations through the whole compass of the circle sooner; for three do not suffice to explicate their Apogees and Eccentricities (as has hitherto been vainly believed, in imitation of Ptolemy and Copernicus) — another inequality, than their hypotheses bear, coinciding, and that Eccentricity which is assigned to the Equant not retaining the same proportion to the other, which they assume and presuppose as known, rather than demonstrate; for it is otherwise, and varies in each [case]. I am silent now how much difficulty they introduce, and how much time they require, the remaining two inferior Planets — especially the wandering Mercury [☿], implicated in various windings, and most rarely appearing — since otherwise, around the horizon (where these two for the most part come to be observed), very little sufficiently pure clear air is granted, on account of the vapours flying about there, the more declining Sphere also offering some obstacle. Nay, the Moon [☾] too, though it recurs each month, has tortured us not a little by its tortuous inequalities — more than has hitherto been believed — until we had to investigate its motions not from only three insufficient Eclipses (like our predecessors), but to draw the same out from six-times-three [18] or more (to pass over other things).

The other obstacle, which I called political, and which is in itself ungrateful, variously agitated and disturbed my intention and study in restoring this art (otherwise untiring) ever since the death of the King of the Danes, Frederick, of most praised memory, my most clement Lord and patron — who, while he lived, clemently and benignly helped and promoted me and my studies. In the eight-year interregnum [regency], the things that were required were more coldly provided — until at last, this new King, a youth, being created [crowned], who (whether on account of his younger age, or with dynasts dissuading or less encouraging — though otherwise he be of heroic mind, and endowed with a sharp wit, and strong in judgement, for this age, more than could be believed) did not think it worthwhile to attend to the promotion of this sublime science in his realm; but, through the Chancellor (I having discreetly forewarned by letter what would follow), permitted me to be answered that it was not in his power to spend anything on the maintenance of the Astronomical exercise and of the instruments pertaining to it — the costs granted me, and assigned thereto, by the best Parent [Frederick] and the Realm's councillors being also successively withdrawn, as soon as he was crowned (those who are the King's intimates not caring); and the Senate-decree given me on parchment immediately after King Frederick's death — subscribed in the hand of the chief of them (who is now Court-Master), and secured with a seal — he caused to be annulled (a thing otherwise unusual). Whence it came about — other molestations supervening too — that, before I should behold with these eyes the unseemly ruin, in that same place, of the Astronomy whose rebuilding I had so long and so intensely laboured at in my fatherland, I much preferred to abandon the fatherland itself, with all my blood-kin and friends in it, and to withdraw into Germany with my wife and children and whole household, taking that huge Astronomical apparatus with me — which (if perhaps you are still unaware) was done by me a year and a half ago, with present and brave mind. Yet I stopped meanwhile, with my [household], near the borders of Denmark and Germany, in a certain Rantzau castle situated near Hamburg, to leave my countrymen, while still studying the fatherland's matter (to which I owe much), time enough to consider it more rightly, and to be the more excusable if I should turn entirely elsewhere. But since this nonetheless came to nothing, and the most august Roman Emperor Rudolf, learning that I had emigrated from Denmark and dwelt on Holstein's southern shore, endowed (as he is) with most clement favour toward the Mathematical arts (which he himself too laudably practises), commanded me to be called to him into Bohemia, with hope given that I should not come in vain. So I am now on the journey, and linger a while here at Dresden, until — letters being sent to the Emperor's court — I am informed where and when His Majesty is to be approached; especially at this time, when on account of the epidemic plague raging there everywhere all things are disturbed, and the Emperor has withdrawn from Prague into another, healthier place, where he lives solitarily. The answer was that the Emperor gladly heard of my coming; but since he himself scarcely finds a place in Bohemia where he is safe from the danger of the increasing contagion, [he] advises and wishes that I keep myself a while here, or elsewhere in the neighbourhood, until the diseases raging popularly there abate; meanwhile he will think of, and provide, a convenient and opportune seat where to place me and my [things]. Acquiescing, as is fitting, in this most clement response of the Emperor's, I have resolved, the supreme Godhead willing, to betake myself these days to some nearby Academy of upper Saxony or Meissen, that I may there have certain things in my Progymnasmata and other works — not yet quite finished in the printing-labour, on account of the disturbances that befell me in the fatherland — completed, until I am ordered by the Emperor to go thither, when a more peaceful and healthful convenience of living is restored in Bohemia. Which I judged should be indicated to you, excellent Magini, that you might be aware of the state of my affairs, and at the same time understand somewhat, from what has now been recounted, what and how great [things] have delayed the restoration of Astronomy which I intend. More, and more particular [things], the bearer of these, Franz Tengnagel by name, will open to you separately. He is a youth noble by birth, German by nation, who was my household-member up to the third year, and the companion of my migration from Denmark; and he is excellently imbued with the liberal arts, and in Mathematics too (while he was with me) bestowed no contemptible labour — habituated at once to observations and to the handling of numbers — otherwise of himself most ingenious, and apt for grasping any disciplines, endowed besides with integral morals and virtue; and so, for such distinguished gifts of mind, he was always most dear to me, and held in a son's place. Him, then, hold commended to you "from the better mark," as they say; I ask you again and again [that], whatever of office and benefit you confer on him — promoting him besides, by your commendation, among the Illustrious and distinguished men there — you would know you have rendered all that not so much to him as to me; and you will find me most ready perpetually, to my power, to repay it. I have given him a certain book, to be presented to you from me, which comprises two [works] in itself for easier carriage: the first exhibits the Mechanical art of Astronomy, as it has hitherto been in use with us [the Mechanica]; the second, the thousand fixed stars, most accurately restored by long and huge labour, and no mediocre expense [the star-catalogue]. Have this gift of books in turn, for the book formerly dedicated to me [the Tabula Tetragonica], until more of mine, which you desire, shall be quite finished by the engraving-labour. He has some other things too at hand, so far as they were ready in this peregrination of mine, which he will at the same time show, and will instruct you about more things concerning me and my studies. I hope, when I have got more convenient and durable leisure in Germany, to satisfy your wishes and others' more fully, in consummating the illustration and enrichment of Astronomy.

I inserted, near the end of the first book, your letters sent to me — when space was free for them and for the Curtius [letters] — and that for certain reasons, especially to excite others to love and cultivate these studies; and although I did this without consulting you, yet I trust it will not be unwelcome, since (as I think) it is not dishonourable to you. The observations you promise in the second Epistle, if you have done any meanwhile, I should wish to obtain. But if it has hitherto been neglected, I ask that it be diligently made up and performed in future; it will turn to the profit of our art (as I hope), and will be praiseworthy both to you and to others who do it. If you wish to make instruments, that book [the Mechanica] will clearly supply the form and method.

I add no more now; perhaps in what is already written I have exceeded the bounds of a letter — which, however, I have gladly admitted for the sake of our friendship and common studies, the places too by which we are separated not admitting frequent conversations, and so [the letters] not to be abbreviated where it can be otherwise. And if you should wish to know more things perhaps here omitted, the youth whom I have now earnestly commended to you, since he is not ignorant of my affairs, will expedite more, and will satisfy your wishes in this part (as I promise myself). So have him welcome again and again, and help and promote him there, to all the better things, so far as in you lies — that one day, God favouring, returned to me, he may deservedly proclaim your benevolence toward him; and that will be far the most welcome to me.

Farewell.
Given at Dresden in Meissen, where is the court of the Electors of Saxony, the 28th of November, Old Style, the year etc. 1598.

[Postscript:] If three Eclipses of this year have been observed there by you or by anyone else, make me partaker of that record, for the difference of meridians to be elicited thence. By my instruments they were noted thus, so that the moments observed and reduced to the middle of the same stood as follows. In the year 1598: Feb. 20, Eclipse [of the Moon], middle 17h 58′ P.M., at Wandsbek near Hamburg; 2 digits of [the Moon] were wanting. March 6, Eclipse of the Sun ☉, middle 11h 5′ A.M., New Style, at Uraniborg in Denmark; the magnitude of the eclipse there appeared, from the upper part, between 9 and 10 digits. August 16, Eclipse [of the Moon], middle 7h 42′ P.M., at Wandsbek. All these times agree rightly with our restitution. But how much they differ from the calculation, both Alphonsine and Copernican, see for yourself. The longitude of Wandsbek I presuppose at 33° 35′; of Uraniborg 36° 45′; and that of Venice nearly the same.

The same young man will also relate to you certain things in my name, secretly, in which — if you should lend me your aid, or through some other suitable man there [in Italy] should render me a service — you will do me a thing most welcome by far; and once his willingness has become known to me through the same [messenger], I shall instruct you more fully how the matter is to be carried through, and shall prescribe the form of it. I have certain reasons why I should wish this to be done in Italy rather than in Germany. It matters much to me that this be accomplished, which can also be carried out without great trouble; I will gladly pay the expenses, and shall in turn be found grateful. The bearer of this letter has with him certain documents of that business, which he will be able to show and explain to you privately.

To Your Excellency,
Most devotedly,
TYCHO BRAHE,
I have written with my own hand.

Tycho acknowledges Magini's table of squares (Tabula Tetragonica, 1592), dedicated to him (received late, second-hand), and judges its labour praiseworthy — but counters that the planetary-equation angles "for which you chiefly recommend it" are better got by prosthaphaereses (addition/subtraction, no multiplication/division) — the trigonometric product-to-sum method, "in every kind of spherical and plane triangles." (Cf. Herwart's own prosthaphaeresis, brahe-t_15990917001.)
  • The scientific obstacles to publishing. A candid statement of why the Progymnasmata is delayed: Saturn's slow orbit (three oppositions don't fix the apogee/eccentricity — Ptolemy/Copernicus "vainly believed"; the equant-eccentricity proportion "varies"); Mercury's elusiveness; and the Moon's complex inequalities, needing "eighteen or more eclipses," not three.
  • ★ The fullest exile narrative. One of Tycho's most complete: Frederick II's patronage; the eight-year regency's cold neglect; Christian IV's refusal "that it was not in his power to spend anything," via the Chancellor; the withdrawn revenues and the annulled parchment Senate-decree (signed by "the chief, now Court-Master"); the choice to leave the fatherland with wife, children, and the "huge astronomical apparatus" "a year and a half ago"; the Wandsbek wait; the imperial call from Rudolf II; and the Dresden interim during the Bohemian plague (the Emperor "living solitarily," advising Tycho to wait nearby).
  • The Saxony-academy plan. Tycho means to go to a upper-Saxony/Meissen academy to finish the Progymnasmata printing (interrupted by the Danish troubles) — the Wittenberg stay that followed.
  • ★ The Mechanica + star-catalogue gift. Tycho sends Magini, via Tengnagel, a bound volume of two works — the Mechanica + the 1000-star catalogue — "in return for" the Tabula Tetragonica. (A documented Mechanica/catalogue distribution event.) And he notes he has printed Magini's letters in his Epistolae (with the Curtius letters), "without consulting you … to excite others."
  • The Tengnagel commendation. A warm character-portrait of Franz Tengnagel — "noble by birth, German, my household-member, companion of my migration … held in a son's place" — commended to Magini and the Bolognese/Roman learned.

  • §1 Dreyer collation. Scientific postscript (three 1598 eclipses + the Wandsbek/Uraniborg/Venice longitudes), restored from Dreyer VIII pp.124–125 (dropped by the working transcription).
  • What it is. The concealed core of a letter Tycho sent from Dresden (28 November 1598) to Giovanni Antonio Magini in Bologna — the surviving published text is only the purely astronomical tail (eclipse timings and longitudes). This recovered passage is the confidential errand referred to only obliquely elsewhere in the letter.
  • The messenger. "The same young man" ("idem adolescens") is almost certainly Tycho's son-in-law and secretary Franz Tengnagel, who in November 1598 travelled to Italy as Tycho's emissary, meeting Magini in Bologna and being made a Knight of San Marco by the Doge of Venice [Christianson, On Tycho's Island (2000)].
  • What the secret was. Dreyer records precisely this errand: Tycho wanted an Italian scholar to write a eulogy of him — a request Magini could not fulfil himself but two years later recommended to Bernardino Baldi, then compiling a collection of Lives of the mathematicians [Dreyer, Tycho Brahe (1890)]. Tycho in fact never appears in Baldi's completed Lives or History [Thoren, The Lord of Uraniborg (1990)].
  • Why Italy, not Germany. Tycho gives a practical reason of reputational strategy — he wanted the panegyrist to be Italian rather than German, presumably to extend his fame into the humanist literary culture Baldi represented, beyond the German-speaking scholarly world where he was already well known.
  • The documents. The "certain documents" Tengnagel carried and could show Magini "privately" were presumably materials to brief a prospective biographer — a self-fashioning dossier consistent with Tycho's documented eagerness to control his own public image during the Dresden exile.

Does not affect the sense of the English translation, which renders the phrase correctly either way. Dreyer prints: MATHEMATUM IN CELEBRI BONONIENSIUM GYMNASIO PROFESSORI EXIMIO (TBOO VIII, p.120).
