The original

MAGNIFICO ET PRÆSTANTISSIMO GENEROSISSIMOQUE VIRO DOMINO TYCHONI BRAHE, ASTRONOMO PRINCIPI, MATHEMATICO CÆSAREO, DOMINO ET PATRONO COLENDO. PRAGAe IN BEHMEN.
MAGNIFICE ET ILLUSTRIS DOMINE, PATRONE COLENDE.
S.

QUAM iamdudum mihi dari exoptavi occasionem literis tuis per MULÆUM in Daniam commeantem mihi traditis respondendi, ea iam tandem subito se aperit, neque tamen satis exoptata, cum Dresdam usque saltem se extendat, unde tamen ad te perlatum iri omnia spero. Est autem ita, ut scribis: neque enim mihi ingrata aut odiosa censura tua est, hominis doctissimi et præstantissimi, præsertim in ijs, quæ ad te ipsum spectant. De carmine elegiaco loquor: in quo quædam tum in concinnitate, tum in expositione te desiderare scribis. Scripsi hoc ad te, et fassus sum, cum mitterem: non eum laborem aut limam accessisse, quæ cæteris meis fere solet. Scis enim ardorem illum Poëticum temporum intervallis minus aut magis incendi: et interdum ita lenti sumus, ut iaceant omnia. Placet itaque mihi, ut premas, donec correctum fuerit: quod quidem a te si factum fuerit, gratiam habebo: nam laus, ea certe, quam scriptor sibi vendicat, mea erit, labor tuus. Ne tamen ingenij me iacturam fecisse existumes, plane mihi persuadeo, metu isto te eximet Carmen Epicum de VARADINO: quod ad te circa idem tempus fere, quo tuæ datæ sunt, etiam tum traditum tibi esse oportuisset, misi per quendam ALBERTUM HOVI, hominem militarem, Domino illustri MELCHIORI RHEDERO addictum eique a me commendatum. Cui ut tu quoque me commendares et illud carmen, quod in honorem eius composui ipsique inscripsi, ijsdem literis a te contendi: neque minus iam contendo, si aut literæ illæ tibi non redditæ essent, aut tu petitioni meæ, quod secus esse puto, hactenus non satisfecisses. Quod secus hoc esse putem, inde fit, quod ipse RHEDERUS literis humanissimis benevolentiam suam mihi ostenderit et gratitudinis ac remunerationis spem fecerit. Constitit mihi certe illud carmen non exiguo labore et animi intentione, quam auxit Historiæ exprimendæ cum veritate et tamen cum decoro Poëtico difficultas: quorum primam dum quidam Poëta Silesius secutus est, alterum ita neglexit in eadem historia reddendâ, ut nihil ineptius fieri possit. Scribit idem RHEDERUS se ex consilio doctissimorum quorundam virorum, inter quos te quoque esse existimo, Gorlitzium recudendum misisse illud meum carmen. MELISSUS quin iam tandem tibi satisfecerit, plane mihi persuadeo: dicerem hominem plane a se discessisse, nisi fecerit.

De scripto aliquo Cæsareæ Maiestati dedicando, quod consilium tuum mihi impertis, pro eo atque volebam, est quod multum tibi debeam. Habeo quidem confectum Philosophiæ Panegyricum, quo omnia ea tribus prolixis satis orationibus complexus sum, quæ de Philosophia mihi videntur posse honorifice dici: sed eum neque iam mecum habeo, neque hactenus quisquam, quos quidem tentavi, Bibliopolarum in eum excudendum sumptus voluit conferre (ita enim agitur cum scriptis elegantibus, dum florent et leguntur inelegantia) nisi ipse conferam. Quod si Poëmata mea mihi remittantur a MELISSO, qui mihi in editione procurandâ minime satisfacit, cum forte posset (nolo dicere plura: hoc ipsum, quod dixi, tecum sit) quæ ante tribus dedicavi, nescio, an rectius facerem, si mutato consilio soli Imperatori dedicarem, plusque forte inde præmij te et alijs commendantibus reportarem. Distribui autem nunc quidem in tres partes, quarum primam partem quinque Elegiarum libros continentem inscripsi Regi Scotiæ ante iam in me munifico, alteram Epicorum quatuor libros et Lyricorum unum continentem, ULRICO Principi Megapolitaniæ, tertiam tres Epigrammatum et unum Sylvarum librum complexam WILHELMO Curlandiæ Duci. Utinam vero tam felix sim, ut aliquando lucem videant, quam illis hactenus invideri equidem nonnihil doleo. Sed præ his duabus vijs illa mihi inprimis arrideret: si materia aliqua mihi offerretur, qua Imperatoris ipsius aut maiorum eius laudes prædicarem, idque carmen hîc in Academia publice decantarem, simile futurum Rhederiano illi. Illam vero viam et materiem præ alijs amplecterer, quod existimarem eâ ratione me æque magnam gratiam a Cæsarea Maiestate initurum quam magni operis dedicatione. Ignosces mihi, vir magnifice, quod hæc tam libere apud te loquar, quod eo facio, ut eo rectius mihi consulere possis, quoniam id libenter facis, et ea re undique gratiam colligis omnium. Si igitur potes, aut talem materiam mihi suppedita, aut de reliquis, quid videatur, scribe, aut certe aliud consilium suggere. Omnia enim mea tibi lubens committo.

Eram finem facturus etiam dudum debuissem: sed tristis adhuc clausula supererat de obitu CHYTRÆI. Quem hîc non laudo aut deploro: feci utrumque tum in Epigrammatis istis, tum in Programmate, cuius et ipse, volente ita Senatu Academiæ, autor sum; quorum utrunque ad te mitto. Tu certe amicum non vulgarem et ingenuum studiorum tuorum et ingenij excellentissimi æstimatorem et præconem amisisti: ego patrem et patronum singularem (ita enim me semper habuit ut filium) nos omnes præceptorem communem. O tristem nuncium, quem tibi allaturus sum! Sed nolo te conturbare, et iam tabellarius astans inclamat aurem et concludere iubet. Bene igitur et diutissime vale, vir præstantissime, et diu vive non solum tuis, sed et BRASCHIO, quamvis et ille inter tuos est.

Datæ festinanter Rostochij 8 Augusti Anno 1600.
T. Magnif. et Generos.
reverenter colens
BRASCHIUS.

[Postscript — restored from Dreyer, Opera Omnia VIII, p. 339; dropped by the working transcription. See Apparatus.]
M. CHRISTIANUM amanter saluto, a quo me literis non salutari miror.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the magnificent, most excellent and most noble man, Lord Tycho Brahe, Prince of Astronomers, Imperial Mathematician, my Lord and Patron to be revered. At Prague in Bohemia.
Magnificent and illustrious Lord, Patron to be revered.
Greeting.

The occasion I had long wished for, of replying to your letter (delivered to me by Mule on his way into Denmark), now at last suddenly opens — yet not quite as wished, since it reaches only as far as Dresden, whence, however, I hope all will be carried on to you. It is as you write: for your censure is not unwelcome or hateful to me — the censure of a most learned and excellent man, especially in things that concern you yourself. I speak of the elegiac poem, in which you write that you found certain things wanting, both in finish and in exposition. I wrote you this, and confessed it when I sent it: that it had not received that labour and file [lima] which my others usually do. For you know that the Poetic ardour is kindled less or more by intervals of time; and sometimes we are so sluggish that everything lies idle. It pleases me, then, that you suppress it until it has been corrected: which, if it be done by you, I shall be grateful — for the praise that a writer claims for himself will indeed be mine, the labour yours. But lest you think I have suffered a loss of talent, I quite persuade myself that the Epic Poem on Várad will free you of that fear — which ought to have been delivered to you about the same time your letter was sent. I sent it by a certain Albert Hovi, a military man, attached to the illustrious Lord Melchior Röder and commended by me to him. That you too would commend me to him, and that poem which I composed in his honour and inscribed to him, I asked of you in the same letter — and I ask no less now, if either that letter was not delivered to you, or you have not yet (which I think otherwise) satisfied my petition. That I think it otherwise comes from this: that Röder himself, by a most courteous letter, has shown me his benevolence and given hope of gratitude and reward. That poem cost me no small labour and intentness of mind — increased by the difficulty of rendering History with truth and yet with Poetic decorum; of which, while a certain Silesian poet followed the first, he so neglected the latter, in rendering the same history, that nothing more inept could be made. The same Röder writes that, on the counsel of certain most learned men — among whom I think you too are — he has sent that poem of mine to Görlitz to be reprinted. That Melissus has at last satisfied you, I quite persuade myself: I should say the man had quite departed from himself, had he not done so.

As to dedicating some writing to the Imperial Majesty — the counsel you impart to me, just as I wished — it is something for which I owe you much. I have indeed finished a Panegyric of Philosophy, in which, in three sufficiently ample orations, I have embraced all that seems to me capable of being said honourably of Philosophy; but I neither have it now with me, nor has any of the booksellers I have tried been willing to lay out the cost of printing it (for so it goes with elegant writings, while inelegant ones flourish and are read) — unless I lay it out myself. But if my Poems are returned to me by Melissus (who by no means satisfies me in procuring their edition, though perhaps he could — I will say no more: let what I have said stay with you) — which I formerly dedicated to three [patrons] — I do not know whether I should do better if, changing my plan, I dedicated them to the Emperor alone, and perhaps got more reward thence, with you and others recommending me. I have for now divided them into three parts: the first, containing five books of Elegies, I inscribed to the King of Scotland, already munificent toward me; the second, containing four books of Epics and one of Lyrics, to Ulrich, Prince of Mecklenburg; the third, comprising three books of Epigrams and one of Sylvae, to Wilhelm, Duke of Courland. But would that I were so fortunate that they might one day see the light — which I grieve not a little that they have hitherto been begrudged! Yet before these two ways, that one above all would smile on me: if some material were offered me by which I might proclaim the praises of the Emperor himself or of his ancestors, and recite that poem publicly here in the Academy — it would be like that of Röder's. That way and that matter I would embrace before others, because I should reckon that thereby I would win as great a favour from the Imperial Majesty as by the dedication of a great work. You will forgive me, magnificent man, that I speak these things so freely to you — which I do that you may the more rightly counsel me, since you do it gladly, and in that way gather everyone's goodwill on all sides. If, then, you can, either supply me with such material, or write what seems best concerning the rest, or at any rate suggest some other plan; for I gladly commit all my [affairs] to you.

I was about to make an end — I ought to have done so long since — but a sad conclusion still remained, concerning the death of Chytraeus. Whom I here neither praise nor lament: I did both, in those Epigrams and in the Programme [funeral notice], of which I am myself the author, the Academy's Senate so wishing; both of which I send to you. You certainly have lost an uncommon and noble friend, an appraiser and herald of your studies and of your most excellent genius; I, a singular father and patron (for he always held me as a son); we all, a common teacher. O the sad news I am to bring you! But I will not disturb you, and now the courier, standing by, dins my ear and bids me conclude. So fare well, and very long, most excellent man, and live long, not only for your own, but for Brasch too — though he also is among your own.

Written in haste at Rostock, 8 August, the year 1600.
Of Your Magnificence and Nobility
the reverent servant,
Braschius.

[Postscript:] I greet Master Christian affectionately, and wonder that I am not greeted by him with a letter.

Apparatus

  • Direction & correspondents. Incoming letter, Nicolaus Brasch (Braschius) — the Rostock Neo-Latin poet — to Tycho at Prague. Brasch is the verse-correspondent whose poems Tycho repeatedly emended (cf. brahe-t_16010415001, where Tycho "writes back to Master Brasch," and the Cramer/Backmeister circle). This letter shows the relationship from Brasch's side.
  • Significance — the literary-patronage world around Tycho, and the death of Chytraeus.
  • Accepting Tycho's criticism. Brasch gracefully accepts Tycho's censure of his elegiac poem (faults of "finish and exposition"), admitting it lacked his usual "labour and file [lima]," and asks Tycho to suppress it until corrected — "the praise will be mine, the labour yours."
  • The Carmen de Varadino. Brasch's epic on the siege of Várad/Großwardein (a Long-Turkish-War subject), sent via the soldier Albert Hovi to Melchior Röder, whom Brasch asks Tycho to cultivate; Röder, on learned men's advice (Tycho included), had it reprinted at Görlitz. Brasch's reflection on "rendering History with truth yet with poetic decorum" — and his scorn for a Silesian poet who botched the latter — is a nice statement of historical-epic poetics.
  • The dedication question (the letter's practical core). Responding to Tycho's advice to dedicate a work to Rudolf II, Brasch surveys his options: a Panegyric of Philosophy (unprinted — no bookseller will fund it); his Poems (held up by Melissus = Paul Schede Melissus), currently dedicated in three parts to James VI of Scotland (Elegies), Duke Ulrich of Mecklenburg (Epics + Lyrics), and Wilhelm Kettler, Duke of Courland (Epigrams + Sylvae) — which he muses he might re-dedicate to the Emperor alone. But his real wish: a commission to praise the Emperor or his ancestors in a poem recited publicly in the Academy, for which he asks Tycho to "supply material."
  • The death of David Chytraeus. The "sad conclusion": David Chytraeus (1530–1600), the great Rostock theologian-historian, had died (25 June 1600). Brasch wrote the official Epigrams and Programme (funeral notice) for the Academy and encloses them. His threefold lament is the emotional heart: Tycho lost "an uncommon friend, an appraiser and herald of your studies and most excellent genius"; Brasch a "father and patron"; the university "a common teacher."
  • Persons named. Nicolaus Brasch; Tycho (addressee, "Prince of Astronomers, Imperial Mathematician"); Claus Mule (Mulaeus, courier); Albert Hovi (soldier-bearer); Melchior Röder (Rhederus, the Várad-poem patron); the unnamed Silesian poet; Paul Schede Melissus; James VI of Scotland; Duke Ulrich of Mecklenburg; Wilhelm, Duke of Courland; David Chytraeus (whose death is reported). Places: Rostock, Prague, Dresden, Görlitz, Várad.
  • Calendar. Rostock/Mecklenburg was Old Style in 1600; no stylo marker; dateline 8 August 1600 read Old Style (per label). Header left unqualified to match. (Chytraeus's death "25 June 1600" is the conventionally cited date; the letter does not give it.)
  • §1 Dreyer collation. Closing greeting (Brasch's, to "M. Christianus"), restored from Dreyer VIII p.339 (after the signature; dropped by the working transcription).
