The original

AMPLISSIMO NOBILISSIMOQUE VIRO, VIRTUTE ET ERUDITIONE PRÆSTANTISSIMO DOMINO TYCHONI BRAHE DOMINO DE KNUDSTRUP ET VRANIENBURG, AMICO SUO CARISSIMO. GENEROSE VIR.

QUAS ad me 13. Novembris dedisti literas, quod humanitatem et eruditionem nulla sui parte non spirarent, præcipue vero quod tui nominis inscriptionem præ se ferrent, mihi certe, ut decuit, acceptissimæ fuerunt. Cum enim in Danica mea peregrinatione nihil tam admirabile excellentis ingenij tui raris dotibus, nihil tam gratum quam sanciendæ tecum amicitiæ occasio contigerit, nec minorem in conservanda constantiam, quam in contrahenda amicitia benignitatem tuam ex amicissimis literis perspiciam, ni et tuam merito humanitatem amplectar et meam in scribendo negligentiam, tum crebrioribus literis tum alijs quibuscunque, quæ mearum virium sunt officijs, sarciam, ingratus prorsus et tanti viri amicitia indignus merito censear. Literis etiam tuis adiunctam effigiem, oris tui dignitatem et temperatam benignitate gravitatem, laudabili sculptoris industria, ad vivum referentem, quod ea oculos imagine pascat, cuius assidua recordatione animus iucundissime afficitur, pretiosissimis quibusque muneribus, quæ tibi fortuna affatim suppetit, præstantiorem certe existimavi. Et quos ad illius (ut ais) ornatum versus a me poscis, hos quidem addidi, non quod, quæ tui nominis fama abunde commendetur, alio quovis ornamento mea sententia indigeat, sed ut quæ virtutum et doctrinæ tuæ per universum orbem diffusa gloria effigiem tuam in omnibus literatorum omnium bibliothecis sublimem constituet, meo etiam nomini locum aliquem tuo merito ibidem inveniat. Ijs itaque utere, non quidem ut digni sunt, sed ut nostra amicitia et tuo iudicio dignum videbitur. Ego pariter, quæ a te Domino PETRO IUNIO mandata sunt, nisi hactenus ab eo effecta sunt, quamprimum illius conveniendi occasio sese obtulerit, ea qua decet fide et diligentia præstabo: quodque de hortulano et aucupe ad me scribis, effectum brevi curabo. Porro IOANNEM CRAIGIUM medicum Edinburgensem, quem mihi tam diligenter ac studiose commendas, ob probitatem et eruditionem antea quidem mihi semper charum, nunc tua causa multo chariorem censeo ac dum vivam censebo: qui cum veritatis eruendæ studio ac fortassis ut te ipso interprete scriptorum tuorum subtilitatem sibi non prius intellectam facilius assequeretur, silentibus imo vero cum admiratione tua inventa approbantibus alijs omnibus Europæ doctissimis mathematicis, Axiomata quædam tua impugnare ausus sit, quamvis nonnullis odium potius quam benevolentiam audacia meruisse videri possit, quæ tamen eximia nullisque perturbationibus obnoxia tua virtus est, sola victoria contentus et devicti hostis magnitudine tuam gloriam illustrari recte iudicans CRAIGIUM sollicite adeo atque accurate commendas, et commodis atque honoribus auctum cupis, ut egregiam de te bene merendi occasionem in eo viro iuvando mihi oblatam perspiciam. Itaque ubi mea vel opera vel ope rem suam vel dignitatem promoveri posse existimaverit, eam certe diligentiam atque alacritatem adhibebo, ut tuam commendationem meritum pondus apud me consecutam et res ipsa et illius literæ ad te abunde testentur. Et si alia qualibet in re tibi ijsve, quos tuorum in numero habes, consilio auxilio gratia vel auctoritate hîc usui esse possum, ita quæso vel negotia vel homines mihi commendes, ut beneficium tum demum contulisse te credas, cum meam operam expetieris. Interim ne te cœlestibus et divino tuo ingenio dignis curis distentum frustra morer, hoc solum a te petam, ut amicitiam nostram (quod humanitas et constantia tua satis spondent) inviolatam a temporis et longe dissitorum locorum iniuria tueri ac paria a me perpetuo expectare velis.

Vale.
Datæ Lethnitoniæ die XVI Maij Anno 1592.
Mag. Tuæ
addictissimus
[IOHANNES METELLANUS.]

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the most worthy and most noble man, most excellent in virtue and learning, Master Tycho Brahe, Lord of Knudstrup and Uraniborg, his most dear friend. Noble Sir.

The letter you sent me on 13 November was assuredly most welcome to me, as was fitting — because it breathed forth humanity and learning in its every part, but above all because it bore the inscription of your name. For since in my journey in Denmark nothing so wonderful befell me as the rare gifts of your excellent genius, and nothing so gratifying as the opportunity of sealing a friendship with you; and since I perceive from your most friendly letter that your kindness is no less in keeping faith than it was in forming our friendship — unless I duly embrace your humanity in return and make good my negligence in writing, both by more frequent letters and by all other services that lie within my power, I should deservedly be reckoned wholly ungrateful and unworthy of the friendship of so great a man. The likeness, too, attached to your letter — rendering to the life, by the praiseworthy industry of the engraver, the dignity of your countenance and a gravity tempered with kindness — I judged assuredly more excellent than any of the most precious gifts that fortune supplies you in abundance, in that it feeds the eyes with that image whose constant remembrance affects the mind most pleasantly. And the verses which you ask of me for its adornment (as you say), these indeed I have added — not because, in my opinion, what is abundantly commended by the fame of your name stands in need of any other ornament, but so that, just as the glory of your virtues and learning, spread through the whole world, will set your likeness on high in the libraries of all the learned everywhere, my name too may by your merit find some place there as well. Use them, therefore — not indeed as they deserve, but as shall seem worthy of our friendship and of your judgement. I likewise, as to the matters entrusted by you to Master Peter Young, unless they have hitherto been carried out by him, will, as soon as an occasion of meeting him presents itself, perform them with the faith and diligence that is fitting; and as to what you write to me about the gardener and the fowler, I will see it accomplished shortly. Furthermore, as to John Craig, physician of Edinburgh, whom you so carefully and earnestly commend to me — dear to me always before, indeed, on account of his honesty and learning, I now reckon him, and while I live shall reckon him, much dearer for your sake. Since, in his zeal for uncovering the truth, and perhaps that with you yourself as expositor he might more easily attain the subtlety of your writings not previously understood by him, he dared to attack certain of your axioms — while all the other most learned mathematicians of Europe were silent, nay rather approved your discoveries with admiration — although to some his boldness may seem to have earned hatred rather than goodwill: yet such is your virtue, surpassing and subject to no perturbations, that, content with victory alone and rightly judging that your glory is set off by the greatness of the enemy overcome, you commend Craig so solicitously and so carefully, and desire him advanced in advantages and honours, that I perceive an outstanding opportunity offered me of deserving well of you in helping that man. And so, wherever he shall judge that his fortune or his standing can be furthered by my work or my aid, I will assuredly apply such diligence and zeal that both the thing itself and his letters to you may abundantly attest that your commendation has obtained its deserved weight with me. And if in any other matter whatsoever I can be of use here, by counsel, aid, favour, or authority, to you or to those whom you count among your own, then, I beg, commend your affairs or your men to me in such a way that you may believe you have only then conferred a kindness when you have sought out my service. Meanwhile, lest I detain you in vain when you are engrossed in cares worthy of the heavens and of your divine genius, this alone I shall ask of you: that you be willing to keep our friendship (as your humanity and constancy sufficiently pledge) inviolate against the injury of time and of places far apart, and to expect the like from me perpetually.

Farewell.
Given at Lethington, the 16th day of May, in the year 1592.
Most devoted to Your Worship,
[John Maitland.]

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Iohannes Metellanus = John Maitland of Thirlestane (c. 1537–1595), Lord Chancellor of Scotland under James VI from 1587. Metellanus is the standard Latinised form of the surname Maitland. The signature stands in [square brackets] in the source — an editorial supply by Dreyer, the name not being in the copy-text witness; preserved as such here.
  • Recipient styling. Domino de Knudstrup et Vranienburg = "Lord of Knudstrup and Uraniborg" — Tycho's ancestral seat in Scania and his observatory-residence on Hven, treated together as his territorial designation.
  • Place. Lethnitonia = Lethington, Maitland's estate in East Lothian (the house, later renamed Lennoxlove, near Haddington). Header gives "Lethington" per the dateline.
  • Occasion / "my journey in Denmark." Maitland had been in Denmark in 1589–90 as a principal of the embassy attending James VI's marriage to Anne of Denmark; the Scottish party (which also included Peter Young and others) visited Tycho on Hven, the meeting to which Danica mea peregrinatione refers.
  • Petro Iunio = Peter Young (Sir Peter Young of Seaton, 1544–1628), James VI's former tutor and a royal diplomat, the Scottish intermediary through whom Tycho's "matters" (mandata) were being handled; Latinised Iunius.
  • Ioannes Craigius medicus Edinburgensis = John Craig (d. 1620), Edinburgh physician and mathematician who had ventured to dispute certain of Tycho's Axiomata (his cosmological/observational tenets), the controversy Tycho recounts in his own letters of c. 1589–90; named in the apparatus by the edition's convention
  • effigiem … sculptoris industria, ad vivum referentem — the engraved portrait Tycho had enclosed (Tycho circulated engraved likenesses of himself), and for which Maitland here supplies commendatory verses (versus … ad illius ornatum). The verses themselves are not transcribed within this letter.
  • hortulano et aucupe — "the gardener and the fowler" (auceps, a bird-catcher / keeper of fowl): two servants or specialists Tycho had asked Maitland to procure or arrange for; the specific arrangement is not elaborated.
  • Axiomata … impugnare ausus sit — "he dared to attack certain of your axioms": Axiomata kept as a technical term for Tycho's stated principles/tenets. The passage trades on the topos that an opponent's stature magnifies the victor's glory (devicti hostis magnitudine tuam gloriam illustrari).
  • No Greek, no astronomical/astrological symbols occur in this letter.
  • Calendar. die XVI Maij Anno 1592 = "the 16th day of May, 1592." Scotland was on the Julian calendar (Old Style) in 1592 (it did not adopt the Gregorian reform until 1600/1752); dated O.S. accordingly. The catalogue label ("1592. 16. maj. Lethington") agrees with the dateline — no discrepancy. The opening reference to Tycho's letter "13. Novembris" (13 November, presumably 1591) is the letter being answered, not the date of this one.
