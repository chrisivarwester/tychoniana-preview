Editorial note. A long, three-stranded letter to the Bavarian Chancellor Herwart von Hohenburg (companion to Tycho's reply of 28 Aug 1600): (i) the exile/patronage narrative — the instruments' transport from Magdeburg down the Elbe, Rudolf II's favour through Barvitius, and the plague-flight from Benátky to Girsitz; (ii) a dense technical argument that the obliquity of the ecliptic is essentially constant (the ancients merely imprecise), with precession at 51″/yr, the year-length against Copernicus, and the solar eccentricity; and (iii) the celebrated prosthaphaeresis-priority story — Rheticus → Werner → Wittich → Bürgi → an unnamed "scurrilous sycophant" (Ursus) → Clavius, plus the Bavarian-library book-requests and the Leovitius/Fugger manuscripts. TYCHO BRAHE AD IOH. G. HERWARTUM.

NOBILIS et Magnifice vir, amice plurimum honorande, utrasque tuas, eodem tenore ad me datas, unâ cum Catalogo Bibliothecae Serenissimorum Principum Bavariae, recte quidem et satis mature accepi; erantque ea omnia mihi longe gratissima et singularem tuum atque sincerum et benevolum erga me affectum liquido ostendebant. Verum quod tardius respondeam quam debebam, mihi invito ob occupationes varias incidentes evenit. Acceperam tuas, cum in scribendis quam plurimis, tam in Daniam quam hinc inde in Germaniam literis, per filium natu maiorem dirigendis, quem ex Clementissima Caes. Mai. Domini nostri clementissimi voluntate in Patriam pro adferendis residuis instrumentis, ablegavi, eram occupatissimus; cumque is unâ secum haberet literas Caesareae Maiestatis ad Magistratum Magdeburgensem, quibus clementissime rogabantur, ut quaedam ex instrumentis meis Astronomicis, simulque Bibliothecam et meam supellectilem isthic relictam, huc per Albim in Bohemiam transvehi curarent, sperabam id intra tres vel quatuor septimanas confici posse, ideoque ubi libri mei advenissent, me promptius quosnam haberem vel desiderarem ex ijs, qui in Catalogo transmisso memorantur, perspecturum mecum statuebam, tum quoque nonnulla inter res nostras librarias et chartas inventurum, quibus tuis desiderijs pro tempore rectius satisfacerem, ideoque rescriptionem nonnihil data opera procrastinavi, existimans illud tritum locum sic inventurum, Differ, habent parvae commoda magna morae. Verum praeter omnem exspectationem accidit, ut Magdeburgenses subterfugia quaedam in literis Caesareanis invenientes, de rerum mearum huc transportatione aliquamdiu haesitarint, tandemque id officij prorsus detrectarint, ut necesse habuerit amicus quidam meus Magdeburgi, cui hoc negotium concredideram, mihi Magistratus illius loci tergiversationem indicare, misso huc proprio tabellario, quem mox in aulam Caesareanam cum literis Magnifico Domino BARVITIO haec significantibus amandavi. Is vero alias a Caesarea Maiestate Magdeburgensibus denuo scriptas hisce diebus mihi retulit, quibus significantius et magis serio ijs mandatur, ut suis sumptibus in gratiam Caesareae Matis hanc operam praestent, quae literae ijs nunc adportantur. At quoniam Albis admodum declivis, imo quibusdam in locis vadosus est, ob diuturnam quae antecessit siccitatem, ignoro, quam cito id fieri possit, ideo responsionem ad tuam epistolam non diutius protrahendam censeo, praesertim cum ante biduum a praedicto Magnif. Domino BARVITIO, communi nostro amico, omni honore dignissimo, per literas de rescriptione maturanda rogatus sim. Taceo nunc quod et alia inciderint non pauca, quae praeter voluntatem in respondendo me tardiorem reddiderunt. Praeterquam enim quod plurimis curis tam domesticis, quam in structuris pro novo Observatorio atque Laboratorio ex Caesareae Matis clementissimo permissu, adornandis et urgendis indies distinerer, accessit et hoc, quod contagia luis epidemicae arci Benachiae appropinquantia Gynaeceum meum (cum sexus ille alias natura sit timidiusculus) ita terruerint ut precibus eius auscultans, huc in aliam arcem Caesareanam Girsitzam prope Albim tribus milliaribus Praga distantem secedere cum tota familia consultius duxerim, quae transmigratio non parum otij mihi ad scribendum praeripuit. His itaque et alijs de caussis (nolo enim nunc singula referre) cum factum sit, ut nolens volens rescriptione hucusque supersedere necesse habuerim, spero me apud te pro eo, quo praeditus es, animi candore, excusationem et veniam reperturum, quod tuae expectationi non prius morem gesserim, ideoque haec purgationis loco, eo latius praefari volui.

Nunc ad litteras tuas, et quae simul missa sunt, venio, quibus haec pro temporis occasione respondentia incidunt. Quae ab initio attingis de Sacrae Caes. Matis clementissimo erga me favore et studiorum meorum benignissima promotione, ita futurum, animum tibi praesagijsse, veridicus id equidem genius tibi suggessit. Nam ipsius Matem non saltem Caesareum sed et paternum (modo sic loqui liceat) erga me gerere affectum, vel inde liquet, quod nuper bis per praenominatum Mag. Dominum BARVITIUM ad vicinum mihi Dominum Capitaneum Brandeisensem, de me clementissime perscribi mandavit, non saltem quoad ea, quae salarium mihi benignissime deputatum, ubi et quando obtinendum concernunt, sed et insuper significari, ipsius Caesaream Matem proprio motu de me clementissime sollicitam esse, ob pericula luis Epidemicae, quae Benachiae invaluerunt, ideoque clementissime concedere, ut alio me reciperem, aut etiam Viennam usque, si sic lubuerit, proficiscerer, ubi de habitatione commoda et necessarijs ipsius Maiestas mihi tempestive et clementissime prospicere vellet. Quae sane benignissimam et non vulgarem Caes. Matis erga me affectionem haud obscure declarant; quod etiam subiectissimo animo quam gratissime accipio, et dabo operam, adiuvante Numine coelesti, ut ea quae ipsius Mati vicissim clementissime accepta et honorifica esse poterint, pro virili quoad vixero humillime et obsequentissime praestem. Licet vero iter Viennense ob longinquitatem et negotia mea necdum Benachiae recte composita, modeste et humiliter excusarim (quod et ipsius Mati non displicuisse postea intellexi) tamen, uti dictum est, huc Girsitzam secedere non inconsultum duxi. Unde Benachiam subinde excurrere, atque structuras isthic pro me inchoatas urgere, quantum per me licet, non intermitto.

Quod de Patria mea mox subiungis, te non mirari, eam me neglexisse, cum ita fieri soleat, ego sane mea de causa nihil inde afficior, cum ibi esse Patriam, ubi bene et commode vivitur, censeam. Sed potius ipsius Patriae causa doleo, quae propterea apud cordatiores male audit, et audiet forsan diutius, quam nonnulli in ea huius mutationis machinatores praevidere aut credere potuerunt. Ut vero rectius perspicias, quam graves habuerim emigrationis huius causas, mitto fasciculum litterarum, eas per circumstantias exponentium, inter quas aliquae sunt, occasionem, cur in Caesareae Matis ditionem transire maluerim, ostendentes. Casum vero hunc iamdudum a me praevisum fuisse, liber noster Epistolarum (quem te ad manus habere, ex prioribus tuis intellexi) innuere poterit, pagina 198 iuxta calcem et in principio sequentis. Cumque praevisa tela minus feriant, ego hanc sortis meae vicissitudinem, utut hac invalescente aetate, et in tanta familiae copia gravem, patientius et fortius fero. Addidi simul carmina quaedam selecta ex quam plurimis in meam gratiam, postquam Dania excessi, a varijs hinc inde Poëtis concinnata; exspecto brevi plura, tum ab illustri Domino IOSEPHO SCALIGERO et Poëtarum nostri seculi antesignano, PAULO MELISSO, tum quoque alijs claris in Italia et Germania ac vicinis Regnis, vatibus; si qui in vestra Bavaria aut propinqua Augusta Vindelicorum sunt (uti esse non dubito) qui de Heliconis fontibus suaviter venas acuerunt, da operam ut numerum adaugeant. Fieri enim potest, ut aliquando ubi in mediocrem libellum excreverint, ea publicari sinam; ita tamen ut Patria et Rex virique in ea integri, quibus invitis talia mihi contigerunt, excusabile sint. Duo ultima Epigrammata, cum saepe dictus Magnif. Dominus BARVITIUS Caesareae Mati nomine meo humillime ostendisset, quaerendo an liceret ianuae utrique novi Observatorij et Laboratorij marmori incisa imponere, ipsius Matas clementissime annuit atque sic fieri iussit. Quando otium a gravioribus occupationibus tibi fuerit, poteris et haec et priora discessum meum e Dania attingentia, obiter perlustrare tibique reservare.

Sed his minus forte huc pertinentibus omissis, venio nunc ad ea, quae Mathematica et Astronomica sunt, in quibus nostra utrinque versatur voluptas. Quod in dubio illo de ♀ stella a ☿ non eclipsata rationibus meis acquieveris, bene est, addo prioribus et hanc. Non in omni coniunctione ☿ et ipsius stellam infra hanc esse, sed nonnunquam etiam supra, sic postulantibus non saltem meis novis hypothesibus, quae Terrae quietem stabiliunt, sed et Coperniceis mobilitatem eidem vel invitae obtrudentibus. Quae de motu Solis et Lunae in prioribus innui, quaeque a te solerter et circumspecte inibi expensa placuisse video, ita omnino se habere apparentiae demonstrant, maioremque inesse Lunae motui varietatem, quam hactenus perspectum est, convincunt. Recte sane de veterum observationibus censuram profers, eas crassa quadam Minerva acceptas esse, quam enim lata indagine ipsa fixarum loca, e quibus Planetarum observationes dependent, denotârint, inde patet quod non saltem ultra sextam gradus partem haud fuerint solliciti, sed ne hanc quidem ubique attigerint, immo nonnunquam vix semissem, prodentibus id etiamnum stellarum distantijs, quae invariatae permanent ipsorum numeris non accordantibus. Quid igitur, quaeso, in caeteris subtiliori indagini obnoxijs praestare potuerint? Quare non iniuria dubitas de obliquatione Signiferi ab HIPPARCHO et PTOLEMÆO iuxta ERATHOSTENIS placitum retenta, atque hinc occasionem satis ingeniose invenis, cur PTOLEMÆUS ☾ae latitudinem maximam tertia gradus parte in □turis minorem, quam a nobis deprehensa est, statuerit. Atque haec iamdudum penitius introspicienti mihi, dum latitudines fixarum, pro ratione variationis obliquitatis Eclipticae alteratas esse deprehendo, quemadmodum Tomo I Progymnasmatum Astronom. e nostris e. 2 luculenter demonstro, attendi quod applicata inclinatione Eclipticae maxima, quam ipsemet aliquoties accuratissime adinveni P. 23 M. 31½ ad eas stellarum declinationes, quas TIMOCHARES, HIPPARCHUS et PTOLEMÆUS recensuerunt, prodire eandem quam proxime latitudinem, quam etiam nunc habere deprehenduntur, alias non. Ex his itaque et similibus suspicari non immerito licet, non tam revera mutatam esse obliquitatem Signiferi, quam a veteribus illis, intra tertiam gradus partem non satis praecise determinatam.

Ut vero inter plura exempla, dicto libro allegata, unum vel alterum huc advocem, primum id probant utraeque stellae illustres in capitibus ♊, Castor et Pollux dictae, quae paulo ante Tropicum aestivum veterum illorum temporibus versabantur, nunc autem eundem dimidio quasi signo praetergressae sunt, ut ob id promptior ex his detur investigationis ratio; adhibita enim declinatione, quam ijs PTOLEMÆUS e TIMOCHARIS, HIPPARCHI et sua animadversione attribuit, et longitudine earundem ad illorum veterum tempora simul rite disposita, deprehenduntur ambas per Triangulorum demonstrata latitudinem obtinuisse 18 vel 19 M. minorem ea quam nunc coelum ipsum ostendit, quod vel mutatae obliquitati Eclipticae ascribendum, aut eandem tunc fuisse, quae nunc est, statuendum, nulla erit in latitudine sensibilis differentia, unde antiquos illos per tertiam gradus partem angulum obliquationis Eclipticae ad Aequatorem nimium auxisse convincetur. Idipsum quoque secundo minori negotio patet ex lucida Vulturis volantis stella, quam TIMOCHARES, HIPPARCHUS et PTOLEMÆUS unanimiter prodiderunt declinare ab Aequatore versus Boream P. 5⅚, quod illa stella eorum temporibus quam proxime esset quoad longitudinem Tropico hiberno, ideoque declinationem insensibiliter variare. Addita itaque declinatione eorum maxima provenit stellae latitudo P. 29 M. 40, nec enim moror quod PTOLEMÆI erroneus abacus circa hanc contra propria assumpta, uti et saepe alias, eum etiam frustra imitantibus neotericis, admisit, quae omnino excedit eam quam illa ipsa stella nunc coelitus exhibet, tertia fere gradus parte, quantum videlicet dubia apud veteres obliquatio requirit, nostra vero si applicetur, eadem manebit latitudo tam olim quam nunc. Quomodo haec, de quibus etiam nonnulla in antecedentibus meis generali indicatione attigi, cum PTOLEMÆI placitis concilianda sint, non satis liquet, sive erroneae fuerint ipsius observationes (nam de nostris subtilissime trutinatis nihil ambigo) sive quod revera talis latitudinibus et limitibus earundem mutatio subrepserit. Unde etiam patet, nodos horum Planetarum et terminos digressionis ab Ecliptica maximae Apogaeorum motui non obtemperare, uti hactenus creditum est, quodque nimis inconsiderate magnus alias superioris aevi Astronomus COPERNICUS, PTOLEMÆI traditionibus, citra propriam experientiam, in his, uti etiam plerisque alijs, acquieverit, ideoque non mirum, latitudines Planetarum longe aliter ab ipso coelo exhiberi, quam ipsius fert numeratio.

Verum non opus est hoc de Eclipticae obliquatione dubium tam longinque petere atque in libellam expendere, siquidem Artificum observationes, qui post PTOLEMÆUM vixerunt, in eadem determinanda nostrae inventioni citra admodum sensibile discrimen adstipulentur. ALBATEGNIJ certe PTOLEMÆO et nobis ferme intermedij tradita indicant, ante 700 annos obliquitatem hanc maximam vix 4½ scrupulis, et ASRAELIS Hispani, 200 fere annis post, saltem 2½ nostra animadversione maiorem fuisse, quod non nisi maximis instrumentis perceptibile est, qualibus illi vix usi sunt, et per parallaxin Solis ab ijs neglectam quodammodo in uno vel sesquialtero minuto excusari potest, PROPHATIO Iudaeo adhuc paulo post, alijs 200 annis ab ASRAELE eandem nobiscum intra dimidium minutum ante 300 retro annos constituente, siquidem is forte maiori et exactiori usus sit organo et parallaxin Solis praecaverit. Ex quibus omnibus satis probabiliter colligitur, vix aliquam subesse sensibilem obliquationi huic mutationem. Mirum autem videri posset ab ARISTARCHO, qui saltem 40 circiter annis ab obitu ALEXANDRI Magni floruit, usque ad PTOLEMÆUM [qui] 460 ab eodem abfuit, per intermedios ERATOSTHENEM et HIPPARCHUM, plane eandem permansisse Eclipticae summam obliquationem, quam illi intervallo maiore 400 unanimiter annis prodiderunt P. 23 M. 51⅓, nunc vero inde ab ALBATEGNIO ad nos per 700 annos intra 3 Min. (tantillulum enim ALBATEGNIJ a nostra inventione solummodo differt, si parallaxin adhibuisset) eandem permanere, veterum illorum annotatis quasi tertia gradus parte minorem, quod suspicionem movet PTOLEMÆUM et HIPPARCHUM, prout tu etiam vis, antecedentium autoritate nimium confisos fuisse, ideoque similia pro libitu admisisse. Quod vero recentiores utpote PURBACHIUS, REGIOMONTANUS, WERNERUS et ipse quoque COPERNICUS eandem 4 proxime scrupulis nostra minorem reddiderint, ob refractionem Solis in decliviori situ brumali, quae ad 8 vel 9 M. excrescere potest et tantundem Solis altitudinem meridianam iusto maiorem reddere (quod pro dimidia parte declinationem maximam ex utroque Tropico petitam nimis coarctat) ab ipsis non praevisam, hic error commissus est; quin et observatae distantiae ☉is a vertice Noribergae, tam a REGIOMONTANO quam eius discipulo WALTHERO ante 100 circiter annos factae, si tam parallaxis quam refractio Solis ijs rite applicentur, convincunt, eandem intra ½ M. fuisse tunc declinationem Solis maximam respectu Aequatoris, cum ea quam nos observavimus.

Sic quoque promotionem fixarum stellarum non adeo irregularem esse existimo, prout COPERNICUS ex veterum minus ratis observationibus, cum suis et illis etiam non satis accuratis collatione facta, per Aequinoctiorum indicatam anticipationem inducere et excusare allaboravit. Si enim observata in stellis HIPPARCHI (neglectis PTOLEMÆI dubijs annotatis) per intermediantem ALBATEGNIUM, qui in his accuratior erat, ad nostra inventa deduxerimus, eas in annis ab HIPPARCHO ad ALBATEGNIUM 1006 P. 14 M. 15 confecisse, ab hoc vero ad nos interiectis annis 705 decem praecise gradus absolvisse constabit. Unde eas in toto hoc interstitio, utrobique etiam subsumpto, confecisse in 70½ annis 1 G. et singulis 51'' aequali et ordinario progressu consequens erit. Quare suspicio non iniuria moveri poterit, PTOLEMÆI tradita aestimative solummodo assumpta esse, ut centenis annis imitatione quorundam antecessorum, quos temporis paucitas et observationum incertitudo fefellit, 1 G. motui fixarum ascriberet. Pari insuper ratione anni inaequalitatem non tam enormem et variam esse, qualis ab eodem COPERNICO nobis obtrusa, censeo, refragantibus vel hoc ipso seculo, ut de antiquis illis nunc nihil dicam REGIOMONTANI et WALTHERI observationibus, cum nostris in trutinam vocatis, quae anni quantitatem ultra dies suos et horas 5 non plane 49 Min. attingere probant, cum tamen iuxta COPERNICUM 6 M. et ultra maior foret, quod per continuam aggregationem efficit, illum in Aequinoctiorum temporibus intolerabiliter hallucinari, adeo ut nunc vernale 14½ horis, id est ultra dimidium diem iusto plus prolonget, qui error adeo foecundus est, ut post 100 annos ipsum integrum diem et tres insuper horas in Aequinoctio verno constituendo nimium habere necessarium evadat. Et si ab HIPPARCHO per ALBATEGNIUM usque ad REGIOMONTANUM et nos derivetur anni quantitas sibi fere similis, nec adeo enormis, uti putavit COPERNICUS, cognoscetur, solo PTOLEMÆO negotium (undecunque tandem in hunc errorem prolapsus) omne interturbante, nec ipso COPERNICO Solis motiones ipsi coelo consonas rite applicant, cuius derivationis occasiones praedicto Progymn. libro c. 1 aperuimus. Hinc insuper adducor, ut de eccentricitate Solis a PTOLEMÆO hucusque tantopere mutata ambigam, siquidem ea quam is unâ cum HIPPARCHO retinuit P. 2½ qualium semidiameter eccentrici est 60, ea postea ab ALBATEGNIO et ASRAELE pariter inventa sit P. solummodo earundem 2 et M. 5, quae a nostra exactissima designatione P. 2 M. 9 non magnopere deficit, et res sane est subtilissimae inquisitionis, ut non mirum sit, in tribus vel quatuor scrupulis hîc discordare vel Lynceo oculatiores Astronomos, quae omnia tuae gravissimae et politissimae censurae expendenda discernendaque subijcio. Ex quibus etiam singulis liquidissime constat, te non frustra de veterum diligentia in talibus administrandis dubitare et nostrae quoque hac in parte haesitationi non inconvenienter subscribere. Animadverti et ego illos harmoniam quandam e numeris et proportionibus Pythagoreis (uti existimo) quaesivisse, atque eidem nimis secure acquievisse; quicunque apparentijs exacte consona sic tradiderit, erit mihi magnus APOLLO, id autem posterius investigandum. Sed hoc opus hic labor est, neque enim dubium, Deum universi autorem omnia etiam in coelestibus harmonice condidisse, quemadmodum in homine μικροκόσμῳ et tota inferiori natura intelligente apparet.

Tabulas nostras de motu luminarium seorsim quidem, quam primum fieri licuerit, imprimi curabo, ac caeteris Planetis, in quibus adhuc nonnulla elaborabo, praemittam, sed non libenter nudos numeros absque observationum et demonstrationum robore emitto. Tycho Brahe to Johann Georg Herwart.

Noble and Magnificent man, much-to-be-honoured friend: both of your letters, given to me with the same tenor, together with the Catalogue of the Library of the most Serene Princes of Bavaria, I received rightly and early enough; and all those [things] were far most welcome to me, and plainly showed your singular, sincere, and benevolent affection toward me. But that I reply later than I ought has befallen me against my will, on account of various occupations arising. I had received yours while I was most busy in writing very many letters, both to Denmark and here and there into Germany, to be directed through my eldest son — whom, by the most clement will of His Imperial Majesty our most clement Lord, I sent off into my homeland to fetch the remaining instruments; and since he had with him letters of the Imperial Majesty to the Magistrate of Magdeburg, by which they were most clemently asked to take care that some of my Astronomical instruments, and at the same time my Library and household goods left there, be conveyed hither into Bohemia by the Elbe — I hoped this could be accomplished within three or four weeks, and therefore resolved that, when my books had arrived, I should more readily perceive which of those mentioned in the Catalogue sent [to me] I had or wanted, and should also find some [things] among our library-goods and papers by which I might better satisfy your desires for the time; and so I somewhat deliberately put off the reply, thinking I should thus find that well-worn line true: "Defer; small delays have great advantages." But, beyond all expectation, it happened that the Magdeburgers, finding certain loopholes in the Imperial letters, hesitated for a while about the transport of my things hither, and at last quite declined that duty — so that a certain friend of mine at Magdeburg, to whom I had entrusted this business, had to indicate to me the magistrate's evasion, a courier of his own being sent hither, whom I soon dispatched to the Imperial court with letters signifying these things to the Magnificent Lord Barvitius. He, however, has these days reported to me other [letters] written anew by the Imperial Majesty to the Magdeburgers, in which it is more significantly and seriously enjoined them to perform this service at their own expense, for the favour of the Imperial Majesty — which letters are now being brought to them. But since the Elbe is very low, nay in some places shoaly, on account of the long preceding drought, I do not know how soon it can be done; and therefore I judge the response to your letter is not to be protracted longer — especially since two days ago I was asked by the aforesaid Magnificent Lord Barvitius, our common friend, most worthy of all honour, by letter, to hasten my reply. I say nothing now of the many other [things] that have fallen out, which have rendered me, against my will, slower in replying. For besides being daily distracted by very many cares, both domestic and in the structures to be furnished and pressed forward for the new Observatory and Laboratory by the Imperial Majesty's most clement permission, there was added this: that the contagions of the epidemic plague, approaching the castle of Benátky, so terrified my women's quarters [Gynaeceum] (since that sex is otherwise by nature rather timid) that, hearkening to their prayers, I judged it more advisable to withdraw hither, with my whole household, to another Imperial castle, Girsitz, near the Elbe, three miles distant from Prague — which migration snatched no little leisure for writing from me. By these, then, and other causes (for I do not wish now to relate each), since it has come about that, willy-nilly, I have had to forbear the reply until now, I hope to find with you — in keeping with that candour of mind with which you are endowed — excuse and pardon, that I have not sooner complied with your expectation; and therefore I have wished to preface these [things] more amply in place of an apology.

Now I come to your letter, and what was sent at the same time, to which these [things] occur as fitting by the occasion of the time. As to what you touch at the beginning, about the most clement favour of the Sacred Imperial Majesty toward me and the most kind promotion of my studies — that your mind foreboded it would be so — a truthful genius indeed suggested that to you. For that His Majesty bears toward me an affection not only Imperial but also (if one may so speak) paternal, is clear even from this: that lately, twice, through the aforenamed Magnificent Lord Barvitius, he commanded that it be written most clemently to my neighbour, the Lord Captain of Brandeis, about me — not only as to those things which concern the salary most kindly assigned me (where and when it is to be obtained), but also that it be signified that His Imperial Majesty, of his own motion, is most clemently solicitous about me, on account of the dangers of the epidemic plague which have grown strong at Benátky, and therefore most clemently grants that I might withdraw elsewhere, or even go as far as Vienna, if it should so please, where His Majesty would provide for me, in good time and most clemently, a convenient dwelling and necessaries. Which truly declare, not obscurely, the Imperial Majesty's most kind and uncommon affection toward me — which too I receive most gratefully with a most submissive mind, and I shall take care, the heavenly Deity helping, to perform to His Majesty in turn, as far as in me lies, while I live, most humbly and obediently, those things which can be most clemently acceptable and honourable to him. But although I modestly and humbly excused the journey to Vienna, on account of [its] distance and my affairs not yet rightly composed at Benátky (which afterward I understood did not displease His Majesty), yet, as has been said, I judged it not unadvised to withdraw hither to Girsitz; whence I do not cease to run over to Benátky from time to time, and to press forward the structures begun there for me, as far as in me lies.

As to what you presently subjoin about my homeland — that you do not wonder that I have neglected it, since it is wont to be so — I indeed, for my own part, am affected by nothing thence, since I judge that one's homeland is there where one lives well and conveniently. But I rather grieve for the homeland's own sake, which on this account is ill-spoken-of among the more sensible, and will be perhaps longer than some of the contrivers of this change in it could foresee or believe. But that you may more rightly perceive how grave were the causes I had for this emigration, I send a packet of letters expounding them through the circumstances — among which are some showing the occasion why I preferred to pass into the Imperial Majesty's dominion. But that this case was long ago foreseen by me, our Book of Letters (which I understood from your earlier [letter] that you have at hand) can hint, on page 198 near the foot and at the beginning of the following [page]. And since foreseen darts strike less, I bear this vicissitude of my lot — grievous though it be in this failing age, and amid so great a multitude of family — more patiently and bravely. I have added at the same time certain poems, selected from the very many composed for my sake, after I left Denmark, by various Poets here and there; I expect more shortly, both from the illustrious Lord Joseph Scaliger and from the standard-bearer of the Poets of our age, Paul Melissus, and also from other famous bards in Italy and Germany and the neighbouring Kingdoms; if there are any in your Bavaria or in neighbouring Augsburg (as I do not doubt there are) who have sweetly sharpened their veins from the founts of Helicon, take care that they increase the number. For it may be that some time, when they have grown into a moderate booklet, I shall let them be published — yet in such a way that the homeland and the King and the upright men in it, against whose will such things befell me, be excusable. The two last Epigrams, when the oft-said Magnificent Lord Barvitius had most humbly shown them to the Imperial Majesty in my name, asking whether it might be allowed to set them, carved in marble, on each door of the new Observatory and Laboratory — His Majesty most clemently assented and ordered it so done. When you have leisure from graver occupations, you can glance over both these and the earlier [pieces] touching my departure from Denmark, and reserve [them] for yourself.

But, omitting these [things], perhaps less pertinent here, I come now to those which are Mathematical and Astronomical, in which our pleasure on both sides is engaged. That you have acquiesced in my reasons in that doubt about the star of Venus [♀] not eclipsed by Mercury [☿], it is well; I add this too to the earlier [reasons]: that not in every conjunction of Mercury is its star below this [Venus], but sometimes also above — so demanding not only my new hypotheses, which establish the rest of the Earth, but also the Copernican [ones], which obtrude mobility on it even unwilling. What I hinted in [my] earlier [letter] about the motion of the Sun and Moon, and which I see pleased [you], weighed there by you skilfully and circumspectly, the appearances demonstrate to be entirely so, and prove that there is a greater variety in the Moon's motion than has hitherto been perceived. You rightly bring forward a censure of the ancients' observations, that they were received with a certain rough Minerva [crudely]; for by how broad an inquiry they noted the very places of the fixed [stars], on which the observations of the Planets depend, is clear from this — that they were solicitous not [even] beyond a sixth part of a degree [10′], but did not everywhere attain even this, nay sometimes scarcely a half [degree] — the distances of the stars (which remain unvaried) still betraying this, not according with their numbers. What, I ask, then, could they accomplish in the rest, subject to subtler inquiry? Wherefore not unjustly do you doubt about the obliquity of the Zodiac retained by Hipparchus and Ptolemy according to Eratosthenes's tenet; and hence you find, ingeniously enough, the occasion why Ptolemy established the Moon's [☾] maximum latitude a third of a degree less, in the quadratures [□], than has been ascertained by us. And these [matters], to me long since looking more deeply into [them] — while I find the latitudes of the fixed [stars] altered in proportion to the variation of the obliquity of the Ecliptic, as I demonstrate amply in Tome I of the Astronomical Progymnasmata, chapter 2, from our [observations] — I attended to [this]: that, the maximum inclination of the Ecliptic applied (which I myself several times most accurately found = 23° 31½′) to those declinations of the stars which Timochares, Hipparchus, and Ptolemy recorded, there comes out very nearly the same latitude as they are now found to have — otherwise not. From these, then, and similar [cases], it is licit not undeservedly to suspect that the obliquity of the Zodiac was not so much really changed as imprecisely determined by those ancients, within a third part of a degree.

But, that I may, among the several examples adduced in the said book, call one or another hither: first this is proved by both the famous stars in the heads of Gemini [♊], called Castor and Pollux, which in the times of those ancients dwelt a little before the summer Tropic, but now have passed it by, as it were, by half a sign — so that hence a readier method of investigation is given: for, the declination which Ptolemy attributed to them from the observation of Timochares, Hipparchus, and his own being applied, and their longitude at the same time rightly disposed to the times of those ancients, both are found, by the demonstrations of Triangles, to have had a latitude 18 or 19′ less than that which the heaven itself now shows; which [discrepancy] is either to be ascribed to the changed obliquity of the Ecliptic, or — if it be established that it was then the same as it is now — there will be no sensible difference in latitude, whence it will be proven that those ancients increased the angle of the Ecliptic's obliquity to the Equator too much, by a third of a degree. The same likewise appears, by a second lesser labour, from the bright star of Vultur volans [Aquila / Altair], which Timochares, Hipparchus, and Ptolemy unanimously reported to decline from the Equator toward the North 5⅚°, because that star, in their times, was very nearly at the winter Tropic as to longitude, and therefore varied its declination insensibly. Their maximum declination [obliquity] being added, therefore, there comes out the star's latitude 29° 40′ (for I do not heed that Ptolemy's erroneous table admitted, about this [star], against his own assumptions — as also often elsewhere, the moderns vainly imitating him too — [a value] which entirely exceeds that which that very star now exhibits from the heaven, by nearly a third of a degree, namely as much as the doubtful obliquity among the ancients requires; but if ours be applied, the same latitude will remain both of old and now). There is added this too (if it please to take the Planets into counsel): that the maximum latitude of the star of Mars [♂] toward the North supports this induction — whose northern limit, by the Apogee at 25½° of Cancer [♋], Ptolemy established, and made the latitude thence 4⅓°. But we have exactly found its Apogee now to dwell at 23½° of Leo [♌] (5° earlier than in Copernicus's opinion), and the northern limit to precede [it] by 6½°, and the northern latitude, when it is there opposed to the Sun, to be 4° 35′ — which exceeds Ptolemy's handed-down [value] by a quarter-degree, as much as the smaller inclination of the Ecliptic very nearly requires in those places. Granted that, to say the truth, the same does not equally square in the other two superior Planets, whose northern limit of maximum latitude Ptolemy would have to be about the beginning of Libra [♎], where the error of the Ecliptic's inclination becomes almost insensible; and whereas he established the maximum northern latitude in Saturn [♄] at 3° 6′, we could find [it] no greater than 2° 49½′, which falls short of his by a little more than a quarter-degree; and likewise in Jupiter [♃] the maximum northern latitude was insinuated by him at 2° 5′, which we have attained at only 1° 39½′, so that 25′ are altogether wanting — which differences in each of these Planets cannot be excused by the uncertain inclination of the Ecliptic to the Equator, since they fall out not far from the concurrence of both circles. Add this too: the northern-latitude limit of Saturn, which Ptolemy believed 50° earlier than its Apogee, now stands distant, in the preceding [direction], 65½° from the Apogee (which sticks at 26° of Sagittarius [♐], 3½° earlier in the Copernican [reckoning]); so too in Jupiter, where he handed down the northern limit of latitude 20° beyond the Apogee, [it is] now found by me only 3° later than the same. For at this time the Apogee of Jupiter occupies 3° of Libra [♎], and by Copernicus's mind is nearly 4° earlier; but the northern limit stands at the completed 6° of Libra. How these [things] — about which I touched some [matters] too in my foregoing [letter] by general indication — are to be reconciled with Ptolemy's tenets, is not sufficiently clear: whether his observations were erroneous (for about ours, most subtly weighed, I doubt nothing), or whether truly such a change has crept into the latitudes and their limits. Whence it also appears that the nodes of these Planets, and the limits of their maximum digression from the Ecliptic, do not obey the motion of the Apogees, as has hitherto been believed; and that the great Astronomer of a former age, Copernicus, too inconsiderately acquiesced in Ptolemy's traditions, without his own experience, in these as also in very many other [matters]; and therefore [it is] no wonder that the latitudes of the Planets are exhibited by the heaven itself far otherwise than his numeration bears.

But there is no need to seek this doubt about the Ecliptic's obliquity so far off and to weigh [it] in the balance, since the observations of the Artificers who lived after Ptolemy support our finding in determining the same, without a very sensible difference. The [data] of al-Battānī [Albategnius] — nearly intermediate between Ptolemy and us — indicate that 700 years ago this maximum obliquity was scarcely 4½′ greater [than now]; and [those] of Azrael the Spaniard [al-Zarqālī/Arzachel], nearly 200 years after, at least 2½′ greater than our observation — which is perceptible only by the greatest instruments, such as they scarcely used, and can in some measure be excused, by the solar parallax neglected by them, within one or one-and-a-half minutes; Prophatius the Jew [Jacob ben Makhir] a little later still, another 200 years from Azrael, establishing the same as we [do] within half a minute, 300 years back, since he perhaps used a larger and more exact instrument and guarded against the solar parallax. From all which it is gathered probably enough that there is scarcely any sensible change underlying this obliquity. But it might seem a wonder that from Aristarchus — who flourished about 40 years from the death of Alexander the Great — down to Ptolemy, who was 460 [years] from the same, through the intermediate Eratosthenes and Hipparchus, the supreme obliquity of the Ecliptic remained plainly the same, which they over a greater interval of 400 years unanimously reported as 23° 51⅓′; but now, from al-Battānī to us through 700 years, [it] remains the same within 3′ (for al-Battānī differs from our finding by only a very little, if he had applied parallax), [though] nearly a third of a degree smaller than the notations of those ancients — which moves the suspicion that Ptolemy and Hipparchus (as you too hold) trusted too much in the authority of their predecessors, and therefore admitted like [values] at [their] pleasure. But that the moderns — namely Peurbach, Regiomontanus, Werner, and Copernicus himself too — rendered the same nearly 4′ smaller than ours: this error was committed on account of the refraction of the Sun in the lower winter position, which can grow to 8 or 9′, and render the Sun's meridian altitude just so much greater than is just (which, by half, too greatly contracts the maximum declination sought from each Tropic), [a refraction] not foreseen by them; nay, even the observed distances of the Sun [☉] from the zenith at Nuremberg, made both by Regiomontanus and his disciple Walther about 100 years ago, if both parallax and the Sun's refraction be rightly applied to them, prove that the Sun's maximum declination with respect to the Equator was then the same, within ½′, as that which we have observed.

So too I think the advancement of the fixed stars [precession] is not so irregular as Copernicus laboured to introduce and excuse, by the anticipation of the equinoxes indicated, from the ancients' less-settled observations, a collation being made with his [own] and theirs (also not accurate enough). For if we deduce the observations on the stars of Hipparchus (Ptolemy's doubtful notations being neglected), through the intermediate al-Battānī (who was more accurate in these), to our findings, it will be established that they accomplished, in the 1006 years from Hipparchus to al-Battānī, 14° 15′, and from him to us, the interposed 705 years, precisely ten degrees. Whence it will follow that in the whole interval, taken on both sides too, they accomplished 1° in 70½ years, and 51″ in each single [year], by an equal and ordinary progress. Wherefore the suspicion can not unjustly be moved that Ptolemy's handed-down [values] were assumed only estimatively, so that he ascribed 1° to the motion of the fixed [stars] in a hundred years, in imitation of certain predecessors whom the paucity of time and the uncertainty of observations deceived. By a like reasoning, moreover, I think the inequality of the year is not so enormous and various as was obtruded on us by the same Copernicus — even this very century gainsaying [it] (to say nothing now of those ancient observations) — [namely] the observations of Regiomontanus and Walther, called to the balance with ours, which prove the quantity of the year to attain, beyond its days, 5 hours [and] not quite 49 minutes — whereas according to Copernicus it would be 6 minutes and more greater, which by continual aggregation effects that he hallucinates intolerably in the times of the equinoxes, so much so that he now prolongs the vernal [equinox] by 14½ hours, that is, more than half a day beyond what is just — an error so fertile that after 100 years it turns out he has too much, by a whole day and three hours besides, in establishing the vernal equinox. And if from Hipparchus through al-Battānī down to Regiomontanus and us the quantity of the year be derived, it will be known [to be] nearly similar to itself, and not so enormous as Copernicus thought — Ptolemy alone (from wherever at last he slipped into this error) disturbing the whole business; nor does Copernicus himself rightly apply the Sun's motions consonant with the heaven itself — the occasions of which derivation we opened in the said Progymnasmata, book [chapter] 1. Hence too I am led to doubt about the eccentricity of the Sun, so greatly changed from Ptolemy down to now — since that which he, together with Hipparchus, retained at 2½ [parts] (of which the semidiameter of the eccentric is 60) was afterward found by al-Battānī and Azrael alike at only 2 [parts] and 5 minutes, which does not greatly fall short of our most exact designation, 2° [parts] 9′ — and the matter is truly of the subtlest inquiry, so that it is no wonder that Astronomers more keen-sighted than Lynceus disagree here in three or four minutes; all which I submit to your most weighty and most polished censure, to be weighed and discerned. From all and each of which it is most clearly established that you do not vainly doubt about the ancients' diligence in administering such [matters], and not unfittingly subscribe to our hesitation in this part too. I too have noticed that they sought a certain harmony from numbers and Pythagorean proportions (as I think), and acquiesced in it too securely; whoever shall hand down [values] thus exactly consonant with the appearances will be to me a great Apollo — but that is to be investigated hereafter. But this is the work, this the labour [Virgil]; for there is no doubt that God, the author of the universe, founded all things harmonically, even in the celestials, as appears in man, the microcosm [μικροκόσμῳ], and in the whole understanding lower nature.

Our Tables of the motion of the luminaries I shall take care to have printed separately, as soon as it can be done, and shall send [them] ahead of the other Planets, in which I shall still elaborate some [things]; but I do not gladly issue bare numbers without the strength of observations and demonstrations. For I wish my [results] to be settled and demonstrated, lest any place be deservedly left for doubt or detraction; yet I shall be at first rather sparing in these, and shall bring forth at least the most necessary. The rest I shall reserve for that great Theatre of Astronomy which I am attempting — if, by God's kindness and the Emperor's subsidies, it shall be permitted to complete it — where I purpose to weave together amply each [thing], both as to observations and as to the Geometrical and Arithmetical [matters] making for this divine art. Meanwhile I would most gladly communicate to you those [things] which concern the calculation of Eclipses, in so far as they have hitherto been elaborated by me; but I have few of them here with me. The chief [ones] are among my library-goods left at Magdeburg; if any have perchance been carried hither by my students, for their daily use, I will soon have inquiry made about them at Benátky (where those which I took with me from Wittenberg are still kept), and will add what shall be found to these — although not all are so polished and brought to the final file as is required, and I have others begun and in manuscript, though not now at hand. A certain Hamburg Printer deceived me, meanwhile, while, making for Bohemia, I [went] thither to Dresden a year ago, and corrupted and badly printed the gatherings on the Moon's [☾] motion wanted in my Progymnasmata; and while afterward, through the winter leisure, I tried to renew this labour at Wittenberg, I found neither the required fidelity nor diligence among the Printers of that place, and soon, being most clemently called hither by the Imperial Majesty, I was forced to break off what was begun there, other impediments too coming on which threw delays in my way. But as soon as everything shall be more fully completed (which I shall strive may be done at the first time too), I will not unwillingly communicate to you, whether printed or in manuscript, not doubting that those [things] which shall be private and not yet made public, entrusted to you and you alone, you will keep reserved. Nor indeed would there have been need of so great a protestation and obligation in this part, since I trust you otherwise — though unknown by face, yet sufficiently perceived by fame and even by these disputations ventilated between us — and do not refuse to attribute [to you] that which your virtue deserves. As for the Epochs which you require, if I find the rest, I will add [them], so that our restitutions may be applied on both sides to Ptolemaic times, or even forward through several centuries, until you have obtained fuller and more exact [ones] from me, in so far as the ancients' observations, taken in a broad manner (which you yourself confess not inconsiderately), allow it. Use these meanwhile.

You exhort that I hasten my Astronomical works — which also many excellent men throughout Europe, as far as Christendom extends, have exhorted, and still exhort; nay, I too bear this delay quite unwillingly. I am now away from my homeland, where my studies were disturbed, for nearly three years, nor was it permitted to find the longed-for rest for them before now, our most clement Emperor granting it; and since I alone do not suffice for so great a burden of observations and computations, I have now at least with me 3 or 4 students, whose work I use in this matter — whereas before, in Denmark, while the kingdom of Urania stood whole and the renowned castle [stood], I was wont to maintain 10 or 12 for this cause. But I hope that, when our most clement Emperor and Lord shall have looked more deeply into the mass of the matter, he will still more kindly extend most clement helping hands, and will not yield to that old Alfonso [X] — although such expenses are not required — and will nonetheless promote more solid [things] (let the saying be without envy).

I gladly understood that you are diligently versed in the compendia of Triangles to be resolved into numbers by prosthaphaereses alone. This ingenious and most useful inquiry I undertook to unfold nearly 20 years ago from the Dialogue of Rheticus, to which he subjoined whole Tables of Triangles, computed by himself only to sextants of degrees. I took the occasion, while he advises that the compendia of Geometrical and Astronomical calculation must be sought — so that, without the Astronomical tables of equations or prosthaphaereses, the true places of the Planets might be drawn more readily from the mean motions alone — which, since it could by no means be done through tedious multiplications and divisions, [showed that] this other way, afterward fruitfully discovered by us, had to be entered; to which Werner too, in his little book On the motion of the eighth sphere, furnished some handle. Afterward, Paul Wittich of good memory, an excellent Mathematician of Breslau, coming upon [me] and visiting me in Denmark at Uraniborg, in the year [15]80, for 4 months — who had also meanwhile sweated over such [things] (admonished by me at Wittenberg when I once passed through there) — the work being communicated with him, I rendered those very [things] more lucid and ever afterward had [them] in use, whereby the expense of time and labour might be lessened for my students reducing our observations into numbers. But Wittich, departing from me and afterward approaching the most illustrious Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse of most praised memory, both expounded there the construction of my instruments, and divulged these mysteries of Triangles to the Prince's clockmaker Jost Bürgi [Justus Byrgius], an excellent mechanic — from whom a certain scurrilous Sycophant (whose genius, if any there be, dwells most basely, [and] whom I deem unworthy to be named here, [but] what a reviler and calumniator he is, I shall leave attested elsewhere) had them, whether by consent or by fraud I would not say, and afterward published [them] as his own at Strasbourg, with certain others scraped together here and there — maimed, however, and mutilated. Whence, taking the occasion, but wrongly attributing [them] to that man, that most excellent Mathematician Christophorus Clavius inserted them into his most erudite volume On the Astrolabe, and in a certain way augmented [them] — occupied, however, as far as I see, only with the Spherical and Right-angled [triangles], which by no means suffice for our intention. The Lord Jacob Curtius of best memory, former Vice-Chancellor of the Empire, a man greater than all praise, and exceedingly fond and understanding of these studies (whom would that the fates had reserved longer for us!), laboured, I say, to adapt these not only to oblique-angled Spherical [triangles] but also to certain plane Triangles. But in this latter, as I hear, he did not altogether attain what he wished — whether hindered by the tedium of the labour, the gravest occupations of so ample an office in so great a Republic calling him away, or also forestalled by untimely death. When I lingered at Wittenberg for a while last winter and noticed that the Typographical labours did not succeed according to my mind, I took up this work again into [my] hands, and, the eminent Professor of Mathematics there Melchior Jöstelius being applied, I caused all those [things] which concern both Spherical and plane Triangles — whatever fall into Astronomical and Geographical use — to be reduced compendiously into order, to be reckoned by prosthaphaereses alone, so that nothing further can now be wanted in these; which too, in its time, I will have made public for the common utility and promotion of these studies — nay, even before [that], if you so request, I will not unwillingly render you a sharer of them, since every good is communicable, especially to the worthy; trusting that you will return like for like in your similar findings which you have ready (for eyes often see more than an eye); if you anticipate [me], so much the more, and so much the sooner, will you instigate me to perform it.

[The books which I destined for your most serene and most illustrious Princes, and also for you, it is not yet permitted to send; for the copies of our Astronomical Mechanics [Mechanica], which I committed to a Prague painter to be illuminated (as they call it), I have not yet received back — for it has not pleased to inquire about them solicitously in this dangerous time; the rest, in this unrest of my affairs and a state not yet sufficiently composed, could not be written out as quickly as I would wish — but it will nonetheless be done, God willing, at the very first opportunity, since every promise falls into a debt. That you have so benevolently communicated to me the Catalogue of Mathematical books found in your Princes' most-furnished Library there, and have wished to be informed about those which I shall dispatch [request], I give the greatest thanks I can. For this matter is most acceptable to me, since I am earnestly delighted by such [things]. And although very many of them pleased, I have nevertheless selected from them only 50, which you see denoted in the attached sheet — among them are also the Hebrew books afterward suggested to me by you. If you send all these to me at the very first occasion, you will have done a most welcome thing; I will restore them all in good faith at the time predefined by me; and if you add at the same time those six which I marked together, excerpted from the Catalogue of the Frankfurt Fairs, I will at once pay the indicated price and send [it] back.]

[There occurs to me also something else, after I saw you dealing with me freely and liberally about this literary matter. I remember, when in my youth I dwelt for nearly two years at Augsburg, a city neighbouring you, and kept watch from time to time over celestial observations there in the garden of the Lord Consul Paul Hainzel [Hentzelius] of good memory, by the great Quadrant having 21 feet from centre to circumference, built there by us — among other [things]] [I] understood that the illustrious and generous Lords Fugger keep in their most ample Library certain excellent books, both in this and in another genre of the arts — of inspecting which, however, the opportunity, then, for I know not what impediments, was not given; and among others I learned that some manuscript works of that most laborious and eminent man, Cyprian Leovitius [Cyprianus Leovitius] the Bohemian, of praised memory, were sought out by [his] widow after his death and are detained there — such as Ephemerides, continued by him for some hundreds of years from the Alphonsine Calculation to ten-day [intervals] at least, and others from the Copernican [Tables] beginning from the year 1500 (how far they extend, I do not now recall); also the same Cyprian's resolved Tables of directions, in which the oblique ascensions are extended to the eighth degree of latitude on each side from the Ecliptic.

[I also had in hand a great work On Genethliacal Astrology, which whether he completed and [whether] it came to the Fuggers, I do not know. If you should procure for me these and similar manuscript and not-yet-published works of this most excellent man — so that I might inspect them for a few days at leisure (for they will be of no use in restoring the Astronomical [science], and in those [things] which I am attempting, since they are, as you know, commonplace) — provided [you do so] by the authority and most clement intercession at least of your most Serene Princes (to whom that Republic and the Fuggers themselves deservedly attribute very much), my name being nonetheless concealed, if you should so judge advisable — you will have done a most acceptable office; I will restore whatever I obtain inviolate, at the appointed day; and if I could at the same time obtain an index of all the books held in that splendid Library, especially of the Mathematicians and Philosophers, it will be the more acceptable to me. Now I add no more, lest I detain and weary you too much at one time. But I officiously and most lovingly ask that you take in good part these [things] which have now come to [my] mouth; and if, as you give hope, you shall some time not be burdened to visit me, you will come not only as a most longed-for guest, but as an intimate friend, to be received with every kind of office according to my measure. Provided it be permitted by the Emperor, and a convenience some time offer itself, that I enter those regions, I shall not forbear to address you in person at Munich too; nay, whatever other offices can proceed from me, I most gladly offer; and I pray from [my] heart that you, with your whole house, fare as well and as long as possible.]

Given with a hastening pen, from the Imperial castle of Girsitz, the 16th day of November, New Style, in the Year 1599.
