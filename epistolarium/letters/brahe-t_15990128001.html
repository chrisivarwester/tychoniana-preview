Sic omnia fatis

Neque enim ulla unquam extitêre tempora hisce nostris turbulentiora, quibus omnia

τινί μηδὲν ποιεῖσθαι προτιμότερον, et publica et privata ita undique in

unâ effusa phialâ, confluxerint, ut nec immerito sola præ cæteris omnibus
anteactis seculis summe deplorata et calamitosa dici queant. Quæ enim fuêre
(dicat mihi quispiam, qui collatas singulorum
temporum historias cognovit) unquam secula, cum his ex
virtutum et vitiorum præposteris exacte extremitatibus conferenda? in quibus sic
omnia sursum deorsumque sacra et profana sibi invicem confusa extiterint et
perplexa? in quibus sic omnia vitia et turpitudines ad summum pervenerint
fastigium, in quo sine rubore etiam et propemodum cum aliqua laudis vindicatione
steterint? virtus, honestas, devolutæ ad imum ubi pessundatæ et proculcatæ
iacuerint? in quibus maior solutioris et petulantis vitæ impunitas et licentia,
maiores passim in nationum et regnorum statibus intestini motus ac seditiones?
Adeo per præceps totus uno veluti coniurato impetu in dedecus et omnium vitiorum
barathrum, in mutuum excidium et perniciem grassatur et ruit mundus. Miserrimus
est profecto miseræ et omni ope destitutæ Germaniæ nostræ status. Non externus hostis hanc populatur, non
aliena fraus diripit, sed suis ipsa viribus ruit, ad seipsam evertendam callida
est et dolos struit. Quid obsecro existimandum, externos populos, Italos puto,
Gallos, Hispanos, loqui de nobis? Quid literæ et scripta testabuntur? Durum est
et asperum dictu, sed dicendum tamen. Nobilium in nobiles, civium in cives, imo
fratrum, adfinium, cognatorum, propinquorum in suos commemorabitur impius et
nefarius furoris atque avaritiæ et invidiæ impetus, insidiæ, circumventio,
inductio, fallaciæ. Non tantum mali in summa calamitate esse potest, quantum in hac ignominia et dedecore. Quæ autem hæc est rabies? Nihil
curare aliquem, quid liberis, uxori, consanguineis, affinibus suis fiat? an
nobiscum extingui delerique cupimus omnia? Et illæ Neronianæ et Caianæ sententiæ
in animo et voces in ore sunt: »Me mortuo terra absumatur
ignibus;« vel magis etiam tristes:

»ignes, ruinæ populum premant,

O gentis et nationis laudatissimæ ingens probrum! Mirantur interim multi, quonam
malo Genio, quove fato nostro, illa morum corruptela totque discordiæ et
dissidia tam in ordine politice, quam in ecclesiastico passim nata sint, et
indies magis magisque augeantur, non abs re quidem id mirantur, et dolent etiam
ob rei, quæ tam multa secum mala invehat, et pietati iustitiæque interitum
minetur, diuturnitatem et magnitudinem. Verum si admiratores illi et animorum
nostrorum pravitatem vitæque labem, de quibus paulo ante dictum, ac gravissima,
quibus ea tota scatet ac sordet, vulnera sibi ob oculos ponant, et, quod
proximum est, in tot et tantis, quibus laboramus morbis, nullam fere sanitatis
speciem et spem reliquam esse considerent, non amplius mirabuntur; sed dolebunt
nihilominus, quod de morbis nostris curandis nullo modo nec cogitemus nec
laboremus. Vitia hæc sunt, ut uno verbo dicam, et
scelera, bellorum et calamitatum sons et caput; quibus nisi exstirpatis,
nullus miseriarum finis sperandus videtur. Non prosequar
omnia, quia et infinita sunt, et alibi ab alijs explicata satis, sed maiori
quidem illorum, qui sapienter ea disseruerunt et nos ad meliora erudierunt,
laude, quam nostrâ, qui nec ullis præceptis moniti sapere, nec ullis malis
affecti resipiscere possumus, utilitate.

Et quid pluribus commemorandis tempus teram, cum, si avaritiam et ambitionem,
teterrimas animorum pestes, unam dissensionum et calamitatum in toto Romano
imperio causam esse dixero, complexus omnia videar? Hic unus malus Genius est,
qui nos tamdiu vexat, hoc ipsum est illud factum, quod et privatis rebus
detrimentum et publicis exitium affert. Hæ duæ inquam sunt pestes, quæ pietati et
iustitiæ, quæ Germaniæ nostræ, quæ Ecclesiæ
extremam, si quæ possint, perniciem machinantur, quæque ἀταξίαν et confusionem
adhuc, proh dolor, regnantem in Ecclesiam invexerunt? Hæc enim cum erat
pauperior et Apostoli ceterique Pastores auro et argento carebant, virtutibus
omnibus circumfluebant. Episcopi erant docti, modesti, prudentes, sobrij,
laboriosi, vigilantes et tanquam lumina universo populo prælucebant. Singuli vero
e populo tantâ charitate alij alios complectebantur, ut nemo, quod
possideret, suum esse diceret, sed omnia essent communia. Erantque concordes et perdurantes
in doctrina Apostolorum, laudantes Deum
eique pro omnibus gratias agentes. Fides vero tanta erat, ut
nec minis nec ullis cruciatibus a confessione Christianâ deterreri possent. Verum
ubi liberalitate et zelo Principum interdum inconsiderato, eadem Ecclesia
in opes et potentiam crevit, ex eo paulatim eius virtus (ut ait D. HIERONYMUS)
decrevit, quoad in eum statum et præcipitium delaberetur, quo in præsentiâ
redacta est, ut difficillimum sit iudicatu, utri sint deteriores, Pastores an
populus; quos inter se concertare dicas, utri alteros ambitione et avaritiâ
vincant. Certe priscos pietatis ac tum fundandæ et ædificandæ autores, Deum,
Prophetas, CHRISTUM, Apostolos et alios pios Doctores, tum eiusdem confirmandæ
adiutores, ut Imperatores, Reges, Principes, piasque Respubl. et populos si
consulunt et audiunt, longe aliam viam inibunt, longe aliam rationem sequentur.
Sed (ut non minus vere quam festive dicitur) famelicus venter auribus caret;
vesana illa habendi et dominandi fames nihil audit, donec plene satiata fuerit,
si plene tamen unquam satiari potest, quæ contra, quo plura absorbuit, eo plura
appetere, et gazas gazis, regnaque regnis cumulare et solet et gaudet.

dotes, adeo charus, ut nulla vis rerum aut temporum præscriberet modum in eo
amando, suspiciendo et observando. Sed quia divinam in rebus humanis voluntatem
dominari non ignoramus, non forti modo, sed æquo etiam animo, quod mutare non

Hartwig von Dassel, Jurisconsult, to Tycho Brahe, most noble Knight and most excellent Mathematician. Greeting.

As often as I turn my eyes and mind to the state of present times — which is most wretched — most noble and magnificent man, friend to be revered (and I turn them thither almost every day and night), so often there comes into my mind what the Poet sings in these verses:

> "Thus all things by the fates rush to the worse, and, slipping back, are carried backward — no otherwise than one who scarce, against the stream, drives his skiff with the oars: if perchance he has slackened his arms, the channel snatches him headlong down the sloping current." [Virgil, Georgics 1.199–203]

For never have there existed times more turbulent than ours, in which all the kingdoms of the world threaten the uttermost fall [τὴν ἐσχάτην πτῶσιν]; in which there is so great and so unbridled a corruption of morals on every side, so many and so great commotions of mortals into divergent pursuits, so many and so great disturbances of affairs that to set them right in due season one man alone would scarcely find occupation enough, or be sufficient for the task; in which likewise religion and the true worship of God were things to which nothing, in anyone's eyes, was held preferable, and public and private affairs alike lay everywhere rolled up into turbulence. For although bygone times always brought with them certain troubles and disturbances of their own, nor were they ever wholly free even of the most atrocious civil wars, yet of this it is most plainly established, that there was never any age of such a kind into which all these evils together — as if brought forth in one birth, poured out from a single vial — flowed together, so that not undeservedly may these times alone, beyond all the foregoing ages, be called supremely deplored and calamitous. For what ages ever were — let anyone tell me who has compared the histories of each several time — that could be matched against these, by the exact extremities to which virtues and vices, turned upside down, have come? In which sacred and profane things thus lay confounded and entangled together, the one with the other, from top to bottom? In which all vices and baseness thus arrived at their utmost height, standing there without a blush, and almost claiming some vindication of praise? In which virtue and honesty lay rolled down to the very bottom, cast down and trampled underfoot? In which there was greater impunity and licence for a looser and more wanton life, and everywhere greater intestine commotions and seditions in the condition of nations and kingdoms? So utterly, by one as it were conspiring onset, does the whole world rush and fall headlong into disgrace and the pit of all vices, into mutual destruction and ruin. Most wretched, indeed, is the state of our wretched Germany, destitute of all help. No foreign enemy lays her waste, no alien fraud plunders her — she falls by her own forces, is cunning for her own overturning, and weaves snares against herself. What, I ask, must we suppose foreign peoples — the Italians, I imagine, the French, the Spanish — say of us? What will letters and writings attest? It is hard and bitter to say, yet it must be said. There will be recounted the impious and nefarious onset of fury, avarice, and envy — of nobles against nobles, citizens against citizens, nay of brothers, kinsfolk, relations, neighbours against their own — the ambushes, the circumvention, the seduction, the deceits. There cannot be as much evil in the utmost calamity as there is in this ignominy and disgrace. But what madness is this? That no one cares what becomes of his children, his wife, his blood-kin, his relations? Or do we wish that, with us, everything should be extinguished and blotted out? And those Neronian and Caligulan sentences are in the mind, and on the tongue: "When I am dead, let the earth be consumed by fires" — or, even sadder:

> "Let fires, ruins press the people down, and base want, with savage hunger and grief."

O the vast reproach of a most praised people and nation! Many meanwhile marvel by what evil genius, or by what fate of ours, that corruption of morals, and so many discords and dissensions born everywhere, alike in the political order and in the ecclesiastical, have arisen, and day by day grow ever greater; and not without reason do they marvel at it, and grieve also at the long duration and the greatness of a thing that brings with it so many evils, and threatens the ruin of piety and justice. But if those who marvel would set before their eyes the depravity of our minds and the taint of our life — of which a little before I spoke — and the most grievous wounds with which that life wholly teems and is befouled, and, which comes next, would consider that in so many and so great diseases as we labour under scarcely any appearance or hope of health remains, they will marvel no longer; but they will grieve nonetheless, that we in no way either think about or labour at the curing of our diseases. These, to say it in one word, are the vices and crimes that are the source and head of wars and calamities; unless these are rooted out, no end of our miseries seems to be hoped for. I will not pursue all of this, since it is both endless and has been sufficiently set forth elsewhere by others — to the greater credit indeed of those who have discussed it wisely and instructed us toward better things, than to any profit of ours, who can neither be made wise by any precepts we are warned with, nor be brought to our senses by any evils that afflict us.

And why should I waste time recounting more, since, if I shall have said that avarice and ambition — the foulest plagues of minds — are the one cause of the dissensions and calamities in the whole Roman Empire, I shall seem to have embraced all? This is that one evil genius which so long vexes us; this same thing it is that brings both loss to private affairs and ruin to public. These two, I say, are the plagues which contrive the uttermost ruin, if they can, of piety and justice, of our Germany, of the Church — and which have brought into the Church that disorder [ἀταξίαν] and confusion that still, alas, reign? For when the Church was poorer, and the Apostles and the other Pastors lacked gold and silver, they overflowed with all virtues. The bishops were learned, modest, prudent, sober, industrious, watchful, and shone before the whole people like lights. And among the people, each embraced the others with such charity that no one called what he possessed his own, but all things were held in common. And they were of one mind and persevered in the teaching of the Apostles, praising God and giving him thanks for all things. And their faith was so great that they could be deterred, neither by threats nor by any torments, from the confession of Christ. But when, by the liberality and (sometimes inconsiderate) zeal of Princes, that same Church grew into wealth and power, from that its virtue (as Saint Jerome says) gradually decreased, until it slipped down into that state and precipice to which it is now reduced, so that it is most difficult to judge which are the worse, the Pastors or the people — you would say they vie with one another as to which shall outdo the other in ambition and avarice. Surely, if they would consult and give ear to the ancient founders of piety — God, the Prophets, Christ, the Apostles, and other pious Doctors — and then to those who helped confirm the same, such as Emperors, Kings, Princes, and pious Commonwealths and peoples, they would enter upon a far different path, and follow a far different plan. But (as is said no less truly than wittily) a hungry belly has no ears; that mad hunger for having and for ruling hears nothing, until it be fully sated — if indeed it can ever be fully sated — which, on the contrary, the more it has swallowed, the more it craves, and is wont, and delights, to heap up treasure upon treasure, and kingdom upon kingdom.

But I now dismiss the sordidness and perfidy of those men, and come to the things by the knowledge of which I am greatly dismayed; for not without a sense of grief did I lately learn from your letter, that the most noble man Lord Heinrich Rantzau, the King of Denmark's Pro-duke of the Cimbric [land] [Statthalter of Holstein], our kinsman, was on the very Kalends of January [1 January] taken, by a blessed end, from the company of mortals — by which news indeed I could not but be greatly moved, especially by so sudden a death. For he was so dear to me, for his singular prudence, his eminent virtues, the most excellent gifts of his mind and body, that no force of circumstances or times prescribed a measure to my loving, looking up to, and observing him. But since we are not ignorant that the divine will rules in human affairs, we ought to bear, not with a brave only but with an even mind, what we cannot change; this our misfortune and most grievous loss we bear with the mind we ought to. But so far is it from our duty to grieve his lot, that we understand we must even rejoice on his own account. For to whom should God be reckoned to have been more propitious and appeased, or to whom should an entrance to that eternal and blessed life be judged to have lain more open, than to him who (as you too think of him) both ever lived most uprightly and most chastely, and departed from this life piously and holily? Surely to grieve his death would be the part of one who envies him, rather than one who loves him. Wherefore it will be your part, since he has so gloriously finished the course of his life, to soften with this thought the longing with which you are affected on his account. Which comfort you will the more easily attain, if you impart some of your study and time to the sacred letters [Scripture], from which — a thing I can myself confirm from experience — besides a certain most sweet fruit and pleasure of mind which you will perceive, you will come especially to understand that all the misfortunes that can befall a man have the power not only to be borne, but even to work toward the health of our souls. But enough of this. He was indeed, the noble Lord Heinrich Rantzau, now taken from human affairs, a man bearing the image of honour both in face and in manner; a contemner of wealth and honours; stubborn for the right; free of soul as of speech, and thereby pleasing to the good, weighty to the wicked; a lover of Doctors and Men of Letters — but of the good ones, whom he never served with idle effort; he embraced grave and serious erudition, from which good morals and good health might flow forth to the public, and turned away from the playthings of idle cleverness and most difficult trifles. Moreover, in piety and strength of mind he was above the common grasp and wit of the crowd. But he was not suited to the taste of this age — a wicked, malicious, thankless age, in which for vices and evil arts there is not only impunity, but among most men even honour, while innocence and virtue you could scarcely cultivate openly anywhere without danger. Dear from boyhood to Christian III, King of Denmark — and not undeservedly; for to Rantzau himself, too, this was, after God, his one care: to have a most merciful King, and to see him most flourishing. He was born of a family noble among the Holsatians, but afterward, for his outstanding virtues and no common erudition, perpetual Vicar [Statthalter], with the highest dignity, of three Kings of Denmark — Christian III, Frederick II, and Christian IV — in the Duchies of Schleswig, Holstein, and Dithmarschen. His first years he gave to the good arts, the rest of his age to the Commonwealth; and when that riper age for conducting and governing affairs together had come upon him, then he gave himself to undertaking embassies, to labouring, to watching; intent upon public business, he neglected his own; he exhorted all good men everywhere to common peace, gave heart to the prudent, and set forth to the imprudent the evils that threatened. But you, fortunate Rantzau, your course innocently finished, now live free of this servitude, and live with God, blessed for eternity. But to the praises of Lord Rantzau I will add no more.

But that you too, most noble man, in the last part of your letter, follow Rantzau's death with no feigned laments — in this you do indeed what becomes not you alone, but all the lettered. And that you proclaim, with full mouth, his wisdom, learning, virtue, and humanity — in that I both recognise and greatly praise the candour of your mind; and since I had noticed you so zealous of his praises, I did not doubt that the reading would be welcome to you of the epigrams with which I adorned his tomb; wherefore I judged they should be appended — expecting yours in turn, if you have written any. You, most noble man, will take in good part this confidence of my addressing you, and the things which — as it were chattering, from compassion at the commotions of this malign age — I have written out to you; and you will fare well.

Lüneburg, the 28th of January, the year [1599].

  • ★ The death of Heinrich Rantzau (1 January 1599). The letter's documentary core: Tycho had announced, in a letter to Dassel, the death of Heinrich Rantzau — the great Holstein humanist-statesman, Statthalter (Royal Vicar) of Schleswig-Holstein under three Danish kings (Christian III, Frederick II, Christian IV), and Tycho's host at Wandsbek — who died on the Kalends of January 1599. This confirms Tycho's closeness to Rantzau (his Wandsbek patron; cf. the Wandsbek letters and brahe-t_15980607001) and his participation in the Rantzau memorial.
  • The Rantzau eulogy. A full humanist portrait: "contemner of wealth and honours … free of soul and speech … lover of [good] Doctors and Men of Letters … grave and serious erudition," dear to Christian III from boyhood, perpetual Statthalter — "now living with God, blessed for eternity."
  • The age-lament. An elaborate jeremiad on the corruption of the age: the world "rushing to the worse" (the Virgilian Georgics boat-against-the-current); Germany "falling by her own forces" (nobles against nobles, kin against kin); avarice and ambition "the one cause of the dissensions and calamities in the whole Roman Empire"; and the decline of the Church from apostolic poverty/virtue to wealth/corruption (citing Jerome), with the tags "a hungry belly has no ears" and the Neronian "Me mortuo terra absumatur ignibus."
  • The tomb-epigram exchange. Dassel encloses his epigrams for Rantzau's tomb and expects Tycho's in return — a record of Tycho composing memorial verse for his patron (cf. Tycho's own poetic interests, brahe-t_16010414001).
  • Cross-references. The Heinrich Rantzau / Wandsbek thread (Tycho's host-patron; the Wandsbek letters); the literary-memorial network.
  • Persons & works named. Hartwig von Dassel; Tycho; Heinrich Rantzau (the deceased Statthalter); Christian III, Frederick II, Christian IV of Denmark; Jerome; Virgil (Georgics); Nero, Caligula (the tyrant-sayings). Places: Lüneburg, Germany, Schleswig/Holstein/Dithmarschen (Rantzau's Statthalterate).
  • Quotations. The opening verses = Virgil, Georgics I.199–203 (the skiff against the current). The "ignes, ruinae populum premant" couplet is a tragic-verse tag (Senecan in tone). The Greek phrases (τὴν ἐσχάτην πτῶσιν "the uttermost fall"; ἀταξίαν "disorder") are reproduced. The text as printed elides some clauses (Dreyer's source); the translation follows the printed text faithfully.
  • Calendar. Luneburg, 28. Ianuarij Anno [1599] — year supplied editorially; Lüneburg (Old Style); → 28 January 1599 [O.S.] (label and identifier agree).
  • Scope of the gap. Roughly two-thirds of Hartwig von Dassel's 28 January 1599 letter to Tycho Brahe was missing from the prior translation, which carried only the opening address, the Virgil epigraph, and disconnected fragments joined by ellipses. The recovered Latin restores the entire moralising excursus on the corruption of the age (the letter's longest single stretch) and roughly half of the eulogy for Heinrich Rantzau; the closing paragraph about the enclosed epitaph-epigrams was already complete and is unchanged here.
  • Sender. Hardewigus/Hartwig von Dassel signs as "I.C." (Iuris Consultus — Doctor of both Laws), writing from Lüneburg. He does not turn up in our absorbed Tycho reference layers (Christianson, Dreyer, Thoren, Mosley) under this name, so he reads as a minor, lettered correspondent in Tycho's wider humanist network rather than a familiar figure of the household (familia).
  • The occasion. The letter is an obituary letter for Heinrich Rantzau (1526–1599), Tycho's patron, kinsman, and host at Wandsbek — his dates and Wandsbek residence are independently confirmed [Christianson et al., Tycho Brahe and Prague: Crossroads of European Science, giving "Heinrich of Rantzau (1526-1599)"]. Von Dassel reports his death "on the very Kalends of January" (1 January 1599) and frames it inside a long jeremiad on the corruption of the age, invoking Virgil (Georgics 1.199-203) and St Jerome on the Church's decline from apostolic poverty into wealth and ambition, before turning to Rantzau's career as thrice-appointed Statthalter of Schleswig-Holstein-Dithmarschen under Christian III, Frederick II, and Christian IV — matching Norlind's identification of Rantzau as "Statthalter in Schleswig-Holstein."
  • Rantzau's epitaph epigrams. The closing paragraph (already sound in the prior translation) shows von Dassel enclosing his own Latin epigrams composed for Rantzau's tomb, inviting Tycho to answer with epigrams of his own — a concrete data point for the corpus of Rantzau memorial verse produced within weeks of his death.
  • Cruxes to flag. (1) The embedded Greek tag "τινί μηδὲν ποιεῖσθαι προτιμότερον" in the first restored paragraph is elliptical — it lacks the expected object of comparison ("preferable to what") — and has been translated by inferred sense rather than settled philology; a genuine, unresolved crux. (2) "illis ignavâ operâ non ferebat" (of Rantzau's support for good scholars) is compressed idiom, rendered here by sense as "whom he never served with idle effort," i.e. he supported them diligently rather than passively.
  • Correction. The salutation header is corrected from 'Doctor of Both Laws' to 'Jurisconsult': Dreyer (TBOO VIII p.137) prints 'HARDEWIGUS A DASSELL I. C.' — I.C. abbreviates Iuris Consultus, not Iuris Utriusque Doctor (I.U.D.). A fabricated editorial bracket claiming an 'apparatus' crux note for the Greek tag τινὶ μηδὲν ποιεἶσθαι προτιμότερον has been removed; Dreyer (TBOO VIII p.137) prints the Greek with no such apparatus.

The translation and its checking

Grade Secure · collated against Dreyer's page images, Opera Omnia VIII pp. 133–141; defects the audit noted: mistranslation (1), fabrication (1) (the translator's own grade before the audit was Probable).

Collated against Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia (the edition of record), reading the printed page images. Dreyer TBOO VIII pp.133-142 read as page images; pp.133-136 proved to belong to three other letters, so the actual Dassel letter was located and collated on pp.137-141 (confirmed by the running headers 'HARDEW. DASSELIUS AD TYCHONEM 28 IAN. 1599'). Checked the Virgil tag (Georgics 1.199-203, correctly identified in the English), both Greek phrases (ὰταξίαν and the one above), the Neronian/Caligulan tags and the two embedded verse quotations, Rantzau's death on the Kalends (1st) of January 1599, his title, the three kings named (Christian III, Frederick II, Christian IV) and three duchies (Schleswig, Holstein, Dithmarschen), the Jerome citation, and the closing dateline 'Luneburg, 28 Ianuarij Anno [1599]' — all agree exactly between Dreyer, the transcribed original and the English, sentence for sentence, across this long philosophical letter. Two low-severity issues found, both editorial/paratextual rather than substantive: an over-specified honorific, and a bracketed reference to a nonexistent apparatus.

2 defects found:
- mistranslation (low). 'I.C.' is the standard early-modern abbreviation for 'Iuris Consultus' (jurisconsult/legal counsellor), not 'Iuris Utriusque Doctor' (which would be abbreviated I.U.D. and means 'Doctor of Both Laws'); the header over-specifies Dassel's degree. At: “Hartwig von Dassel, Doctor of Both Laws, to Tycho Brahe, most noble Knight and most excellent Mathematician.”.
- fabrication (low). The bracket invents a pointer to 'the crux noted in the apparatus'; no apparatus criticus exists anywhere in this document or accompanies the source text — the note promises documentation that was never supplied. At: “[a Greek tag whose comparandum is missing — see the crux noted in the apparatus]”.

Repaired. Both defects were verified directly against Dreyer's page images, TBOO VIII p.137 (the letter's opening page). The heading 'I. C.' is legible and unambiguous. The Greek tag τινὶ μηδὲν ποιεἶσθαι προτιμότερον is likewise legible and confirmed to stand alone with no footnote or apparatus marker on the page. No text error was flagged for this letter and none was found; the Latin is left untouched. Nothing remains uncertain for the two audited defects.
