The original

TYCHO BRAHE AD ANDREAM SEVERINI VELLEIUM.

S. P.

CLARISSIME et eruditissime Domine Magister ANDREA, veteri amicitia mihi iunctissime. Scripsi ad te ante aliquot hebdomadas per tabellarium quendam Ripensem, qui semel atque iterum hîc fuit, et inter alia de victualibus isthic in meum usum conquirendis denuo admonui. Nec dubito eas te recepisse litteras, utut altum agens silentium nihil interea responderis. Quod tamen te facturum existimabam, si non prius, saltem circa hoc tempus, cum boum mercatores inde Hamburgum frequentes veniunt. Rogo itaque ut id, quod omissum est, emendes, atque de victualibus procurandis quae spes sit, indices. Scripsi denuo ea de re ANDREAe SEVERINI, civi isthic primario, qui suam mihi hoc in negotio dudum addixit operam. Spero ipsum suo officio non defuturum. Tu tamen nihilominus, quantum in te est, fac ut desiderijs meis respondeat eventus, nec tot pollicitationes nimium frustra. Adhortare etiam amicos et consanguineos meos per occasionem, si quos faventes et fidos inveneris, ut mihi iam extero, et amplam hîc in summa annonae caritate alenti familiam id praestent officij, quod sibi vellent, si eorum talis esset conditio. A sorore MARGARETA, quam mihi non defuturam putabas, ne verbum quidem accepi, licet bis illi scripserim, vix tertio facturus. Scio quidem victualium exportationem severe isthic prohibitam esse; attamen non me indigenam Regni Nobilem, sed mercatores hoc edictum concernere crediderim, nisi forte odio mei id fiat. Quod quomodo in Patria promeritus sim, ignoro. Id tamen scio, me isthic ultra tonnam auri insumpsisse, ut eius honori studerem; et quod plus est, prorsusque irreparabile, viginti unum Annos de potissima aetatis parte isthic non satis commode contrivisse. Quod quam maxime doleo. Nec mihi haec temporis et aetatis iactura evenisset, nisi ab optimo et laudatiss. mem. Rege FRIDERICO ita persuasus fuissem, cui et Patriae id concedendum ratus sum. Neque enim ullo modo persentiscere tunc potui, me nihil aliud quam invidiam, odium, calumnias et fraudulentas persecutiones inde reportaturum, unâ cum rei familiaris non exigua iactura. Sed ita est, uti nuper ad me clarissimus ille IACOBUS MONAVIUS Vratislavia perscripsit, inter alia hunc Kluppelhardum addens: »Est, erit atque fuit Vndanck in fine laborum«. Imo is et altius ea quae mihi in patria evenerunt, si non nimis hyperbolice exaggerans, in haec verba prorumpit: »Sed et hoc mihi incidit: Quid si mundus hic non sit dignus istis Atlanticis bonis et donis nostri TYCHONIS. Etiam hoc cogitavi: quid si haec Τύχη, quae Domino TYCHONI contigit, nunciet instare finem mundi? Utut sit, adhuc valde dubito, tuamne vicem an Patriae potius tuae, imo totius Reip. Christianae magis dolere debeam«. Moxque addit: »Miratus sum Regem nihil voluisse conferre in haec studia Regibus et eorum subsidijs atque adiumento dignissima, qui tamen nuper tantos sumptus fecit in coronationis festo. Aliud fuit iudicium ALPHONSI, imo aliud etiam ipsius patris FRIDERICI«. Haec et similia non saltem ille sed et multi alij viri in Germania atque alibi cordatiores et mihi et sibi invicem de me meisque rebus saepius scribunt. Ego vero invite fero quod Regi meo serenissimo et toti Patriae imputentur ea, quae paucorum machinata est perversitas, ab unico et veteri meo osore, quem tute nosti, de quo etiam ex Anglia mihi relatum est, praestantibus viris ibi innotuisse eum has turbas ex inveterata contra me invidia subdole et Machiavellice solum movisse, idque per alios, cum ipsemet par non esset, proficiscens. Quod et multis Germanis, nescio quibus indicijs innotuit; adeo ut vel sic (si non satis ante) famosus evadat. Ego semper Regem et Patriam, omnes in ea probos et integros viros excusare soleo. De caeteris taceo, et dissimulo ad tempus. Omnia successive et per intervalla suam sortiuntur maturationem et fructuum edunt specimen, ut quivis ea quae sevit, metat. Sed valeant ista unâ cum istis.

Tu vero mi amantissime Domine Magister ANDREA me, prout antea constituisti, hîc invise, ut semel adhuc, siquidem saepius non liceat, nos invicem intueamur et colloquamur, antequam disiungamur longius. Hyems nunc non obstat. Est inter Pascha et Pentecosten iucundissimum Anni tempus et profectionibus imprimis aptum. Valetudo si qua tibi est remissior, motu corporis inter peregrinandum et aëris mutatione recuperatur potius quam infringitur, cum quies illi minus conveniat. Nihil igitur aliud obstare poterit, quin post paucas hebdomadas hîc adsis, quam cura domestica et parsimonia; domui satis uxor providebit. Et maior est in amoris constantia parsimonia Amicum veterem negligere quam aliquam pecuniulae partem, quae resarciri potest, atque alias interdum profluit? Dedi etiam literas eodem tempore ad CHRISTIERNUM LANGIUM quo ad te, cuius filius etiamnum mecum est, et ne quidem ab illo quidpiam responsi accepi. Adest iam tempus ad quod usque filium eius in meam familiam me recepturum addixi. Vellem itaque ut Parens se, quid in posterum de eo agi velit, resolveret. Est quidem is satis modestus, et quantum ego scio, honeste se gerens, attamen hisce sublimioribus, quae ego tracto studijs, minus idoneus. Nescio an alijs. Neque ea quibus ego incumbo, ipsi in posterum multum usui esse poterunt. Oportet enim in ijs excellere, si quid hinc emolumenti sperandum. Effice igitur ut Parens mihi mature de sua voluntate scribat. Alterum quoque LANGIUM IOHANNEM per literas suadente OLIGERO ROSENKRANTZIO nostro sollicitavi, qui etiam nihil rescripsit. Miror vestram taciturnitatem, cum ego multo forte pluribus onustus occupationibus responsione non supersedeam, undecunque tandem ad me perferantur literae, quod creberrime fit ex varijs Europae locis, sed nihil novi est me a meis in Patria negligi, apud exteros non item.

Vale et si potes audesque, veni, nihilque metue, res enim caret periculo. Stans tegor in solido, ventus strepat, ignis et unda. Saluta ex me uxorem tuam unâ cum alijs amicis Ripensibus.

Dabantur Wandesburgi die 10. Aprilis Anno 98.
Tuus TYCHO BRAHE,
manu propria.

Tycho Brahe to Anders Sørensen Vedel.

Heartiest greeting.

Most distinguished and most learned Master Anders, joined to me most closely by an old friendship. I wrote to you some weeks ago by a certain courier of Ribe, who has been here once and again, and among other things reminded you once more about the provisions to be procured there for my use. Nor do I doubt that you received that letter, even though, keeping a deep silence, you have meanwhile sent no reply — which yet I thought you would do, if not sooner, at least about this time, when the cattle-merchants come in numbers from there to Hamburg. I ask you therefore to make good what has been omitted, and to indicate what hope there is of provisions being procured. I have written again on this matter to Anders Sørensen, a leading citizen there, who long ago pledged me his service in this business. I hope he will not fail in his duty. You none the less, so far as in you lies, see to it that the outcome answers to my wishes, and that so many promises [come] not too much to nothing. Exhort too my friends and kinsmen, as occasion offers — if you find any well-disposed and faithful — that to me, now an exile, and here maintaining a large household amid the utmost dearth of provisions, they may render that good office which they would wish [done] for themselves, were their own condition such. From my sister Margrete, who you thought would not fail me, I have not had so much as a word, though I have written to her twice, and shall scarcely do so a third time. I know indeed that the export of provisions is there strictly forbidden; yet I should think that this edict concerns not me, a native nobleman of the Realm, but the merchants — unless perhaps it is done out of hatred of me. How I have deserved [this] in [my] Fatherland, I do not know. This, however, I do know: that I spent there above a barrel of gold to study its honour; and, what is more, and altogether beyond repair, that I wore away there one-and-twenty years of the best part of [my] life, none too comfortably. Which I grieve over as deeply as can be. Nor would this loss of time and of [my] prime have befallen me, had I not been so persuaded by the most excellent King Frederick, of most praiseworthy memory, to whom and to the Fatherland I judged that it should be granted. For in no way could I then have foreseen that I should carry off thence nothing other than envy, hatred, slanders, and fraudulent persecutions, together with no small loss to my estate. But so it is, as that most distinguished Jacob Monau lately wrote to me at length from Breslau, adding among other things this blunt German saying [Kluppelhard]: »Ingratitude [Vndanck] is, will be, and was at the end of [one's] labours.« Nay, he breaks out — going deeper into the things that have befallen me in my homeland, if [he is] not exaggerating too hyperbolically — into these words: »But this too has occurred to me: What if this world be not worthy of those Atlantean goods and gifts of our Tycho? This too I have pondered: what if this Týchē [Fortune] that has befallen Master Tycho announces that the end of the world is at hand? However that be, I am still much in doubt whether I ought rather to grieve over your case, or over that of your Fatherland — nay, of the whole Christian commonwealth.« And presently he adds: »I have wondered that the King was unwilling to bestow anything upon these studies — most worthy of kings and of their support and aid — who nevertheless lately made such great outlays at the coronation feast. Other was the judgement of Alfonso — nay, other too [the judgement] of his own father Frederick.« These and like things not only he, but many other men of sounder sense in Germany and elsewhere, write often, both to me and to one another, about me and my affairs. But I bear it unwillingly that there are charged to my most serene King and to the whole Fatherland those things which the perversity of a few has contrived — by a single old hater of mine, whom you yourself know; of whom it has also been reported to me from England that it became known to eminent men there that he alone stirred up these troubles, out of an inveterate envy against me, by stealth and in Machiavellian fashion, and that, going about it through others (since he himself was not equal [to it]), [he did so]. Which has become known to many Germans too, by I know not what indications — so that even thus (if not enough before) he turns out infamous. I am always wont to excuse the King and the Fatherland, and all upright and honest men in it. Of the rest I keep silent, and dissemble for a time. All things, successively and at intervals, attain their ripening and yield a showing of their fruits, so that each man reaps what he has sown. But let these matters fare well, along with those [men].

But you, my most beloved Master Anders, visit me here, as you earlier resolved, that — once more at least, if it may not be oftener — we may look upon each other and converse together before we are parted further apart. Winter is now no hindrance. Between Easter and Pentecost is the most delightful time of the year, and especially apt for journeys. If your health is at all the weaker, it is restored rather than impaired by the body's motion in travelling and by the change of air, since rest suits it less well. Nothing else, therefore, will be able to stand in the way of your being here after a few weeks, save household care and thrift; your wife will provide for the house well enough. And is it the greater thrift, in the constancy of love, to neglect an old friend than [to spend] some little portion of money, which can be made good, and which besides flows away from time to time anyhow? I also gave a letter, at the same time as [the one] to you, to Christiern Lange, whose son is even now with me, and from him I have not had any reply at all. The time is now at hand up to which I pledged that I would receive his son into my household. I should wish, therefore, that the father would resolve what he wishes done about him hereafter. He is indeed modest enough, and, so far as I know, behaves honestly, yet [is] less suited to these loftier studies which I treat. I do not know whether [he is suited] to others. Nor will the things on which I am bent be able to be of much use to him hereafter. For one must excel in them, if any profit is to be hoped for from this. See to it, therefore, that the father writes to me in good time about his will [in the matter]. I have also solicited by letter the other Lange, Johan — at the urging of our Holger Rosenkrantz — who likewise has written nothing back. I marvel at your [pl.] silence, since I, burdened perhaps with far more occupations, do not put off a reply, from wherever at length the letters are brought to me — which happens very frequently, from various places of Europe; but it is nothing new that I am neglected by my own people in the Fatherland, [though] not so among foreigners.

Farewell, and if you can and dare, come, and fear nothing — for the matter is without danger. Standing, I am sheltered on firm ground; let the wind bluster, [and] fire and wave. Greet for me your wife, together with the other friends of Ribe.

Given at Wandsbek, on the 10th day of April, in the year [15]98.
Yours, Tycho Brahe,
with his own hand.

Apparatus

  • Sender. Tycho Brahe, writing from Wandsbek (Lat. Wandesburgum, then in Holstein, now part of Hamburg), where he stayed 1597–98 with Heinrich Rantzau after leaving Denmark in voluntary exile (June 1597). The letter is suffused with the bitterness of that exile.
  • Recipient. Andreas Severini Velleius = Anders Sørensen Vedel (1542–1616), the Danish royal historiographer and ballad-editor, resident at Ribe (Lat. Ripa; tabellarius Ripensis = "courier of Ribe"; amici Ripenses = "friends of Ribe"); an old friend of Tycho's. Salutation/subscription formulae translated by this chapter's convention (S. P. = salutem plurimam → "Heartiest greeting"; Vale → "Farewell"; manu propria → "with his own hand").
  • Anders Sørensen, leading citizen of Ribe. A second, distinct Andreas Severini — civis primarius of Ribe acting as Tycho's purchasing agent — not the recipient (who is also Andreas Severini); the doubling of the patronymic is in the source and is kept.
  • Sister Margrete. soror Margareta = Margrete Brahe, Tycho's sister.
  • ultra tonnam auri — "above a barrel of gold," Danish en tønde guld, a proverbial vast sum (≈ 100,000 rixdollars); rendered literally and glossed.
  • viginti unum Annos … isthic … contrivisse — "one-and-twenty years … wore away there": Tycho counts from c. 1576 (the grant of Hven) to his 1597 departure.
  • King Frederick. Rex Fridericus, laudatiss. mem. = Frederik II (r. 1559–88), "of most praiseworthy memory," who granted Tycho Hven; contrasted implicitly with the young Christian IV (crowned 1596), under whom Tycho's support lapsed.
  • Jacob Monau. Iacobus Monavius = Jacob Monau (Monawius, 1546–1603), the Breslau (Vratislavia = Wrocław/Breslau) humanist and patron, a correspondent of Tycho's. The passages in guillemets (»…«) are Tycho's verbatim quotation of Monau's letter to him.
  • Kluppelhardum — a Latinised accusative of a German coinage (cf. Klüppel/Knüppel "cudgel" + hart "hard"), i.e. a "blunt / rough-hewn German saying." Tycho's own nonce-word; rendered "this blunt German saying" with the original bracketed, the word flagged rather than smoothed. Crux: the exact register (jocular vs. coarse) is not certain.
  • Vndanck in fine laborum — Monau's macaronic proverb embedding the German Undank ("ingratitude") in a Latin frame: "Ingratitude is, will be, and was at the end of [one's] labours" — a variant of the German Undank ist der Welt Lohn ("ingratitude is the world's reward"). The German word Vndanck is kept in the source block and bracketed in the translation per the code-switch policy.
  • Greek. Τύχη — Týchē, "Fortune / [bad] luck"; Monau puns on Tycho's name (Tycho ↔ týchē), echoing the established wordplay (cf. Wolf 1571, εὐτυχέστατε Τυχών). Transliterated and glossed, the pun flagged not flattened. Atlanticis bonis et donis = "Atlantean goods and gifts," i.e. burdens/gifts worthy of Atlas (the Titan who bears the heavens) — apt for an astronomer.
  • Alphonsi — Alfonso X of Castile ("the Wise," r. 1252–84), patron of the Alfonsine Tables, the archetype of the astronomy-funding prince; Monau contrasts his liberality (and Frederik II's) with the present king's.
  • coronationis festo — the coronation feast of Christian IV (crowned 29 August 1596), on which the crown spent lavishly while withdrawing Tycho's support.
  • The "single old hater." unicus et vetus osor … quem tute nosti … Machiavellice — Tycho's unnamed enemy at court, whom Vedel is presumed to know; not identified in the text and left unidentified here. The "report from England" of his exposure is given as Tycho relays it (hearsay), and the hedges are preserved.
  • Christiernus Langius / Iohannes Langius — Christiern (Christian) Lange and Johan Lange, Danish noblemen; Christiern's son was then a pupil in Tycho's household. Oligerus Rosenkrantzius = Holger Rosenkrantz (here "our Holger," a mutual friend; likely Holger Rosenkrantz "the Learned," 1574–1642, though the elder Holger Rosenkrantz is also possible — identification left open).
  • vestram taciturnitatem — "your [plural] silence": Tycho widens the reproach from Vedel alone to the whole circle of unresponsive Danish correspondents.
  • Stans tegor in solido, ventus strepat, ignis et unda. — a self-standing sententia / motto, roughly hexametric in cadence: "Standing, I am sheltered on firm ground; let the wind bluster, [and] fire and wave." Kept as a maxim; strepat read as a jussive/concessive subjunctive ("let it rage"), with ignis et unda governed by it in parallel. A defiant Stoic emblem of constancy in exile.
  • Calendar / place. Dabantur Wandesburgi die 10. Aprilis Anno 98 = "Given at Wandsbek, the 10th day of April, [15]98" (O.S. — Tycho, a Dane in Holstein, still reckoning Old Style). The edition label ("1598. 10. april. Wandsburg") agrees with the dateline; no date discrepancy. (A neighbouring letter in the source CSV, Tycho → Maestlin, is dated 21 April 1598 from the same place — a different letter, not a variant of this one.)
  • Allusion. quivis ea quae sevit, metat — "each man reaps what he has sown" (Galatians 6:7), woven into Tycho's threat-tinged forecast that wrongs will bear fruit in time; the Scriptural register is kept.
  • Correction. English: fixed a wrong-antecedent mistranslation in Jacob Monau's embedded remark — 'ipsius patris Friderici' refers to the young King Christian IV's own father, Frederick II, not (as the bracketed '[your]' falsely implied) to Tycho's own father Otte Brahe.

1 defect found:
- mistranslation (high). This is Monau's embedded quote about the young King (Christian IV); 'ipsius' is reflexive to 'Regem' (the King), so 'ipsius patris Friderici' means the King's own father, Frederick II — not Tycho's father. The bracketed '[your]' fabricates a false genealogical claim (Tycho's actual father was Otte Brahe), a wrong-antecedent error of the exact type this process is known to produce. At: “Other was the judgement of Alfonso — nay, other too [the judgement] of [your] father Frederick himself.”.

Repaired. Verified against the Dreyer page image TBOO VIII p.49, which prints 'Aliud fuit iudicium ALPHONSI, imo aliud etiam ipfius patris FRIDERICI«' verbatim as in the transcribed original, so no Latin change was needed. The surrounding context on the same page (Monau's complaint that 'the King' declined to fund Tycho's studies despite lavish coronation spending) confirms 'ipsius' refers back to 'Regem', not to Tycho. Only the English mistranslation required correction; the remainder of the letter was not re-collated beyond the single defect site per the prime rule.
