To Master Holger Rosenkrantz, son of Jørgen, the Dane, hereditary lord of Rosenholm, learned among nobles and noble among the learned, his lord and friend to be honoured for ever — into [his] hands [ΕΙΣ ΧΕΙΡΑ]. To be opened and read no less by Tycho Brahe.

Fare well [ΕΥ ΠΡΑΤΤΕΙΝ].

How fondly I kissed your two letters — the one sent me from Lübeck, the other from Hamburg — my mind, bound to you by many benefits received and known to you over many years as though in a mirror and on a tablet, can declare to you, noble Rosenkrantz, though my tongue cannot. For you yourself know how earnestly I love you, honour you, and how much I esteem you, so that as often as I see your letters, so often a cold sweat is poured over me, and I am persuaded that I am spurred on by you to a more extended course of friendship. And since I perceive that you perceive this from your own letters, there is no need for me to assure you of it at greater length. Yet however sweet each of your letters was to me, they were nonetheless somewhat the more bitter because in them you at the same time told of the Liddelian plagiarism committed against the illustrious astronomer Tycho Brahe — your kinsman and relative by marriage, a man to be admired for his manifold learning. What struck my mind the more was that Master Tycho himself had attested the same to me in his own letters. He complained to you of the singular impudence and ingratitude alike of Duncan Liddel the Scot — who now acts as Professor of Mathematics in the Illustrious [Academia] Julia — in that, not only in letters privately given to his own circle, but also from the public chair of his lectures, he had boasted himself to be the first inventor of the Tychonic hypotheses. That complaint was the more certain because, by the many testimonies and letters of good men, I saw that the matter had been brilliantly brought home to Tycho [as proven]; and the more grievous because Liddel had dared so great a crime against Tycho not alone, but together with two other plagiarists. I was indignant within myself, and yet, with Favorinus, I laughed at those crows — men strutting in others' feathers — ridiculous, because they ambitiously aspired to greater things; odious, because they attained them with some people; pitiable, because in the end before all men they fell shamefully from praise. For those who conceive praise from the counterfeit of virtue, and not from virtue, are wont (as the saying is) to bring forth wind-eggs. With this hope I could have made both my own mind and yours tranquil, had there not been added besides what touches me more nearly and stings me more sharply. For I have read the words of that Liddel, [namely] that in testimony of his own plagiarism (with which, however, he wishes preposterously to charge Tycho) he calls and brings me forward as a witness. For thus he writes: »The first theories of mine according to the threefold hypothesis, quite imperfect, which seven years ago I set forth at Rostock, Tycho received from Doctor Cramer, now pastor at Stettin.« So he says. Tycho Brahe, then, drew his discoveries from my book, from the lectures of Liddel — who is the inventor of these hypotheses, who first deduced them, who first expounded them, if you please — and in testimony of that matter, trustworthy: am I co-opted by Liddel? I was dumbfounded, I frankly confess, when I read this — that I was being summoned to give false testimony in the matter, and that by the abuse of my name (which up to now, by God's favour, I have striven to keep untainted) a thing so alien from the truth was being wrapped up and plastered over as with a cloak. My lord Rosenkrantz, you yourself well know — since we lived together so many years — in what regard I always held this Duncan, how honourably I spoke of him to you, because he had opened to me as a youth the doors of the School of Mathematics and of certain other parts of Philosophy; but that I am now compelled to speak against him, that I could wish there had never been need of. But you remember with me that [saying] of Pericles of Athens, when a friend asked him to swear a false oath on his behalf and for his cause: δεῖ με συμπράττειν τοῖς φίλοις ἀλλὰ μέχρι τοῦ βωμοῦ ["I must aid my friends, but only as far as the altar"]; and what our Philosopher [said]: ἀμφοῖν ὄντοιν φίλοιν ὅσιον προτιμᾶν τὴν ἀλήθειαν ["both being dear, it is right to prefer the truth"]. For I cannot [act] against the truth — unless someone should wish to use me at once as flatterer and as friend, which I hold to be alien from a pious mind. From that Master of mine I could not have hoped for so great an injury against me as that with which he has now afflicted me, in that he has alleged me — who am in no way privy to it — for false testimony in the matter. He commits forgery [falsimonium] (what in the courts they call the crime of falsehood) who seals letters full of danger and deceit with another's signet-ring, secretly, without the owner's knowledge; would you not believe that man to perpetrate the same thing, who confirms his own falsehoods by the subscriptions of others' names — names of others called to give testimony without [giving any] testimony? It is a preposterous thing for you to speak truths when you do not even speak; to affirm those things which you do not know whether you affirm. So Liddel deals with me — that I should be a witness, a witness never heard; that I should affirm, [though] I am ignorant; that I should speak truths, when I do not even speak. Unworthy! Insane! But since he seems so greatly to demand my own testimony on this matter, I give this true testimony of mine, against that [testimony] of his — [which is] not mine. To you and to Tycho, seeking the truth about this Liddelian plagiarism, I shall depose, as far at least as is clear to me, and so plainly that, if you renew with yourself at leisure the recollection [ἀνάμνησιν] of the years a little earlier, you will of your own accord supply me all your points. Duncan Liddel the Scot taught Mathematics in the Academy of Rostock — when I was a hearer in the year [15]88 and the following — among other things expounding also the second motion according to a threefold hypothesis: the first, that of the Alphonsines; the second, that of Copernicus; and another, a third, a new one. It is about this third, the new one, that the matter turns: whose author is it? Must I give testimony? Am I to answer? Let Duncan himself answer — and that from his written Commentaries, which to this very day I still keep with me in good faith, as do many others also: youths then, men now, who at that time made use of the same private instruction by the same man; among whom is also, to name but one, our common friend Master Joachim Praetorius, my colleague to be honoured in this church and Gymnasium. In these Commentaries (you are an eye-witness for me; you saw, you read) Duncan called that new Diatyposis [outline] "Tychonic," and that in express syllables and letters, thus framing his question: "In that new, or Tychonic, Diatyposis, how are the motions of these five planets saved?" etc. So at that time he taught, so he said, so he repeatedly said, so we learned, so we heard, so we wrote down, so we believed. But, you say, does he now say otherwise at Helmstedt, teach otherwise? That indeed I cannot grasp: how, if Duncan is and wishes to be one [and the same man], he could over so brief an interval of place and time deny this hypothesis to himself at Rostock, yet at Helmstedt claim that same one for himself? Unless he has changed mind and tongue along with the horizon — which I would scarcely grant. I rather believe Duncan Liddel the Rostock student against Duncan Liddel the Helmstedt professor. Does he perhaps say that out of modesty and shamefastness he on that occasion ascribed the invention to Tycho? Then did he on that occasion leap over the bounds of shamefastness? Then is this contrary [conduct] to be ascribed to immodesty? And whoever knows the man's character will not admit even this. Why [say] more? The barbarians used to say, "as the letter lies"; so here too it must now be said and pronounced, that — out of the Rostock dictations of Liddel alone — the third hypothesis, which he used also to call "the new tenets," is not Duncan's but Tycho's; one's own confession, the jurists say, is the strongest proof. These things might be enough and more than enough for you, for me, and for others, were it not that — since the discourse has begun thus — I am minded to add still a word or two from the same [Commentaries]. It cannot be said how splendidly, at that time, in the very treatment of that hypothesis, whenever occasion offered, the Scot thought of Tycho, and preferred him to all the ancients, and joined him with the Most Illustrious Landgrave of Hesse of blessed memory. For thus in his Commentaries, under the heading "On longitude and latitude," in [the section] on the Sphere of Brucaeus, one reads: »But whether the fixed stars have varied their latitudes or distances from the Ecliptic, none of the ancients knew; but the Landgrave and Tycho Brahe, from frequent observations, found that the fixed stars have varied their latitudes in accordance with the change of the obliquity of the Zodiac.« And on the latitude of the Moon [☾] he says: »What was previously observed by no one before Tycho — the maximum latitude of the Moon has changed, and today it is 5° 15'.« Likewise on the eccentricity of the Sun, under the heading "On the motion of the Sun," he says: »in all which matters the craftsmen of this age, such as the Most Illustrious Prince the Landgrave and Tycho Brahe, have found many things which do not sufficiently agree with the observations of Copernicus.« So he. Who here, I ask, even in a fever, could have suspected that Liddel was the teacher of so great a craftsman, whom he himself thus commended — and that craftsman his pupil, as he now dares to boast? Unless the Scot had set himself up as so great a craftsman, or as more skilful and superior to those craftsmen, he would not be set up as such. But you, lord Rosenkrantz, by your shrewdness [ἀγχινοίᾳ], in which you are strong — even with me silent — will, I think, gather another argument of certainty in Tycho's favour, when you hear that he ascribes to Tycho the observations which do not sufficiently agree with Copernicus; so that you may reason thus with yourself: He who wishes to invent and establish new hypotheses in Astronomy against all his predecessors must necessarily have first acquired the new observations of the celestial phenomena [τῶν φαινομένων]; why? because new hypotheses, without the foundations of new phenomena [φαινομένων] presupposed, are old wives' jinglings [τερετίσματα], since it is [the task] of a hypothesis to save the observed motions. If, therefore, Liddel the Scot, lurking in obscurity and bereft of instruments, was the first observer of no new phenomena [φαινόμενα], what impelled him at the outset to leap away from the Copernican hypothesis (which he used to defend tooth and nail) and to begin to think of a new one? For it would be a marvel if he could grant that he had first received the observations from Tycho. So, in my judgment, you would reckon this no light argument for proving Duncan's plagiarism. I say nothing of the fact that, in the very treatment of this new hypothesis, Liddel showed himself such that from many indications one may gather that he is not its author and inventor. For one who is himself the inventor and originator, in a demonstrative science, of some certain hypothesis which he teaches others, it ill becomes to waver or to leave any part of it hanging undecided in the middle. For thus Duncan, where he had generally depicted the Tychonic scheme [typum]: »Whether the first motion, by which all things are borne from rising to setting, is more rightly said [to be] on account of the motion of the Earth or of the outermost heaven, we here leave in the middle; perhaps it will seem more probable if it be ascribed to the outermost heaven.« You hear how he does not arrogate to himself the judgment of establishing, of deciding, but as it were awaits inspiration from elsewhere as to what is here to be thought and what not — [inspiration] which without doubt he had at that time not yet drawn from Tycho. Stronger is the fact that, a little later in the same place, he furnishes a handle for thinking and disputing against this hypothesis, when he says: »The hypothesis of Copernicus seems absurd to most, but this one (he means the Tychonic) is in no way repugnant to the principles of Geometry, although it seems to lay down some things physically absurd.« He understood, however, the aethereal orbs after the opinion of Aristotle — which I learned a little afterwards in practice, from a disputation on Aristotelian Physics held with him. For he set forth there Physical Disputations, in the ninth of which, when he had come to the earlier books On the Heavens, he wished intrepidly to defend the real orbs; and when, for exercise' sake, I refuted them by opposing Tychonic observations, the man flared up to such a degree that he even plainly repudiated and condemned this very granted hypothesis — forgetful perhaps of the shift of genus [τῆς μεταβάσεως τοῦ γένους]. Whereupon, yielding to his bitterness, I rose up at that time, and bade farewell to his classes, as you remember I have often told you; and very many are still living who were both spectators and hearers of that comedy. These things I repeat not in order to treat of the matter itself (which I rank among our boyish doings), but to prove that Liddel always so handled that hypothesis, so disputed against it, that he most clearly gave anyone to witness that it was another's invention, not his own. For he would have fought more stiffly for it had it been his own figment (from which he would have hoped to live immortally). Such, as you hear, Rosenkrantz, was the Scot in that Astronomical School at Rostock, as often as, in a gathered flock, we sat by him teaching. But there is yet something I cannot conceal, which also others of my fellow-soldiers [fellow-students] of that time (whom I am able to name) know. When Master Duncan gathered, none too obscurely, that Tycho's new and so elegant hypothesis was being received with great applause, then — burning, I think, with a premature greed for praise (for he was by nature rather vainglorious) — he used to whisper to one and another of his hearers (after the assembly was dismissed) that he had a letter written in Tycho's own hand, in which Tycho ascribes this hypothesis to him, among other things in these words: »You have hit the matter with the needle [i.e. exactly]«. But he never wished to open those letters fully for me, at least, to see and read — I know not why — even when asked by many. And because it was not permitted to read them whole, many with me rather believed Duncan's public Commentaries than his clandestine and stealthy whisperings. A copy of those letters which he vaunts, transcribed at Helmstedt and communicated to me, I have now read; but, having read them, I justly exclaim: What is "to be blear-eyed at pots and gourds" [i.e. to mistake one obvious thing for another], if this be not it? To love oneself and be wise is scarcely granted to Jupiter [himself]. For self-love blinds the eyes, so that one cannot discern how white differs from black. But I am not bound to treat that here at greater length. The letter, once it has been brought into the light, will speak for itself.

You have, lord Rosenkrantz, what is clear to me from the Liddelian Commentaries about the invention of the new, or Tychonic, hypothesis. And although I could almost have passed it over, yet, lest I should seem to have been unwilling to meet your request, I would not omit it. But it concerns me more whether the Scot speaks truth in [saying] that Tycho received from me the theories he set forth seven years before — as he does not blush to affirm. I shall relate in brief how the matter stands — and [it is] known no less to you than to me, so that you may hear me as it were narrating you to yourself, if only you recall. When in the year [15]91, on the fourteenth day before the Kalends of June [19 May], we had been summoned away from the Academic wrestling-ground [palaestra] of Rostock by your illustrious lord father Jørgen, Viceroy of Denmark of blessed memory, and had stopped at Copenhagen for some while, leave was granted to us both to make an excursion to the island of Hven (where Tycho's Urania had at that time fixed her foot), because your lord father understood that we too were kindled with a singular love of Astronomy and made much of Master Tycho on account of that art. On this occasion I, that I might confer about the art with the craftsman, began to ruminate the more recent notes [ὑπομνήματα] which I had imbibed both from Duncan and from Master Brucaeus — that man of great fame — and from my other teachers. And because part of the way had to be made by ship, I began to take along, as an eloquent companion for the journey, the commentaries which I had folded up in one large volume, and still have, as you know. In which volume, among other things, Liddel's dictations too were contained. Thus incidentally [κατὰ συμβεβηκὸς] I carried the book to the Tychonic island for my own sake, not for his. When we had arrived there, after many — as happens — familiar and learned conferences about various matters (for the whole island was a horn of plenty of things wonderful to see and to hear), there fell in [conversation] also about the art of Astronomy, and, by I know not what occasion, about Brucaeus, about Liddel, and others at that time famous in this science in the Academy of the Roses [Rostock]. But I sensed at once, from no slight signs, that Tycho thought ill of Liddel, because Liddel had, unbidden, divulged to others in the Academy those things which he had filched by stealth from a private conversation with Tycho, when he had earlier been there. I excused the man, on the ground that he had not arrogated those new tenets to himself, but had set them forth expressly under the Tychonic title and honour. And since Master Tycho suffered himself to be persuaded of this with difficulty, it fell out opportunely that I, by my sudden intervention, could give proof by my own book, which I had taken down with my own hand from his [Liddel's] very mouth. So behold, the book is produced; I show the one place and the other where this was [written], as I noted above. Which seen, the Noble Man was fully satisfied, and nothing whatever more was done about those Commentaries between us, nothing thought, nothing dreamed of by him from that whole time up to the present day. It is now the seventh year being completed since that time when you and I were at Uraniborg. Before that time neither I, nor my letters, nor my hand, nor my name ever came into Denmark, to Tycho, so far as I have known. For who was I, a youth at that time — who, I say, was I in age, in talent, in fame — that I should have contracted a friendship by letters with so great a man, most noble in both lineage and fame, and proclaimed often by our own Master himself the craftsman of this age (who with the Landgrave was the sole craftsman in Astronomy), or could have sent anything of mine to him? After that time, when a second time too, with three years elapsed, we returned into Denmark, I gave, and could give, no letters about Astronomical matters either to Tycho or to anyone else. For you remember the Herculean troubles of my professorship at Wittenberg, and a kind of studies far more sacred, than that I could — concerning those matters, always by-works [παρέργοις] to me in theological study — so much as think by [a single] sigh, much less confer anything to the craftsman. With brazen brow, therefore, he lies (pardon the word) who says that Tycho ever received Liddelian theories from me. Take it as said. In testimony of the truth I call my conscience, which is a thousand witnesses to me, and you, Master Holger, who, if I am not mistaken, still duly recount the annals of that passing of time [διατριβῆς] of our Academy, in which, joined under one roof, at one table, in one bed, day and night, at home and abroad, we lived together. It is therefore an injury of the Scot, and that most injurious toward me, that he invokes me — never heard — in this manner in testimony of his own forgery, only that he may render me hateful and suspect to the good men against whom he rises up; [it is] malice, that he has deceived others by the abuse of my name; [it is] impudence, that he forces me — ignorant and thinking nothing of the kind — into his prescribed circles, so that my tongue may speak, not what my mind has conceived to be true, but what he has fabricated, or may, [though] absent, blurt out. Would not my mouth in this fashion be more servile than any slave's? Enough by way of command. But whence does he know that Tycho received these theories from me? I did not say it, nor could I say it; he did not see it, nor could he see it; nor did any other. If he should say that he heard it, I shall return the answer with Thales of Miletus, who used to say that the true is as far from the false as the eyes are distant from the ears — meaning that those mostly lie who report things heard, not seen. Or what if I should say that he says he heard things which he never heard? If he attained it by divination, let him say whether from lots, or auguries, or from constellations, or dreams. But [he attained it] from these, without doubt — unless perhaps it be ascertained — from the chattering of jackdaws: εἶπε καὶ ἁ Γραῖα τἀλαθέα κοσκινόμαντις ["the old woman, the sieve-diviner, spoke the truth too"], said that one in Theocritus.

You have, most noble lord Rosenkrantz, the true testimony rendered — not out of ingratitude toward him who was once my teacher (for what gratitude is this, to lie for a teacher's favour?), but out of the candour and truth on which our friendship is founded — against that fabricated [testimony], not mine, concerning the plagiarism, as you call it, of Liddel. Taught by this example, remember your accustomed motto, Νῆφε καὶ μέμνησο ἀπιστεῖν ["Be sober and remember to distrust"]: both on other occasions, and especially if others should hereafter try to fill your ears with similar wisps of lies about me, keep the other [ear] free for me; and when you have done so, both the truth and our friendship will stand firm between us for ever. With these [words] I would have you commended to God. Farewell. Hurried off at Stettin, in the year of salvation restored 1598, on the second day before the Kalends of April [31 March]. Your friend ever, Daniel Cramer, Pastor and Professor of the Church and Gymnasium of the Stettiners.

Apparatus

  • Sender. Daniel Cramer (1568–1637), Pomeranian Lutheran theologian; in 1591 the young preceptor accompanying Holger Rosenkrantz, later professor and pastor at Stettin (Szczecin), having held a professorship at Wittenberg. The literature confirms he visited Uraniborg with Rosenkrantz in 1591 (Christianson, On Tycho's Island).
  • Recipient. Holger Rosenkrantz "the Learned" (Oligerus; 1574–1642), Danish nobleman of Rosenholm, son of Jørgen (Georgius) Rosenkrantz (here Prorege Daniae b.m. = "Viceroy of Denmark, of blessed memory"), and Cramer's former pupil and fellow-lodger at Rostock; Tycho's consanguineus et affinis (kinsman and relative by marriage). The address directs that the letter be opened and read also by Tycho Brahe — i.e. it is the deposition Tycho had requested through Rosenkrantz.
  • Subject of the dispute. Duncan Liddel (1561–1613), Scottish mathematician-physician, then Professor of Mathematics at Helmstedt (Inclyta Iulia = the Academia Julia, the University of Helmstedt; Helmstadij). Liddel had earlier taught at Rostock (Academia Rosarum, "Academy of the Roses" — a humanist pun on Rostock's civic rose). The literature (Thoren, Lord of Uraniborg; Mosley, Bearing the Heavens) confirms the affair: Liddel was accused of claiming Tycho's geoheliocentric world-system as his own, and of citing Cramer ("the learned Cramer, pastor at Stettin") as the channel by which Tycho supposedly received "the hypothesis I proposed seven years ago at Rostock." This letter is Cramer's indignant denial.
  • Other persons. Brucaeus = Heinrich Brucaeus (Brucäus, 1531–1593), professor of mathematics/medicine at Rostock (his Sphaera is the textbook on which Liddel's Commentaries comment). M. Ioachimus Praetorius = Joachim Praetorius, Cramer's colleague at the Stettin Gymnasium, cited as a fellow-witness to Liddel's Rostock dictations. Illustrissimus Landgravius Hassiae b.m. = Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel (d. 1592; b.m. = beatae memoriae, "of blessed memory"). Phavorinus = Favorinus of Arelate (the 2nd-c. sophist), source of the "crows in others' feathers" topos. Thales Milesius = Thales of Miletus. Theocritus — the citation is from Idyll III (the goatherd's serenade), of the sieve-diviner old woman; see Greek note below.
  • The technical core. triplex hypothesis = the threefold hypothesis of the second (planetary) motion taught by Liddel — first the Alphonsine (Ptolemaic), second the Copernican, and "another, third, new one" (tertia nova), i.e. the Tychonic geoheliocentric system. Diatyposis (Gk διατύπωσις, "outline/sketch/scheme") and typus both = Liddel's diagram/scheme of the system; kept transliterated and glossed. salvantur / salvare motus = "are saved / to save the motions" (the salvare apparentias topos), per glossary. eccentricitas Solis → eccentricity of the Sun; latitudo ☾ maxima → maximum latitude of the Moon (the glyph ☼/☾ is kept in the Source block and named "Moon"); obliquitas Zodiaci → obliquity of the Zodiac; orbes aethereos / orbes reales → aethereal orbs / real orbs (the physical solid-sphere question Liddel defended after Aristotle and Tycho's comet observations had undermined). Copernici hypothesin absurdam … principijs Geometriae nihil repugnare … physica absurda ponere — Cramer quotes Liddel's own hedged verdict; the hedges (forte, videatur) are preserved, not sharpened.
  • Greek interpolations (transcribed and translated; printed forms kept in Source block, including final-sigma and the corrupt forms flagged below).
  • ΕΙΣ ΧΕΙΡΑ (printed "ΕΙΣ XEIPE." with Latin Ε/Χ/Ρ letterforms) — "into [his] hand(s)," an address-direction; the printed truncation is editorial. Flagged: the printed "XEIPE" is a garbled χεῖρα(ς).
  • ΕΥ ΠΡΑΤΤΕΙΝ — "to fare well / do well," the standard Greek epistolary salutation (used by Plato; pointedly preferred to χαίρειν). Rendered "Fare well" as the opening greeting.
  • δεῖ με συμπράττειν τοῖς φίλοις ἀλλὰ μέχρι τοῦ βωμοῦ — "I must cooperate with my friends, but [only] as far as the altar," the apophthegm ascribed to Pericles (Plutarch, Mor. 186C / 531D), i.e. not to the point of perjury.
  • ἀμφοῖν ὄντοιν φίλοιν ὅσιον προτιμᾶν τὴν ἀλήθειαν — "both being friends [dear], it is right/holy to prefer the truth," the Aristotelian amicus Plato sed magis amica veritas topos (cf. Nic. Eth. I.6); "our Philosopher" = Aristotle.
  • ἀνάμνησιν — "recollection" (Platonic anamnesis); rendered "recollection."
  • ἀγχινοίᾳ — "shrewdness, quickness of mind"; rendered "shrewdness."
  • τῶν φαινομένων … φαινομένων … φαινόμενα — "the phenomena / appearances" (celestial); kept as phenomena per glossary.
  • τερετίσματα — "jinglings, twitterings, idle trills"; here "old wives' jinglings" (paired with anilia).
  • τῆς μεταβάσεως τοῦ γένους — "the shifting/transition of genus" (Aristotelian metabasis eis allo genos, the fallacy of crossing from one domain to another); rendered "shift of genus."
  • ὑπομνήματα — "memoranda, notes, lecture-notes"; rendered "notes."
  • κατὰ συμβεβηκὸς — "incidentally, per accidens" (Aristotelian); rendered "incidentally."
  • παρέργοις — "as by-works / side-matters"; rendered "by-works."
  • διατριβῆς — "spending of time / sojourn / [academic] disputation-study"; here the shared student years, rendered "passing of time."
  • Νῆφε καὶ μέμνησο ἀπιστεῖν — "Be sober and remember to distrust," ascribed to Epicharmus and a famous early-modern scholarly motto; given here as Rosenkrantz's personal symbolum (motto).
  • εἶπε καὶ ἁ Γραῖα τἀλαθέα κοσκινόμαντις (printed "εἶπε καὶ ὰ Γραίω τἀλαϑέα κοσκινόμαντις") — Theocritus, Idyll III.31: "and the old sieve-diviner woman spoke the truth too." Flagged textual crux: the printed witness reads ὰ Γραίω (with a grave accent and an apparent dative/genitive form) where the vulgate Theocritus has ἁ Γραῖα (nominative, "the old woman"); the printed corruption is retained in the Source block and the standard reading is followed in the translation. Cramer's point: if Liddel "heard" it, it was as worthless as a sieve-diviner's chatter.
  • Latin idioms / proverbs. subventanea parere — literally "to bring forth wind-eggs" (ova subventanea, unfertilised eggs), i.e. to produce nothing of substance; kept as image. cornices … in alienis plumis tripudiantes — Aesop's crow in borrowed/peacock feathers (the plagiarist topos). ollas et cucurbitas lippire — a proverbial expression of confusing two obvious things ("to be blear-eyed at pots and gourds"); rendered with a gloss. Rem acu tetigisti — "you have touched the matter with the needle," i.e. "you have hit it exactly" (Plautus); here quoted as the alleged words of Tycho's letter to Liddel, kept in the Source quotation marks. prout iacet litera — "as the letter lies," i.e. according to the literal text. mordicus tueri — "to defend tooth and nail." falsimonium / crimen falsi — the legal crime of falsehood/forgery; propria confessio … fortissima probatio — the maxim "confession is the strongest proof." Satis pro imperio — a Terentian tag, "enough of issuing commands."
  • Calendar / dateline. Deproperatum Stetini, Anno salutis reparatae CIϽ IϽ IIC , II Calend. Aprilis = "Hurried off at Stettin, in the year of restored salvation 1598, on the second day before the Kalends of April." The Roman numeral CIϽ IϽ IIC = 1000 + 500 + 98 = 1598 (note the subtractive IIC = 98). a.d. II Kal. Apr. (the day before pridie Kal. Apr.) = 31 March. The header date (31 March 1598) is therefore taken from the source's own dateline and agrees with the edition label ("1598. 31. marts. Stettin"). Stettin used the Julian (Old Style) calendar (Lutheran Pomerania did not adopt the Gregorian reform until 1700), so the date is O.S. Internal date: Anno 91, a.d. 14 Calend. Iunij = "the year [15]91, on the 14th day before the Kalends of June" = 19 May 1591 (the date of the Rostock summons); not converted, glossed only.
