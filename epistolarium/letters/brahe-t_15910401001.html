ILLVSTRISSIMO PRINCIPI AC DOMINO, DOMINO WILHELMO, LANDGRAVIO HASSIÆ, COMITI IN CATZENELLEBOGEN, DIETZ, ZIEGENHAIN ET NIDDA: PRINCIPI AC DOMINO CLEMENTISSIMO. S. D.

REDDIDIT mihi, Illustrissime Princeps, a Celsit. T. amandatus hoc nomine Tabellarius, die 23. Martij, literas Celsit. T. 3. Februarij labentis Anni ad me scriptas: in quibus Celsit. T. statim ab initio, de tarditate et raritate mearum literarum, quodque ex Obseruationibus meis Mathematicis nihil adipiscatur, mecum expostulat. At ego superiori æstate Celsit. T. scripsi, atque in Cometa proxime elapsi Anni meas Obseruationes communicaui, et simul, quæ de illo Mathematice breuiter dicenda habui, exposui: Ad quæ omnia nihil adhuc responsi accepi, adeo vt addubitem, an ea ad Celsit. T. manus sarta tecta peruenerint, nec ne: præsertim, cum ROTHMANNO a Celsit. T. huc ablegato, qui ante Hyemem præteritam, circa Septembris finem, hinc decessit, nihil de his constiterit. Scripsi etiam denuo per eundem ROTHMANNVM, sed meas Celsit. T. non fuisse allatas, cum ipse ROTHMANNVS nondum sese in Aula Celsit. T. stiterit, haud obscure conijcio. Mansit ille hîc mecum quinque circiter septimanas, intereaque cum illo de rebus Astronomicis non inuite contuli, vnaque Instrumentorum meorum formam, Magnitudinem, Diuisionem et Pinnacidiorum rationem ipsum designare, vti ipsi libuit, libenter permisi: quorum aliqua, dum hîc moraretur, ad T. Celsit. literis consignata, se missurum aiebat, nonnulla vero plenius perlustrata et accuratius descripta secum Cassellas allaturum indicabat. Non tamen omnia ad T. Celsit. quæ in Machinis Astronomicis peruiderat se referre audere tacite insinuabat: neque enim Celsit. T. in omnibus illi fidem habituram existimabat, siquidem pleraque in illis omnem fidem excedere, nec vestra his conferri posse, sponte fateretur, ne, si forte horum magnitudinem, exquisitam structuram, et inter obseruandum tractandi stabilitatem, prout res erat, luculenter exponeret, in gratiam mei id loqui, et vestris hac in parte fabricis, quippiam minus decore derogare videretur: At ego illi liberum reliqui, ea, quæ hic viderat, siue dicere, siue tacere, prout ipsemet consultius id esse arbitraretur. Sed cum intra semestre iam elapsum ad T. Celsit. nondum reuersus sit, satis se Pythagoreum in meis non omnimode istic propalandis, exhibuit. Ego sane, cum Brumam instare viderem, vtpote tum Michaëlis Festo præ foribus existente, a quo tempore, ob appropinquantem Hyemem, nauigationes maris Baltici magis magisque importunæ euadunt, ipsum adhortatus sum, imo quantum licuit vrsi, vt quam citissime fieri posset, se Cassellas reciperet. Neque enim dubitabam, Celsi!. T. desiderio cognoscendi ea, quæ hîc viderat, quæque mecum egerat, impense teneri. Is quidem propediem se istuc rediturum spondebat, at interim per patriam suam sibi transeundum, negotiorum quorundam expediendorum gratia, referebat. Fieri itaque potest, vt istic valetudinarius delitescat, erat enim cum hinc decederet, minus adhuc confirmatâ sanitate, quod color faciei admodum plalescens, satis innuebat. At quod per literas idipsum, vel si quas alias habuerit diuturnæ istius moræ causas, non significarit, miror: neque enim facile crediderim, humanitus ipsi quippiam accidisse. Videbatur alias T. Celsit. vti par erat, obsequentissimus: Et Stellarum descriptionem atque ex obseruationibus vestris demonstrationem, prout illi iniunctum erat, breui se animo lubenti absoluturum satis promisit: quod vt mature etiam efficeret, ipsum sedulo et sæpius instigaui, quo collatio tandem inter nostras vtrinque Obseruationes institui queat, et qua de causa Quina illa Scrupula, in quibus differimus, desiderentur, euidenti aliqua comprobatione enodari possit. Cum autem ex eo tempore, quo hinc decessit, omnino nihil de eo, vbi locorum hæreat, constiterit (in Daniâ enim eum esse non est quod T. Celsit. suspicetur) remitto literas, quas T. Celsit. mihi per hunc eundem Tabellarium ad illum dirigendas transmisit: cum Celsit. T. multo commodius possit, in quâ Germaniæ parte nunc commoretur, explicari (si modo nondum Cassellas reuersus sit) atque eo literas, siue has siue alias propria autoritate dirigere. Quantum ad Mathematicas Obseruationes et Stellarum Fixarum designationes, quas Cel. T. sibi communicari clementer cupit, sciat me Octingentarum circiter Fixarum canonicam descriptionem nunc moliri, eamque Tomi nostri primi Progymnasmatum Astronomicorum (qui adhuc sub Prælo est) Capiti 2. inserere decreuisse, vnaque earum Obseruationes quas habui accuratiores, et his innixas demonstrationes Geometricas. Quin et alia de Sole et Luna cælitus deducta Obseruata, atque in Numeros per Triangulorum rationem digesta, idem Tomus cap. 1. complectitur; vt nihil dicam de ijs, quæ nouam illam et supra modum mirandam Anni 1572. Stellam, alias luculenter tractata concernunt. Quibus subinde nonnulla ad reliquos quoque Planetas pertinentia, oportunitate sic exigente, intermiscentur. Ostendi autem ROTHMANNO hîc præsenti Volumen illud Progymnasmatum, quoad hactenus absolutum est, in quo etiam pauculi desiderantur quaterniones, cum per se alias magnum sit, et vsque in quintum Alphabetum excreuerit. Is T. C. de contentis in eo, quatenus in memoria habet, vbi reuersus fuerit, certiorem facile reddet. Quam primum vero Opus illud, totaliter labore Typographico absolutum fuerit, nullatenus intermittam quin C. T. eius reddam participem. Sunt quædam ingrata obstacula, quibus quo minus non solum hoc, sed et alia quæ meditor, Astronomiæ instaurandæ sustentacula ad optatum finem, eâ quâ maxime cuperem maturatione perducere queam, nolens volens impediar, sed spero et ab his atque alijs felicem et exoptatam liberationem, quacunque tandem id ratione fieri queat. Omne solum forti Patria, et cœlum vndique supra est. Interim C. T. non ægre ferat, quod plura ex meis Obseruationibus et inuentis Astronomicis non mittam, id enim minus consultum, nec etiam, ob tot viarum mari terrâque anfractus, atque hominum, in quorum forte manus interea peruenire possent, non omnino perspectam sinceritatem, satis tutum arbitror. Mora hæc qualiscunque fuerit, fenore, vti spero, compensabitur. Daniæ Patriæ nostræ status, quem scire iam denuo expetis, est in hoc quasi interregno, adhuc (quod diuinæ bonitati acceptum ferimus) satis tranquillus et fortunatus. Serenissimus Princeps noster in Regem electus, indies proficit non saltem ætate et corporis vigore atque robore, sed et, quod pluris faciendum duco, virtute atque bonis artibus; ita vt non dubiam conceperimus spem, illum optimi et laudatissimæ memoriæ Patris heroica vestigia non segniter suo tempore ingressurum; atque ad Rempub. laudabiliter et decenter administrandam eximie idoneum futurum, tanquam ad hoc natum, et diuinitus nobis in locum præstantissimi Parentis regno huic præmature adempti, restitutum. Interim autem dum iunior hic Princeps adhuc sub Curatoribus, viris præcipuis, ob ætatem versatur, Quatuor Seniores e regni Consiliarijs Reipu. gubernacula administrant: Inter quos Primarius est Dn. NICOLAVS CAASIVS, Cancellarius, vir vsu rerum, iudicio et consultâ prudentiâ, insigniter, præter Generis antiquam Nobilitatem, clarus. In literis etiam humanioribus, præsertim vero Historijs, et quæ ad Politiarum gubernationem conducunt, apprime versatus. Si quid vero arduum et solito grauius inciderit, in Comitia Regni, quæ quotannis semel circa Solstitium æstiuum celebrantur, ad vniuersum Regni Senatum, vel etiam ante statutum tempus, si opus fuerit, conuocatum, defertur. Ita vt Aristocratia quædam, quæ etiam reipub. forma non admodum improbata est, apud nos, donec Serenissimus Rex Electus ad maturiorem ætatem peruenerit, existat. Intereaque omnia moderate et consulte administrantur, vna cum Principis nostri, vti par est, summa obseruantia. DEVS in posterum etiam benedictionem clementer largiatur, vt omnia pacate et feliciter succedant et conseruentur, exoptatissimumque nostrum Principem benigne protegat, et in suscepto virtutis tramite confirmet. Quantum ad me meaque attinet, ea pristinam ferme retinent conditionem. Ego in hac Insula priuatus dego nec me rebus immisceo politicis, quod etiam nunquam libens feci, nisi necessitate aliqua compulsus. Sunt alias satis multi qui ista affectant, quique talibus Officijs, me forte multo melius præesse queant, quibus ego hanc, vbi eam assequuntur, fortunam nullatenus inuideo, sed multo potius gaudeo, quod in copia reperiantur, qui me talibus verius oneribus quam honoribus liberent, vt eo commodius et tranquillius, ocio frui Philosophico liceat, atque rebus cœlestibus potius quam terrenis vacare; quamuis ne sic quidem ab impedimentis et tumultuationibus, quas vita suggerit ciuilis, plane exemtus, etiam cum maxime cupiam, esse queam; siue id sic quodam fato meo, siue quorundam sinistrâ intelligentiâ eueniat. Verum ego animi magnitudine, integritate, et veri iustique obseruantiâ id, quicquid erit, fauente nobis diuino, quod omnia æquis introspicit oculis, numine, exsuperare adnitar; et altiores curas cœlesti Philosophiæ, cui me hactenus totum deuoui, aspirante etiam nostro conatui cœlestium opifice, porro impendere pergam. De Animali isto, cuius etiam Celsit. T. in literis suis meminit, sic habeat: Nullum eiuscemodi hîc in Dania reperitur, neque Hafniæ, neque prope Friderisburgum. Fui enim aliquoties cum Sereniss. Rege nostro, laudatissimæ memoriæ, in ferarum horto Friderifburgensi, neque tamen huiusmodi animal vnquam conspexi, nec a quoquam relatione audiui illud istic nutriri. Hafniæ autem cognoui ante Annum vnum vel alterum animal fuisse e Noruegia oriundum, quod Rangiferum Latini vocant, Noruegi autem Rinsdüer/ quod an adhuc ibi sit valde dubito, non enim possunt istæ bestiæ calorem, qui vel apud nos est, sustinere. Scripsi nihilominus ad præfectum Arcis Hafniacæ, qui itidem affirmabat Ceruum istum Rangiferum in æstate præterita præ calore expirasse, alias eum facile ab ipso pro T. Celsit. impetrassem. Existimo autem hanc ipsam esse feram de qua Celsit. T. scribit, et aberratum ab eo saltem esse in nomine, tunc quoque proceritate, ob cornuum longitudinem et multifariam intricationem, quisquis ille tandem fuerit, qui apud Celsit. T. hac de re mentionem fecerit. Adiunxi vero huius Animalis genuinam designationem, non tam ex Icone Gesneriana, quæ tamen non multum aberrauit, quam indicatione cuiusdam ex Studiosis meis, qui Bergis in Noruegia natus est, atque se aliquoties huiuscemodi feras vidisse refert. Tunc quoque de natura earum, quæ illi comperta erant, nonnulla significauit, quæ etiam annotanda duxi, vt T. Celsit. desiderijs etiam hac in parte, quantum in me est, satisfieret. Si in posterum aliquid certius vel de hoc, vel alijs, quibus Celsit. T. delectari cognouero, intelligere licuerit, haud intermittam, quin Celsit. T. de ijs certiorem reddam. Habeo ipsemet Alcem quandam iuniorem, mihi nuper dono datam, quam si Celsit. T. expetit, non grauatim eam per occasionem mittam, et si in alijs longe potioribus quippiam per me fieri poterit, quod Celsit. T. acceptum siet, inueniar in exsequendo quam paratissimus.

Valeat T. Celsit. quam diutissime et felicissime.
Datum Vraniburgi, Cal. Aprilis, Anno 91.
T. Celsit. Addictiss. TYCHO BRAHE.

English translation machine translation, Probable

To the Most Illustrious Prince and Lord, Lord Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, Count in Katzenelnbogen, Dietz, Ziegenhain and Nidda: most clement Prince and Lord — greeting.

The courier dispatched for this purpose by Your Highness delivered to me, Most Illustrious Prince, on 23 March, the letter Your Highness had written to me on 3 February of the current year: in which Your Highness, right from the outset, complains to me of the tardiness and rarity of my letters, and that from my mathematical observations [you] gain nothing. But I wrote to Your Highness last summer, and communicated my observations of the comet of the year just past, and at the same time set out what I had briefly to say about it mathematically: to all of which I have as yet received no reply, so that I begin to doubt whether or not these reached Your Highness's hands sound and entire — especially since, of these matters, nothing was known to Rothmann, who was sent here by Your Highness and who departed hence before this past winter, around the end of September. I also wrote again by the same Rothmann, but I conjecture, not obscurely, that mine were not brought to Your Highness, since Rothmann himself has not yet presented himself at Your Highness's court. He stayed here with me about five weeks, and in the meantime I conferred with him about astronomical matters not unwillingly, and gladly permitted him, as it pleased him, to sketch the form, magnitude, division and arrangement of the sights [pinnacidia] of my instruments — some of which, while he tarried here, he said he would send to Your Highness committed to writing, while others, more fully surveyed and more accurately described, he indicated he would bring with him to Kassel. Yet he tacitly hinted that he did not dare to report to Your Highness everything he had observed in the astronomical machines: for he reckoned that Your Highness would not give him credence in all things, since he would freely confess that most things in them surpass all belief, and that yours cannot be compared with these — lest, if perchance he were to set out plainly, as the matter stood, their magnitude, their exquisite construction, and their steadiness in handling while observing, he should seem to be saying it to gratify me, and on that head to detract somewhat unbecomingly from your own apparatus. But I left him free either to tell or to keep silent about what he had seen here, as he himself judged the more advisable. Yet since within the half-year now elapsed he has not yet returned to Your Highness, he has shown himself Pythagorean enough in not making my affairs known there in every particular. I, for my part, when I saw mid-winter at hand — Michaelmas being then before the door, from which season, owing to the approaching winter, voyages on the Baltic Sea become more and more troublesome — urged him, indeed pressed him as far as I could, to betake himself to Kassel as soon as ever might be. For I did not doubt that Your Highness was held by an eager desire to learn the things he had seen here and had transacted with me. He for his part pledged that he would return thither shortly, but said that in the meantime he had to pass through his own homeland, for the sake of dispatching certain business. It may therefore be that he lies hidden there in poor health, for when he departed hence his health was as yet not fully restored, as the rather pallid colour of his face sufficiently intimated. But that he has not signified this very thing by letter, or whatever other causes he may have had for that long delay, I marvel at; for I should not easily believe that anything has befallen him in the way of mortality. He seemed otherwise, as was fitting, most obedient to Your Highness; and he promised fully that he would shortly, with a willing mind, complete the description of the stars and the demonstration from your observations, as had been enjoined upon him: and that he might accomplish this in good time, I diligently and repeatedly urged him on, so that a collation might at length be set up between our observations on both sides, and that the reason why those five minutes [scrupula], in which we differ, are wanting might be unravelled by some evident proof. But since, from the time when he departed hence, absolutely nothing has been known of him — where in the world he is lodged (for there is no reason for Your Highness to suspect that he is in Denmark) — I send back the letter which Your Highness transmitted to me by this same courier to be directed to him: since Your Highness can much more conveniently find out in what part of Germany he now resides (if only he has not yet returned to Kassel), and direct thither, on your own authority, the letter, whether this one or others. As for the mathematical observations and the designations of the fixed stars which Your Highness clemently desires to be communicated to him, let it be known that I am now at work on a canonical description of about eight hundred fixed stars, and have determined to insert it into Chapter 2 of the first volume of our Astronomical Preliminary Exercises [Progymnasmata] (which is still under the press), together with the more accurate observations of them which I have had, and the geometrical demonstrations resting upon these. Indeed the same volume, in Chapter 1, also embraces other observed data concerning the Sun and Moon, derived from the heavens and reduced into numbers by the method of triangles; to say nothing of those things that concern that new and beyond-measure marvellous Star of the year 1572, elsewhere fully treated. With these are interspersed, from time to time, as occasion so requires, some things pertaining also to the remaining planets. Now I showed Rothmann, while he was present here, that volume of the Progymnasmata, so far as it has hitherto been completed, in which even a few quaternions are still wanting, although it is otherwise great in itself and has grown right up to the fifth alphabet. He, when he has returned, will easily inform Your Highness of its contents, so far as he holds them in memory. But as soon as that work has been wholly completed by the labour of the press, I shall by no means fail to make Your Highness a sharer in it. There are certain unwelcome obstacles by which, willy-nilly, I am hindered from bringing not only this, but also the other supports of the astronomy to be restored which I am planning, to their wished-for end with that swiftness which I should most greatly desire; but I hope for a happy and longed-for deliverance from these and others, by whatever means it may at last come to pass. To the brave man every soil is a fatherland, and the heaven is overhead on every side. Meanwhile let Your Highness not take it ill that I do not send more of my observations and astronomical findings, for I judge that less advisable, nor indeed sufficiently safe, on account of so many windings of the roads by sea and land, and the not entirely well-ascertained good faith of the men into whose hands they might perchance fall in the meantime. This delay, of whatever sort it shall have been, will, as I hope, be compensated with interest. The state of our fatherland Denmark, which you now ask again to know, is in this quasi-interregnum still (a thing we set down to the divine goodness) tranquil and fortunate enough. Our Most Serene Prince, elected as King, advances day by day not only in age and in the vigour and strength of body, but also — what I reckon should be valued more highly — in virtue and in good accomplishments; so that we have conceived no doubtful hope that he will in his own time tread, not slothfully, in the heroic footsteps of his Father of best and most praiseworthy memory, and will be exceedingly fit to administer the commonwealth laudably and becomingly, as one born for this and divinely restored to us in the place of that most excellent Parent prematurely taken from this kingdom. In the meantime, however, while this younger Prince still abides, on account of his age, under Guardians, men of the foremost rank, Four Elders from the Councillors of the realm administer the helm of the commonwealth: among whom the chief is Master Niels Kaas, the Chancellor, a man notably distinguished — besides the ancient nobility of his line — by experience of affairs, by judgment and by considered prudence. He is also exceedingly well-versed in the more humane letters, but especially in history and in the things that conduce to the government of polities. But if anything arduous and weightier than usual arises, it is referred to the Diet of the realm — which is held once each year about the summer solstice — to the whole Senate of the realm, convened even before the appointed time, if there be need. So that a certain Aristocracy — which is also a form of commonwealth not altogether disapproved — exists among us, until the Most Serene Elected King shall have reached a riper age. And in the meantime all things are administered with moderation and counsel, together with the highest deference, as is fitting, toward our Prince. May GOD also for the future clemently bestow His blessing, that all things may turn out and be preserved peacefully and happily, and may He kindly protect our most longed-for Prince and confirm him in the path of virtue he has taken up. As for what concerns me and mine, they keep almost their former condition. I live as a private man on this island and do not mix myself in political affairs, which indeed I never did willingly, unless compelled by some necessity. There are otherwise quite enough who court such things, and who could preside over such offices perhaps far better than I — to whom I in no way envy this fortune, when they attain it, but rather rejoice much more that there are found in abundance those who may free me from such burdens (more truly so than honours), so that I may the more conveniently and tranquilly enjoy philosophical leisure, and be free for heavenly rather than earthly matters; although not even thus can I be wholly exempt from the impediments and tumults that civil life supplies, even when I most desire it — whether this comes about by some fate of mine, or by the sinister contrivance of certain persons. But I shall strive to surmount it, whatever it shall be, by greatness of spirit, by integrity, and by observance of the true and the just, the divine power favouring us — which looks into all things with impartial eyes; and I shall go on henceforth to devote my higher cares to that heavenly Philosophy to which I have hitherto wholly given myself, the Maker of the heavens likewise breathing favour upon our endeavour. Concerning that Animal, of which Your Highness also makes mention in your letter, let the matter stand thus: no creature of that kind is to be found here in Denmark, neither at Copenhagen nor near Frederiksborg. For I have several times been with our Most Serene King, of most praiseworthy memory, in the game-park at Frederiksborg, and yet never beheld any animal of this sort, nor heard from anyone by report that it is kept there. At Copenhagen, however, I learned that a year or two ago there had been an animal originating from Norway, which the Latins call Rangifer [reindeer], but the Norwegians Rinsdüer [Rensdyr]; whether it is still there I greatly doubt, for these beasts cannot endure the heat that there is even among us. I wrote nonetheless to the governor of the Castle of Copenhagen, who likewise affirmed that that Rangifer deer had expired this past summer from the heat — otherwise I should easily have obtained it from him for Your Highness. I judge, however, that this very beast is the one of which Your Highness writes, and that he at least went astray in the name — and also in the stature, on account of the length and the manifold tangling of the horns — whoever in the end it was who made mention of this matter to Your Highness. I have, moreover, appended a true depiction of this Animal, not so much from the figure in Gesner, which nevertheless did not greatly err, as from the information of one of my students who was born at Bergen in Norway, and who relates that he has several times seen beasts of this kind. Then too he signified some things, which were known to him, about their nature, which I have thought worth noting down, so that Your Highness's wishes might be satisfied in this part also, so far as is in me. If hereafter I shall have come to learn or to understand anything more certain, whether about this or about other things in which I shall have learned that Your Highness takes delight, I shall by no means fail to inform Your Highness of them. I have myself a certain young Elk [Alces], lately given to me as a gift, which, if Your Highness desires it, I shall not grudgingly send as occasion offers; and if in other and far weightier matters anything can be done by me that may be acceptable to Your Highness, I shall be found most ready in the carrying-out.

May it fare with Your Highness as long and as happily as may be.
Given at Uraniborg, the Kalends of April, the year [15]91.
Your Highness's most devoted, TYCHO BRAHE.

Apparatus

  • Recipient (edition-tag discrepancy — flagged). The edition records the recipient as Christoph Rothmann, but the letter is addressed and written to Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel (1532–1592): the salutation names him with full titulature, and the body addresses Celsitudo Tua / Cels. T. ("Your Highness") throughout. Rothmann is the subject of the letter, discussed in the third person — Tycho reports on Rothmann's visit and unexplained failure to return to Kassel. The header therefore follows the source's own salutation; the edition's recipient:Christopher Rothmann is an indexing error.
  • Sender / place / date. Tycho Brahe, writing from Uraniborg on Hven. Dateline Datum Vraniburgi, Cal. Aprilis, Anno 91 = "Given at Uraniborg, the Kalends of April [1 April], 1591" (O.S.; Denmark kept the Julian calendar). The Roman-calendar idiom Cal. Aprilis is the 1st of the month; rendered with idiom and civil gloss by the edition's convention The catalogue label ("1591. 1. april. Uraniborg") agrees with the dateline.
  • Edition of record. Tycho's own Epistolarum astronomicarum libri (Liber I), printed at Uraniborg/Wandsbek, 1596 — reprinted in Dreyer, Opera Omnia VI. The 1596 volume is the publication in which Tycho assembled his exchange with the Hesse-Kassel court (the Landgrave and Rothmann). No manuscript witness is recorded in the edition.
  • Wilhelm, Landgravius Hassiae — Landgrave Wilhelm IV "the Wise" of Hesse-Kassel, the leading princely patron of observational astronomy of the day, with his own observatory at Kassel; Catzenellebogen, Dietz, Ziegenhain et Nidda are the subsidiary Hessian comital titles, retained as in the source.
  • Rothmannus — Christoph Rothmann, the Landgrave's mathematician at Kassel. He had travelled to Hven and stayed with Tycho "about five weeks" in 1590, and at the date of this letter had still not returned to Kassel; Tycho's report of his pallor and disappearance is historically the last clear contemporary trace of Rothmann's movements. "satis se Pythagoreum … exhibuit" — "he has shown himself Pythagorean enough," i.e. as discreetly silent (about Tycho's instruments) as the secrecy-bound Pythagoreans; kept as the allusion.
  • Instruments. Instrumentorum … formam, Magnitudinem, Diuisionem et Pinnacidiorum rationem = "the form, magnitude, division and arrangement of the sights of [my] instruments." Pinnacidium → sight (pinnule) per the controlled instrument glossary. Machinis Astronomicis rendered "astronomical machines" (Tycho's large instruments). vestra … fabricis / vestris … fabricis — the Landgrave's own apparatus at Kassel, with which Rothmann diplomatically declined to compare Tycho's.
  • Technical: the five minutes. Quina illa Scrupula, in quibus differimus = "those five minutes [of arc] in which we differ." Scrupulum here is the astronomical minute (the c. 5′ standing discrepancy between the Hven and Kassel stellar positions that the planned collatio — collation — was to resolve). Rendered "minutes," with the Latin retained in brackets.
  • The Progymnasmata. Tomi nostri primi Progymnasmatum Astronomicorum … adhuc sub Praelo = the first volume of the Astronomiae Instauratae Progymnasmata ("Preliminary Exercises for the Restoration of Astronomy"), then in press (it appeared posthumously, 1602). Octingentarum circiter Fixarum canonicam descriptionem = "a canonical description of about eight hundred fixed stars" (Tycho's star catalogue). demonstrationes Geometricas … per Triangulorum rationem = "geometrical demonstrations … by the method of triangles" (trigonometric reduction). nouam illam … Anni 1572. Stellam = the new star (nova) of 1572, the subject of the volume's later parts.
  • Printing units. pauculi … quaterniones = "a few quaternions" (gatherings of four sheets); vsque in quintum Alphabetum excreuerit = "has grown right up to the fifth alphabet" — signature-alphabets (each c. 23 gatherings), i.e. the book had already run to a very large extent. Kept as period printing-house idiom.
  • Astronomy programme. Astronomiae instaurandae sustentacula = "the supports of the astronomy to be restored" — Tycho's instauratio astronomiae programme. Omne solum forti Patria, et coelum vndique supra est — a sententia ("To the brave man every soil is a fatherland, and the heaven is everywhere overhead"), echoing the Ovid/proverbial omne solum forti patria est; kept as the maxim it is, foreshadowing Tycho's own later exile.
  • Danish politics (the regency). Written during the minority of Christian IV (b. 1577; elected king 1580, crowned only 1596): Serenissimus Princeps noster in Regem electus = "our Most Serene Prince, elected as King." Patris … laudatissimae memoriae = Frederik II (d. 4 April 1588), the "Father of most praiseworthy memory." Quatuor Seniores e regni Consiliarijs = the four regents of the Rigsråd governing during the minority; Dn. NICOLAVS CAASIVS, Cancellarius = Niels Kaas (1535–1594), Chancellor of Denmark and chief of the regency. Comitia Regni … circa Solstitium aestiuum = the Diet (assembly of the Rigsråd) meeting about midsummer. Aristocratia … reipub. forma = "an Aristocracy … a form of commonwealth," the regency described in classical constitutional terms.
  • The animals (reindeer and elk). Animal … quod Rangiferum Latini vocant, Noruegi autem Rinsdüer = "the animal which the Latins call Rangifer [reindeer], but the Norwegians Rinsdüer" (Danish/Norwegian rensdyr); the vernacular word is set off by the source's own virgule (Rinsdüer/), preserved in the Source block. The Copenhagen specimen had died of heat (prae calore expirasse). Icone Gesneriana = the woodcut in Conrad Gessner's Historia animalium (1551–58). Studiosis meis, qui Bergis in Noruegia natus est = one of Tycho's students born at Bergen, Norway. Alcem quandam iuniorem = "a certain young Elk" (Alces, the European elk/moose), which Tycho offers to send — the very animal that (per the later tradition) is said to have died at Landskrona; here only the offer is made. praefectum Arcis Hafniacae = the governor (lensmand) of Copenhagen Castle.
  • Places. Hafnia → Copenhagen; Friderisburgum / Friderifburgensi → Frederiksborg (the long-s misprint Friderifburgensi kept in Source); Cassellae → Kassel; Bergae → Bergen; Vraniburgum → Uraniborg.
  • Epistolary formulae. S. D. = salutem dicit, "sends greeting". Valeat T. Celsit. quam diutissime et felicissime = "May it fare with Your Highness as long and as happily as may be." T. Celsit. Addictiss. = Tuae Celsitudini Addictissimus, "Your Highness's most devoted."
  • Source orthography / printer's slips (kept in Source block). Celsi!. T. (a printer's exclamation-point for Celsit.), plalescens (for pallescens, "growing pale"), Friderifburgensi (long-s/f confusion), Rinsdüer/ (with virgule): all reproduced verbatim by the edition's convention and read in the translation as the obvious intended forms, noted here rather than emended in place.
