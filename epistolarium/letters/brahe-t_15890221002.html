CLARISSIMO ET ERVDITISSIMO VIRO, D. DOCTORI CHRISTOPHORO ROTHMANNO, ILLVSTRISSIMI PRINCIPIS WILHELMI LANDTGRAVII HASSIAe, ETC. MATHEMATICO EXIMIO, AMICO SUO PLURIMUM DILECTO. S. D. QVONIAM nulla interea, ex quo tuas vltimo accepi literas, Eruditissime et amantissime ROTHMANNE, tibi rescribendi ob internuntiorum defectum, oblata est occasio, in hoc vsque tempus responsionem differre coactus sum; Cum vero quidam eorum, qui quotannis ad Nundinas Francofurtianas proficiscuntur, mihi hisce diebus indicari curâsset, se breui illuc abiturum, non intermittendum duxi, quin aliqua saltem tuis responderem; vtvt hoc praesertim tempore plurimis distrahar occupationibus, adeo vt ad rescribendum vix aliquid suppetat otij.

Quantum vero ad priorem Epistolae tuae partem attinet, in qua copiose et perseueranter materiam Caeli esse Aërem elementarem contendis, nec a semel arrepta sententia, te vllis ratiocinijs dimoueri sinis, eaque quae in contrarium merito dicuntur, speciosis subterfugijs eludere conaris, hisce non constitui sigillatim multa respondere. Quin per me tibi liberum sit, Elementum Aëris Caelo miscere, siquidem non aliter vis. Imo si quis Caelum Aqueum esse, vt Hebraei: vel Igneum, vt Paracelsistae; Aut etiam Terreum (modo cui sic lubeat) aut quiddam ex his quatuor compositum, vt nuper Franciscus Vallesius affirmare praesumpserit, mea etiam non intererit, modo et mihi quod sentiam, libere proferre liceat; Caeli videlicet, substantiam esse Aetheream et liquidissimam, purissimamque quandam materiam, supra omnem Elementorum naturam exaltatam, et ad singularem quandam perfectionem deductam, velut quintam quandam Essentiam, nulli mutationi aut alterationi obnoxiam, quaeque peregrinas impressiones naturaliter non recipiat. Sin autem alicuius Elementi naturam Caelo affingere non admodum absurdum videretur, ego potius illud Igneum, quam Aëreum esse concederem, prout a Paracelso traditum est, qui illud quartum et Igneum Elementum nuncupat. Ex quo enim Stellae et lumina caelestia Ignea flammantiaque appareant; conueniensque sit, quaelibet corpora in Iliado suae naturae Analogo versari; quemadmodum videmus in hac inferiori rerum natura: Animantia, quae de Mumia Terrae constant in Terris degere: Volucres de Mumia Aëria in Aëre; Pisces de Mumia Aquea, in Aquis versari, et sic de caeteris, vt ob id verisimilius appareat, Solem et Stellas, quae Ignis cuiusdam incombustibilis speciem repraesentant, in Aethere Igneo et incombustibili, (vnde etiam Aetheris nomen apud Graecos illi attributum quasi Ardens seu Igneum, idque perpetuo cum sit inconsummabilis) suas Reuolutiones exercere. Attamen mihi ne haec quidem sententia, vtvt Aëris assumtione rationabilior sit, satis probatur. Existimo enim Caelum eam habere ad Elementarem Mundum rationem, quae est animae ad corpus. Qui itaque plene exprimere poterit, quid sit anima in corporibus, et quomodo a corpore ipso differat, is etiam caelestis et Elementaris Mundi naturas et discrimina rectius enodabit. Dicat insuper mihi aliquis, ex qua materia Sol, Luna et reliqua omnia Sidera existant, tum demum illi de Caeli substantia, in quo haec versantur, disserenti facilius aurem praebebo. Quod si Contenti naturam et compositionem explicare non dabitur, vtique Continentis multo minus. Existimo autem, omnia quae in Caelo voluuntur corpora, e caelesti, et Aetherea constare materia, quae qualis sit, vix exprimi potest. Supra tamen omnem Elementarem qualitatem graduatam necesse est, quemadmodum ea, quae ex Elementis componuntur, in Mundo Elementari continentur. Quod si omnia, et quae in Caelo et Terra, caeterisque Elementis sunt corpora, ex tribus illis principijs constare, quae Paracelsus superiori Aeuo in lucem reuocauit, ab antiquissimis Philosophis, tam ante Aristotelem, quam post, non minus animaduersa et approbata, Trinitatis in opifice DEO mysterium, quasi in imagine quadam per singulas Creaturas, representantia, satis ab omnibus receptum foret; Oportebit nihilominus hîc longe aliam in caelestibus intelligere depurationem, et ad praecipuam perfectionem summamque Diaphanitatem et subtilitatem, exaltationem, quam in his sublunaribus; adeo vt omnis labis et defectus expers reperiatur, inperscrutabili Opificis sapientia, omnia sic disponente et perpetuo conseruante. Verum ego de hac intricata, et quae vix in caduca hac vita sufficienter enodari potest, quaestione, quae etiam ad Astronomiae redintegrationem minus est necessaria, nolo vlterius disceptationem aliquam mouere, neque tuis obiectionibus speciatim respondere, quod per se non foret difficile: Idque si vlterius instas, alias non grauatim sum facturus. Nunc breuitati studens alijsque implicitus, pleraque volens dissimulo, praesertim cum videam me tibi non persuasurum, rem aliter se habere, quam praeoccupata fert opinio. Vnicum saltem opponam praecipuo tuo Argumento, quo Caelum et Aërem Elementarem prorsus vnum et idem esse, ex eo, quod Refractiones non ad verticem pertingant, sed vltra dimidium Altitudinis Quadrantem insensibiles euadant, astruere conaris; Siquidem id probet non esse diuersa Aëris atque Aetheris Diaphana, et per consequens eandem vtrumque obtinere materiam et substantiam. Hîc mihi videris plura inducere, quam Assumtio ista sustineat; Nam licet concedatur, nullam animaduerti Refractionem in Sole vel Stellis in data aliqua sublimitate, inde quidem satis sequitur, Diaphanorum vel nullam vel inperceptibilem esse discrepantiam, Sed non ob id necessario infertur, vtriusque Diaphani eandem prorsus qualitatem et substantiam existere; Possunt enim diuersae substantiae et qualitates eidem Diaphanitati patere. Dabo Exemplum e rebus inferioribus, quae manibus tractari et visu discerni possunt. Si Aquam pluuiam vel niualem praesertim, aut etiam communem fontanam per saepius reiteratam eleuationem, defecatissimam reddiderimus, idemque in vino quouis generoso fiat, donec ab omni Phlegmate et impuritate liberetur, amboque hi liquores consimili Chrystallino excipiantur, vtique Diaphanitas vnius non discrepabit ab altera, quantum vllis sentibus dignoscere liceat, ideoque Refractionum, si quae forte hinc fierent, nulla erit differentia, attamen substantia et natura vnius liquoris atque corporis, diuersissima ab altera inuenietur. Id enim quod e vino elicitum est, Igneam et Sulphuream redolebit qualitatem, adeo vt incensum totum flammis absorbeatur, nullo post se relicto vestigio; Quod vero ex Aqua constat, Aqueum permanebit, et tantum abest, vt ardeat, vt Ignem etiam citius, quam antea extinguat, illique vi contraria resistet. Sic quoque Caeli et Aëris Diaphanitates, si vel prorsus eaedem forent, quod tamen nondum satis obtentum est, nihilominus diuersam substantiam atque materiam habere nihil impedit. Plura nunc de his aggregare nolo, non enim haec quaestio ad Astronomum proprie spectat, vt antea quoque monui, neque Cometarum generationi atque explicationi vllum, vt tu existimas, fert subsidium, si Caelum Aëreum statuatur: Imo hoc potius quadraret, si Paracelso Igneum id esse concederemus: Nam Cometae non Aëream, sed Igneam potius quandam naturam prae se ferunt. Quod enim tu e fumositatibus Terrenis etiam in Aërem altissimum, quem intra ipsas Reuolutiones Siderum contineri vis, sublatis et illic illuminatis, Cometarum procreationem saluari posse existimes, ex vna absurditate et incongrua assumtione, alteram stabilire incassum elaboras, vt alibi, cum ex professo de his agam, adeo euidenter, vt nemo iuste refragari queat, ostendere decreui.

Quantum ad Crepuscula attinet, refers te vespertinum illic animaduertisse iuxta Aequinoctia, Sole infra Horizontem depresso 24. Partibus, vnde valde miraris, quod ego vix 17. Partium idem in Crepusculo matutino expertus sim. Reiteraui itaque his ipsis diebus eius rei experientiam, vt tuis dubijs satisfieret, idque Sole quidem nondum ad Aequinoctium vernum progresso, sed iuxta Principia piscium, adhuc versante. Ratio tamen est eadem, neque magna hoc loco, ab Arietis primordijs incidit differentia. Adinueni autem die 10. Februarij proxime elapso, Crepusculi vespertini extremum limitem fuisse, quando media Balthei Orionis Meridianum obtinebat. Ideoque Sol, qui tunc erat in Part. 2¼. ♓ ad summum 17. Gradibus infra Horizontem, versus Nadir, delapsus erat. Postea die 12. mane, Obseruabatur hîc primum diluculum, cum Spica ♍ abesset a Meridiano versus Occidentem P. 35½. de Aequatore. Ergo Sole tunc existente in P. 3⅔., ♓ non poterat is sub Finitore in eo circulo, qui versus Nadir tendit, depressior esse Part. 16½. Eodem die Crepusculum etiam vespertinum denuo diligenter notaui, eo ipso temporis articulo, cum Humerus dexter Orionis ante Meridianum cerneretur versus Ortum Part. 1. Min. 55. Ergo Arcus Crepusculi, vt antea acceptus, euadit Part. 16⅚. erat enim Sol in Part. 4¼. ♓. Haud aliter die 15. Februarij, vltimum vestigium diei apparuit, quando eadem lucidior Stella Humeri Orionis Meridianum praetergressa erat Part. 1½. Ideoque Sol, qui Partem 7¼. ♓ occupabat, infra Horizontem, permodicum vltra 16. Gradus delituit. Vides itaque Crepusculi siue matutini, siue vespertini mensuram in Circulo verticali, nusquam 17. Gradus excedere, quod nimis magno interuallo tuam animaduersionem anteuertit. Atque hinc liquet, quam parum valida sint, non saltem quae de Crepusculis, sed etiam Refractionibus, quas apud Vos aliter, quam hîc se habere existimas, proponis. Quae de veriore et secretiore Philosophia, quam ea quae hactenus in Scholis vulgariter docetur, breuiter in posterioribus literis attigi, et aliquatenus tantum indicaui, tibi placere video; plura itaque, tum eorum quae desideras, tum etiam alia his longe praestantiora in scriptis eorum Philosophorum, quos recensui, diligenti adhibita intentione inuenies, praesertim vero ipsa Experientia rerum magistra omnia explorante, praemissa tamen caussarum secundum naturam ipsam sedula inquisitione. Mihi nunc pluribus de ijs Philosophari non vacat. Neque enim est id huius loci, nec satis tuto literis committitur.

Secundum Tomum Operis nostri, De Caeli recentioribus Phaenomenis, tibi gratum fuisse lubens audio. Et Latitudinem Lunae maximam a te eandem fere, quam illic capit. 3. indico, adinuentam, mihi acceptum est. Scripsit non ita dudum ad me Clarissimus et Eruditissimus vir D. HENRICVS BRVCÆVS, apud Rostochienses Medicinae Galenicae et Philosophiae Aristotelicae Professor inprimis celebris, Mathematicarum etiam rerum, praesertim Geometricarum apprime peritus, et in dubium vocauit hanc meam de mutata Latitudine Lunae assertionem, autoritatemque Regiomontani, Copernici, Werneri et similium praestantium Mathematicorum opposuit, quod ij nihil tale vnquam deprehendissent: et quia id Eclipsium magnitudini, alias satis bene calculo correspondenti, officeret. Verum ego illi respondi, Autoritates horum excellentium Astronomorum, nihil derogare meis inuentis; siquidem illi constitutioni Ptolemaicae, hac in parte prorsus acquieuerunt. Neque ipsimet certam aliquam experimentationem instituerunt; an res ita ad vnguem se haberet, nec ne. Quamuis autoritates per se in Mathematicis, sicubi alias, quamminimum habeant ponderis, nisi accedat, certis Obseruationibus fundata, euidens demonstratio. In Eclipsibus vero hanc differentiam esse vix perceptibilem, ob propinquitatem Nodorum, indicaui. Deinde illi vicissim tuas quoque animaduersiones obieci, quibus idem proxime mecum deprehendisse asseueras. Cupio itaque ex te vlterius cognoscere, quo pacto hanc explorationem institueris, et quid, quantumque in Latitudine Lunae mutatum animaduerteris, vt illi atque alijs, inuentis vnius non acquiescentibus, alterius etiam suffragium, qui Caelum diligenter vna inspicit (quod nimis secure ab istis negligitur, cum tamen Astronomi haberi velint) pro me facere, vlterius ostendere queam. Licet ego nihil ex meis Obseruationibus depromere, atque in lucem edere decreui, quod non per se satis ratum exploratumque sit, nulloque alterius indigeat suffragio.

Id vero quod ais, Caput Draconis abesse a Tabulis plus quatuor Gradibus, teque per distantias et Globum id inuenisse, cuperem vt plenius indicares, quomodo collegeris; hoc enim videtur mihi salua Eclipsium apparente magnitudine, vna cum earundem tempore minime consentaneum. Siquidem quatuor Graduum differentia a Nodis Latitudinem Lunae immutet 21. Minut. per duas videlicet tertias proxime Lunaris corporis partes; quod sane in Eclipsium apparentijs admodum perceptibilem induceret discrepantiam: Qualem tamen in multis deliquijs non saltem Lunaribus, sed etiam Solaribus, quae sedulo pluribus ab hinc Annis denotaui, animaduertere non potui; Nam licet tempora Eclipsium, ex calculo quocunque hactenus in vsu habito, cum Caelo ipso non rite conuenerint, sed sensibili differentia interdum maiori, nonnunquam vero minori anticipârint, vel tardius sese exhibuerint; tamen quo ad magnitudinum formam, nequaquam adeo euidentem diuersitatem vnquam adinueni, quantam tuae Nodorum alterationes exigunt. Neque enim multo plus discriminis hac in parte cognoscere licuit, quam quantum aberratio in motu Luminarium, adeoque in tempore Zyzigiarum suggerere valebat, Nodis ipsis in loco suo, quo Tabulae Alphonsinae et Copernianae (quae permodicum in his discrepant) eas reponunt, quamproxime permanentibus. Rogo itaque vt vlterius, cur ita statuas, examinatione diligentiore facta, declares. Neque enim adeo euidentem, in Eclipticae et viae Lunaris intersectionibus, errorem Tabulis sine causa obijcere decet.

Refers praeterea, te in libro transmisso, quid de Cometarum materia sentiam, eorundemque generatione nihil reperisse, et recte quidem id asseris. Nam nusquam ex professo, hac de re illic tracto: Sed ad Epilogum totius Operis, hanc et plerasque similes quaestiones, tum multa alia in loca, tum etiam in vltimam libri clausulam reijcio. Interim tamen Pag. 254. vbi quartum caput Maesthliniani scripti de hoc Cometa expendo, haec verba inuenies. Ego materiam omnium Cometarum prorsus caelestem esse iudico; siquidem omnes etiam in ipso Caelo generentur. Vnde vero haec materia illis in Aetherea Regione suppetat, et quomodo illic procreentur, suo loco in Conclusione totius Operis meam expositurus sententiam. Ex his aliquo modo colligere potuisses, quid de eorum materia, quod non sit elementaris, et e vaporibus terrestribus, vt tu sentis, conflata, iudicem. Vbi vero, DEO volente, totum Opus ad Colophonem deductum fuerit, tum veterum Philosophorum, tum quoque recentium, inter quos etiam tuam de Cometarum materia et generatione sententiam discutiam, et minime reipsa constare posse, luculentis certisque rationibus fidem faciam. Interea quae de motu et Apparentijs talium Phaenomenon trado et demonstro, sufficiant; Neque enim Physicam considerationem, quae inde maxima ex parte deriuanda venit, confuse admiscendam duxi, vt Methodus debita seruaretur. At postquam non solum nouam Stellam (quae etiam non minimum adfert subsidij huic negotio penitius disquirendo) sed omnes Cometas, a me diligenter obseruatos, adhibitis etiam quibusdam alijs antea conspectis, sicubi res postulat, sub incudem veritatis per suas Apparentias reuocauero, tum demum consequens erit, Physicam et Astrologicam considerationem, generali quadam indicatione subiungere; Neque enim res haec est parui momenti, sed a paucissimis, ne dicam prorsus nullis, antea rite pertractata. Ideoque non perfunctorie aut promiscue, sed decenti ordine, debitaque diligentia obeunda venit.

Quantum ad caudae eductionem attinet, ostendi eam in Cometa Anni 1577. Veneris Stellam perpetuo respexisse, quamuis ad Solis partes postmodum potius inclinârim, ob opticam aliquam latentem rationem, cum vix credi possit, Veneris Sidus tam fortes eiaculari radios: Licet nunc demum aliquatenus persentiscere queam, id non adeo alienum a Veneris Sidere, velut tunc suspicabar, ex quo non longe ab eius Reuolutionibus Cometa iter suum direxerit. Veneris enim Stella, visa est mihi aliquando eam partem Lunae, quae a Sole auersa erat, et ipsi obiecta, satis sensibiliter illuminare, vtvt Luna longe remotius a Veneris circuitibus distiterit, quam Cometa. Simile quid etiam IOANNEM BAPTISTAM Benedictum, Patricium Venetum Philosophum et Mathematicum inprimis excellentem, animaduertisse reperio, in erudito illo Opere, quod de Mathematicis et Physicis speculationibus inscripsit. Sic enim in fine Epistolae, ad Baronem quendam Sabaudum Philibertum scribens, ait: Manifeste videtur, dum Luna reperitur secundum Longitudinem inter Solem et Venerem, quod pars Lunae lumine Solis destituta, a lumine Veneris aliquantulum illustratur, quod ego saepe vidi, et multis ostendi. Haec ille. Vt tamen verum fatear, neque haec ratiocinatio mihi ita satisfacit, vt Veneri tantam radiorum potentiam, quae Cometae caudam procreare potuerit, attribuere ausim. Verum sit, quoad caudae rationem, quicquid velit, et siue lateat in his Opticum quid, cur Solem perpetuo non ad amussim respiciant siue non, certe id nullatenus subsistere poterit, quod tu intendis, caudam esse partem corporis Cometae, et eiusdem cum capite substantiae, eodemque modo per illuminationem apparere. Id enim si omnimode ita se haberet, non tot Cometae a Regiomontano, Fracastorio, Apiano, Gemma Frisio, et alijs animaduersi, perpetuo Comam in oppositas fere Soli partes obuertissent, vt ex eorum scriptis colligere licet: Id quod etiam in ijs, qui a nobis obseruati sunt, quam proxime locum habuit. Nulla etenim ratio subest, cur protractio corporis Cometae in longum, si eiusdem esset cum ipso capite substantiae, semper a Sole propemodum auerteretur. Et nos vidimus in crinita Anni 77. cuius cauda, praesertim ab initio valde erat notabilis, eam admodum raram fuisse, et instar radiorum tenuium apparuisse, adeo quod Stellae quaedam, quas praetergrediebatur, aliquoties per illam transparuerint. Neque etiam erat eiusdem cum capite coloris, sed et hunc subinde alterabat, quod tamen ob Aëris interpositi diuersam qualitatem, praesertim prope Horizontem euenisse censeo; Sed et caput ipsum erat solide compactum, multoque cauda lucidius, et exacte rotundum conspiciebatur, adeo vt paruam Lunam ab initio diceres. Ipsi etiam crines capiti acuminatim copulabantur, et postea in latum longumque diffundebantur: Vnde facile colligere licuit, per penetrationem luminis cuiusdam externi, quocunque id modo contingeret, et vndecunque proueniret, caudam illam fuisse procreatam. Idem quoque in alijs Cometis animaduertere licuit, et eo euidentius, quo maiores illustrioresque fulserint. Praeterea illi qui ad oppositum Solis tendere visi sunt, caudam vel nullam, vel perexiguam ostendebant, eam potius versus superiora auertentes. Huic autem proximiores barbam magis magisque exerere cernebantur, quod in Cometa Anni 80. et 82. discrete apparuit, vt de eo, qui Anno 85. omnis caudae expers putabatur, quem vterque nostrum, et nullus, quod sciam alius, diligenter obseruauit, multa non dicam. Hunc, caudam ob id nullam ostendisse censeo, quod perpetuo iuxta Solis oppositum moueretur, nimia etiam a nobis remotione huc aliquid faciente. Si igitur cauda eorum pars aliqua corporis esset, et de eadem materia constaret, nequaquam eiusmodi relationem ad Solem conseruaret. Nec enim, quae de transitu radiorum in puro Aëre sine materia obijcis, satis adhuc perspecta sunt. Etsi enim Caelum tenuissimum sit, tamen non prorsus est immateriale vel incorporeum. Quid diceres ad hoc, quod Aristoteles refert capite 6. libro 1. Meteorologicorum, nonnullas ex vagis Stellis crinem aliquando nancisci, quod Aegyptios tradidisse innuit, quibus etiam fidem adhibendam censet, siquidem ipsemet viderit Stellam quandam in Caniculae femore, crinem licet obscurum habuisse, ita vt qui in eam oculorum aciem intendebant, obscurum lumen cernerent, si vero qui leuiter intuerentur, clarius. Ego vero existimo rem ita se habere, et saepius hoc etiam in pluribus Stellis in Aegypto, vel alio consimili loco, vbi Aër minimis vaporibus est obnoxius, quando praesertim purissimus existit, conspici: Nobis autem in crassiore Aëre habitantibus non item. Sunt enim Radij illi admodum tenues, nec oculis patent, si Diaphanum intermedium minus defaecatum fuerit.

To the most distinguished and learned man, Master Doctor Christoph Rothmann, eminent mathematician to the Most Illustrious Prince Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, etc., his most dearly beloved friend — heartiest greeting. Since meanwhile, from the time I received your last letter, most learned and most loving Rothmann, no opportunity to write back to you was afforded, owing to the lack of couriers, I was forced to defer my reply until this very time. But when one of those men who travel each year to the Frankfurt fair had these days had it made known to me that he was shortly going off thither, I thought it not to be passed over without at least replying something to your letters — even though at this season especially I am pulled apart by very many occupations, so that scarcely any leisure is left over for writing back.

As for the earlier part of your letter, in which copiously and persistently you contend that the matter of the heaven is elemental air, and do not allow yourself to be moved by any reasonings from the opinion once seized upon, and try to elude by specious subterfuges the things rightly said to the contrary — to these I have not resolved to reply much, point by point. Indeed for my part you may be free to mingle the element of air with the heaven, if you will have it no otherwise. Nay, if anyone presume to affirm the heaven to be watery, as the Hebrews [do]; or fiery, as the Paracelsians; or even earthy (provided it so please him); or something compounded of these four — as Franciscus Vallesius lately ventured — that too will be no concern of mine, provided that to me also it be permitted freely to put forward what I think: namely, that the substance of the heaven is ethereal and most fluid, and a certain most pure matter, exalted above all the nature of the elements, and brought to a certain singular perfection, like a kind of fifth essence, subject to no change or alteration, and one that naturally does not receive foreign impressions. But if to ascribe to the heaven the nature of some element should not seem altogether absurd, I would rather concede it to be fiery than airy, as is handed down by Paracelsus, who calls it the fourth and fiery element. For since the stars and heavenly lights appear fiery and flaming, and it is fitting that any given bodies should dwell in an Iliad analogous to their own nature — just as we see in this lower nature of things: living creatures that consist of the mumia of earth dwell on the earth; birds of the mumia of air [dwell] in the air; fishes of the mumia of water dwell in the waters, and so of the rest — so that for this reason it appear the more probable that the Sun and the stars, which present the appearance of a certain incombustible fire, exercise their revolutions in a fiery and incombustible ether (whence indeed the name of aether was attributed to it among the Greeks, as it were "burning" or "fiery," and that perpetually, since it is unconsumable). And yet not even this opinion, however more rational it be than the supposition of air, is sufficiently proved to me. For I judge the heaven to have the same relation to the elemental world that the soul has to the body. Whoever, therefore, shall be able fully to express what the soul is in bodies, and how it differs from the body itself, he too will more rightly unfold the natures and distinctions of the celestial and the elemental world. Let someone tell me besides out of what matter the Sun, Moon, and all the rest of the heavenly bodies exist; then at last I shall the more easily lend an ear to him discoursing of the substance of the heaven in which these things dwell. But if it shall not be granted to explain the nature and composition of the contained, then much less [that] of the container. I judge, however, that all the bodies that revolve in the heaven consist of celestial and ethereal matter, of what sort this is can scarcely be expressed. Yet it must be above every graduated elemental quality, just as those things that are composed of the elements are contained in the elemental world. But if it were granted by all that all bodies — both those in heaven and earth and the rest of the elements — consist of those three principles which Paracelsus in the foregoing age brought into the light, no less observed and approved by the most ancient philosophers, both before Aristotle and after, [these principles] representing the mystery of the Trinity in God the Maker, as it were in a certain image, throughout each of his creatures: it will nonetheless be necessary here to understand in celestial things a far different purification, and one [carried] to a special perfection and to the highest transparency and subtlety, an exaltation [greater] than in these sublunary things — so much so that it be found free of every blemish and defect, by the inscrutable wisdom of the Maker, who so disposes all things and perpetually preserves them. But on this intricate question — which can scarcely be sufficiently unraveled in this fleeting life, and which is also less necessary to the restoration [redintegratio] of astronomy — I do not wish to stir up any further dispute, nor to reply in detail to your objections, which in itself would not be difficult; and this, if you press further, I shall on another occasion do not grudgingly. Now, studying brevity and entangled in other things, I willingly pass over most matters in silence, especially since I see that I shall not persuade you that the matter stands otherwise than a preoccupied opinion holds. Yet one thing at least I shall set against your chief argument, by which you try to establish that the heaven and elemental air are wholly one and the same, from the fact that refractions do not reach to the zenith but become imperceptible beyond half the altitude-quadrant; inasmuch as this proves the transparent media of air and of ether not to be diverse, and consequently that both possess the same matter and substance. Here you seem to me to bring in more than that assumption sustains. For although it be granted that no refraction is observed in the Sun or stars at some given altitude, it indeed follows well enough from this that the discrepancy of the transparent media is either none or imperceptible; but it is not on that account necessarily inferred that there exists in both transparent media exactly the same quality and substance — for diverse substances and qualities can be open to the same transparency. I shall give an example from lower things, which can be handled with the hands and discerned by sight. If we render rain-water, or especially snow-water, or even common spring-water, most thoroughly clarified by often-repeated elevation [distillation], and the same be done in any noble wine, until it be freed from all phlegm and impurity, and both these liquors be received in a similar crystalline [vessel]: assuredly the transparency of the one will not differ from the other, so far as may be discerned by any senses, and therefore of the refractions, if perchance any should arise from them, there will be no difference; and yet the substance and nature of the one liquid and body will be found most diverse from the other. For that which is drawn off from the wine will smack of a fiery and sulphureous quality, so much so that when kindled it is wholly swallowed up in flames, leaving no trace of itself behind; whereas that which consists of the water will remain watery, and so far is it from burning that it even extinguishes fire more quickly than before, and resists it with a contrary force. So too the transparencies of the heaven and of the air, even if they were wholly the same — which however has not yet been sufficiently attained — nothing nonetheless hinders their having a diverse substance and matter. I do not wish now to heap up more on these matters, for this question does not properly concern the astronomer, as I have warned before, nor does it bring any aid, as you suppose, to the generation and explanation of comets, if the heaven be supposed to be airy. Nay, this would rather square [with the facts] if we conceded to Paracelsus that it is fiery; for comets present not an airy but rather a certain fiery nature. For in that you suppose that the procreation of comets can be saved by terrestrial fumes raised even into the highest air — which you would have contained within the very revolutions of the heavenly bodies — and there illuminated, you labor in vain to establish one absurdity from another and from an incongruous assumption, as I have resolved to show elsewhere, when I treat of these matters expressly, so evidently that no one can justly gainsay it.

As for the twilights, you report that you observed the evening one there, near the equinoxes, when the Sun was depressed 24 degrees below the horizon, whence you greatly wonder that I have found the same to be scarcely 17 degrees in the morning twilight. I therefore repeated, these very days, the experience of this matter, that your doubts might be satisfied — and that indeed with the Sun not yet advanced to the vernal equinox, but still dwelling near the beginning of Pisces. The proportion, however, is the same, nor does a great difference fall out at this place from the beginnings of Aries. I found, then, on the 10th of February last past, that the extreme limit of the evening twilight was when the middle of the Belt of Orion held the meridian. And therefore the Sun, which was then in 2¼ degrees of ♓ Pisces, had at most sunk 17 degrees below the horizon, toward the nadir. Afterward, on the 12th in the morning, the first dawn was observed here, when Spica of ♍ Virgo was distant from the meridian toward the west 35½ degrees of the equator. Therefore, the Sun then being in 3⅔ degrees of ♓ Pisces, it could not be more depressed under the horizon, in that circle which tends toward the nadir, than 16½ degrees. On the same day I again carefully noted the evening twilight too, at that very point of time when the right Shoulder of Orion was discerned before the meridian, toward the rising, by 1 degree 55 minutes. Therefore the arc of the twilight, taken as before, comes out at 16⅚ degrees, for the Sun was in 4¼ degrees of ♓ Pisces. Not otherwise, on the 15th of February, the last trace of day appeared when the same brighter star of Orion's Shoulder had passed the meridian by 1½ degrees. And therefore the Sun, which occupied 7¼ degrees of ♓ Pisces, lay hidden below the horizon a very little beyond 16 degrees. You see, therefore, that the measure of the twilight, whether morning or evening, in the vertical circle, nowhere exceeds 17 degrees — which forestalls your observation by too great an interval. And hence it is plain how little valid are the things you put forward not only about the twilights but also about the refractions, which you suppose to stand otherwise among you than they do here. What I touched briefly in my later letters, and only somewhat indicated, concerning a truer and more secret philosophy than that which is hitherto commonly taught in the schools, I see pleases you. More therefore — both of the things you desire and also others far more excellent than these — you will find, in the writings of those philosophers whom I have enumerated, by diligently applied attention; and especially with experience herself, the mistress of things, exploring all, yet with a careful inquiry into causes according to nature herself first set before [you]. It is not now free to me to philosophize at greater length about these things. For it is neither the place for it, nor is it committed to letters safely enough.

I gladly hear that the second volume of our work, On the more recent phenomena of the heaven, was welcome to you. And it is gratifying to me that the maximum latitude of the Moon was found by you nearly the same as I indicate there in chapter 3. There wrote to me not so long ago the most distinguished and learned man Master Heinrich Brucaeus, professor among the Rostockers of Galenic medicine and Aristotelian philosophy, especially celebrated, and also exceedingly skilled in mathematical matters, especially geometrical ones; and he called into doubt this assertion of mine concerning the changed latitude of the Moon, and set against [it] the authority of Regiomontanus, Copernicus, Werner, and similar eminent mathematicians, [arguing] that they had never detected any such thing, and because it would stand in the way of the magnitude of eclipses, which otherwise corresponds well enough to calculation. But I answered him that the authorities of these excellent astronomers detract nothing from my findings, inasmuch as they wholly acquiesced, in this part, in the Ptolemaic arrangement, nor did they themselves institute any sure experimentation [to test] whether the matter held thus to a nicety or not — although authorities in mathematics, if anywhere, have least weight of themselves, unless there be added an evident demonstration founded on sure observations. And I indicated that in eclipses this difference is scarcely perceptible, owing to the proximity of the nodes. Then in turn I cast back at him your observations too, by which you assert that you have detected the same [thing] almost as I [have]. I therefore desire to learn further from you in what manner you instituted this investigation, and what, and how much, you observed to be changed in the latitude of the Moon — so that to him and to others, not acquiescing in the findings of one [man], I may further show that the suffrage of another also, who diligently inspects the heaven together [with me] (which is too carelessly neglected by those men, although they wish to be held astronomers), makes for me. Although I have resolved to draw forth from my observations, and to publish, nothing that is not of itself sufficiently sure and explored, and that needs the suffrage of no other.

But as for what you say — that the Head of the Dragon [the ascending node] is distant from the tables by more than four degrees, and that you have found this by distances and by the globe — I could wish that you would indicate more fully how you gathered it; for this seems to me, with the apparent magnitude of eclipses safe, together with their time, by no means consistent. Inasmuch as a difference of four degrees from the nodes would alter the latitude of the Moon by 21 minutes — that is, by nearly two-thirds of the lunar body — which would surely induce a quite perceptible discrepancy in the appearances of eclipses; such as, however, in many eclipses, not only lunar but also solar, which I have diligently noted for several years back, I have not been able to observe. For although the times of eclipses, by whatever calculation hitherto in use, have not duly agreed with the heaven itself, but have sometimes anticipated [them] by a sensible difference, at times greater, at times smaller, or have shown themselves later, yet as to the form of the magnitudes I have never found so evident a diversity as your alterations of the nodes require. For not much more discrepancy could be known in this part than such as the aberration in the motion of the luminaries — and so in the time of the syzygies — was able to suggest, the nodes themselves remaining as nearly as possible in their place, where the Alphonsine and Copernican tables (which differ very little in these) put them. I therefore ask that you declare further, after a more diligent examination has been made, why you so determine. For it is not seemly to charge the tables, without cause, with so evident an error in the intersections of the ecliptic and of the lunar path.

You report, moreover, that in the book sent [you] you found nothing of what I think about the matter of comets and their generation, and you assert this rightly indeed. For nowhere there do I treat of this matter expressly; but I refer this and most similar questions, and many other things, both to other places and also to the very last clause of the book, into the Epilogue of the whole work. In the meantime, however, on page 254, where I weigh the fourth chapter of Maestlin's writing on this comet, you will find these words: "I judge the matter of all comets to be wholly celestial, inasmuch as all are generated even in the heaven itself. But whence this matter is supplied to them in the ethereal region, and how they are procreated there, [I shall set out] in its place, in the Conclusion of the whole work, where I shall expound my opinion." From these you could in some way have gathered what I judge concerning their matter — namely that it is not elemental, nor compounded out of terrestrial vapors, as you think. But when, God willing, the whole work has been brought to its colophon, then I shall discuss the opinion both of the ancient philosophers and also of the moderns — among whom also your opinion on the matter and generation of comets — and I shall give assurance, by clear and sure reasons, that it can by no means really stand. Meanwhile let the things I deliver and demonstrate concerning the motion and appearances of such phenomena suffice; for I judged that the physical consideration, which thence is for the most part to be derived, should not be confusedly mixed in, that the due method might be preserved. But after I shall have called back to the anvil of truth, through their appearances, not only the new star (which also brings no small aid to investigating this business more deeply), but all the comets diligently observed by me — employing also certain others previously seen, wherever the matter requires it — then at last it will be consequent to subjoin the physical and astrological consideration, with a certain general indication. For this matter is not of small moment, but has been duly treated before by very few, not to say by absolutely none. And therefore it must be gone about not perfunctorily or promiscuously, but in seemly order and with due diligence.

As for the drawing-out of the tail, I showed that in the comet of the year 1577 it always regarded the star of Venus, although afterward I rather inclined to the parts of the Sun, on account of some hidden optical reason, since it can scarcely be believed that the star of Venus shoots forth such strong rays; though now at last I can to some extent perceive that this is not so foreign to the star of Venus, as I then suspected, since the comet directed its course not far from its [Venus's] revolutions. For the star of Venus seemed to me sometimes to illuminate, quite sensibly, that part of the Moon which was averted from the Sun and turned toward it [Venus], although the Moon stood far more remote from the circuits of Venus than the comet [did]. I find that Giovanni Battista Benedetti, a Venetian patrician, philosopher and especially excellent mathematician, observed something similar, in that learned work which he entitled On mathematical and physical speculations. For thus, at the end of a letter, writing to a certain Savoyard baron, Philibert, he says: "It manifestly appears, when the Moon is found, according to longitude, between the Sun and Venus, that the part of the Moon destitute of the Sun's light is somewhat illuminated by the light of Venus — which I have often seen, and have shown to many." Thus he. Yet, to confess the truth, neither does this reasoning so satisfy me that I would dare to attribute to Venus so great a power of rays as could have procreated the comet's tail. Be it true, as to the reason of the tail, whatever you will, and whether there lurk in these things something optical — why they do not always regard the Sun exactly — or not: certainly that which you intend can by no means subsist, namely that the tail is a part of the comet's body, and of the same substance as the head, and appears in the same manner by illumination. For if this held altogether thus, so many comets observed by Regiomontanus, Fracastoro, Apian, Gemma Frisius, and others would not always have turned their coma to the parts nearly opposite the Sun, as one may gather from their writings; which was the case very nearly also in those that have been observed by us. For there is no reason why the protraction of the comet's body lengthwise, if it were of the same substance as the head itself, should always be turned away well-nigh from the Sun. And we saw in the hairy [star] of the year [15]77, whose tail, especially at the beginning, was very notable, that it was exceedingly rare [thin], and appeared after the manner of thin rays, so much so that certain stars which it passed by sometimes shone through it. Nor was it of the same color as the head, but it kept altering this too — which however I judge happened on account of the diverse quality of the interposed air, especially near the horizon. But the head itself was solidly compacted, and far brighter than the tail, and was seen exactly round, so much so that at the beginning you would have called it a little Moon. The hairs themselves too were joined to the head in a point, and afterward were diffused broad and long; whence one could easily gather that that tail was procreated by the penetration of some external light, in whatever manner it happened, and from wherever it came. The same too could be observed in other comets, and the more evidently the greater and more brilliant they shone. Besides, those that seemed to tend toward the opposite of the Sun showed a tail either none or very small, turning it rather toward the upper parts. But those nearer to this [the Sun] were discerned to put forth a beard more and more — which appeared distinctly in the comet of the year [15]80 and [15]82; so that of that one which in the year [15]85 was thought to be wholly devoid of a tail — which each of us, and no other, that I know, diligently observed — I shall not say much. This one, I judge, showed no tail for the reason that it moved perpetually near the opposite of the Sun, the excessive remoteness from us also contributing somewhat here. If, therefore, the tail were some part of their body, and consisted of the same matter, it would by no means preserve such a relation to the Sun. Nor indeed are the things you object concerning the passage of rays in pure air without matter sufficiently seen into as yet. For although the heaven be most tenuous, yet it is not altogether immaterial or incorporeal. What would you say to this, that Aristotle reports in chapter 6 of book 1 of the Meteorologica, that some of the wandering stars sometimes acquire a coma — which he hints the Egyptians handed down, to whom too he thinks credit should be given, inasmuch as he himself saw a certain star in the thigh of the Dog-star [Canicula] to have had a coma, albeit obscure, in such a way that those who fixed the keenness of their eyes upon it saw an obscure light, but those who looked lightly [casually] saw it more clearly. I, for my part, judge that the matter holds thus, and that this is often seen in several stars too, in Egypt or another similar place where the air is liable to the least vapors — when especially it is at its purest; but not so by us, dwelling in a grosser air. For those rays are exceedingly tenuous, nor are they open to the eyes, if the intermediate transparent medium be less clarified.

Yet, to confirm your opinion from some celestial observation, you object to us an observation of your Most Illustrious Prince, made by means of the torquetum, in the comet of the year 1558; from which you asseverate that it is manifestly gathered that its tail regarded neither the Sun nor any other of the planets, but appeared of itself, by its own course. I confess indeed that, if that record which was had on the 13th of the Kalends of September [20 August] — the only day on which the course of the tail is assigned — is to be taken as valid, then, the arc of a great circle being set, according to our reckonings, from the Sun in 6 degrees 36 minutes of ♍ Virgo, drawn out through the comet's head toward the last [star] in the tail of Ursa Major, does not approach it within 10½ degrees, but deflects just as much from this toward Boötes; so much so that the Sun would have had to have traversed the 21st degree of ♍ Virgo, if it, and the head and tail of the comet, together with the said star, were set in the same arc. But although not even here does so utterly immoderate a misshapen deviation occur — given what could be discerned at first glance from sight alone — that sufficient occasion is thereby afforded to conclude from this [something] plainly diverse from the observation of very many in other comets: yet, because this difference seems to you excessive, as it is in itself quite ample, I shall adduce something else to the contrary about this same comet, from the observations of the most distinguished and learned man Master Cornelius Gemma, who in the second book On the divine characterisms of nature, chapter one, where he proposes some things about this very [comet], besides that he says it stood in the west and stretched its tail toward the eastern region (and therefore to the parts opposite the Sun, which had already set), chiefly records this observation: that on the 20th of August — which is plainly the same as that on which the Most Illustrious Prince designated the comet together with the course of the tail toward the last [star] of Ursa Major — in the evening he measured its distance from Arcturus at 30 degrees 32 minutes, and from that very [star] which is at the end of the tail of Ursa Major at 28 degrees 33 minutes. And since for that year the longitude of Arcturus was in 18 degrees 4 minutes of ♎ Libra, latitude 31 degrees 2 minutes north, and [that] of the [star] which is in the tail of the Bear [in] 20 degrees 44 minutes of ♍ Virgo, latitude 54 degrees 25 minutes north, according to our restitution of the places of the fixed stars: it will follow, from the assigned intervals, that the place of the comet fell, as to longitude, in 13 degrees 36 minutes of ♍ Virgo, with a latitude of 26 degrees 23 minutes north — which differs quite sensibly from the annotation which you adduce. But if, through this place, the great arc be drawn from the Sun dwelling in 6 degrees 36 minutes of ♍ Virgo, as I said, toward the last [star] of the Bear's tail, it will approach near enough to that star, so that it really comes within 3¼ degrees of it, digressing only a very little toward Boötes; so much so that if the Sun's place be assumed only three degrees further on, namely in 9⅔ of ♍ Virgo, the head of the comet, the drawing-out of the tail, and the last [star] in Ursa Major coincide exactly in one arc of a great circle. But this not-so-great discrepancy easily merits excuse among those who understand this business. Inasmuch as the course of the tail was at least noted by sight, which in so great a distance could easily insinuate an aberration of three degrees. For supreme precision is not elicited in this way, and it is likely that the Most Illustrious Prince, since no other clear star occurred so near by which the extension of the tail might be designated, seized this one as the nearest, from which too the indication of the tail differed imperceptibly, so far as could be discerned by sight. It appears, therefore, how little aid you obtain from this for establishing your opinion concerning the beards of comets and overturning that of others, which has been confirmed by many examples — since rather the contrary to your induction follows from this. For that the tail of this comet too observed [faced] the parts opposite the Sun fairly closely, those weighing the whole matter circumspectly, without prejudice, will not unwillingly concede. Make at least the trial yourself on the globe, and mark the place of the comet according to the distances measured by Cornelius Gemma from the said fixed stars, and draw out its tail somewhat from the body into the parts contrary to the Sun placed in 6⅗ of ♍ Virgo, and the face of the heaven being disposed such as it was near the ninth hour of that day, so that 21 degrees of ♏ Scorpio occupies the meridian; and the globe being thus fixed, look to where the tail is directed at first glance: you will assuredly see it tend as it were toward the zenith, and, if it were understood to be produced [extended], to meet no notable star nearer than that which holds the last place in the tail of Ursa Major. For although there is a certain very small one in the left arm of Boötes, which it more nearly regarded, yet since this is at least of the fifth magnitude, it could not be discerned. And since no other in that tract lay evidently open to the eyes, toward which the protension of the tail might be more nearly directed than this very oft-mentioned star of Ursa Major, the Most Illustrious Prince justly seized this one as indicated by the tail — which ocular sight too sufficiently proves — neglecting that very small difference which did not even readily lie open to sight alone. But that one ought rather to stand by the place of the comet which is derived from Cornelius's observations admits no doubt. For he, in the very scruples [minutes], by means of the cross-staff [radius astronomicus] (an instrument diligently perfected by his father), subtly measured the distances of the comet from two more conspicuous fixed stars, which with it constituted a nearly equilateral triangle (which makes not a little toward inquiring after the certainty of the place from this). And although the cross-staff of itself does not everywhere afford sufficiently precise distances within a few minutes, especially in the larger intervals, yet its aberration is not so great as to dispose the comet here otherwise than it ought by more than a quarter of a degree — which variation in no way disproves what we intend. But the matter stands far otherwise with the torquetum, by which instrument the observations of the Most Illustrious Prince were taken. For even if it were of just size, yet it is intricately composed of too many and diverse parts, and many things are required for the disposition of the several ones, which during observation are with difficulty managed at once, so much so that while one is guarded against, another is easily neglected; and any one of them by itself can suggest some sensible error. I say nothing of the fact that the instrument itself too too much burdens itself by its own mass, since its dispositions are not adapted to its turning according to equilibrium. So that there is none among all the astronomical instruments devised by the ancients which exhibits to the observer a less ready and assured certainty — however ingeniously its structure [be devised], by which the Arabs, the first inventors (as I think) of this, tried to reduce the armillae used by Hipparchus and Ptolemy into a certain flat framework of circles, more compendiously and at less cost. I now say nothing of the fact that the Most Illustrious Prince's torquetum, by which those observations were performed — although it was made of solid brass — was nonetheless too small, as I saw, when fifteen years ago I tarried in person for some days with the Most Illustrious Prince at Kassel. For he then had two such torqueta, neither of which presented a sufficiently capacious size, and both indeed had long since been antiquated by His Highness, because they by no means exhibited sure markings of the heavenly bodies, the Prince himself even hinting as much. It therefore easily happened, observing through a torquetum not so large, that a deviation of several degrees from the mark was committed — which by the benefit of the cross-staff, with the investigation sought from the distances of the stars, could safely enough be guarded against; and the tail, which of itself appeared thin, as one may gather from the very observations of the Most Illustrious Prince, in its extension not with difficulty, as to its long interval, suggested some frustration to the sight. Wherefore, if from observations of comets you labor to prove that their tail is of the very substance of the body, and is borne of itself, and does not always regard the Sun, it will be necessary that you implore aid from elsewhere; for this comet, which you allege, makes much rather against you than for you, as has now been shown enough and more.

Now to other matters. The things you object to our reasonings concerning the curvature of the tail make little to the point. If you can find a fitter occasion for the bending of the hairs than [one] from optics, bring it forward. That the tail is, however, the comet's own portion, existing of the same matter, so that for this reason it can of itself exhibit various forms — this I shall by no means have conceded to you. Not otherwise do you less aptly apply the things I wrote about Graminaeus at the end of the work. For I did not say that he, as to the situation of comets, adheres to the Aristotelian opinion, but only that, as to matter, [it] consists of terrestrial fumes, as he [Aristotle] will have it, and [is] drawn up into the lofty ether. But since you yourself confess this to be your opinion too, you assuredly slip into the same absurdities which I shall amply show elsewhere; for it makes no difference whether you call those vapors slow and tenacious, or thin and light, or burning or illuminated, provided you admit them to be terrestrial and sublunary, and raised in whatever manner into the highest heaven. Moreover, since from certain words written to the Most Illustrious Prince — by which I named my work to be published On the new generations of the ethereal world — you gather that I introduce generation into the heaven, and consequently corruption too: you seem here to catch at my words too strictly. For I there took the word "generation" in a broader sense, for that which newly exists or appears, not seen before, nor did I sustain the part of the grammarian in the propriety of that expression. But why did you not rather seize on the printed title of the book itself, which I inscribed more circumspectly than is required in any letter, On the new phenomena of the ethereal world, deliberately avoiding the word "generation," that there might be no place for cavil? Elsewhere you will see yourself to be still far from my opinion.

Concerning the constitution of the celestial hypotheses devised by me, I do not wish at present to bring much forward by way of defense and confirmation; for that would be too prolix, nor would it be sufficiently accomplished without a manifold demonstration sought from sure observations. I shall rather give my effort, that on another occasion, when I am about to treat expressly of the restitution of the celestial motions — the Author of the heaven favoring [me] — it may be known in fact that these our hypotheses satisfy the celestial appearances to a nicety, and far excel both the Ptolemaic and the Copernican, and correspond better to truth itself. But if those [hypotheses] so displease you that you prefer, with Copernicus, to revolve the earth and seas in the heaven yearly together with the neighboring Moon, and so to exalt [the earth] with a threefold motion — though it be one and simple body — beyond the lot of the stars (than which it is nonetheless far inferior and more unfit for moving): for my part that too will be open and entire to you. Nor is the way now difficult, since you have mingled elemental air with the heaven and the revolutions of the stars; why not the earth and seas too? Is this not to confound the lower regions with the upper, and to invert the whole order of the nature of things? Yet, lest you be deceived: there is no [reason] that you should persuade yourself that the physical absurdities which accompany the Copernican hypothesis have been sufficiently refuted by him; nor do the things adduced by you hither confirm the whole matter beyond doubt, as I shall show elsewhere, discussing these and many more of the kind. Much less will the things put forward by you in excusing those passages in which Sacred Scripture asserts the contrary be able to merit a place. For the authority and reverence of the divine letters both is and ought to be greater than that it should be seemly for them to be dragged this way, after the manner of a buskin [cothurnus]. For although they themselves, in physical matters and certain others, for the most part accommodate themselves to the grasp of the common crowd, yet far be it that on that account we should hold them to speak so vulgarly [that we should not] also believe them to put forward true things. Thus Moses, although in the first chapter of Genesis, treating of the creation of the world, he does not unlock the inner shrines of astronomy — writing as he was for an uncultivated people — nonetheless brings forward nothing that could not be conceded even by the astronomers themselves. For what you think follows from him — that the Moon, contrary to geometrical demonstrations, would be larger than the rest of the stars — the Text itself does not admit. For it has it thus: "And God made two great lights, the greater for the ruling of the day, the lesser for [that] of the night, and the stars too, and he placed them in the fluid heaven to illuminate the lands." Here Moses speaks at least about the magnitude of the light by which the Sun and Moon illuminate the lands beyond the other stars, not about the quantity of bodies. And therefore he calls the Moon the lesser light, because it does not illuminate the earth in the nocturnal time so much as the Sun in the diurnal. For if he had understood these things of the magnitude of the body, and had accommodated himself at least to the common crowd's view, he would not have called the Moon less than the Sun; inasmuch as its visible diameter is certainly discerned scarcely less than the Sun's, if not rather, for the most part, somewhat larger. But no astronomer would deny that the Moon, after the Sun, is the great luminary before the other heavenly bodies, and that it illuminates the earth by night far more than the rest, and dispels the darkness. Thus the astrologers themselves call the Sun and Moon — the latter the luminary of the night, the former of the day — which agrees exceedingly well with Moses. About the other stars I do not now dispute, whether round about, near their boundaries, they pour out more brightness than the Moon itself, although on account of the excessive distance that light does not strike the earth. For Moses too in that place was treating at least of this light which touches the lands and serves the use of living creatures. So too you detract too much from the Prophets, when you say that they understood no more of the nature of things than other common men. For although they did not treat physics expressly — for that was not [part] of their office — yet they mingle into their oracles many things drawn from physics, which no one, however much imbued with natural philosophy, will be able to gainsay. Nor does it follow from this that they were ignorant of astronomy and physics, which flowed down to them even from the first patriarchs, because they treat of these things more sparingly. For it was not seemly to mingle philosophical and worldly things too much with the theological mysteries which they chiefly treated. The authority of the sacred letters therefore still stands unmoved, as we shall see more fully in its place. Nor will the position of Copernicus be vindicated from this by any effort of yours.

But as to what you object to my invention of the hypotheses — a certain violent dragging, and a confusion of order, and a blemish of retrogradation in the five planets — we do not delay over it. For it is consonant [with reason] that it is not a certain involuntary dragging, but a natural and divinely implanted concomitance and observance, by which the five planets always regard the Sun, wherever it go, in the middle [meditullium] of their revolutions. And by what corporeal and tenacious matter, I ask, are the magnet and iron coupled, so that the one seeks the other, even with another body interposed? If this can happen naturally, without violence, in terrestrial inanimate things, what [shall we say] of celestial things, which the Platonists and the wiser of the philosophizers would not deny to be animate and to have a divinely innate knowledge, wonderful and more excellent than is thought? Weigh more diligently those things which are adduced by Pliny at the end of the 16th chapter of book 2, concerning the reason of the stations and regressions of the three superior planets — which, although they seem ambiguous and obscure or altogether absurd, yet are not all plainly about nothing. And it is sufficiently gathered hence that the most ancient mathematicians, who also set the earth resting in the middle of the world, were wholly ignorant of epicycles, and by some other more occult reasoning referred those appearances, which are thence saved, to the Sun; nor did they judge it inconvenient that the planets be so subject to the Sun, although no material connection intervened. But the Text itself will admonish you of more. Nor is any confusion committed in my hypotheses; for the heaven is single and like to itself from the Moon to the eighth sphere, in which the planets freely ascend and descend, as they go around the Sun walking in the middle in a most beautiful harmony. For I do not believe that you wish again to introduce some real distinction of the celestial orbs, contrary to what was long ago agreed between us. The retrogradation of the planets, which is discerned with respect to us and to the eighth sphere, afflicts them with no fault, inasmuch as, with respect to themselves, they always regard the Sun in the same manner, uniformly intent on their destined course. But these things, and many others which can perhaps be plausibly inferred by some against our reckoning of the motions, I shall in their time, God breathing upon our endeavors, discuss sufficiently.

But I did not take the occasion of constructing these hypotheses from the Copernican [ones] inverted; and if you ever thought any such thing, that, as you well enough know, was not made known to me, nor was anything similar ever to be gathered from Rheticus or Reinhold. But when I had observed, by subtle and accurate observation — especially that had in the year [15]82 — that Mars at acronychal [rising, in opposition to the Sun] becomes nearer to the earth than the Sun itself, and that on that account the long-received Ptolemaic hypotheses cannot stand; and moreover that comets seen in the heaven and dwelling opposite the Sun are not rendered subject to the earth's annual motion (although they are not so far distant that it [the motion] plainly vanishes, as happens in the fixed stars), [and] that the Copernican assumption too in the earth's motion totters: there remained no other way of saving the celestial appearances than this very one which has lately been put forward by me with a general indication. If, therefore, I shall have demonstrated those two [points] to hold thus, then assuredly it will be sufficiently confirmed that neither the Ptolemaic nor the Copernican hypothesis agrees with the truth of the matter, but this our [hypothesis] alone — [to say] nothing now of many other things which make not a little hither. For it does not seem convenient to report particular and individual matters in this place.

I greatly wondered, at first, when I read in your letters that these my hypotheses had been previously known to the Most Illustrious Prince — inasmuch as he had them expressed in a certain automaton [model]. Nor could I sufficiently think how this could come about, since I was perfectly conscious to myself that I had learned these from no mortal before I myself was led thither from observations, and that meanwhile they had lain hidden with me, not previously published. But after the little book of that Ursus of Dithmarschen — whom you once, in certain letters given to me, adorned with a worthy eulogy, [now] calling [him] an impure scoundrel — had been sent to me (and that by the most learned man Master Georg Rollenhagen, dwelling at Magdeburg, who also had indicated long before that these our hypotheses had been communicated to him two years earlier by a certain fugitive servant of mine; and therefore, knowing them to have been first invented by me, and grieving that that Ursus undeservedly attributed them to himself, he wished that book to become known to me, so that I might recognize that theft and again claim for myself what had been cast out abroad by others): from that I sufficiently perceived whence this ordering of the celestial revolutions had reached you. Inasmuch as that plagiarist reports that, about three years ago, after he had devised this in a certain corner of the kingdom of Poland (understand rather on the island Hven of the kingdom of Denmark, when in the year [15]84, in September, he was present here with the most noble and learned man Master Erik Lange, my singular friend, whom he was then serving, [these things] secretly snatched from me, like certain others too), he offered [them] to the Most Illustrious Prince of Hesse, and they were reduced into a brazen automaton by His Highness's craftsman Jost Bürgi. But that impostor sufficiently betrays his theft, and along with it his ignorance of the matter which he peddles. For while he keeps the orb of Mars wholly encompassing [the orbit around] the Sun, he imitates a certain false delineation, which, by I know not what carelessness, was here so depicted, and on that account, as inept, thrown away somewhere among my papers — which I still have at hand, sought out under the same name, that the very thing might plainly appear. And since he could not amend that fault committed in depicting the diagram, he sufficiently makes known to the intelligent that he does not understand that invention, much less is its author — however he has not blushed to arrogate to himself what was got together here, impudently, and to dedicate it to the Most Illustrious Prince Wilhelm as his own contrivance. Besides, he has many other things in that little book, which, although he has not carried them all off from here, yet he has partly scraped together from you, partly from other mathematicians in Germany, whether secretly or openly; so much so that, if the things belonging to others be taken away, almost nothing remains that will be his. But let that fellow be off with his plagiarism — to you and other learned men, among whom he once was, [it is] sufficiently clear who he is, and how brazen-faced and arrogant a trifler. These things I wished to answer briefly concerning my hypotheses; more you will understand in a more convenient place and time, which without doubt (unless you have sworn too obstinately to the words and opinion of Copernicus) will satisfy you.

That I could have prefixed in every respect a more fitting hypothesis for the comet of the year [15]77 — namely, out of several composite circular motions — I myself had it well enough known, and this is sufficiently indicated in the book itself, and the reason added why I forbore to furnish it; whence there was no need of that replication. But to have proposed this very hypothesis which I indicated there is not without a certain utility, as you suppose; for thus it is the more confirmed that comets are really celestial, and that their motions are not stirred up tumultuously. You rightly excused yourself to the Most Illustrious Prince [in saying] that that second volume was not transmitted to His Highness but to you, and as it were in my name you promised that, when the whole work has been finished, I would communicate it entire to His Highness. For this I determined beforehand with myself, and there is no [reason] for you to doubt that your faith [pledge] in this part is to be redeemed. The remaining two princes too will have copies, as you advise. The Most Illustrious Maurice, the Landgrave's son — whom I rejoice to have advanced so much in letters and languages — would that he had also tasted astronomical matters, that he might thus also succeed his father, laudably practiced in these. Likewise the Most Illustrious Bernhard of Anhalt will not be to be passed over, since he is imbued with the sacred [things] of mathematics. But that the first volume did not come out at this fair — the impediment was that lately I did not obtain the printing-paper for which I had sent, the excessive abundance of waters delaying the messenger, and on account of certain other obstacles which it is not pertinent to relate. But as for those things which you judge to make for you concerning your observation in ♀ Venus and ☉ the Sun, made for the verification of the fixed stars, and what I think about this matter, I have answered briefly in a letter given to the Most Illustrious Prince; wherefore it is not worth the trouble to repeat them here. Nor is the opportunity granted me of writing more now — nay, I have scratched out these very things in haste, not only [jotting them down] among many other occupations as they came to mind in passing, but moreover laboring under a heavy pain of the forehead and of the half-head [hemicrania], on account of a head-cold which a few days before I had contracted from the nocturnal chill while observing, [and which was] prematurely stopped up [congested] — so that I have conceived these very things with the greatest difficulty. For there is not so much leisure left over that it is free [to me] to attend to medication. It will therefore be yours, in these difficulties, candidly to interpret things heaped together less neatly, and, if leisure and health did not allow [me] to reply now to everything point by point, to take in good part these things written back in a general way, and not to cease loving me, as you do, and more often, as opportunity is offered, to revisit [me] with letters. Farewell. Given at Uraniborg, 21 February, the year 1589. Tycho Brahe.

Christophorus Rothmannus = Christoph Rothmann, mathematician/astronomer at the Hesse-Kassel court of Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse (Illustrissimus Princeps Wilhelmus Landtgravius Hassiae). 2 defects found:
- mistranslation (low). "capit. 3." is almost certainly an abbreviated chapter citation ("capite 3" = chapter 3) to Tycho's own printed book, and "indico" is Tycho's own verb ("I indicate"); the English recasts the citation as an unexplained "indication [section] 3" and shifts the verb to a passive "it is taken", losing the citation form and Tycho's agency without changing the substantive claim. At: “the maximum latitude of the Moon was found by you nearly the same as it is taken there, indication [section] 3”.
- mistranslation (low). "Canicula" is the standard Latin name for Sirius/the Dog-star in Canis Major (the star Aristotle references, Meteorologica I.6); "the Lesser Dog" specifically denotes the separate constellation Canis Minor (Procyon), so the gloss misidentifies which star/constellation is meant, though it does not affect Tycho's substantive point about faint comae being visible only in very clear air. At: “he himself saw a certain star in the thigh of the Lesser Dog [Canicula] to have had a coma”.
