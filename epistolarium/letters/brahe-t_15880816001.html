Editorial note. A published letter — one of the set Tycho himself printed at his Uraniborg press in the Epistolae astronomicae (1596) — and the locus classicus, on Tycho's side, of the "five-minutes controversy": the Landgrave's mathematician Christoph Rothmann had made the fixed-star longitudes about 5′ greater than Tycho's, and Tycho here defends his own values at length, tracing Rothmann's excess to refraction in the southern (low-altitude) half of the zodiac and to the reliance on the Sun/Venus too near the horizon. The text is clean (printed source) and preserves genuine early-modern v/u orthography (vt, vbi, varie, obseruatio), reproduced as printed. Two declared interventions in the source block: (1) the digitised text layer-intruded apparatus "A" sigla (footnote-anchors keying words to the apparatus) appended to some 25 words have been removed as non-textual (the same as in the Scaliger letter of 23 Aug 1598 and the undated Peucer letter of 1590); (2) the opening word's dittography "ILLVSTRISSIMOILLVSTRISSIMO" (the word transcribed twice) is reduced to a single ILLVSTRISSIMO (flagged). The zodiac glyph ♒ (Aquarius) is reproduced as printed. The dateline ("Vraniburgi, Die 16. Augusti, Anno 1588") is Old Style (Danish Julian); it agrees with the catalogue label. Cross-check: page-collated against Dreyer's Opera Omnia, vol. VI pp. 121–132. All cruxes confirmed: the 5′ longitude discrepancy (and "duodecima gradus portiuncula" = 5′); the 4-or-5′ refraction advancement and the 30°-altitude floor (p. 140); the reciprocal-Venus chronology 1582 / 1585 / 1587 / 1588 with the 17–18 Aug Venus–Jupiter conjunction sighted "from both heads of Gemini" (p. 143); the 5-year solar Ephemeris; the Copernicus/mid-Scorpio solar-apogee point (p. 140); and the dateline (p. 146). The flagged opening dittography is vindicated — Dreyer prints ILLVSTRISSIMO once (p. 135), so the reduction is correct.

ILLVSTRISSIMO PRINCIPI AC DOMINO D. WILHELMO HASSIÆ LANDTGRAVIO, COMITI IN CATZENELLEBOGEN, DIETZ, ZIEGENHAIN ET NIDDA: PRINCIPI AC DOMINO SUO CLEMENTISSIMO S. P.

QVOD tardius tam Tuæ Celsitudinis expectatione, quam mea ipsius voluntate ad literas quas circa Autumnale Æquinoctium antecedentis anni, Tua Celsitudo clementer ad me dedit, nunc respondeam, Illustriss. Generosissimeque Princeps, occasionem præbuerunt occupationes variæ et impedimenta quædam, quæ circa Veris initium inciderunt, effeceruntque quo minus per eos, qui ad proxime præteritas vernales Nundinas hinc profecti sunt, has ipsas quas tunc adornâram Tuæ Celsitud. transmittendas curare licuerit: accessit et hoc quod in Martio et Aprili plures in Venere interdiu apparente, et Occidentali a Sole, animaduersiones obtinere sperabam, vt eas cum prioribus matutinis, præsertim antecedente Anno in Augusto habitis, plenius conferrem, et quinorum istorum Scrupulorum, de quibus in Longitudinibus Fixarum constituendis inter nos controuersia est, dijudicationem ratam et vberiorem explorationem instituerem, quod etiam aliquoties diligenter a me præstitum est, veluti postea T. Cels. quid hinc eruerim adinuenerimque, plenius exponam. Submisse itaque tuam Celsitud. rogatam habeo, vt hanc in rescribendo dilationem, præsertim cum iam cum fænore resarciatur, candide et clementer excusatam habere dignetur. Scripsi etiam interea Tuæ Cels. Mathematico, eique moræ huius rationes, T. Cels. referendas exposui.

Ne itaque diutius id quod hactenus intermissum est differatur, nunc ijs, quæ Celsitud. Tuæ ad me missæ literæ continebant, ordine respondebo. Obseruationes meas T. Cels. communicatas perplacuisse, exactamque illam congruentiam inter Declinationes, et distantias Affixarum Stellarum Tuæ Cels. Instrumentis et meis investigatas, summam peperisse admirationem, ex ipso frontispicio T. Celsitud. literarum non sine voluptate percepi. Et sane, tam exquisita ratio Sidera scrutandi, vt in diuersis locis Obseruationes acceptæ tam præcise consentiant, hactenus insolita prorsusque inaudita mansit; vt rectissime etiam Tua Celsitudo suam de his sententiam pronunciat. Atque hinc liquido patet, Instrumenta nostra, quibus Altitudines et Distantiæ Stellarum capiuntur, vtrinque adamussim recte se habere, adeoque perfecte elaborata esse, vt nulli sensibili vitio sint obnoxia, præsertim postquam perpendiculi deuiatio illa minutula in Tuæ Celsitudinis Quadrante per Mathematicum est redintegrata. Plurimum itaque Astronomiæ instaurationi congratulor, quod ratio illa Pinnacidiorum et Diuisionis, structuræque exactæ Instrumentorum, quibus Siderum loca in ipso Scrupulo, adeoque eius aliquota parte, cælitus inuestigare licet, (quod tam veteribus quam modernis Astronomis hactenus insolens visum est) qua ego aliquot ab hinc Annis, non vti spero, sine fructu vsus sum, Tuæ etiam Celsitud. innotuerit, quodque a Tua Celsitud. approbata in praxin Astronomicam, magno huius laudatissimæ scientiæ emolumento et successu, deducatur; sine qua sane in Instrumentorum fabrica et Diuisione, ac Dioptrarum applicatione, inter nos conformitate, ad tam exactam Obseruationum conuenientiam, in qua etiam nihil ab ipsius cælestibus apparentijs aberrationis lateat, perueniendi omnis aliâs præclusa fuisset via. Quod autem nihilominus in Stellarum Longitudinibus quinis proxime Minutis differamus, quibus meæ anteriores inueniuntur, suam apud eos, qui huius subtilis inquisitionis perplexos labyrinthos satis habent perspectos, excusationem facile meretur. Ideoque recte Tua Celsit. asserit, hanc discrepantiam non adeo admiratione dignam; siquidem veteres illi Astronomiæ Principes, vix in sexta parte Gradus, veritatem in his assequuti sunt, neque etiam ad maiorem præcisionem, tanquam id minus foret possibile arbitrantes, aspirare tentârunt; quod tamen non tam ob Instrumentorum paruitatem, et Armillarum variam hinc inde inter obseruandum distractionem, illis euenisse iudico, quam quod per foramina dioptrica saltem Stellas obseruare didicerant; vbi nisi id quod in remotiori ab oculo Tabella constituitur, maiusculum fuerit, Stellæ etiam clariores ægerrime conspiciuntur; et si hoc detur, ob foraminis magnitudinem, quæ aliquotam Diuisionis Graduum partem occupat, in pauculis scrupulis, obseruatio exquisita constare minime poterit. Armillas vero Astrolabicas eorum iustæ fuisse magnitudinis, inde satis liquet, quod Hipparchus et Ptolemæus etiam Æquinoctiales ingressus per eas deprehenderint; et ipsemet Ptolemæus quasdam Armillas Alexandriæ constitutas, quæ nimium magnæ erant, tanquam minus idoneas reicere videtur. Possunt tamen adhibita peculiari diligentia, Armillæ etiam maiusculæ ita affabre componi, vt inter obseruandum, per suam Reuolutionem, aut circulorum disiunctionem, nihil vitiositatis insinuent; quamuis non exigua in his requiratur circumspectio, quam præcipua ex parte veteres etiam adhibuisse non dubito.

Equidem et ego per Armillas Siderum loca satis commode et præcise obseruari, multoties experior. In quaternis enim meis machinis Armillaribus, partim Æquinoctialibus, partim Zodiacalibus, quæ tantæ sunt magnitudinis, vt singula Minuta iuxta diuisionem nobis vsitatam, discriminatim exhibeant (quas tamen aptiori compendio, quam a veteribus factitatum est, construxi) quacunque earum vti libuerit, verus Stellæ locus in ipso Minuto exhibetur, prorsus cum eo congruens, qui vel per distantias beneficio Sextantum, vel etiam per Azimuthales Quadrantes peculiari solertia reuolutos, inuenitur, præsertim si par vtrobique inter obseruandum adhibeatur sedulitas. Alias enim nihil diuersimode fabrefactis Organis tribuerem, nisi in eundem prorsus veritatis scopum consentirent; Atque eam etiam ob causam, copiam et varietatem in eorum structura hactenus expetiui, vt non vno modo sed multifariam obseruando, enucleatior certitudo, expeditius citra omne dubium comprobetur. In his vero omnibus, quia per Rimulas illas Parallelas, Stellas collineandi, a me aliquot ab hinc annis excogitatam rationem adhibeo, multo promtius, et scrupulosius, ipsarum exquisita loca, quam veteribus per foramina Tabularum adeo errori, vti dixi, obnoxia, obseruationi indubitatæ patescunt. Per has itaque varias machinas, omni possibili subtilitate, et perfectione affabre elaboratas, Stellarum Longitudines multiformiter conquisiui, et non vni alicui Instrumento confisus, sed diuersis rem omnem varie examinando, et magna circumspectione singula trutinando, hoc tantum negotium administraui; idque non saltem a Solis loco adamussim prius correcto mediante Luna, vt veteres tentârunt, sed etiam intercedente Veneris Stella multoties a me exploratum est; vt ob id nihil prorsus dubitem, Longitudines a me constitutas omni sensibili carere vitio, et ad tantam præcisionem esse deductas, quantam vllius hominis diligentia et oculorum acies comprehendere poterit. Neque tamen propterea T. C. obseruationes improbandas iudico, sed multo potius admiror, quod vel ad eam amussim, et cum nostris admodum propinquam concordantiam, accesserint; præsertim in ea, quæ per Altitudines et transitus Meridianos, cum temporis annotatione adhibita est, Obseruationis praxi, quam sane maxima diligentia administratam liquet, cum aliâs non paucis difficultatibus, et anfractibus sit exposita.

Quod itaque T. Cels. a suis inuentis discedere nolit, ob sedulitatem non mediocrem in Obseruationibus adhibitam, rationes proculdubio habet non contemnendas, quibus ego etiam refragari nolo; neque enim differentia inter nos adeo magna est, vt Astronomicæ certitudini sensibiliter officiat. Verum quia Celsitudo Tua existimat suas in Longitudinibus constitutiones ideo tam ratas exactasque esse, vt ab ijs discedere nequeat, quod non solum post correctum Quadrantis perpendiculum, per transitus Azimuthales beneficio exquisiti Horologij adinuentæ sint, sed etiam per Veneris Stellam, in Ianuario præcedentis anni aliquoties modo etiam mihi vsitato, diligenter exploratæ, comprobatæque, de his T. Cels. meam sententiam si paulo liberius indicâro, id Tuam Celsit. clementi et benigno animo accepturam non diffido. Quantum ad priorem, per Altitudines et Azimutha, cum tempore Obseruationis simul dato, loca Stellarum obseruandi rationem attinet, licet ea post emendatum Quadrantis perpendiculum, earum Longitudines in eundem prorsus situm, quo antea, reponat, non tamen ob id omnem hæsitandi ansam sublatam existimo. Significaui vero T. Celsit. in vltimis meis literis, quas ob causas, hæc Siderum positus scrutandi via, mihi non satis tuta videatur. Siquidem quacunque diligentia Quadrans ordinetur, et quam affabre Horologia elaborata sint; tamen illic vbi vel minime putatur, error aliquis sese furtim ingerit, qui facile paucorum Scrupulorum præcisionem impediat, siue quod Horologium singula temporum momenta non perpetuo æquali tenore indicet, siue quod in ipso Quadrante, quo ad planum eius, non omni ex parte plano verticalium circulorum correspondente, aliquid minutuli lateat vitij, siue etiam quacunque alia non facile visui obuia de causa, idipsum contingat. Si tamen Tua Celsitud. huic modo, Sidera obseruandi nullatenus diffidit, fiat saltem iuxta vtrunque Æquinoctium, denuo huius rei periculum quam diligentissime, ita, vt vtrobique locus Solis per Meridianam Altitudinem diligenter emendatus, vel etiam e nostra restitutione in motu eius depromtus, adhibeatur; adsit etiam in hac praxi Horologiorum exquisitissimorum aliqua varietas, et copia, vtque inuicem, in Minutorum minimis particulis exacte correspondeant procuretur, quod si tunc (non semel sed aliquoties rem omnem reiterando) Stellarum loca, vtrinque, et viceversa examinando, in idem Minutum (adhibita intercapedinis earum ratione, siquidem diuersis Stellis hîc vti oportebit) reciderint, tunc loca earum hac Methodo satis scrupulose adinuenta, facilius concesserim. Verum quacunque sedulitate et præcautione tractetur hæc Sidera obseruandi ratio, lubrica nihilominus, et ad deuiandum, in pauculis præsertim Scrupulis admodum prona mihi censetur.

Alteram autem, qua per Veneris Stellam Tuæ Celsit. Mathematicus idem aggressus est, ratiocinationem multo potius huic præferrem, eo quod ad veritatis penitiorem tramitem tutius ducat. Quod tamen per hanc ipsam, in Ianuario præcedentis Anni, easdem Stellarum Longitudines, quas prius et a meis adhuc quinis Scrupulis dissidentes, adinuenerit, nondum satis confirmat, nihil prorsus in vestris Longitudinibus abundare, easque omnimode absolutas esse. Nam per Solem et Venerem, quo ad inquisitionem Longitudinis Affixarum Stellarum, debita limitatio tunc temporis fieri nequaquam poterat, eo quod Sol nimium esset decliuis, ob magnam Declinationem Meridionalem, et ipsa Venus etiam circa Æquatorem, non admodum sublimis ferebatur: adeo vt post Occasum Solis, quamprimum Stellæ conspicuæ essent, tam alta non extitit, quin per Refractionem, quo ad Longitudinem, vlterior quam reuera erat cerneretur. Atque hinc factum est, vt neutiquam per Venerem et Solem, hoc negotium tunc temporis exacta præcisione in praxin deduci potuerit; imo necessarium erat Stellarum Longitudines talibus adhibitis medijs, debito vlteriores aliquantulum euadere. Obseruabam quidem et ego multoties circa eosdem dies Venerem interdiu a Sole, et noctu ad Stellas, non quod vllam exquisitam in Fixarum locis comprobationem inde sperarem, satis conscius et diuturna experientia edoctus, frustra id in medietate Zodiaci australi tentari, nisi Refractionum impedimenta, quæ non semper sunt præcise æqualia, ob Aëris variam dispositionem, præcaueantur, sed saltem vt Veneris et Solis apparentes motus, e prius recte constitutis Fixarum locis inquirerem, et Refractionum insinuationes plenius expiscarer. Non itaque mirum est, ROTHMANNVM tunc temporis quinorum illorum Scrupulorum in vestris Longitudinibus abundantiam non animaduertisse; Siquidem tam Sol quam Venus secundum Zodiaci Longitudinem in pauculis Scrupulis per Refractionem obortis, vlteriores necessario apparebant, quam reuera in Cælo constituebantur, ideoque Fixarum ab ijs mensuratarum loca, etiam vlteriora quam oportuit, exhibuerunt. Sique meas Obseruationes tunc in Venere et Sole habitas, ad limitanda Fixarum loca examinâro, etiam inde colligetur 4. vel 5. Scrupulorum nimia promotio, neglecto videlicet Refractionum impedimento; si vero hoc per Tabulam ab experientia crebriore, a nobis huic negotio destinatam præcaueatur, loca Fixarum cadent præcise in eum situm, quo a nobis prius disposita sunt, quinis illis Scrupulis nullatenus deficientibus.

Verum enimuero etiamsi Refractionum et Parallaxium impedimenta in hac collatione omni diligentia excludantur, et cætera requisita, rectissime administrentur: tamen nondum omnis dubitandi occasio prærepta est, nisi etiam idem in Venere matutina, æque ac vespertina, et in consimili fere supra Horizontem situ, parique a Terris remotione, vel ea quæ vtrobique hinc incidit differentia debito modo correcta pari trutina diligenter expendatur et comprobetur, adhibita etiam Solis et Veneris ad vtraque tempora, quo ad Parallaxin, si qua forte inciderit, diuersitate; vt de Refractionibus nunc nihil dicam, quæ sane etiam limitandæ veniunt, nisi ab vtraque parte, in consimili tam Solis quam Veneris Altitudine, instituatur Obseruatio. Vbi enim viceuersa, eiusdem rei examinatio non fuerit adhibita, omniaque subtilissime inuicem collata, ad exactam Longitudinis Fixarum notitiam, etiam hac minus fallaci via procedendo, peruenire vix licebit: adeo in profundo, et interiori nucleo absconditur veritatis vnicum et immotum Centrum. Si itaque T. Cels. Mathematicus, aliquando per Venerem matutinam, pari adhibita diligentia, rem omnem expertus fuerit, et obstacula sese per Refractionum et Parallaxium diuersitatem ingerentia semouerit, non dubito, quin hac Methodo in Longitudines Affixarum meis apprime astipulantes, deueniet; antea vero absolutam in his certitudinem statuere, superuacaneum est. Quod autem per meum motum Solis, quem ROTHMANNVS existimauit circa 8. Gradum ♒ pauculis Minutis iusto tardiorem, defectionem quinorum illorum Scrupulorum, quæ meis numeris inesse putatur, excusare laborat, frustraneum videtur; Siquidem locus Solis verus in tam decliui situ per Instrumenta exquisitissima, et adhibita etiam Parallaxi, præcise cælitus non innotescit. Ideoque præmonui in primis meis literis, infra tricesimum Gradum Altitudinis, non dari per Obseruationem, Locum Solis ratum, ob Refractionem in decliuiori situ irrepentem; Et Tuæ Cels. Mathematicum etiam per literas admonui, in Boreali Semicirculo cursum Solis solummodo citra errorem examinandum, non item in Australi, ob Refractionum sæpedictam insinuationem. Atque hæc fuit non minima occasio, cur summus ille Copernicus in Apogæo, Eccentricitateque Solis constituendis, tantopere deuiârit, quod videlicet ad medias partes Scorpij ingressum eius, vna cum Æquinoctialibus transitibus contulerit, Sole tunc ob Refractionem sensibilem non in eo loco apparente, in quo reuera constituebatur.

Cum autem Obseruationibus per T. Cels. Instrumenta factis, locus Solis e nostra Ephemeride depromtus, tum circa ambo Æquinoctia, tum etiam in toto Boreali Semicirculo apprime correspondeat, vt ex literis T. Cels. Mathematici ad me datis liquet; necesse est, et in reliquo Semicirculo Australi, nullam ab ipso Cælo contingere discrepantiam, et si qua illic per Instrumenta diligentius animaduertenti apparuerit, ea citra omne dubium per accidens, ob Refractionis hoc incommodum irrepit; ideoque cautionem diligentem hac in parte adhibendam antea serio indicaui. Quod enim in fine Ianuarij T. C. indicat, Locum Solis per T. C. Quadrantem inuentum non discrepasse a mea Ephemeride, ideoque T. Cels. Mathematicum in examinatione Fixarum, per locum Veneris, eodem vsum fuisse, id, quomodo cum prioribus consistere possit, non satis assequor; vt potius vitium aliquod Obseruationi illi subesse suspicer. Nam licet Sol circa vltimos dies Ianuarij, quinis proxime Gradibus apud vos sit eleuatior quam hîc, tamen nondum adeo in sua Altitudine meridiana sublimatur, quin Refractioni adhuc satis sensibili obnoxius sit, ideoque tunc etiam cum nostra Ephemeride adamussim quadrare non poterit, nisi Refractionis impedimentum excludatur. Quia vero T. Cels. indicat per suum Mathematicum, Ephemeridem nostram ad elapsum 87. annum conuenienter fuisse extensam; vt igitur eum ab illo labore, ad plures annos hunc calculum continuandi, inposterum leuem, curaui quinque proximorum annorum Ephemerides Solis, e nostris Obseruationibus et Tabulis circa motum eius deriuatas, in adiunctis chartis describi, e quibus ad aliquot antecedentes, et sequentes annos, facilior est reductio, cum quarto quolibet Sol in eundem proxime locum, ijsdem Mensium diebus et horis reuertatur. Spero autem locum Solis per accuratam Obseruationem cælitus quæsitum, cum hac nostra restitutione, collatione facta, nihil discriminis, quod vllo modo in sensus incurrere possit, illaturum, modo in Australi Semicirculo Refractionis, de qua aliquoties sedulo monui, obstacula remoueantur.

Porro quod T. Celsit. Mathematicus ex eo, quod Longitudines meæ a vestris quinis Scrupulis deficiant, suspicatur me nimium tribuere Refractionibus, id longe aliter se habere, ipsemet confiteretur, si, quam subtili diligentia, quamque varijs modis Refractionum dimensionem, per diuersimoda Instrumenta, tam in Sole quam Stellis, aliquot ab hinc annis scrutatus sum, satis perspectum haberet. Non enim eas ad libitum, sed quales ipsa Experientia cælitus suppeditat, assumendas censeo. Quæ res non mediocri studio a me multoties, si quid aliud, elaborata est. Siquidem sine Refractionum exquisita discretione, verorum in Stellis locorum non conceditur omnis erroris expers inquisitio. Licet autem Aëris diuersitas in his nonnihil aliquando alterare possit, tamen si Cælum vndiquaque fuerit apprime serenum, nullique crassiores vapores Horizontem obtenebrârint, semper eandem pene adinuenio Refractionum in consimili Altitudine quantitatem, vt citra omne dubium persuasum habeam, illarum aliam, quam oportuit, assumtionem a nobis non esse commissam, neque hinc in Stellarum a me redintegratis locis, aliquid vitij obortum. Nec etiam tempus in meis Obseruationibus, siue per Armillas, siue per Horologia, siue etiam per Quadrantem Azimuthalem adhibitum, vitam parere potuit in his ipsis ambiguitatem, quemadmodum Tuæ Celsitud. Mathematicus frustra etiam suspicatur. Cum enim a Sole per Venerem ad Stellas, vel viceuersa instituitur obseruatio, duorum vel trium Scrupulorum, aut etiam paulo plurium, non venit necessario adhibenda in temporis denotatione exacta ratio, siquidem nec Sol nec Venus interuallo decimæ vel octauæ partis vnius horæ, sensibiliter locum varient. Nihilominus tamen vbique verum temporis momentum in ipso Scrupulo varia ratione a nobis applicatur. Si per transitus Meridianos vel Azimuthales Siderum situs rimarer, iuxta modum Tuæ Celsitudini vsitatum, vtique exactissima temporis, vel in ipsis Scrupulis secundis cognitio requireretur. Verum tum hanc ipsam ob causam, tum etiam plerasque alias, eam obseruandi rationem, tanquam hallucinationi facile obnoxiam, minus probo. Refractionem Solis tantummodo in circulo Verticali exhiberi non equidem ignoro, sed eam nihilominus, tum ad Eclipticam, tum etiam Æquatorem, prout opus fuerit, applicare didici, adeo, vt Tabulas in eum vsum e Triangulorum fontibus deriuatus, et votis meis hac in parte abunde satisfacientes, in promptu habeam; vt inde in tempore etiam per Armillas æquatorias præcise inueniendo, si quando ijs ad hoc officium vti libuerit, nulla incidere possit, vel minima deuiatio; Quamuis in temporum Minutijs per Armillas hasce exactius scrutandis, libentius vtar Stellis iuxta Meridianum, potissimum vero circa ipsam Æquatoris Altitudinem versantibus, eo, quod eo in loco Refractio nihil dispendij inferat, et tali in situ ocyssime motu primo reuoluantur, temporisque minutissimas differentias commodissime discernant.

Verum ne pluribus opus sit, his circa nostras Obseruationes dubitationibus obuiare, vnicum saltem addam, quod citra omnem controuersiam est irrefragabile. Si aliquis in meis Fixarum locis, vel minimus lapsus subesset, quibuscunque tandem occasionibus ille siue his, siue alijs incidisset, certe in reciproca Obseruatione, tam ad Venerem vespertinam, quam matutinam accurata diligentia a nobis instituta, is qualiscunque esset, sese aperte manifestasset; Quemadmodum in vltimis literis T. Celsit. significaui, me non solum Anno 82. multoties per Venerem occidentalem a Sole, et interdiu admodum alte euidenterque apparentem, sed etiam Anno 85. per eandem orientalem, iuxta medietatem Septembris, loca Stellarum Fixarum diligentissime, mediante loco Solis vtrobique exacte comperto, restituisse, et examine accurato, ea citra omne sensibile vitium recte se habere deprehendisse. Ob id etiam Cels. Tua non abs re asseuerat; si per Venerem matutinam omnia viceuersa comprobata habeam, non facile constare, qua in parte discrepantiæ istius inter nos occasio lateat. Reuera enim cætera omnia, quæ prius in dubium vocata sunt, soluuntur, irritaque euadunt, vbi circa Orientem idem quod in Occasu prius fiebat, alternatim exploratum fuerit. To the most illustrious Prince and Lord, Lord Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, Count in Katzenelnbogen, Dietz, Ziegenhain, and Nidda: his most clement Prince and Lord. Heartiest greeting.

That I reply only now — later both than Your Highness's expectation and than my own will — to the letter which, about the autumnal equinox of the preceding year [1587], Your Highness clemently sent me, most Illustrious and most Generous Prince: occasion was given by various occupations and certain impediments which fell about the beginning of Spring, and brought it about that I could not have these very [letters], which I had then prepared, conveyed to Your Highness by those who set out hence to the recent spring Fairs [Frankfurt]. There was added this too: that in March and April I hoped to obtain more observations of Venus, appearing by day and western of the Sun [as morning star], so that I might more fully compare them with the prior morning [observations], especially those had in August of the preceding year, and might institute a settled judgment and a fuller exploration of those five minutes about which there is controversy between us in establishing the Longitudes of the Fixed [stars] — which indeed I have several times diligently accomplished, as I shall afterward expound more fully [to] Your Highness, [namely] what I have drawn out and found out hence. Submissively, therefore, I have Your Highness asked to deign to hold this delay in replying — especially since it is now made good with interest — candidly and clemently excused. I wrote also meanwhile to Your Highness's Mathematician [Rothmann], and to him I set forth the reasons of this delay, to be referred to Your Highness.

Lest, therefore, what has hitherto been omitted be deferred longer, I will now reply in order to those [things] which Your Highness's letter sent to me contained. That my Observations communicated to Your Highness pleased greatly, and that that exact agreement between the Declinations and distances of the Fixed Stars investigated by Your Highness's Instruments and by mine begot the highest admiration — this I perceived, not without pleasure, from the very front of Your Highness's letter. And truly, so exquisite a method of scrutinizing the stars — that Observations taken in different places agree so precisely — has hitherto remained unusual and utterly unheard-of; as Your Highness too most rightly pronounces your opinion about these [matters]. And hence it plainly appears that our Instruments, by which the Altitudes and Distances of the stars are taken, are on both sides exactly right, and so perfectly elaborated that they are subject to no sensible fault — especially after that tiny deviation of the perpendicular in Your Highness's Quadrant was set right by the Mathematician. I therefore greatly congratulate the restoration of Astronomy that the method of the Sights [Pinnacidia] and of the Division, and of the exact structure of the Instruments — by which it is permitted to investigate the places of the stars from the heaven to the very minute, nay to an aliquot part of it (which hitherto seemed unwonted to ancient and modern Astronomers alike), [and] which I have used some years ago, not, I hope, without fruit — has become known to Your Highness too; and that, approved by Your Highness, it is brought into Astronomical practice, to the great profit and success of this most praiseworthy science: without which, indeed, every way of arriving at so exact an agreement of Observations (in which no aberration from the celestial appearances themselves lie hidden) would otherwise have been closed off — [namely] the conformity between us in the fabric and Division of the Instruments and the application of the Dioptres [sights]. But that we nonetheless differ in the Longitudes of the Stars by nearly five Minutes — by which mine are found earlier [smaller] — easily merits its excuse among those who have sufficiently surveyed the perplexed labyrinths of this subtle inquiry. And therefore Your Highness rightly asserts that this discrepancy is not so worthy of wonder; since those ancient Princes of Astronomy scarcely attained the truth in these [matters] within a sixth part of a Degree [10′], nor even attempted to aspire to greater precision — judging it, as it were, less possible. Which, however, I judge befell them not so much on account of the smallness of the Instruments, and the various distraction of the Armillae here and there during observing, as because they had learned to observe the Stars only through dioptric holes [foramina]; where, unless that which is set on the little plate [tablet] more remote from the eye be largish, even the brighter Stars are seen with the greatest difficulty; and if this be granted, on account of the size of the hole (which occupies an aliquot part of the Division of Degrees), an exquisite observation can by no means hold within a few minutes. But that their Astrolabic Armillae were of just magnitude is clear enough from this, that Hipparchus and Ptolemy detected even the equinoctial ingresses by them; and Ptolemy himself seems to reject certain Armillae set up at Alexandria, which were too large, as less suitable. Yet, with peculiar diligence applied, even largish Armillae can be so skilfully composed that, during observing, by their Revolution or the disjunction of the circles, they insinuate nothing of faultiness — although no small circumspection is required in these, which I do not doubt the ancients too applied for the most part.

Indeed I too experience, very often, that the places of the stars are observed conveniently and precisely enough by Armillae. For in my four Armillary machines — partly Equinoctial, partly Zodiacal, which are of such magnitude that they exhibit single Minutes distinctly according to the division customary to us (which, however, I have constructed by a more apt compendium than was done by the ancients) — by whichever of them it pleases to use, the true place of the Star is exhibited in the very Minute, agreeing entirely with that which is found either by distances with the help of the Sextants, or also by Azimuthal Quadrants revolved with peculiar skill — especially if equal diligence be applied on both sides during observing. For otherwise I would attribute nothing to Instruments fashioned in diverse manners, unless they agreed on entirely the same mark of truth; and for that cause too I have hitherto sought abundance and variety in their structure, so that, by observing not in one way but in manifold [ways], a more unfolded certainty might be more expeditiously, beyond all doubt, confirmed. But in all these — because I use the method, devised by me some years ago, of collimating the Stars through those Parallel Slits [Rimulae Parallelae] — their exquisite places lie open to indubitable observation far more readily and scrupulously than [they did] to the ancients through the holes of the Tablets, so subject (as I said) to error. Through these various machines, therefore, skilfully elaborated with every possible subtlety and perfection, I have sought out the Longitudes of the Stars in manifold ways; and, trusting not to some one Instrument, but examining the whole matter variously by diverse [instruments] and weighing each [thing] with great circumspection, I have administered this great business — and that not only from the Sun's place exactly corrected beforehand by means of the Moon (as the ancients attempted), but also with the star of Venus intervening, it has been explored by me very often; so that I doubt nothing at all that the Longitudes established by me lack all sensible fault, and have been brought to as great a precision as the diligence of any man and the keenness of [his] eyes will be able to comprehend. Nor yet do I therefore judge Your Highness's observations to be disapproved, but much rather I admire that they have come even to that exactness, and to a concord very near to ours — especially in that practice of Observation which is applied through Altitudes and Meridian transits, with the notation of time added, which is clearly administered with the greatest diligence, since otherwise it is exposed to not a few difficulties and windings.

That, therefore, Your Highness is unwilling to depart from your findings, on account of the no-mean diligence applied in the Observations, you doubtless have reasons not to be despised, which I too am unwilling to gainsay; for the difference between us is not so great as to be sensibly prejudicial to Astronomical certainty. But since Your Highness thinks your constitutions in the Longitudes to be so settled and exact that you cannot depart from them — because they were found not only after the corrected perpendicular of the Quadrant, by Azimuthal transits with the help of an exquisite Clock [Horologium], but also by the star of Venus, in January of the preceding year, several times explored and confirmed by a method also used by me — if I shall indicate my opinion about these a little more freely [to] Your Highness, I do not doubt that Your Highness will receive it with a clement and kind mind. As regards the former method — of observing the places of the Stars through Altitudes and Azimuths, with the time of Observation given at the same time — although, after the emended perpendicular of the Quadrant, it replaces their Longitudes in entirely the same position as before, I do not for that reason think every handle of hesitation removed. I signified to Your Highness in my last letter for what reasons this way of scrutinizing the positions of the stars seems to me not safe enough. For with whatever diligence the Quadrant be ordered, and however skilfully the Clocks be elaborated, yet there, where it is least thought, some error stealthily insinuates itself, which easily impedes the precision of a few minutes — whether because the Clock does not indicate the single moments of time with a perpetually equal tenor, or because in the Quadrant itself, as to its plane (not on every side corresponding to the plane of the vertical circles), some tiny fault lies hidden, or because the same happens from any other cause not easily obvious to sight. If, however, Your Highness by no means distrusts this manner of observing the stars, let at least the test of this matter be made anew, most diligently, at both equinoxes, in such a way that on both [occasions] the place of the Sun be diligently emended by the Meridian Altitude, or even drawn from our restitution of its motion; let there also be present in this practice some variety and abundance of most exquisite Clocks, and let care be taken that they correspond exactly to one another in the least particles of Minutes; [and] if then (the whole matter being reiterated, not once but several times), examining the places of the Stars on both sides and vice versa, they fall back into the same Minute (the reckoning of their interval being applied, since one must here use different Stars), then I shall more easily concede that their places have been found scrupulously enough by this Method. But with whatever diligence and precaution this way of observing the stars be handled, it is nonetheless judged by me to be slippery, and very prone to deviation, especially in a few minutes.

The other reasoning, however — by which Your Highness's Mathematician undertook the same [task] through the star of Venus — I would much prefer to this, because it leads more safely to the deeper track of truth. Yet that he found, by this very [method], in January of the preceding year, the same Longitudes of the Stars as before, and still differing from mine by five Minutes, does not yet sufficiently confirm that nothing at all is in excess in your Longitudes, and that they are in every way absolute. For through the Sun and Venus, as regards the inquiry into the Longitude of the Fixed Stars, the due limitation could by no means be made at that time, because the Sun was too low, on account of [its] great Southern Declination, and Venus itself too was borne about the Equator, not very lofty: so much so that after the Sun's setting, as soon as the Stars were visible, [Venus] did not stand so high but that, through Refraction, as to Longitude, it was seen further [on] than it really was. And hence it came about that this business could by no means be brought into practice with exact precision, through Venus and the Sun, at that time; nay, it was necessary that the Longitudes of the Stars, with such means applied, should come out somewhat further than due. I too indeed observed, very often about the same days, Venus by day [westward] from the Sun, and by night to the Stars — not that I hoped thence for any exquisite confirmation in the places of the Fixed [stars], being sufficiently aware and taught by long experience that it is attempted in vain in the southern half of the Zodiac, unless the impediments of Refractions (which are not always precisely equal, on account of the various disposition of the Air) be guarded against — but only that I might inquire into the apparent motions of Venus and the Sun from the previously rightly-established places of the Fixed [stars], and might more fully fish out the insinuations of the Refractions. It is therefore no wonder that Rothmann did not at that time notice the excess of those five Minutes in your Longitudes; since both the Sun and Venus, according to the Longitude of the Zodiac, necessarily appeared further [on] by a few minutes arising through Refraction than they were really constituted in the Heaven, and therefore exhibited the places of the Fixed [stars] measured from them also further than was due. And if I shall examine my Observations had then in Venus and the Sun, for limiting the places of the Fixed [stars], a too-great advancement of 4 or 5 Minutes will also be gathered thence, the impediment of the Refractions being neglected; but if this be guarded against by a Table destined for this business by us, [drawn] from more frequent experience, the places of the Fixed [stars] will fall precisely into that position in which they were disposed by us before, those five Minutes by no means being wanting.

But truly, even if the impediments of Refractions and Parallaxes be excluded in this collation with all diligence, and the other requisites be most rightly administered, yet not every occasion of doubting is forestalled, unless the same be diligently weighed and confirmed in morning Venus equally as in evening [Venus], and in a nearly similar position above the Horizon, and at an equal distance from the Earth — or [unless] that difference which here falls on both sides be duly corrected on an equal balance — with the diversity of the Sun and Venus at both times, as regards Parallax (if any perchance fall), also applied; to say nothing now of the Refractions, which truly must also be limited, unless the Observation be instituted on both sides at a similar Altitude both of the Sun and of Venus. For where the examination of the same matter has not been applied vice versa, and all [things] most subtly collated with one another, it will scarcely be permitted to arrive at exact knowledge of the Longitude of the Fixed [stars], even proceeding by this less fallacious way: so deep, and in an inner kernel, is hidden the single and immovable Centre of truth. If, therefore, Your Highness's Mathematician shall some time have tested the whole matter through morning Venus, with equal diligence applied, and shall have removed the obstacles which insinuate themselves through the diversity of Refractions and Parallaxes, I do not doubt that by this Method he will come to Longitudes of the Fixed [stars] perfectly agreeing with mine; but to establish absolute certainty in these beforehand is superfluous. But that he labours to excuse the defect of those five Minutes, which is thought to be in my numbers, through my motion of the Sun — which Rothmann thought, about the 8th degree of Aquarius [♒], slower than just by a few Minutes — seems vain; since the true place of the Sun, in so low a position, is not known precisely from the heaven even through the most exquisite Instruments, even with Parallax applied. And therefore I forewarned in my first letter that, below the thirtieth Degree of Altitude, the place of the Sun is not given as settled by Observation, on account of the Refraction creeping in at a lower position; and I admonished Your Highness's Mathematician too, by letter, that the course of the Sun is to be examined without error only in the Northern Semicircle, not likewise in the Southern, on account of the oft-mentioned insinuation of Refractions. And this was no small occasion why that supreme Copernicus deviated so greatly in establishing the Apogee and Eccentricity of the Sun — namely, that he compared its ingress into the middle parts of Scorpio together with the equinoctial transits, the Sun then, on account of sensible Refraction, not appearing in that place in which it was really constituted.

But since, in the Observations made by Your Highness's Instruments, the place of the Sun drawn from our Ephemeris corresponds perfectly both about both Equinoxes and also in the whole Northern Semicircle — as is clear from the letters of Your Highness's Mathematician given to me — it is necessary that in the remaining Southern Semicircle too no discrepancy arise from the Heaven itself; and if any there appear to one observing more diligently by the Instruments, it without all doubt crept in per accidens, on account of this inconvenience of Refraction; and therefore I before seriously indicated that diligent caution must be applied in this regard. For that which Your Highness indicates [happened] at the end of January — that the place of the Sun found by Your Highness's Quadrant did not differ from my Ephemeris, and that therefore Your Highness's Mathematician, in the examination of the Fixed [stars] through the place of Venus, used the same — how it can be consistent with the foregoing I do not sufficiently grasp; so that I rather suspect some fault to underlie that Observation. For although the Sun, about the last days of January, is higher with you by nearly five Degrees than here, yet it is not yet so raised in its meridian Altitude but that it is subject to a still sensible Refraction, and therefore even then it cannot square exactly with our Ephemeris, unless the impediment of Refraction be excluded. But since Your Highness indicates, through your Mathematician, that our Ephemeris had been conveniently extended to the elapsed [15]87th year — so that I might relieve him hereafter of that labour of continuing this calculation for several years, I have taken care to describe, in the attached sheets, Ephemerides of the Sun for the next five years, derived from our Observations and Tables concerning its motion; from which the reduction to several preceding and following years is easier, since every fourth [year] the Sun returns to nearly the same place on the same days and hours of the Months. But I hope that the place of the Sun sought from the heaven by accurate Observation, a collation being made with this our restitution, will introduce no difference which could in any way fall under the senses — provided that in the Southern Semicircle the obstacles of Refraction, of which I have several times diligently warned, be removed.

Furthermore, that Your Highness's Mathematician, from the fact that my Longitudes fall short of yours by five Minutes, suspects that I attribute too much to Refractions — that this is far otherwise, he would himself confess, if he had sufficiently surveyed with what subtle diligence, and in how various ways, I have scrutinized the measurement of Refractions, through diverse Instruments, both in the Sun and in the Stars, for some years now. For I judge that they must be assumed not at pleasure, but such as Experience itself supplies from the heaven. Which matter has been elaborated by me very often, if anything [has], with no mean study. For without an exquisite discrimination of the Refractions, an inquiry free of all error into the true places in the Stars is not granted. But although the diversity of the Air can sometimes alter these somewhat, yet if the Heaven be on every side perfectly serene, and no thicker vapours have darkened the Horizon, I always find nearly the same quantity of Refractions at a similar Altitude, so that I hold it persuaded beyond all doubt that no other assumption of them than was due has been committed by us, nor that anything of fault has arisen hence in the places of the Stars restored by me. Nor could the time in my Observations — whether by Armillae, or by Clocks, or even by the Azimuthal Quadrant applied — beget in these very [matters] an ambiguity, as Your Highness's Mathematician also vainly suspects. For when an observation is instituted from the Sun through Venus to the Stars, or vice versa, no exact reckoning of two or three Minutes (or even a little more) needs necessarily to be applied in the precise notation of time, since neither the Sun nor Venus varies its place sensibly in the interval of a tenth or eighth part of one hour. Nonetheless, everywhere the true moment of time in the very Minute is applied by us by a various method. If I were to investigate the positions of the stars through Meridian or Azimuthal transits, according to the manner customary to Your Highness, the most exact knowledge of time — even in the very seconds — would certainly be required. But both for this very cause, and also for very many others, I less approve that way of observing, as easily subject to hallucination. That the Refraction of the Sun is exhibited only in the Vertical circle I am indeed not ignorant; but I have nonetheless learned to apply it, both to the Ecliptic and also to the Equator, as need shall be — so much so that I have ready to hand Tables derived for that use from the founts of Triangles [trigonometry], and abundantly satisfying my wishes in this regard; so that thence, in finding the time precisely even by the equatorial Armillae (if ever it shall please to use them for this office), no deviation, even the least, can fall. Although, in scrutinizing the minutiae of time more exactly by these Armillae, I more gladly use Stars near the Meridian — especially those moving about the very Altitude of the Equator — because in that place the Refraction inflicts no loss, and in such a position they are revolved by the primary [diurnal] motion most swiftly, and most conveniently discern the minutest differences of time.

But, lest more be needed to meet these doubts about our Observations, I will add at least one [thing], which beyond all controversy is irrefragable. If any, even the least, lapse underlay my places of the Fixed [stars] — by whatever occasions it had befallen, whether these or others — surely in the reciprocal Observation, instituted by us with accurate diligence both to evening Venus and to morning [Venus], it, whatever it were, would have openly manifested itself. As I signified in [my] last letter to Your Highness, I restored the places of the Fixed Stars most diligently, the place of the Sun being on both sides exactly ascertained, not only in the year [15]82 several times through Venus western of the Sun (and appearing by day quite high and evidently), but also in the year [15]85 through the same eastern [Venus], about the middle of September; and by accurate examination I found that they stood right, free of all sensible fault. On which account too Your Highness not without reason asserts that, if I have everything confirmed vice versa through morning Venus, it does not easily appear in what part the occasion of that discrepancy between us lies hidden. For in reality all the other [things] which were before called into doubt are resolved, and turn out null, when about the East the same that was before done in the West has been alternately explored. For if any faultiness should stealthily insinuate itself, whether in Refraction, or Parallax, or even in the place of the Sun, the reciprocal Observation does not allow it to lie hidden; for then every proclivity to error is easily guarded against.

Wherefore no doubt at all is left with me that the Longitudes of the Stars restored by my Instruments agree most exquisitely with the Heaven itself, and that those five Minutes, in which we differ between us, are by no means wanting in them. Nay, for the sake of confirming a more evident certainty, I made the test of this matter anew in the month of August of the preceding year [1587], and that on two days, namely the 17th and 18th, on which, about the nearly partile conjunction of Venus with Jupiter, having obtained a quite pure and opportune serenity, I most diligently observed the star of Venus in the morning from certain fixed Stars, especially from both heads of Gemini [Castor and Pollux]; and afterwards I directed everything to the Sun raised above the Horizon at a sufficient Altitude; and then I not only accurately compared [them] with the observations had in the year [15]82 in those same Stars, by the benefit of evening Venus (the reckoning being had also of the discrepancy falling in the Sun and Venus from some diversity of Parallax), but also weighed [them] anew with the Observations of Venus, to those same and other neighbouring Stars, in this very Year [1588], in the months of March and April, when Venus appeared sufficiently by day, western of the Sun, several times most diligently explored. And when I had weighed all these everywhere on a quite precise balance, so far is it [from being the case] that I found the Longitudes of the Fixed [stars] previously established by me to be carried out further, that, if anything were to be altered in them, rather a redundant half-Minute would have to be taken away — a precision, however, which is idle and well-nigh imperceptible. Since therefore I have confirmed twice, vice versa, morning and evening, through Venus and the Sun, the precise restitution of the Fixed Stars — namely in [15]82 evening and [15]85 morning, and now again recently in the elapsed [15]87 morning, and in this same passing [15]88 evening — so that, the single [places] being collated and weighed vice versa with one another, their places fall back into entirely the same Minute in which they were ordered by me before, and suggest no sensible variety in any way: I cannot but acquiesce in a verification so frequently tried by various Instruments and confirmed in diverse and reciprocal ways — especially since the last two Observations had vice versa have borne such proper suffrage to the prior ones, and besides have had this convenience, that Venus on both [occasions] stood nearly equally distant from the Earth, and therefore offered insensible differences of Parallax at a nearly similar Altitude, the obstacle of Refractions too at about an equal elevation (for I so order the Observations that those taken at about the same Altitudes are compared with one another) introducing nothing of faultiness. A not-obscure testimony to this our emendation is also furnished by that Star which is placed in the Spike of Virgo [Spica]; for this, from the Declination alone together with the Latitude — by the distance from a certain little star of Draco near the Pole of the Ecliptic, sought beforehand by me diligently by a peculiar method — agrees perfectly with our predefined Longitudes of the Stars, without the knowledge of other means, and consents into the same Minute (as I shall shortly, God favouring, render this and very many other [things] making hither more richly manifest and confirmed, from certain Observations and Demonstrations).

But whether any of the Ancients, from the very beginning of cultivated Astronomy, or any of the Moderns down to these times, have arrived at so exact a precision — and how much industry, labour, and time (to say nothing of the costs of the Instruments, which are greater than all estimation) I have spent in investigating these [things] more unfoldedly — let the judgment be of others who understand the whole matter fundamentally; among whom Your Highness, as a Prince born most generous of an illustrious lineage of ancestors, so also by the acumen of [your] talent, the gravity of [your] judgment, and indeed experience itself, deserves a truly princely place, by no less clarity of Erudition than of birth. Although these [things] are so — that in the Longitudes of the Fixed [stars] established by me nothing at all of sensible deviation or doubt is committed — Your Highness nonetheless acts advisedly in adhering constantly to the Observations sought out by your own Instruments, until you shall have had some more exact inquiry instituted in these. For it is not fitting, in those [matters] which are exposed to the senses and are investigated Mechanically with competent diligence applied, and thence can be resolved into numbers by Geometrical certainty, to be content with the findings of others, unless the same have been confirmed by one's own and least-doubtful experience. I understand, besides, that Your Highness is thinking about the publication of the places of the Fixed Stars hitherto restored by your Instruments, and has assigned this eminent province to your Mathematician — which indeed I deem most worthy of all praise, lest the celestial Observations acquired by so many labours and vigils, and so great industry and diligence, over the course of many years, be frustrated of their usufruct. Wherefore by this sole reason Your Highness will deserve most excellently of all Posterity, and will carry back from it a never-dying fame, with grateful celebration of [your] name, which among the erudite and intelligent will perpetuate clarity and eminent reverence, no less than [your] Illustrious lineage and dignity of Government.

But I should wish very greatly that Your Highness would, with equal diligence, take care that the apparent motions of the Planets too be investigated from the heaven — which is not difficultly accomplished through the restored places of the Fixed [stars]. And it would conduce very much, if not elsewhere, at least to investigate their appearances exquisitely near the Acronychal shinings [oppositions] of the three superior Planets, and in Venus and Mercury near their extreme digressions from the Sun [maximum elongations], but sometimes, in all these, about the intermediate places — so that a more precise knowledge than hitherto could be inquired concerning the reckoning of the Apogees and Eccentricities, and the proportion of the circular motions to one another. In all which I too, for many years now, have bestowed not-useless labour (as I hope), as will appear in its time, the Deity favouring — when occasion will be given of communicating to the cultivators of Astronomy those [things] which I have found out in these with great and long diligence, where I shall have examined, by accurate Observations, more shinings of Saturn especially at the extremity of night [opposition], in the more remote places of the Eccentric, because it is more slowly completed in it. That Your Highness so clemently and kindly promises that you will not allow my Observations and verifications in the places of the Fixed [stars] to be scattered here and there falls out for me most according to [my] wish. For I myself have resolved, by an opportune convenience, to publish my findings in the restitution of the Fixed Stars, and to transmit [them] to posterity. But the difference which falls between us in this part — even if perchance it be not meanwhile reconciled — imports almost nothing that need be reckoned; and Your Highness rightly asserts that the whole matter will better merit faith among others, where nothing has been admitted for favour, by mutual consent, without the experience of a sure Observation. But when Your Highness shall have restored your Instruments by another method (as you write you have in mind), I do not doubt that you will most surely find by experience that those five Minutes about which we deal are in excess in Your Highness's previous restitution of the Fixed [stars] — especially if you shall have had an accurate Observation instituted, and examined in the due manner, chiefly through the star of Venus, both morning and evening, by a double method; and then at last, your own experience persuading, you will more gladly correct this excess, and willingly extend your hand to the more accurate finding.

Meanwhile Your Highness most rightly concludes that so tiny a discrepancy between us is not of such moment as to come greatly into mention. For that, in so perplexed a business concerning the due predefining of the Longitudes of the Stars, five or six Minutes very easily slip away, those are not ignorant who have attained, by long handling, some fundamental knowledge of Astronomical Observations. But would that the celestial appearances, especially as to the course of the Planets, were surely established for us within that twelfth little part of a degree [5′] — how well and happily would the whole of Astronomy be provided for, and from how great a labour, in restoring it anew, would it be permitted us to forbear! But I do not wish to detain Your Highness with more now; and if any [things] making more fully for the whole matter have here been omitted, about these I shall deal more copiously in other letters with Your Highness's Mathematician. But I desire Your Highness humbly asked not to cease to deal and confer with me further by letter about this sublime Astronomical material, and to deign clemently to communicate to me your re-integrated calculations concerning the places of the Fixed Stars, as soon as they shall be completed, and if you have besides anything elaborated which earnestly makes for the Astronomical [science]. I in turn devote myself and my studies to Your Highness's will and desires.

May Your Highness fare as long and as happily as possible.
Given at Uraniborg, the 16th Day of August, in the Year 1588.
Your Highness's devoted, Tycho Brahe.

  • Edition of record. Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia vol. VI (the Epistolae astronomicae). The edition's source_edition ("Tychonis Brahe Dani epistolarum astronomicarum libri…") is Tycho's own 1596 print (Liber I, the volume he produced at his Wandsbek/Uraniborg press), in which this letter appears; no manuscript witness is recorded. Cross-check: page-collated at vol. VI pp. 121–132; every crux confirmed (anchors in the Editorial note).
  • Sender / recipient. Tycho (Uraniborg). Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel (1532–1592) — the "astronomical Landgrave," himself an accomplished observer with a Kassel observatory; titles Count in Katzenelnbogen, Dietz, Ziegenhain, and Nidda. His Mathematician throughout is Christoph Rothmann, the working partner in the dispute (the great Tycho–Rothmann technical exchange — the four largest letters of this corpus — runs alongside this princely-level letter).
  • The controversy (the spine): the "five minutes." Rothmann's fixed-star longitudes ran ~5′ greater than Tycho's. Tycho's defence:
  • Instrument concord. The declinations and distances from both observatories agree exactly (a thing "unheard-of"), proving the instruments sound — especially after the tiny perpendicular deviation in the Kassel quadrant was fixed. Tycho credits his sights/Division method and (as the key advance) the parallel slits (Rimulae Parallelae) for collimating stars, vs. the ancients' error-prone sight-holes (foramina). His four armillary machines (equatorial + zodiacal), sextants, and azimuthal quadrants all agree to the minute.
  • Why the ancients reached only 10′. Hipparchus/Ptolemy worked to a "sixth of a degree" because of the sight-hole problem; their armillae were of "just magnitude" (Ptolemy even rejected over-large Alexandria armillae).
  • The two methods. (a) Altitude + azimuth + clock-time (Rothmann's primary method): Tycho judges it "slippery" — clocks may run unevenly, the quadrant-plane may not match the vertical circle; he asks for a fresh test at both equinoxes with multiple clocks. (b) The Venus method (linking Sun → Venus → stars by day) he prefers — but Rothmann's January [1587] Venus work was vitiated because Sun and Venus were too low (great southern declination), so refraction pushed both c. 5′ further in longitude, inflating the star-places. Hence Rothmann "did not notice the 5′ excess."
  • Refraction is the crux. Below 30° altitude the Sun's place is unreliable; the southern zodiac half is refraction-spoiled; Tycho even attributes Copernicus's error in the solar apogee/eccentricity to taking the Sun's mid-Scorpio entry against the equinoxes while it was refraction-displaced. His Ephemeris matches Kassel at the equinoxes and in the northern semicircle; any southern discrepancy is refraction per accidens. He defends his refraction tables as drawn from experience (constant at equal altitude under clear skies), not assumed ad libitum, and denies that time-keeping introduces error in his Sun→Venus→star method (Sun/Venus barely move in 1/8 hour).
  • The irrefragable proof: reciprocal Venus. Any error would show up in morning-vs-evening Venus observations at like altitude/distance. Tycho's run: 1582 evening (Venus west of Sun), 1585 morning (Sept), 1587 morning (17–18 Aug, at the near-partile Venus–Jupiter conjunction, sighted from both heads of Gemini = Castor & Pollux), 1588 evening (Mar–Apr) — all fall to the same minute; if anything, a "redundant half-minute" might be shaved. The Spica test (its place fixed via a small Draco star near the ecliptic pole, from declination + latitude alone) independently confirms his longitudes. Conclusion: the 5′ are in excess in Rothmann's values.
  • Forward programme. Tycho encloses a 5-year solar Ephemeris (1588–92, replacing the one extended to 1587); praises the Landgrave's plan to publish his star catalogue (assigned to Rothmann); urges him to observe the planets (the three superiors at opposition, Venus/Mercury at max elongation, plus intermediate places) to fix the apogees, eccentricities, and orbit-proportions — noting his own work on Saturn at opposition in the remote eccentric. He will publish his own star-restitution; the 5′ difference is minor, best left until "sure observation," not "mutual consent for favour."
  • Persons & places. Christoph Rothmann (the Landgrave's mathematician); Hipparchus, Ptolemy (and the Alexandria armillae); Copernicus (the solar-apogee refraction error); the Frankfurt Fairs (postal channel); Alexandria; Uraniborg. (Jost Bürgi, the Kassel clockmaker behind the "exquisite Horologia," is not named but is implied.) Stars named: Spica (Virginis Spica), both heads of Gemini (Castor & Pollux), a small Draco star near the ecliptic pole.
