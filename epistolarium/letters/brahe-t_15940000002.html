The original

NOBILI AC GENEROSO DOMINO,
DOMINO TYCHONI BRAHÆ. MAGNIFICE ET
GENEROSE DOMINE.

GRATA mihi inprimis erat illa mei mentio,
quam in literis a te ad Clarissimum Dominum IUNIUM Serenissimi Regis
consiliarium et iam apud vos legatum missis perlegeram. Nam licet mihi probe
conscius essem, minime me concernere, quæ de me scripseras, tamen laudari a tali viro, in quo
tot generis, tot fortunæ et eruditionis ornamenta sunt, nemini non acceptum vel
gratum esse potest, et nisi aliqua literarum significatione id agnovissem,
fuissem profecto ingratissimus. Spondeo itaque Magnifice Domine, me tuo merito
et tui amantissimum et posthac studiosissimum futurum et officijs, quibus
possim, curaturum, ut vere id de me possis iudicare. Unum hoc peto, ut si quæ
offensionum causæ adversus fratrem meum supersint, illas mihi velis pro tua
humanitate condonare. Nam licet in ea qua frater opinione sim, supra cœlum Lunæ
nihil corruptioni obnoxium gigni posse, tamen te virum tantum, tot annorum
experientia confirmatum, tot Mathematicis organis instructum aliter sentire,
alijsque viris magnis tuam sententiam probasse, nisi multa essent, quæ tecum
facerent, nunquam mihi persuadebo. Sed in hoc
doctiorum sit iudicium. Rem ipsam mitto, scripti fraterni genus non probo.
Acerbius enim, quam par erat, adversus virum tantum se gessit, quem pro
admirabili eruditione colere debuerat. Sed in philosophicis concertationibus a
prima ætate versatus usu acrimoniam disputandi sibi comparavit. Scriptum illud
nunquam exijt, et scio me apud fratrem posse ut perpetuo supprimatur, vel si
malis cum honorifica tui nominis et eruditionis mentione, et omni felle purgatum
exeat; immo ut cum HORATIO loquar, sive
flammis mandare velis, sive mari Adriano, utrumvis pro tuo arbitrio expectabis.
Interea me tui amantissimum et in eo nulli concedentem in futurum pro tuo
amplectere. Deus optimus maximus in omnibus rebus tuis gerendis semper adsit,
semper te et tua gubernet precor.

Vale.

Tui semper observantissimus
THO.
CRAGIUS

Expectant nostri omnes tuum de felicissima et auspicatissima principis nostri
genesi iudicium.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the noble and well-born lord, the Lord Tycho Brahe. Magnificent and well-born Lord.

Especially welcome to me was that mention of myself which I read through in the letter sent by you to the most distinguished Master Young, councillor of the Most Serene King and now legate among you. For although I was well aware in my own conscience that what you had written about me concerned me but little, yet to be praised by such a man — in whom there are so many ornaments of birth, of fortune, and of learning — cannot but be acceptable or pleasing to anyone; and had I not acknowledged it by some token of a letter, I should truly have been most ungrateful. I pledge therefore, Magnificent Lord, that by your desert I shall both be most devoted to you and hereafter most zealous [for you], and shall see to it, by such services as I can, that you may judge this of me truly. This one thing I ask: that, if any grounds of offence against my brother yet remain, you would be willing of your kindness to forgive me [them]. For although I am of the same opinion as my brother — that above the heaven of the Moon nothing subject to corruption can be brought forth — yet that you, so great a man, confirmed by the experience of so many years, equipped with so many mathematical instruments, should think otherwise, and that you should have proved your view to other great men, I shall never persuade myself [of this], unless there were many things that made on your side. But on this point let the judgement be the more learned men's. The matter itself I set aside; the manner of my brother's writing I do not approve. For he bore himself more sharply than was fitting against so great a man, whom for his admirable learning he ought to have honoured. But, engaged from his earliest years in philosophical contests, he has by habit acquired for himself a keenness in disputing. That writing has never come out, and I know that I have it in my power with my brother that it be suppressed for ever — or, if you should prefer, that it come out with honourable mention of your name and learning, and purged of all gall; nay, to speak with Horace, whether you would wish to consign it to the flames or to the Adriatic sea, you shall await either one as you please. Meanwhile embrace me, as your own, as most devoted to you and yielding to none in this in time to come. May God, Best and Greatest, ever be at hand in the conduct of all your affairs; ever, I pray, may He govern you and yours.

Farewell.
Ever most observant of you,
Thomas Craig. All our people await your judgement upon the most happy and most auspicious nativity of our prince.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Tho. Cragius, Doctor Medicus = Thomas Craig (Cragius), the Scotsman writing to excuse his brother. The signature gives "Doctor of Medicine"; the family is the Craig family of Edinburgh. The letter is the conciliatory counterpart to the bitter cometary dispute (1589–1594) between Tycho and the writer's brother.
  • frater meus — "my brother": John Craig (d. 1620), the Edinburgh physician and Aristotelian who, between 1589 and 1594, conducted a sharp epistolary controversy with Tycho, disputing Tycho's supralunary (superlunary) placement of the comet of 1577. Tycho circulated copies of the Craig exchange to correspondents such as Brucaeus and Hagecius. This letter is Thomas Craig's distancing of himself from the manner (not entirely the matter) of his brother's polemic.
  • Dominum IUNIUM … Serenissimi Regis consiliarium et iam apud vos legatum — "Master Young … councillor of the Most Serene King and now legate among you." Iunius is the Latinised form of the Scots surname Young: Peter Young (Petrus Junius, 1544–1628), the royal tutor and councillor of James VI of Scotland, who served as the king's ambassador (legate) to Denmark in 1594. iam apud vos legatum ("now legate among you [pl.]") locates Young in Denmark at the time of writing — the channel through which Tycho's favourable mention of Thomas Craig reached him. Serenissimi Regis = James VI of Scotland (the "Most Serene King").
  • Astronomical doctrine. supra coelum Lunae nihil corruptioni obnoxium gigni posse = "that above the heaven of the Moon nothing subject to corruption can be brought forth" — the Aristotelian doctrine of the incorruptible heavens, the very position from which John Craig denied that the 1577 comet could be a supralunary (celestial) body. Thomas Craig states he shares his brother's Aristotelian opinion, while declining to set his own judgement against Tycho's instrument-based demonstration.
  • tot Mathematicis organis instructum = "equipped with so many mathematical instruments" (organa → instruments, this chapter's convention): a deferential nod to Tycho's observational apparatus at Uraniborg as the ground of his authority.
  • Allusion (Horace). ut cum Horatio loquar, sive flammis mandare velis, sive mari Adriano = "to speak with Horace, whether you would wish to consign [it] to the flames or to the Adriatic sea." An echo of Horace, Odes I.16 (and the topos of Ars Poetica on suppressing one's writings): Thomas Craig offers Tycho absolute discretion over the unpublished tract — burn it, drown it, or let it appear emended. The metaphor is kept, not flattened.
  • Rem ipsam mitto — "the matter itself I set aside / I let go": mitto here in the rhetorical sense "I pass over, I waive," i.e. Craig does not contest the substance of the dispute, only the tone. Rendered "I set aside" to preserve the deliberate reservation (he does not concede the substance).
  • Dating. The edition label is "1594. empty" — i.e. assigned to the year 1594 with no day or month in the source. The letter carries no dateline and no place in the transcription: the subscription ends at the signature. The header therefore gives "[s.l.], 1594" (year only, no place stated) rather than asserting a day the source does not substantiate. The year is consistent with Peter Young's 1594 Danish embassy and with the close of the Tycho–Craig controversy; it is editorial (Dreyer's), not from an internal dateline. O.S. is assumed (Scotland and Denmark both kept the Julian calendar in 1594), but the letter states no style.
  • Honorifics. Magnifice et Generose Domine → "Magnificent and well-born Lord"; S. (implied salutation idiom) and the closing Vale → "Farewell"; Tui semper observantissimus → "Ever most observant of you".
  • Negation check. The source carries the conciliatory negations exactly: minime me concernere ("concerned me but little"), nunquam mihi persuadebo ("I shall never persuade myself"), scripti fraterni genus non probo ("the manner … I do not approve"), Scriptum illud nunquam exijt ("that writing has never come out"). All four are reproduced; none added or dropped. The double construction nunquam mihi persuadebo … nisi multa essent, quae tecum facerent is rendered with its conditional intact ("I shall never persuade myself … unless there were many things that made on your side"), preserving Craig's guarded acknowledgement that Tycho must have strong grounds.
  • Names the occasion: the young Prince's nativity. The recovered closing sentence asks Tycho for his astrological judgement on 'the most happy and most auspicious nativity of our prince' — i.e. a horoscope for the newborn heir of Scotland.
  • Cross-reference to the companion letter. The same request is pursued in far greater detail in John Craig's letter of 22 April [1594] (brahe-t_15940422001), which reports the birth of 'a most longed-for infant Prince' — Prince Henry Frederick, Duke of Rothesay, born to James VI and Anne of Denmark at Stirling Castle on 19 February 1594 (Old Style) — and supplies the birth-time and latitude Tycho would need to cast the nativity. Read together, the two Craig letters (brothers, both signing 'Doctor Medicus') show the Scottish circle soliciting Tycho's genethliacal judgement on the new heir in tandem.
  • Correction. Latin and English: the title 'Doctor Medicus' / 'Doctor of Medicine' removed from Thomas Craig's signature — Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII, p. 365 closes the signature at 'THO. CRAGIUS.' with no title; the title belongs to the preceding, separate letter of his brother John Craig ('IOAN. CRAIGIUS, Doctor Medicus.', p.364) and had contaminated this letter's signature in error.

Collated against Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia (the edition of record), reading the printed page images. Collated the full letter (the Peter Young/consiliarium opening, the brother-John-Craig comet/superlunary-corruption passage, the Horace flames-or-Adriatic clause, the closing nativity postscript) against Dreyer TBOO VII pp.364-365 as page images. Body text is a word-for-word match with no fabrication, omission, or mistranslation in the letter proper — including the knotty 'nunquam mihi persuadebo, nisi multa essent quae tecum facerent' clause, which is faithfully and correctly rendered. The signature block, however, carries a false professional title not present in Dreyer, evidently bled over from the adjacent John Craig letter.

1 defect found:
- text error (medium). Dreyer's signature block for this letter reads only 'THO. CRAGIUS.' with no title. The transcribed original has appended 'Doctor Medicus', which is not in Dreyer here — it appears to be contamination from the immediately preceding, separate letter of John Craig on the same page spread, which genuinely closes 'IOAN. CRAIGIUS, Doctor Medicus.' Thomas Craig of Riccarton was a jurist/advocate, not a physician (his brother John Craig was the royal physician), so the English's 'Doctor of Medicine' misattributes a professional identity to the wrong Craig. Dreyer prints: Tui semper observantissimus THO. CRAGIUS. [no title follows].

Repaired. Verified by direct visual inspection of the Dreyer TBOO VII pp.363-366 page images, comparing this letter's closing (p.365, 'Tui femper obfervantißimus THO. CRAGIUS.') against the immediately preceding letter's closing (p.364, 'Tui obfervantiß. IOAN. CRAIGIUS, Doctor Medicus.'). The contamination is unambiguous. Both Latin and English corrected as instructed for a text error that misled the translation.
