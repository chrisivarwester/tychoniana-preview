The original

CLARISSIMO ET PRÆSTANTISSIMO VIRO DOMINO M. MICHAELI MÆSTLINO, MATHEMATICARUM ARTIUM INSIGNITER PERITO, EARUNDEMQUE IN INCLYTA ACADEMIA TUBINGENSI PROFESSORI CELEBERRIMO, MIHI PLURIMUM DILECTO. TÜBINGEN.

S.

ANNUS iam agitur decimus, eruditissime et præstantissime MÆSTLINE, ex quo quidam e meis domesticis, qui aliquandiu mihi in rebus Astronomicis inservierat, exemplum tuarum literarum Tubingâ ad se datarum in Daniam mihi transmisit, quibus pollicebaris, te librum nostrum de Cometa anni 77 tibi ab eodem communicatum diligenter perlecturum, tuamque de eius contentis sententiam conscripturum, atque mecum de eis per literas collaturum, quas te Marpurgum ad eundem mittere decrevisse, ad me postea procurandas innuisti. At ego toto interlapsis annis, neque hac de re aut quapiam alia, negocium Astronomicum, quo utrinque afficimur, concernente, ne verbulum quidem a te accepi, sive scripto, sive aliorum indicatione, qui isthinc venientes me Uraniburgi, dum adhuc in Patria degerem, inviserunt. Existimassem equidem te ob communia nobis studia scriptione aliqua saltem tot interlabentibus annis non supersessurum, præsertim cum id facturum sponte in te receperis. Ne vero ego ob id tecum per literas, siquidem aliter non liceat, familiaritatem contrahendi omnem occasionem respuisse videar, quod ab humanitate et exercitijs, quibus delectamur, sublimioribus alienum foret: en nunc prævenio, et ad scribendum respondendumque ultro te invito: data nimirum hac commodiore, qua nunc fruor, oportunitate, quam antea in Dania, ex quo in arce quadam Ranzoviana, dimidio saltem miliari Hamburgo remota, nunc habitem, unde in varia, non saltem Germaniæ, sed totius pene Europæ loca creber et commodus datur nuncius. Si itaque quid habes, de quo mecum in rebus Astronomicis, sive Cometicum illud negocium, sive quidvis aliud concernat, conferre libuerit, invenies me pro meo modulo, et quantum hactenus in ijs assequi licuit, ad respondendum non difficilem.

Allatum mihi nuper est eruditum quoddam scriptum Mathematici excellentis JOANNIS KEPLERI e Styria, in quo revolutiones Planetarum secundum formam Coperniceam ad 5 regularia corpora harmonice redigere ingeniosa sane speculatione conatur, qua etiam de re nuper ad me perscripsit, egoque illi, quantum præ occupationum mole licuit, meam vicissim de talibus aperui sententiam. Video autem te in eodem libro de Hypothesi a nobis ante annos complures adinventa nonnulla quasi dubitanter adferre, cum promiscue eam etiam alijs tribuas, plagiarijs forte meis nimium auscultans, tum quoque plura de eadem inferens, quæ si consideratius expendisses, et sententiam nostram atque omnes occasiones huius Hypotheseos constituendæ undiquaque perspectas habuisses, hæsitationibus eiuscemodi forte supersedisses. Atqui ego, si tibi quid in hac inventione scrupuli restet, id amovere atque penitius rem omnem explanare paratus sum, quod etiam res ipsa docebit, ubi Planetarum motus ad eam normam a nobis adeo accurate expansos cognoveris, ut nihil sit, quod sensus amplius requirant, utut tu in eodem libello de Astronomiæ correctione non saltem dubitare, sed et animum abijcere videaris, licet antea aliter spem feceris. Si ab anteriore per corpora ista regularia dimensione facta, ea restituenda erit, potius quam ex accuratis observationibus a posteriori sumptis, veluti insinuas, utique nimis diu, si non prorsus in perpetuum frustra expectabimus, antequam tale quid a quoquam præstari poterit. Cum enim istorum regularium corporum dimensionis explicatio, si omni ex parte admittenda foret, observationibus antecedentibus niti et comprobari debeat, nec etiam particularia quæque præter generaliora illa, utcunque se habentia, hinc ea qua opus est præcisione derivari possunt, quod te non latere arbitror. Neque etiam adhuc satis extricatæ sunt Planetarum biformes inæqualitates, quas duplici eccentrico vel circello excusant. Ignoratur enim iusta harum proportio, ut de Apogæis non dicam longe aliter se habentibus, quam fert non saltem Alphonsina, sed et Copernicea ratiocinatio. Quin et annua illa diversitatis evidens insinuatio necdum satis comperta est, ita ut undiquaque suis numeris absolute constet, ut ob id e Coperniceis proportionibus circulorum hæc ea, qua requiritur, subtilitate rimari invium sit; qua etiam de re eruditissimum KEPLERUM per literas admonui, et in Marte, ubi hæ differentiæ magis sunt sensibiles, exemplum indicavi. Verum de his et similibus nimis prolixum foret hîc singula exequi.

Ubi opera mea Astronomica lucem publicam viderint, quod nunc magnis viribus nec minoribus quam antea in Patria urgeo, perspicies, uti spero, rem Astronomicam aliter quam putatur redintegrari posse, et me non contemnendam in ea locasse operam; quare de his plura nunc non addo. Aliud quiddam est, etsi minoris momenti, quod tamen ad negocium hoc omnimode exequendum non parum conducit, pro ea videlicet, quam meridianorum ingerit in rotundo Terræ globo alteratio, diversitate necdum satis accurate explorata, de quo tecum agere averem. Si itaque aliquot Eclipses, præsertim Lunares, isthic exquisita diligentia præteritis annis observatas habes (de quo non dubito) earum momenta per observationum etiam circumstantias mecum mature communices velim, quo collatione facta cum 20 aut pluribus Lunæ deliquijs a me non paucis iam transactis annis diligenter consideratis conferri queant. Existimo me aliqua inter ea, quæ ego denotavi, inventurum, quæ etiam a te isthic observata sunt, atque hinc meridianorum intercapedo, aut ad minimum ductus alicuius meridiani per certa quædam Germaniæ loca pervestigari poterit, præsupposito nimirum quodam cognito ad rationem eam, quam PTOLEMÆUS et COPERNICUS assumserunt. Hoc si mihi præstiteris officij, rem non saltem gratam, sed etiam Astronomiæ promotioni non inutilem effeceris. Idem quoque apud alios, quotquot novi cœlestibus apparentijs fideliter attendere, qui sane perpauci (quod dolendum est) existunt, experior, ut certi quid in his, quoad licuerit, statuatur. Ad ultima duo luminarium deliquia, quæ in Februario proxime elapso contigerunt, te sedulo attendisse non dubito; quare eorum etiam momenta et phases isthic animadversas indica. Lunæ Eclipsis, quæ 11. eius mensis die contigit, hîc ubi polus elevatur 53° 35½', principium habuit, quando superior Lunæ limbus attollebatur supra horizontem 24° fere. Hinc tempus provenit, adhibito viso loco Lunæ iuxta nostram restitutionem et refractionum impedimento sublato, H. 4. 14' post mediam noctem antecedentem. Finis deliquij infra horizontem evenit. Visa autem est Luna ferme tota offuscari, minima eius portiuncula ab umbra Terræ libera existente. Solis eclipsatio, quæ 25. die accidit, hîc, quantum inter recurrentes nubes animadvertere licuit, initium habuit H. 9. 52' A.M. per Armillas Æquatorias 6 pedum in diametro (nam Instrumenta mea Astronomica huc in Germaniam e Dania, ut conscius sis, transtuli) temporis momenta denotando. Medium eius, uti neque finem, ob crassiusculas et continuas nubes intervenientes conspicere non dabatur. Conveniunt hæc tempora in utraque Eclipsi satis præcise cum nostro redintegrato calculo, ex 18 Lunaribus et 6 Solaribus prius subtiliter denotatis eclipsationibus depromto, ut de alijs hincinde sæpenumero expensis amborum luminarium motionibus huc etiam conducentibus nihil addam. Quantitas corporis Solaris Lunæ interventu offuscati debuit iuxta mea ratiocinia esse digit. 10¾ in hoc horizonte, idque a superiori parte, cum calculus Coperniceus et phasin et momenta huius obscurationis aliter exhibeat, uti et in Lunari Eclipsi tam is quam Alphonsinus nimium cis et ultra exorbitârunt. An deliquij Solaris magnitudinem nostris numeris recte attigerimus, iudicent illi, qui eius medium sereniore aura usi conspexerunt. Id vero sciendum est, Lunam in novilunijs eclipticis non apparere ea magnitudine, qua alias in plenilunijs, utut in eadem fuerit a Terra remotione, sed quasi pro quinta parte eam certis de causis alibi reserandis constringere; unde fit ut Luna nunquam totum Solem prorsus obtegere queat, etiamsi centraliter quoad visum interponatur, lumine Solari eam aliquali adhuc vestigio ambiente, quod et in hoc ultimo deliquio animadvertere poterant illi, quibus maxima offuscatio patuit. Oportuit enim tunc Solem de Lunari corpore utrinque plus includere, idque corniculatim, quam linea per diametrum ducta exigebat, utut Lunæ diameter tunc fuerit iuxta nostrum etiam calculum 34¾', apparere autem apud Solem non potuit maior quam 28', quam limitationem a nemine antea animadversam cognovi, sed in aliquot Solis observationibus, cum tam a superiore quam inferiore parte tegeretur, sic me docuit experientia. Tuum erit, excell. MÆSTLINE, quid in his et similibus compertum habeas, mihi etiam aperire, atque in posterum iucundam inter nos de rebus Astronomicis collationem, quam et ego non invite admittam, exercere. De Eclipsium autem, potissimum Lunarium, a te subtiliter animadversis momentis me, quam primum fieri queat, certiorem redde, ut correctionem longitudinum, de qua dixi, quam brevi in quadam tabella adornare constitui, tam hinc, quam ex alijs quibusdam aliunde conquisitis indicijs eo promtius expediam. Iam vale, v. cl. et de rebus Astronomicis laudabiliter merite, in quibus etiam expoliendis et locupletandis ut in posterum perseveres, te adhortor. Ubi rescripseris, effice, ut literæ quocunque tandem viarum ductu, sive per Norimbergam, sive aliquam aliam celebrem Germaniæ urbem Hamburgum perferantur, in hospitium isthic meum, quod est apud HERM. REUTTER sub signo coronæ aureæ, sicque eas me certo obtenturum non ambigo.

Iamque iterum et sæpius vale.
Dabantur Wandesburgo die 21 Apr. 1598.
TYCHO BRAHE, manu propria.

To the most renowned and excellent man, Master Michael Maestlin, signally skilled in the Mathematical Arts, and most celebrated Professor of the same in the renowned Academy of Tübingen, by me most beloved. Tübingen.

Greeting.

It is now the tenth year, most learned and excellent Maestlin, since one of my household — who for some while had served me in Astronomical matters — sent me into Denmark a copy of your letter, written from Tübingen to him, in which you promised that you would diligently read through our book on the Comet of the year [15]77 (communicated to you by him), and write your opinion of its contents, and confer with me by letter about them — which you had resolved to send to Marburg, to that same man, to be afterward procured to me. But in all the intervening years I have received not even a little word from you — on this or any other matter concerning the Astronomical business by which we are both affected — whether by writing or by the report of others who, coming thence, visited me at Uraniborg while I still dwelt in my fatherland. I should indeed have thought that you, on account of our common studies, would not have refrained from at least some writing in so many passing years — especially since you had of your own accord undertaken to do it. But lest I should seem, on that account, to have spurned every occasion of contracting a familiarity with you by letter (which would be alien to humanity, and to the loftier exercises in which we delight) — behold, I now anticipate [you], and of my own accord invite you to write and reply: this more convenient opportunity being now given me than before in Denmark, since I now dwell in a certain Rantzau castle, but half a mile distant from Hamburg, whence a frequent and convenient messenger is given to various places, not only of Germany, but of almost all Europe. If, then, you have anything you would confer with me about in Astronomical matters — whether the Cometary business or anything else — you will find me, to my small measure (and so far as I have hitherto been able to attain in them), not difficult in replying.

There was lately brought to me a certain erudite writing of the excellent Mathematician Johannes Kepler, from Styria, in which, by an ingenious speculation indeed, he attempts to reduce the revolutions of the Planets (according to the Copernican form) harmonically to the 5 regular [Platonic] solids — about which matter he lately wrote me, and I in turn opened to him my view of such things, so far as the mass of [my] occupations allowed. But I see that in the same book [Kepler's Mysterium, with Maestlin's notes] you bring forth some things, as if doubtfully, about the Hypothesis I invented several years ago — since you promiscuously attribute it also to others (perhaps too much hearkening to my plagiarists), and infer more about it which, had you weighed it more considerately, and had a thorough view of my opinion and of all the occasions of establishing this Hypothesis, you would perhaps have abstained from such hesitations. But I, if any scruple remains with you in this invention, am ready to remove it, and to explain the whole matter more deeply — as the thing itself will also teach, when you have learned the Planets' motions, expanded by me so accurately to that norm that there is nothing the senses can further require — though you, in that same booklet, seem not only to doubt about the correction of Astronomy, but even to cast away [your] courage, although before you gave hope otherwise. If [Astronomy] is to be restored from the prior dimension made through those regular solids, rather than from accurate observations taken a posteriori (as you insinuate), we shall assuredly wait too long — if not utterly in vain forever — before any such thing can be furnished by anyone. For since the explication of those regular solids' dimension — if it were to be admitted in every part — must rest on, and be proved by, antecedent observations; nor can the particulars (beyond those more general things, however they stand) be derived hence with the precision that is needed — which I think does not escape you. Nor, moreover, are the biform inequalities of the Planets yet sufficiently extricated, which they excuse by a double eccentric or epicyclet; for the just proportion of these is unknown — to say nothing of the Apogees standing far otherwise than not only the Alphonsine, but the Copernican reckoning bears. Nay, that evident insinuation of an annual diversity is not yet sufficiently ascertained, so as to agree absolutely, in all its numbers, on every side — so that to search this out, from the Copernican proportions of circles, with the subtlety required, is impassable; of which matter too I have admonished the most learned Kepler by letter, and have indicated an example in Mars, where these differences are more perceptible. But of these and the like, it would be too prolix to pursue each here.

When my Astronomical works shall have seen the public light — which I now urge with great forces, no less than before in my fatherland — you will perceive, as I hope, that the Astronomical business can be restored otherwise than is thought, and that I have placed in it no contemptible labour; wherefore of these I add no more now. There is another matter, though of less moment, which yet conduces not a little to fully carrying out this business: namely, that diversity — not yet accurately enough explored — which the alteration of meridians introduces on the round globe of the Earth [longitude], about which I should wish to deal with you. If, then, you have several Eclipses, especially Lunar, observed there with exquisite diligence in past years (which I do not doubt), I would have you communicate their moments to me in good time, with the circumstances too of the observations — so that, a comparison being made, they can be compared with the 20 or more lunar eclipses I have diligently considered now over not a few past years. I think I shall find among those I have noted some which were also observed by you there; and thence the interval of meridians — or at least the draw of some meridian through certain places of Germany — can be investigated, presupposing, of course, something known, according to the reckoning which Ptolemy and Copernicus assumed. If you render me this service, you will do a thing not only welcome, but also not useless to the promotion of Astronomy. The same I seek too from others, as many as I know to attend faithfully to the celestial appearances — who are indeed very few (which is to be lamented) — that something certain may be established in these, so far as may be. To the last two eclipses of the luminaries, which happened in the February just past [1598], I do not doubt you attended diligently; wherefore indicate the moments and phases of these observed there too. The Lunar Eclipse which happened on the 11th of that month — here, where the pole is elevated 53° 35½′ [Wandsbek] — had its beginning when the Moon's upper limb was raised about 24° above the horizon. Hence the time comes out — using the Moon's seen place by our restitution, and the impediment of refractions removed — 4h 14′ after the preceding midnight. The end of the eclipse occurred below the horizon. The Moon was seen almost wholly obscured, the smallest little portion of it being free from the Earth's shadow. The Solar eclipse which fell on the 25th — here, as far as could be noted among recurring clouds — had its beginning at 9h 52′ A.M., by the Equatorial Armillae of 6 feet in diameter (for I have transferred my Astronomical Instruments hither into Germany from Denmark, that you may be aware), noting the moments of time. Its middle — as also its end — could not be seen, on account of the rather thick and continuous intervening clouds. These times, in both Eclipses, agree precisely enough with my restored calculation, drawn from 18 Lunar and 6 Solar eclipses previously subtly noted (to say nothing of the other motions of both luminaries, often weighed here and there, also conducing hither). The quantity of the Solar body obscured by the Moon's intervention ought, by my reckonings, to have been 10¾ digits in this horizon, and that from the upper part — whereas the Copernican calculation shows both the phase and the moments of this obscuration otherwise, as also in the Lunar Eclipse both it and the Alphonsine deviated too much, this side and beyond. Whether I have rightly attained the magnitude of the Solar eclipse by my numbers, let those judge who beheld its middle, enjoying clearer air.

But this must be known: that the Moon, in ecliptic new-moons [conjunctions], does not appear of that magnitude which it has otherwise in full-moons — even though it be at the same distance from the Earth — but constricts it, as it were by a fifth part, for certain causes to be disclosed elsewhere; whence it comes about that the Moon can never wholly cover the whole Sun, even if it be centrally interposed to the sight, the Solar light still surrounding it with some trace — which those to whom the maximum obscuration was visible could note even in this last eclipse. For the Sun then had to include more of the Lunar body on both sides — and that horn-wise [corniculatim] — than the line drawn through the diameter demanded; for although the Moon's diameter then was, by my calculation too, 34¾′, yet it could not appear, at the Sun, greater than 28′ — which limitation I have found noticed by no one before, but experience taught me so, in several observations of the Sun, when it was covered both from the upper and from the lower part. It will be for you, excellent Maestlin, to open to me too what you have ascertained in these and the like, and henceforth to exercise that pleasant collation between us about Astronomical matters, which I too will not unwillingly admit. But of the moments of the Eclipses — especially Lunar — subtly noted by you, make me more certain as soon as can be, that I may the more readily expedite the correction of longitudes of which I spoke (which I have resolved shortly to set forth in a certain little table), both from these and from certain other indications gathered elsewhere. Now farewell, most renowned man, and laudably deserving of Astronomical matters — in the polishing and enriching of which I exhort you also to persevere hereafter. When you have replied, see that the letters be carried to Hamburg, by whatever route at last — whether through Nuremberg or some other famous German city — to my lodging there, which is at Herman Reutter's, under the sign of the Golden Crown; and so I do not doubt I shall certainly obtain them.

And now again, and oftener, farewell.
Given at Wandsbek, the 21st day of April, 1598.
Tycho Brahe, in his own hand.

[Postscript:] Since I had understood that the courier would tarry the whole of this day at Hamburg, I have had a copy made of Kepler's letter to me, together with my reply, and joined to these — that you might be made the more certain of their contents too: where you will find more things bearing on this matter.

Apparatus

  • Recipient. Michael Maestlin (1550–1631), professor of mathematics at Tübingen — the Copernican astronomer who was Kepler's teacher and mentor (and the intermediary in the Ursus affair, cf. brahe-t_15990200001).
  • Significance — Tycho's engagement with Kepler's Mysterium, his empiricist credo, and the "look at Mars" hint (a momentous letter).
  • The renewed contact. After a ten-year silence (Maestlin had once promised, c. 1588, to comment on Tycho's Comet-of-1577 book but never did), Tycho — now at Wandsbek, "half a mile from Hamburg," with good post — reopens correspondence.
  • The Mysterium Cosmographicum critiqued. Tycho has read Kepler's "erudite writing … reducing the planets' revolutions, in the Copernican form, harmonically to the 5 regular [Platonic] solids" (the Mysterium Cosmographicum, 1596) — "an ingenious speculation indeed" — and has written Kepler his view. He objects that Maestlin's notes in the same book treat Tycho's world-system "doubtfully," attributing it "also to others" (heeding "my plagiarists" — Ursus) and that Maestlin seems to "despair of correcting astronomy."
  • Tycho's empiricist credo (the methodological core). The heart of the letter — and of Tychonic astronomy: "If astronomy is to be restored from the a priori dimension through those regular solids, rather than from accurate observations taken a posteriori … we shall wait too long, if not in vain forever." The Platonic-solid scheme "must rest on antecedent observations"; the planets' biform inequalities, the apogees (far off from Alphonsine and Copernican), and the annual equation are not yet pinned down. A frontal statement of observation-first empiricism against Kepler's a-priori Platonism.
  • "Look at Mars" — the seed of the Astronomia nova. Tycho records: "of which matter too I have admonished the most learned Kepler by letter, and have indicated an example in Mars, where these differences are more perceptible." This is the documentary trace of Tycho directing Kepler to Mars — the planet whose orbit Kepler would, on Tycho's data, crack open into the elliptical Astronomia nova (1609). A historically momentous pointer.
  • The eclipse-longitude project. Tycho asks Maestlin for lunar-eclipse timings to compare with his own "20+ lunar eclipses" and so determine meridian-differences (longitude) through German places — a "little table" he means to compile.
  • The February-1598 eclipses at Wandsbek (precise data). The lunar eclipse of 11 Feb 1598 (Wandsbek pole-height 53° 35½′; begin 4h 14′ after midnight; Moon nearly total); the solar eclipse of 25 Feb 1598 (begin 9h 52′ A.M., by the 6-foot Equatorial Armillae Tycho had brought from Denmark; 10¾ digits, from the upper part) — agreeing with Tycho's calculation "drawn from 18 lunar and 6 solar eclipses," against deviating Copernican/Alphonsine numbers.
  • The lunar-diameter-at-conjunction claim. A genuine (if physically mistaken) Tychonic empirical observation: the Moon at conjunction appears ~⅕ smaller than at opposition (34¾′ → only 28′ "at the Sun"), so it can never wholly cover the Sun — Tycho's explanation for the residual ring of solar light (bearing on the annular-eclipse question), "noticed by no one before."
  • Cross-references. Pair with Kepler's Ursus apologia (brahe-t_15990200001) (Maestlin the shared link); a keystone for the Tycho–Kepler convergence and the genesis of the Astronomia nova (the Mars hint); the eclipse-longitude thread (brahe-t_16000405001).
  • Persons & places named. Michael Maestlin; Johannes Kepler (the Mysterium); the unnamed former household-assistant; Ptolemy, Copernicus; the Alphonsine reckoning; Mars; Herman Reutter (Tycho's Hamburg lodging, "the Golden Crown"). Places: Wandsbek (the Rantzau castle), Hamburg, Tübingen, Marburg, Nuremberg, Uraniborg/Denmark.
