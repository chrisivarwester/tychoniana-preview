Editorial note. A capital document in the history of cosmology: Christoph Rothmann's long reply to Tycho, defending three theses — (i) the matter of the heavens is pure air, not a distinct aether (a sustained optical argument from refraction, citing Alhazen and Witelo); (ii) comet tails are of the head's own matter, self-luminous, and do not point at the Sun (proved from the Landgrave's record of the 1558 comet in Leovitius's Ephemerides); and (iii) an unreserved defence of Copernicanism against Tycho's "new hypotheses," which Rothmann reads as merely "inverted Copernicus." NOBILISSIMO ET VIRTVTE SAPIENTIA INGENIOSA ERVDITAQVE DOCTRINA, CLARISSIMO VIRO, DOMINO TYCHONI BRAHE, DOMINO DE KNVDSTRVP, EQVITI DANO, MATHEMATICORVM NOSTRO SECULO OMNIUM PRÆSTANTISSIMO, ETC. DOMINO SUO CUM OBSERUANTIA COLENDO, ETC. S. P.

ACCEPTATISSIMÆ mihi fuerunt vtræque tuæ literas, Nobilissime ac Præstantissime D. TYCHO, animoque meo longe gratissimæ, et priores cum Tomo secundo Phænomenon Cæli recentiorum, quas 11. Iulij a Gellio Sasceride accepi, et posteriores, quas iam. Verum ad has tibi prius respondebo, ad priores inferius. De materia igitur Cæli vt adhuc paucis tecum agam, peto mihi concedas. Ab ea enim diuelli non possum. Et nisi hæc quæstio a nobis decisa fuerit, a nullo vnquam, siue Theologo siue Physico decidetur. Non enim Deus in verbo suo quicquam de hac reuelauit, cum nihil ad salutem nostram pertineat, nec eiusmodi disputationes, quas pauci admodum etiam inter Doctos intelligunt, immiscere voluit sacris literis, quæ omnibus omnino hominibus, Indoctis pariter ac Doctis, Rudibus pariter ac Ingeniosis scriptæ sunt, vt et ipse Christus Ioan. 3. ad Nicodemum testatur. Physici quoque quomodo de hac quicquam certi scire poterunt? Tantum enim de eiusmodi sublimibus et tam procul a nobis dissitis rebus scimus ac intelligimus, quantum per Doctrinam Triangulorum et Mathematicas demonstrationes inuenimus. Sine his qui de eiusmodi rebus disserit, vanissimus est prorsusque delirat. Nec ego Aëris materiam vnquam introduxissem, nisi defectus Refractionum et Demonstrationes opticæ me ad id impulissent. Quousque enim verum erit, quod diuersa Diaphana efficiant Refractionem, eousque etiam mea assertio vera erit. Nec huic quicquam derogant ea quæ a te afferuntur. Quod enim a Physicis traditur, in Cælo esse nihil elementare, nihil corruptibile, nihil variabile, sed æterna omnia: tam facile negatur quam asseritur. Probandum enim est, cur ita sit et vnde illud sciatur, non pro hypothesi et axiomate concesso assumendum. Quod si cum Aristotele ideo id esse dixeris, quoniam nulla mutatio vnquam in corporibus cælestibus vel potius in magnitudine et claritate Siderum depræhensa sit, nihil dixeris. Nam nec magnitudo Terræ vnquam mutata est, et tamen in Terra et circa Terram sunt corruptibilia omnia. Nihil igitur magnitudinis perseuerantia in Stellis probat. Nec tamen interim nego, ipsa corpora cælestia esse perfectissima, simplicissima, nullique prorsus corruptioni aut generationi obnoxia; sed tantum de circumfusa materia loquor, quam non impurum sed purissimum dico esse Aërem, qui tamen ab Aëre puro infra Lunam nihil differat. Nam et hic nihil claritati Stellarum detrahit. Nec inde sequitur, si ipsa corpora sint perfectissima et ab Elementis diuersa, etiam circumfusam materiam perfectam esse oportere: sed potius, cum Cometæ in ea generentur, crescant, decrescant, et tandem etiam euanescant ac dissipentur, tantæque interdum sint magnitudinis, vt etiam quinquaginta Gradus occupare possint; non vsque adeo a mutatione et corruptione erit immunis, nec tam æterna, diuina et imperscrutabilis vt fingitur. Nec si huic rationi et superiori principio Optico innitor, Cælum (si modo circumfusa illa materia Cælum appellanda est; quanquam non ignorem, Aërem quoque interdum Cælum appellari.) Non inquam Cælum ideo aliqua labe aspersero, sed potius illud a vana et præter omnem causam et experientiam otiose afficta substantia liberauero, veramque sententiam substituero. Nec sonitus vllus e concitato Siderum motu in Aëre illo puro ciebitur. Quod enim Aër illisus et impulsus sonitum edat, id in hoc crasso a vento experti sumus (sonitum enim qui ratione vacui fit, huc non quadrare manifestum est) nec id caret ratione. Quoniam enim Aër hic crassus semper aliquid aqueum in se continet, Aqua vero sit eiusmodi res, quæ penetrationem subitam non admittat: non immerito Aër quoque crassus in impulsione et illisione resistet quodammodo et sonitum edet. At idem in puro quoque Aëre accidere, nemo facile probauerit. Eum enim esse tenuissimum ac rarissimum, satis inde liquet, quod hic crassus, licet tenuissimus putetur, tamen radios Solis etiam serenissimo tempore non irreflexos et sine lumine transmittat; Illum autem purum radij Solis absque vllo lumine et reflexione penetrent, vt ex Crepusculis manifestum est. Ideo ob tenuitatem et raritatem purus ille Aër illidi et impelli non poterit, sed absque vllo obstaculo cedet, adeoque perpetuitati reuolutionis cælestium corporum et puritati eorum longo vsu temporeque non detrahet, multo minus atterendo constantissimam harmoniam labefactabit. Quod si purissimus ille Aër hoc posset, vererer vt a vehementissimis ventorum flatibus in hoc crasso Aëre iamdudum tota Terra esset attrita et absumta, ac etiam de suo loco mota. Si item tantus sonus a puro illo Aëre excitari posset, vt nostrum auditum percelleret, omnes Superos præ nimio metu iamdudum suffocatos putarem. Multo enim magis sonitus crassi huius Aëris a vehementissimis ventis excitatus a Superis perciperetur, quam ille a nobis. Sed tamen memini, fuisse aliquando in loco quinque milliaribus hinc sito vehementissimum ventum, adeo vt ædificia et arbores euerteret; de cuius tamen sono ego hîc nihil audiebam. Adeo, quæ vera sunt, coniecturis euerti non possunt. De proportione quoque Elementorum scis quomodo PHILIPPVS MELANCHTHON in sua Physica Peripateticos reprehendat, veramque Aristotelis sententiam doceat. Et sane cum concedis, vsque ad Lunam Aërem elementarem esse, eo ipso proportionem Elementorum tollis. Cum enim vsque ad infimam Lunam, vt te inuenisse in secundo Tomo mihi transmisso testaris, sint Semidiametri Terræ 52. plus centies millies Aër maior erit ipso Terræ et Aquæ globo simul. Quæ autem proportio est 1. ad 100000. vel potius 1. ad 140608. vt interim de Terra ab Aqua separata taceam? Non igitur proportio Elementorum obstat. Sed nec mea ipsius demonstratio, vt obijcis. Non enim volui, in Altitudine 30. Grad. nullam amplius fieri posse Refractionem, sed hoc tantum tanquam petitum seu postulatum ex primis tuis literis ad Illustrissimum Principem nostrum missis assumsi, vt demonstrare possem, si detur altitudo, in qua Refractiones desinant, dari etiam quanto spatio radij Solis Aërem crassum irrefracti penetrent: Eademque demonstratio permanebit, licet Refractiones vix in 40. Altitudinis Gradu desinant. Cumque ex ea proueniat, vapores circa Horizontem sextuplo proxime densiores esse quam in Altitudine 30. partium, sequitur inde, minorem proportionem Refractiones efficere non posse in ea Altitudine; multo minus spatium eiusdem medij circa Horizontem, irrefracte penetratum maius esse eo quod refracte penetratur. Vide igitur an diuerso Ætheris et Aëris Diaphano ea demonstratio applicari possit. Quæ enim multiplicatio diuersi Diaphani aut potius Aëris hîc esse potest? Pone enim combinationem illam mutuam Ætheris et Aëris fieri in distantia 30. Semidiam. Terrenarum â superficie Terræ, in Horizonte quæ erit multiplicatio? Nondum enim in Horizonte 31. Semidiam. erunt. Scilicet radij tot Semidiametros Terrenas diuersi Diaphani irrefracti penetrare poterunt, vnicam autem Semidiametrum non possint? Quod vero existimas, etiamsi concedatur, quod nulla fiat Refractio in loco proxime sublunari, non tamen inde sequi, vt Cælum (si modo circumfusa illa materia, vt antea quoque dixi, Cælum appellanda est: de ea enim loquimur,) vt inquam Cælum ex Aëre Elementari constet: Illud longe aliter se habet. Quandocunque enim dantur diuersa Diaphana, datur etiam Refractio extra perpendicularem, et quandocunque Refractio extra perpendicularem tollitur, tollitur etiam diuersitas Diaphanorum, vt manifestum est ex capit. 2. et 4. Alhazeni lib. 7. et ex Vitellion. lib. 10. P. 3. et passim ex tota Optica. Diuersa enim media diuersimode transmittunt imagines rei visæ ad oculum, et cum primum medium fuerit subtilius et rarius quam secundum, imago facilius et velocius transmittitur per primum medium quam per secundum, et cum ad secundum peruenit, retardatur quasi, vt ex dictis Opticis notum est. Vnde quoque hîc locum habere non potest ea excusatio, quod media illa diuersa in mutua connexione sibi inuicem, vt sine causa fingis, assimilentur, et successiue alterum in alterum mutetur. Erit enim nihilominus in ea mutua combinatione et assimilatione punctum, in quo imago Stellarum quasi retardetur, quod est punctum Refractionis. Sæpius hyeme expertus sum, cum aperta fenestra starem in medio Obseruatorij in calido Aëre, rem visam in frigido Aëre consistentem refracte videri. Hîc certe nec similitudo, nec successiua combinatio et assimilatio Refractionem tollere potuerunt. Si vero media illa per se tam similia sunt, vt inter illa nulla sit differentia, cur diuersa appellas? cur non etiam sublunarem illum purum Aërem Æthera, seu, vt Plinius loquitur, Æthereum spiritum appellas? Ita enim tecum non pugnarem. Tantum enim id contendo, non esse diuersa Ætheris et Aëris puri Diaphana, cum nulla ab ijs generetur Refractio. Sed nescio quomodo adeo inhæreas tuæ sententiæ, vt etiam præter omnem experientiam concedas, Refractionem Ætheris et Aëris durare vsque ad verticem, cum tamen eam nunquam Instrumentis senseris. Certe hac ratione quiduis confingere licet. Sed tamen sequeretur inde, nullam Stellam præter eas, quæ verticem transeunt et in vertice sunt, vero lumine videri, verum confuso et debilitato. Omnis enim Refractio debilitat lucem et colorem rei visæ, vt demonstrat Alhazen lib. 7. capit. 7. et Vitellio, lib. 10. P. 10. Cum igitur diuersitas ea ex collatione Stellæ verticalis ad reliquas a vaporibus liberas non depræhendatur, nec Refractio concedi poterit. Verum tu hîc potius in omnia diuersa. Ais enim, has Ætheris et Aëris Refractiones infra 30. Gradum esse sensibiles, in altiori vero situ insensibiles, et tamen postea addis, Refractionem Solis, quam in Altitudine 30. Grad. inuenisti 1½. Minutorum, per solos vapores vix excusari posse. Vtinam diceres, eam totam esse a diuerso Ætheris et Aëris Diaphano, demonstrarem vtique, in Altitudine 45. Grad. eam adhuc debere esse vnius proxime Minuti. Ipso enim vero verius est, si infra 30. Gradum sensibilis aliqua Refractio Ætheris et Aëris concedatur, eam in altiori situ non tam subito insensibilem fieri posse. Nam ijsdem medijs permanentibus non tam subito mutari possunt Refractiones ipsæ, cum earum Anguli non tam subito decrescant, quinimo siue ab Aëre ad Aquam, siue ab Aëre ad vitrum, siue ab Aqua ad vitrum, siue contra fiant, eandem fere in crescendo et decrescendo retineant proportionem, vt ex Tabula in 8. P. 10. Vitellion. posita videre est. Ad quam etiam respiciens scripsi in superioribus literis, si circa Horizontem detur Refractio Solis 3. Minut. a diuerso Ætheris et Aëris Diaphano proueniens, dari eam in Altitudine 45. Graduum 1½. Minuti. Etsi enim aliqua, tamen non admodum dissimili proportione decrescent Refractiones ipsæ et Refractionum Anguli; Quod, si mihi tempus non deesset, facile Calculo examinarem. Quod autem circa Horizontem tam subito decrescunt Refractiones, id a meris vaporibus est, qui circa Terram et crassiores sunt, et, vt demonstraui, multiplicantur, et etiam a radijs a latere petuntur. Aliâs si eadem media manerent, eâdem quantitate eodemque modo disposita, tam subita mutatio Refractionum accidere non posset, vt demonstrari potest. Sed restat adhuc demonstratio, quam nemo vnquam infringere poterit. Si enim est Refractio a diuerso Ætheris et Aëris Diaphano proueniens, ea necessario maior erit in Stellis Fixis, quæ longius distant, quam in Sole, qui propior est, vt interim de Luna taceam. Nam in Triangulo manente vnico Angulo et vnico Latere, reliquis lateribus productis, Angulus reliquus ad manens latus maior redditur, per 21. P. 1. Euclid. Necessario enim idem Refractionis Angulus in eâdem Altitudine manebit, cum maneant eadem media, nec alius poterit esse Refractionis Angulus in Sole aut Luna, quam in Fixis. Ex quo manifestissimum est, si ponas, Refractionem in Sole in certa aliqua Altitudine insensibilem fieri, eam in Stellis Fixis in eadem Altitudine adhuc sensibilem esse oportere. Cum igitur per Obseruationes non hoc sed potius contrarium te ipso teste reperiatur: Refractio a diuerso Ætheris et Aëris Diaphano generari non poterit: Et propterea, vt sæpius iam ex Opticis probaui, Diaphana Ætheris et Aëris diuersa esse non possunt sed eadem. Si igitur tu purum illum Aërem Cælum appellare volueris, nihil pugno, tantum ne purus Aër a cælesti differre statuatur, adeoque diuersa Diaphana introducantur, cum vt alterum altero subtilius sit, defectus Refractionis non permittat. Quod autem ex Chymicis affers, latere in terrenis rebus quintam quandam essentiam, quam tu cælestem appellas, illud et credo et iucundissime cogito. Verumtamen inde non sequitur, quod hæc quinta essentia eadem sit cum illa materia quæ Planetis est circumfusa, sed potius eam â Sole generari. Quemadmodum enim Sol Microcosmi, vt iucundissime doces, ex sanguine et attracto Aëre (ex quo propter respirationem videtur ingenita sua natura subtiliorem partem retinere, reliquam respuere.) Quemadmodum inquam Sol Microcosmi ex Aëre et sanguine in dextro thalamo præparato spiritum vitalem in sinistro thalamo generat; Ita et Sol totius vniuersi ex Terra, Aqua et Aëre quintam illam essentiam in rebus naturalibus generare videtur. Aut si hæc essentia eadem est cum illa Planetis circumfusa materia, eadem erit et cum puro Aëre, propter adductas demonstrationes. Hæc paulo fusius, quam initio proposueram, de hac adhuc materia ad te perscribere volui, non quidem studio contradicendi, sed ex mera φιλαληθέια. Nec quicquam certi de generatione et materia Cometarum afferri poterit, nisi hæc quæstio fuerit decisa. Sed de hac pluribus, Deo dante, in nostris Stellarum Fixarum Obseruationibus agam, omissa tamen huius nostræ dissensionis mentione, ne hæc veritati rei forte quid detrahat. Nec etiam tuî, nisi sine reprehensione possim, mentionem facio, sed tunc potius omisso nomine res ipsas tracto.

Nunc ad Philosophica illa. Sed prius adhuc vnum. Satis mirari non possum, quod initium Crepusculi matutini circa Æquinoctia inueneris Sole vix 16½. Grad. infra Horizontem in circulo verticali depresso, cum ego finem Crepusculi vespertini circa Æquinoctium vernum hîc inuenerim Sole 24. Grad. infra Horizontem depresso, ita tamen, vt nullum amplius albedinis vestigium appareret. Tu igitur idem quoque tenta in vespertinis Crepusculis, vbi has Obseruationes exactiores esse arbitror propterea quod facilius animaduerti possit, quando vestigium albicantis Aëris desinat, quam quando incipiat, dubitante semper Obseruatore, an vestigium illud albedinis pertineat ad Crepusculum matutinum. Ego quoque in matutinis idem experiar. Ad dulcissima igitur illa ex intima et profunda Philosophia hausta nunc venio. Reliqua enim responsione non indigere videntur, nec de Eclipsibus quicquam hîc propter Cælum pluuiosum obseruare potui. Gratias igitur ago tibi quas possum maximas, quod me in secretioribus istis tam luculenter erudire dignatus es, atque vt id in posterum quoque facias, a te etiam atque etiam maiorem in modum peto. Cum ea legerem, pene exclamaui gaudio. Quam longe veriora sunt et pulchriora, quam quæ in Aristotele traduntur. Sed tu fac vt plura intelligam. Scripsisti in superioribus literis cum Rami mentionem faceres, omnia constare numeris, ponderibus, et mensura. Hoc igitur quid sit, etiam atque etiam velim mihi explices: addasque, si placet, de spiritibus Sulphure, Sale, et Mercurio, quomodo hi conueniant non solum alijs rebus naturalibus, verumetiam corporibus humanis; qui ex his vegetet, nutriat et augmentet corpus nostrum, qui sentire faciat, qui moueat; seu, vt vulgariter loquuntur, qui sit naturalis, qui vitalis, qui animalis. Admodum enim placet mihi vestra Medicina. Item de Fixatione spirituum, quomodo spiritus spiritum figere possit, et alter alterius anima sit, et cur a vobis anima dicatur medium quod spiritum et corpus vnit, cum in nostro corpore non anima sit vinculum spirituum, sed potius spiritus sint vinculum animæ. Item vnde calculus in homine generetur, et vnde Podagra existat. Si enim de his me certiorem feceris, nihil iucundius aut gratius quicquam mihi accidere poterit. Sed fortassis nimis impudenter te onero, nimiumque multus sum in responsione ad posteriores literas. Quapropter ad priores iam accedo.

Quod igitur in vtrisque tam posterioribus quam prioribus literis iudicium meum de ijs, quæ in secundo Cometarum Tomo mihi transmisso continentur, requiris; id equidem eiusmodi agnosco esse, facile vt negligi possit. Per te enim tanquam summus artifex ipse intelligis, quid esse possit, quid non possit. Sed tamen vt habeas quod expetis, ita rem deduxisti, Cometamque illum in æthere cursum suum absoluisse ita demonstrasti, vt nemo hominum artem saltem Mathematicam non ignorans, tibi contradicere possit: idque tam luculenter et perspicue, vt nemo luculentius aut enucleatius. Quapropter vide vt quamprimum reliquos quoque Tomos habeamus. Ita enim non tantum negotium Cometarum verumetiam Artem nostram summe promouebis. In tertio Capite facis mentionem maximæ Latitudinis Lunæ, quam et ipse tecum eandem fere inueni: sed Caput Draconis abest a Tabulis plus quatuor Gradibus, vt per distantias et Globum reperi, nec arbitror calculum minorem differentiam producturum, quod facile examinarem si tempus mihi non deesset. De materia Cometarum et quomodo generentur, nihil in eo reperire potui, nisi cap. 9. de generatione caudæ, de qua tamen an a Sole an vero a Venere, in cuius oppositas partes ad amussim, vt cap. 7. doces, toto apparitionis tempore porrecta fuit, generetur dubitas, sed tamen in Solis partes tandem inclinas, idque latente ac incomperta adhuc Optica ratione fieri posse putas. Verum me de tota caudæ generatione, an ea per transmissos radios fiat, dubitare non ignoras ex cap. 7. scripti mei ad te transmissi. Cum enim Cardani, Scaligeri, et Apiani sententiam de generatione caudæ recitassem, tandem subiunxi, me, vt ingenue faterer, in hac de generatione caudæ sententia potius aliorum iudicium quam meum sequi. Hærebat enim tunc temporis semper in animo, caudam eiusdem materiæ quamuis rarioris cum ipso Cometæ corpore esse oportere, propterea quod radij Solis etiamsi corpus Cometæ transeant, tamen post transitum sine materia, in qua similiter reflectantur, in puro illo Aëre apparere non possint: cum nec post Crystallum aut eiusmodi corpora lux Solis condensata appareat, nisi materiata res subijciatur, in quam Pyramis illa impingat: Item quod cauda illa sit dispersa, nec radij eius continuentur, sed interdum longe post caput abrupte apparere incipiant, et propter alias causas. Cum tamen eiusmodi caudatas Stellas nunquam obseruassem, nec dum formam et habitum caudæ diligenter in vlla considerassem, videremque opinionem illam a nullo esse repræhensam: tantis et tam summis Viris cessi, quod tamen sine protestatione, vt scis, facere non potui. Fuisse vero iudicium illud meum de cauda contra summos illos viros verum, superiore hyeme cognoui. Cum enim forte fortuna inciderem in illas Cypriani Ephemeridas, quas deauratas vna cum sacris Biblijs et Globo seipsum mouente paruoque Quadrante semper secum vehere solet Illustrissimus Princeps noster; in principio earum ita scriptum reperi. Anno post exhibitam toti Terrarum orbi salutem supra millesimum quingentesimum quinquagesimo octauo comparuit Cometa, qui 13. Calend. Septemb. obseruamus est ab Illustrissimo Principe ac Domino, D. To the most noble and — in virtue, wisdom, and ingenious and erudite learning — most distinguished man, Lord Tycho Brahe, Lord of Knudstrup, Danish Knight, of all the Mathematicians of our age the most excellent, etc., his Lord to be cultivated with observance, etc. Heartiest greeting.

Most acceptable to me were both your letters, most Noble and most Excellent Lord Tycho, and far most welcome to my mind — both the earlier, with the Second Tome of the recent Phenomena of the Heaven [De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis], which I received on 11 July from Gellius Sascerides, and the later, which [I received] just now. But I will reply to these [latter] first, to the earlier below. About the matter of the Heaven, then, that I may still deal with you in few [words], I ask you to grant me [leave]. For I cannot be torn from it. And unless this question be decided by us, it will never be decided by anyone, whether Theologian or Physicist. For God revealed nothing about this in his word, since it pertains nothing to our salvation; nor did he wish to mix into the sacred writings disputations of this kind, which very few even among the Learned understand — [writings] which are written for absolutely all men, the Unlearned equally with the Learned, the Rude equally with the Ingenious, as Christ himself testifies (John 3) to Nicodemus. And how will the Physicists be able to know anything certain about this? For we know and understand only so much about such sublime things, so far removed from us, as we find out through the Doctrine of Triangles and Mathematical demonstrations. Without these, whoever discourses about such things is most empty and raves utterly. Nor would I ever have introduced the matter of Air, had not the defect of Refractions and the optical Demonstrations impelled me to it. For as far as it shall be true that diverse Transparencies [Diaphana] produce Refraction, so far also will my assertion be true. Nor do those [things] which are brought forward by you derogate anything from this. For what is handed down by the Physicists — that in the Heaven there is nothing elemental, nothing corruptible, nothing variable, but all things eternal — is as easily denied as asserted. For one must prove why it is so, and whence it is known, not assume [it] as a granted hypothesis and axiom. And if you should say, with Aristotle, that it is so because no change was ever detected in the celestial bodies — or rather in the magnitude and brightness of the Stars — you will have said nothing. For neither was the magnitude of the Earth ever changed, and yet in the Earth and about the Earth all things are corruptible. The perseverance of magnitude in the Stars therefore proves nothing. Nor yet do I meanwhile deny that the celestial bodies themselves are most perfect, most simple, and subject to no corruption or generation at all; but I speak only of the surrounding matter, which I say is not impure but most pure Air, which nevertheless differs in nothing from the pure Air below the Moon. For this too detracts nothing from the brightness of the Stars. Nor does it follow, if the bodies themselves are most perfect and diverse from the Elements, that the surrounding matter too must be perfect; but rather, since Comets are generated in it, grow, decrease, and at last also vanish and are dissipated — and are sometimes of such magnitude that they can occupy even fifty degrees — it will not be so far immune from change and corruption, nor so eternal, divine, and inscrutable as is feigned. Nor, if I rely on this reasoning and the foregoing Optical principle, shall I [thereby] sprinkle the Heaven (if indeed that surrounding matter is to be called Heaven — though I am not ignorant that Air too is sometimes called Heaven) — I say I shall not on that account sprinkle the Heaven with any stain, but rather shall free it from a vain substance, idly feigned beyond all cause and experience, and substitute the true opinion. Nor will any sound be stirred up from the violent motion of the Stars in that pure Air. For that Air, struck and impelled, gives a sound, we have experienced in this gross [air] from the wind (for the sound which comes about by reason of a vacuum manifestly does not fit here); nor is that without reason. For since this gross Air always contains something watery in itself, and Water is a thing of the kind that does not admit sudden penetration, not undeservedly the gross Air too, in impulsion and striking, resists somehow and gives a sound. But that the same happens in pure Air too, no one will easily prove. For that it is most tenuous and most rare is clear enough from this: that this gross [air], though thought most tenuous, nevertheless transmits the Sun's rays, even in the most serene weather, not unreflected and without light; but that pure [air] the Sun's rays penetrate without any light and reflection, as is manifest from the Twilights [Crepuscula]. Therefore, on account of [its] tenuity and rarity, that pure Air cannot be struck and impelled, but yields without any obstacle, and so detracts nothing from the perpetuity of the revolution of the celestial bodies and from their purity by long use and time — much less, by wearing [them down], will it shake the most constant harmony. For if that purest Air could do this, I should fear lest, from the most violent blasts of the winds in this gross Air, the whole Earth had long since been worn away and consumed, and even moved from its place. Likewise, if so great a sound could be excited from that pure Air as to strike our hearing, I should think all the Gods above [Superi] long since suffocated from excessive fear. For much more would the sound of this gross Air, excited by the most violent winds, be perceived by the Gods above than that [pure-air sound] by us. But yet I remember that there was once, in a place situated five miles from here, a most violent wind, so much so that it overturned buildings and trees; of whose sound, however, I heard nothing here. So [true is it that] the things which are true cannot be overturned by conjectures. About the proportion of the Elements too, you know how Philip Melanchthon in his Physics [Initia doctrinae physicae] reprehends the Peripatetics and teaches the true opinion of Aristotle. And surely, when you grant that up to the Moon the Air is elemental, by that very [fact] you abolish the proportion of the Elements. For since up to the lowest [point of] the Moon there are, as you testify you found in the second Tome sent to me, 52 Earth-radii, the Air will be more than a hundred thousand times greater than the globe of Earth and Water together. But what is the proportion 1 to 100,000 — or rather 1 to 140,608 — to say nothing meanwhile of the Earth separated from the Water? The proportion of the Elements, therefore, does not stand in the way. But neither does my own demonstration, as you object. For I did not mean that at an Altitude of 30° no Refraction can any longer occur, but I assumed this only as a thing-postulated (petitum or postulate) from your first letter sent to our most Illustrious Prince, so that I might demonstrate [that], if an altitude be given at which the Refractions cease, there is also given how great a space the Sun's rays penetrate the gross Air unrefracted; and the same demonstration will hold, even if the Refractions scarcely cease at the 40th degree of Altitude. And since from it [it] comes out that the vapours about the Horizon are nearly sixfold denser than at an Altitude of 30°, it follows thence that the Refractions cannot produce a smaller proportion at that Altitude — much less [can] the space of the same medium about the Horizon, penetrated unrefracted, be greater than that which is penetrated refracted. See, therefore, whether that demonstration can be applied to a diverse Transparency of aether and air. For what multiplication of the diverse Transparency, or rather of Air, can there be here? For suppose that mutual combination of aether and air to occur at a distance of 30 Earth-radii from the Earth's surface: at the Horizon, what will the multiplication be? For at the Horizon there will not yet be 31 radii. Forsooth, will the rays be able to penetrate so many Earth-radii of the diverse Transparency unrefracted, but not be able [to penetrate] a single radius [of it]? But as to what you think — that even if it be granted that no Refraction occurs in the place nearest the sublunary [region], it does not yet follow that the Heaven (if indeed that surrounding matter, as I said before too, is to be called Heaven — for of it we speak), that, I say, the Heaven consists of elemental Air — that is far otherwise. For whenever diverse Transparencies are given, Refraction outside the perpendicular is also given; and whenever Refraction outside the perpendicular is removed, the diversity of Transparencies is also removed — as is manifest from chapters 2 and 4 of Alhazen book 7, and from Witelo book 10, prop. 3, and everywhere throughout the whole of Optics. For diverse media transmit the images of the thing seen to the eye in diverse ways; and when the first medium is subtler and rarer than the second, the image is more easily and swiftly transmitted through the first medium than through the second, and when it reaches the second, it is as it were retarded, as is known from the said Optical [writers]. Whence too that excuse cannot have place here — that those diverse media, in mutual connexion, are assimilated to one another (as you feign without cause), and one is successively changed into the other. For there will nonetheless be, in that mutual combination and assimilation, a point at which the image of the Stars is as it were retarded — which is the point of Refraction. Often in winter I have experienced, when I stood with the window open in the middle of the Observatory in warm Air, that a thing seen standing in cold Air is seen refracted. Here certainly neither similitude nor successive combination and assimilation could remove the Refraction. But if those media are in themselves so similar that there is no difference between them, why do you call [them] diverse? Why do you not call even that sublunary pure Air "aether," or, as Pliny speaks, "aethereal spirit"? For thus I should not be fighting with you. For I contend only this: that the Transparencies of aether and of pure air are not diverse, since no Refraction is generated by them. But I know not how you so cleave to your opinion that, even beyond all experience, you grant that the Refraction of aether and air lasts up to the zenith — though you have never sensed it with Instruments. Certainly by this reasoning one may feign anything. But yet it would follow thence that no Star, except those which pass the zenith and are at the zenith, is seen with true light, but [with light] confused and weakened. For every Refraction weakens the light and colour of the thing seen, as Alhazen book 7, chapter 7 and Witelo book 10, prop. 10 demonstrate. Since therefore that diversity is not detected from the comparison of a zenith-Star to the rest free from vapours, neither will Refraction be able to be granted. But you here [go] rather into all things diverse [self-contradiction]. For you say that these Refractions of aether and air are sensible below 30°, but in a higher position insensible; and yet afterward you add that the Refraction of the Sun, which you found at an Altitude of 30° [to be] 1½ minutes, can scarcely be excused by vapours alone. Would that you said it is wholly from the diverse Transparency of aether and air — I should certainly demonstrate that at an Altitude of 45° it ought still to be nearly one minute. For it is truer than truth itself that, if some sensible Refraction of aether and air be granted below 30°, it cannot become insensible so suddenly in a higher position. For, the same media remaining, the Refractions themselves cannot be changed so suddenly, since their Angles do not decrease so suddenly — nay, whether they occur from Air to Water, or from Air to glass, or from Water to glass, or contrariwise, they retain nearly the same proportion in increasing and decreasing, as may be seen from the Table set in Witelo book 10, prop. 8. Looking to which too I wrote in [my] earlier letter that, if about the Horizon there be given a Solar Refraction of 3 minutes arising from the diverse Transparency of aether and air, it is given at an Altitude of 45° [as] 1½ minutes. For although the Refractions themselves and the Angles of Refraction will decrease by some — yet not very dissimilar — proportion; which, if time did not fail me, I would easily examine by Calculation. But that the Refractions decrease so suddenly about the Horizon, that is from the mere vapours, which about the Earth are both grosser and (as I demonstrated) multiplied, and are also sought [reached] by rays from the side. Otherwise, if the same media remained, disposed in the same quantity and the same manner, so sudden a change of Refractions could not happen, as can be demonstrated. But there still remains a demonstration which no one will ever be able to break. For if there is a Refraction arising from the diverse Transparency of aether and air, it will necessarily be greater in the Fixed Stars, which are farther distant, than in the Sun, which is nearer (to say nothing meanwhile of the Moon). For in a Triangle, one Angle and one Side remaining, the other sides being produced, the remaining Angle at the remaining side is made greater, by Euclid I.21. For necessarily the same Angle of Refraction will remain at the same Altitude, since the same media remain; nor can the Angle of Refraction in the Sun or Moon be other than in the Fixed [stars]. From which it is most manifest that, if you posit the Refraction in the Sun to become insensible at some certain Altitude, it ought still to be sensible in the Fixed Stars at the same Altitude. Since, therefore, through Observations not this but rather the contrary is found, you yourself being witness — Refraction cannot be generated by a diverse Transparency of aether and air; and therefore, as I have several times now proved from Optics, the Transparencies of aether and air cannot be diverse, but [are] the same. If therefore you wish to call that pure Air "Heaven," I do not fight at all — only let not pure Air be determined to differ from the celestial, and so let not diverse Transparencies be introduced, since the defect of Refraction does not permit that one be subtler than the other. But as to what you bring from the Chymists — that there lurks in earthly things a certain fifth essence, which you call celestial — that I both believe and most pleasantly contemplate. Nevertheless it does not follow thence that this fifth essence is the same as that matter which is poured around the Planets; but rather that it is generated by the Sun. For just as the Sun of the Microcosm (as you most pleasantly teach), from blood and attracted Air (from which, on account of respiration, its inborn nature seems to retain the subtler part and reject the rest) — just as, I say, the Sun of the Microcosm, from Air and blood prepared in the right chamber [of the heart], generates the vital spirit in the left chamber — so too the Sun of the whole universe, from Earth, Water, and Air, seems to generate that fifth essence in natural things. Or, if this essence is the same as that matter poured around the Planets, it will be the same also as the pure Air, on account of the demonstrations adduced. These [things], a little more fully than I had proposed at the beginning, I wished to write out to you about this matter still — not indeed from a zeal for contradicting, but from mere love of truth [φιλαληθέια]. Nor can anything certain about the generation and matter of Comets be brought forward, unless this question be decided. But about this I will deal at more length, God granting, in our Observations of the Fixed Stars — omitting, however, mention of this our dissension, lest it perchance detract something from the truth of the matter. Nor do I make mention even of you, unless I can [do so] without reproach; but then rather, the name omitted, I treat the things themselves.

Now to those Philosophical [matters]. But first one [thing] still. I cannot sufficiently wonder that you have found the beginning of morning Twilight, about the equinoxes, [with] the Sun scarcely 16½° below the Horizon, depressed in the vertical circle — whereas I have found the end of evening Twilight about the vernal equinox here [with] the Sun depressed 24° below the Horizon, in such a way, however, that no further trace of whiteness appeared. You, therefore, attempt the same too in the evening Twilights, where I judge these Observations to be more exact, because it can more easily be noticed when the trace of whitening Air ceases than when it begins — the Observer always doubting whether that trace of whiteness pertains to the morning Twilight. I too will try the same in the morning [twilights]. To those sweetest [things], then, drawn from the inmost and profound Philosophy, I now come. For the rest do not seem to need a reply; nor could I observe anything here about Eclipses, on account of the rainy Sky. I give you, therefore, the greatest thanks I can, that you have deigned to instruct me so amply in those more secret [matters]; and I ask you again and again, in greater measure, that you do this in the future too. When I read them, I almost cried out for joy. How far truer and more beautiful they are than what is handed down in Aristotle! But do you make me understand more. You wrote in [your] earlier letter, when you made mention of Ramus, that all things consist of number, weight, and measure. What this is, then, I would wish you to explain to me again and again; and add, if it please, about the spirits — Sulphur, Salt, and Mercury — how these agree not only with other natural things but also with human bodies; which of these vegetates, nourishes, and increases our body, which makes [it] feel, which moves [it]; or, as they commonly speak, which is the natural, which the vital, which the animal [spirit]. For your Medicine pleases me greatly. Likewise about the Fixation of spirits: how spirit can fix spirit, and one be the soul of the other, and why by you the soul is said [to be] the medium that unites spirit and body, whereas in our body the soul is not the bond of the spirits, but rather the spirits are the bond of the soul. Likewise whence the stone [calculus] is generated in man, and whence Gout [Podagra] arises. For if you inform me about these, nothing more pleasant or welcome could befall me. But perhaps I burden you too impudently, and am too long in [my] reply to the later letter. Wherefore I now proceed to the earlier.

As to your requiring, then, in both letters — the later as well as the earlier — my judgment about those [things] contained in the second Tome of the Comet[s] sent to me: I indeed acknowledge it to be of such a kind that it can easily be neglected. For through yourself, as a supreme artificer, you understand what can be, what cannot. But yet, that you may have what you seek: you have so carried out the matter, and so demonstrated that that Comet completed its course in the aether, that no man not ignorant of the Mathematical art at least can contradict you — and that so amply and clearly that none [could do so] more amply or more unfoldedly. Wherefore see that we have the remaining Tomes too as soon as possible. For thus you will most greatly promote not only the business of Comets but also our Art. In the third Chapter you make mention of the maximum Latitude of the Moon, which I too found nearly the same as you; but the Head of the Dragon [the ascending node] is distant from the Tables by more than four degrees, as I found by distances and the Globe, nor do I think the calculation will produce a smaller difference — which I would easily examine if time did not fail me. About the matter of Comets and how they are generated, I could find nothing in it, except in chapter 9, on the generation of the tail — about which, however, whether it is generated from the Sun or from Venus (into whose opposite parts exactly, as you teach in chapter 7, it was stretched out through the whole time of the apparition), you doubt; but yet you at last incline to the Sun's side, and think this can happen by a still-latent and undiscovered Optical reasoning. But you are not ignorant, from chapter 7 of my writing sent to you, that I doubt about the whole generation of the tail — whether it occurs through transmitted rays. For when I had recited the opinion of Cardano, Scaliger, and Apian about the generation of the tail, I at last subjoined that I, to confess frankly, in this opinion about the generation of the tail follow the judgment of others rather than my own. For there always stuck then in my mind that the tail must be of the same matter — though rarer — as the body of the Comet itself, because the Sun's rays, even if they pass through the Comet's body, yet after the passage cannot appear, without matter in which they may similarly be reflected, in that pure Air — since not even after Crystal or bodies of that kind does the condensed light of the Sun appear, unless a material thing be set [under it] against which that Pyramid [cone of rays] strikes; likewise because that tail is dispersed, nor are its rays continuous, but sometimes begin to appear abruptly far behind the head, and for other causes. Since, however, I had never observed such tailed Stars, nor had yet diligently considered the form and habit of the tail in any [comet], and saw that opinion reprehended by no one, I yielded to such and so supreme Men — which, however, as you know, I could not do without protestation. But that that judgment of mine about the tail, against those supreme men, was true, I learned last winter. For when by chance I happened upon those Ephemerides of Cyprian [Leovitius], which, gilded, together with the sacred Bible and a self-moving Globe and a small Quadrant, our most Illustrious Prince is wont always to carry with him, I found written at the beginning of them thus: "In the year 1558 after the salvation shown to the whole globe of lands, there appeared a Comet, which on the 13th [day before the] Kalends of September [20 Aug] was observed by the most Illustrious Prince and Lord, Lord Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, by a Torquetum, about the ninth hour, at 21° of Virgo [♍], distant in Latitude 31° from the Ecliptic, stretching its coma toward the extremity of the tail of Ursa Major, while it itself was in the Asterism of Coma Berenices, where there are stars Venereal and Lunar. Thence on the 12th [before the] Kalends [21 Aug] it advanced to 23° of Virgo, [but] in Latitude it advanced either little or nothing. Lastly, on the 10th [before the] Kalends [23 Aug], advanced to 28° of Virgo, into 35½° of Latitude. And since it was quite obscure, it ceased to be observed by the most Illustrious Prince. The Length of the tail could not easily be attained by sight. It is established that the Pole of the Comet's motion was not far from Alrucaba [the pole-star region] toward the head of Ursa Major." Such was the whole note. From which it is most manifest that the tail of that Comet regarded neither the Sun nor any other Planet, but appeared by itself, by its own course. Wherefore I now establish that the tails are of the same matter as the head, and appear in the same way as the head, by illumination — which is also manifest from this: that the head of the Comet, in its disappearance, appears dispersed in the same way as the tail. Nor is that curvature of the tail, as I think, rightly excused by you, since nothing such appears in the Stars and Planets similarly existing in a straight line, especially in so small an interval as that tail was. Do you see whether this my opinion can be true. For thus those great Comets in Seneca and Pontanus — of which the one equalled the whole Milky Way, the other occupied fifty degrees — will be easily explained, and the curvatures of the tails will need no excuse. But although you do not assert what kind of matter of Comets you posit, yet at the end of the Work, dealing with Graminaeus, you seem to reject my opinion and to build up that the opinion is Aristotle's. But [mine] differs much from Aristotle's opinion. For he posits it [the matter] tenacious, slow, gross, and viscous; I [posit it] tenuous, light, and thoroughly purged and concocted by the rays. He wills it to be burned and to appear on account of the burning; I [will] it not to be burned but to be consumed and dispersed by the rays of the Stars, and to appear by illumination. He [places it] below the Moon; I [place it] not only below the Moon but also in the Spheres of the Planets. What your opinion is, I do not know. For I am a Davus, not an Oedipus [no riddle-solver]. From the Title, however — On the new generations of the aethereal World — which you wished [it] to be thus, in the letters both to our most Illustrious Prince and also in the recent [ones] sent to me, I conjecture that you, according to your opinion, make it celestial, and introduce generation into the Heaven. Whether, then, you think it is exhaled from the bodies of the Planets, or otherwise generated by them, I cannot know. This, I think, is certain: that, since you introduce generation into the Heaven, you also introduce corruption. For according to your Art, generation does not exist except from corruption. But I cannot deal more with this matter, since I am ignorant of your opinion.

About your new Hypotheses too, of which you make mention in the 8th chapter, I do not follow you in all [respects]. I myself indeed, when I was writing my Astronomical Elements, in the same way placed the Moon, then the Sun, about the Earth — but in homocentric-epicycle-upon-epicycle [constructions] [Homocentrepicyclepicyclis]; and at the centre of the greater and first Epicycle of the Sun, again [I placed] the homocentric-epicycle-upon-epicycles of the remaining Planets, so that the centre of the homocentrics in the remaining Planets was always the same as the centre of the greater Epicycle of the Sun, and was carried around together, through the mean motion of the Sun, in the homocentric of the Sun. But I did this by no new reasoning, but entirely according to Copernicus; nor could I do otherwise, so as to transfer conversely, through all [points], the Hypotheses of Copernicus to the mobility of the Sun; and I saw that both Rheticus and Reinhold had understood that reasoning. And according to this manner our most Illustrious Prince too had, last year, an automaton [ἀυτόματον] constructed — of wondrous smallness indeed, but yet exhibiting the motions of all the Planets. For it is a little orb, flat on both sides, containing scarcely 6 inches in diameter, on one plane face of which is contained the Theoric of the Moon with its Epicycles and the Head of the Dragon; but on the other, the Theorics of the Sun and of all the remaining Planets; and all the motions are exhibited, not only of the Anomalies and of the centres, and the true [motion] in Longitude, but also, in the three superior [planets], in Latitude. But whether your Hypotheses are the same with these and agree, I cannot know for certain. For although I see clearly enough that the general sketch [Hypotyposis] and order of the Spheres, and the very circles of the Planets — whether homocentric or eccentric — are the same, yet, since you call yours new, and write in [your] recent letter that they satisfy the particular appearances most aptly on every side (which, however, neither of the others does in every way), they seem to be diverse from the inverted Copernican [hypotheses]. For if they were the same, they would not satisfy the appearances more than the Copernican [hypotheses] themselves, since whatever appearances arise from the Copernican [hypotheses] themselves, those too arise entirely, through all [points], from the inverted [ones] without any difference. Then you seem to carry the centres of the homocentrics of the remaining Planets around not in the homocentric or centre of the greater Epicycle, but in the very Eccentric of the Sun — since you do the same too in the Comet. And if it were so, they would deservedly be new and diverse, since by this reasoning the Eccentricity of the Sun would necessarily mix itself into all the Planets, and would introduce other appearances. But that the Observations answer to this supposition, I should scarcely believe. I know indeed that Copernicus demonstrates that the change of the Sun's Eccentricity is noticed in the remaining Planets too, which, however, he neglected in the Tables as less sensible. (Whence I too transposed that little Epicycle of the Sun into the greater Epicycle, which otherwise would have had to be placed back at the Earth, in such a way that it would have passed through the centre of the Earth by its lowest part.) Nevertheless, that is altogether necessary from his Hypothesis, since by the Eccentric of the Sun he understands the Eccentric of the Earth, whose centre, if it be changed, will necessarily change the Eccentricities of the remaining Planets, since they all regard this centre and their magnitudes are numbered from this centre. Whether, then, as I said, yours are entirely the same with the inverted Copernican [hypotheses], I cannot know for certain. But as to what you think — that the more diligently I shall have surveyed that general disposition of the Spheres, the more it will please me — know that the entirely contrary has long since happened to me (about which [I have spoken] also with the most Erudite man Gellius Sascerides). For the longer I consider it, the less it pleases me. For although it shows how, according to the mean motion of the Sun, the superior Planets are rendered direct, stationary, or retrograde, yet (which the Ptolemaic [system] also does) it only shows that they so come about, not why they so come about, and sprinkles the Planets with the stain of retrogression. But the Copernican [system] shows not only that those retrogressions and stations occur, but also why they so occur — not that there is so great an inconstancy of motion in the Planets themselves, which always regard their own proper motion, but that those retrogressions so appear to us on account of the motion of the Earth, and are thought to be in the Planets, whereas in reality they are in the Earth. Thus most beautifully no motion is left except the proper [one]: the first in the diurnal Revolution of the Earth, the second in its annual motion, the third in the Moon, the fourth in Venus, the fifth in Mercury, the sixth in Mars, the seventh in Jupiter, and the eighth at last in Saturn — without any stain of violence or retrogression. Then, who will ever believe that the Centre of the greater Epicycle of the Sun is endowed with such efficacy that it can drag all the Planets after itself — nay, drag [them] from their Spheres and lift [them] up again — when they are connected by no corporeal and tenacious matter? For which cause too, when I showed our most Illustrious Prince that Hypothesis of yours in your second Tome of the Comet[s], which he soon, as he beheld it, recognized (being sufficiently known to him from the Automaton): "Good gods," he said in jest, "that circle of the Sun must be far stronger than bronze, since it can drag so many Planets with it." For there stuck in his mind what I had so often sung into his most Illustrious Highness's ear — that the motion of Traction must be removed from the Heaven. Nor, as I think, will it easily be able to be granted to the Sun that it disturb the proper motions of the remaining Planets by this its traction. Besides, this inverted Copernican reasoning introduces confusion into the Spheres of the Planets. For although no collision of celestial bodies can follow thence (as you strive to excuse), yet no true or determined distinction of the Spheres remains, but all are confused among themselves. But the Copernican Hypothesis attributes to each Planet its own and determined space, from which it cannot go out by its motion. But you know that God is the author not of confusion but of order, and so founded nature that it abhors confusion — much less would he have wished to look upon this most beautiful edifice of inexhaustible wisdom with so great a stain. There is added this too: that Nature does not act through more [means] when it can [act] through fewer. Likewise that the change of the Sun's Eccentricity is felt in the remaining Planets too — which, although it can in some way be excused by the transposition of the Sun's lesser Epicycle to the Earth (as I said), yet nonetheless all things are by this reasoning feigned to come about through traction. These, then, and many other [things] considering again and again, I can find nothing other than that no Hypothesis except the single one of Copernicus is true. Nor does it sin against Physical principles; nor, because the Earth is a gross body, will it on that account be unfit for moving. For what do you think the other Stars are? For I think Copernicus does not err when he says that gravity is nothing other than a certain natural appetite implanted in the parts by the divine providence of the Maker of the universe, that they may bring themselves into their unity and integrity, coming together into the form of a globe; and that this affection is also in the Sun, the Moon, and the other lights of the Wanderers, so that by its efficacy they remain in that roundness in which they present themselves — which nonetheless effect their circuits in many ways. And surely, since the Earth, like the other Planets, is endowed with a round figure, and likewise hangs free in the Air, why do we say it is unfit for moving? We see that if we erect or suspend a globe most exactly and subtly on [its] Poles and Axis, and so, hanging freely, impel it into a gyre, it retains this motion long enough, nor can it suddenly be checked. If, therefore, an artificial motion, even impeded, can do this, how much more a natural [one], unimpeded? But the Physical absurdities Copernicus has more than sufficiently refuted. Nor does the authority of the sacred writings stand in the way either. For these are written not for me and you alone, but for absolutely all men, to whose grasp they also speak — as all the Theologians too confess in the explication of Genesis chapter 1. Otherwise the Moon would, against the Geometrical demonstrations, be greater than the rest of the Stars. But this too is manifest from the following. The Hebrews thought that the clouds could not hang on high unless there underlay some hard and impervious matter which sustained the Water. Accommodating himself, therefore, to their grasp, God says, "I will make the Heaven iron, lest it rain," etc.; likewise, "he opened the windows of Heaven" in the Flood, etc.; likewise Moses, when he says that on the first day the Firmament was founded, to separate the Waters above us from the Waters below us. By which I think nothing else can be understood than that that force, by which the clouds so hang on high, was founded on the first day. So too, since we for Optical reasons think the Heaven to be as it were a stretched-out tent, therefore the sacred writings, following this opinion of the common [people], call the Heaven "stretched-out" [expansum], and the Prophets say that God stretches out the Heaven like a tent. For neither did these understand more about this matter than other common men, or even than Lactantius; nor did the Holy Spirit wish to reveal to them the wisdom which God poured into Nature, but that which is contained in that stupendous and supernatural mystery of the Redemption of the human race. And why was Solomon held wise even to a miracle, except that he had a far more excellent knowledge of the whole of Nature than anyone else before him? Paul too, when he says (Romans 1) that God is recognized from these visible [things], argues not obscurely that a far greater wisdom of God lies hidden in Nature than is revealed in the sacred writings. In Nature, I say: for of the mystery of Redemption, which the sacred writings properly treat, and of the recognition of the true God, I now do not speak. Thus the authority of the sacred writings, although it be brought forward quite plausibly, yet in reality in this matter will be able to stand in the way not at all; but we shall know only so much as we find out through Mathematical Demonstrations. But although Gellius Sascerides said that you have Geometrical Demonstrations of your assertion, yet this I, for a certain cause, should scarcely believe. Yet I do not on that account judge that the Copernican Hypotheses are to be proposed to learners, since they are scarcely perceived by the Artificers [skilled] — as not even Maestlin rightly understands the motion of libration in the Latitudes and the obliquity of the Zodiac; but [I judge] that those [hypotheses] which are easiest of all [should be proposed], so that step by step the access to Copernicus may be opened. Wherefore I too, in my Astronomical Elements, expunged that foregoing inversion, and substituted Eccentrics on a mobile centre, so that the learners might the more easily understand the motions and places of the Apogees, and, an Epicycle being added in the Planets, the directions, stations, and retrogressions might become the more manifest, nor the reckoning of the Latitudes be so obscure and intricate. Yet, lest I should fail the ingenious and those who already understand the common forms of the single motions, or wish to build Automata, I subjoined at the end, as a Corollary, that Inversion of Copernicus of which I spoke above, so that thus I might prepare the learners for the reading and easier understanding of Copernicus. But perhaps [I have said] more about this matter than was enough: to other [things], then.

As to what you think, toward the end of the refutation of Roeslin, that the minimum Eccentricity of the Sun has passed [occurred] — the same I too establish. For I refer the minimum Eccentricity and obliquity of the Sun to Peurbach. Nor is Rheticus to be heard either, who, while he writes that al-Battānī [Albategnius] too freely abuses the mysteries of Astrology, here falls into the same fault himself. For how can the change of the Sun's Eccentricity effect a change of empires? Nor do you unjustly reprehend that which Philip Melanchthon first introduced — namely, that the Sun has now been made nearer to the Earth — although Cardano too, in book 3 of the Subtleties, erroneously establishes the same. For they did not attain Copernicus's mind in this matter. I do not doubt that you too could find a far more exact Hypothesis of the Comet's motion, since the proportional decrement of its motion sufficiently argues that it was composed of circular [motions]. Nevertheless I judge it altogether superfluous to constitute Hypotheses of Comets, since none completes a Period, nor can any utility redound thence. Wherefore in my writing too I omitted the constitution of a Hypothesis. I myself beheld that Comet [of 1585] of which you deal in that Tome at Bernburg, 10 November, together with Johann Troldenirer, then prefect of the castle of Bernburg, by whom I had been invited to the Martinmas feast; it appeared to all dwelling at Bernburg on that day. For the Sky there was serene. When I showed that your second Tome to our most Illustrious Prince, he was occupied more than three whole hours in diligently unrolling it, and it pleased his Highness wondrously, so much so that he did not cease to praise it even to the bystanders; and he contended there with me that that book had been sent to him by you, but I argued from the inscription that it was mine, and subjoined that you would send the whole Work, if it were completed, to his most Illustrious Highness. See, therefore, that you redeem my pledge. And if [you should send] the same also to [his] Son Maurice — though not in this doctrine, yet in Latin, Greek, and Hebrew splendidly instructed and erudite beyond his Age — and likewise to Bernhard, Prince of Anhalt, dwelling here in our Court with Maurice, whom his parent Joachim Ernst of most happy memory had, together with [his] brothers, instructed in these Arts too, through Caspar Gottschalk, a man not unskilled in Mathematics: if, I say, you send that completed Work of yours hither to these two also, I do not doubt that you will do them a most welcome thing, by this reasoning tacitly inciting them to the love and cultivation of these most pleasant Arts, according to the grasp of [their] age. Those Mathematical works of which I wrote in [my] earlier letter I am about to publish shortly, the divine clemency favouring, when I will transmit them to you. But it would now be time that I at last close this too-prolix Letter, had not our most Illustrious Prince given me another [thing] in [his] commands. For since his most Illustrious Highness is so occupied in the marrying-off of [his] Daughter that he cannot reply, he commanded me to reply myself to those [things] which your Letter contains. But I find nothing in it which much needs a reply, or which has not been more than sufficiently set forth in his most Illustrious Highness's earlier letters sent to you — except that, in the Observations instituted through Venus, you seem to accuse us of error, and to confer the fault on the Refractions. But the Refractions could not stand in my way, since I instituted the Observations through Azimuths too, and, the distance of Venus from the Third [star] of Aries [♈] and others taken after the Sun's setting, on account of the Refractions (which, however, I separated [out]), it would necessarily have been found smaller than just, and so the places of the Stars would have been drawn back into the preceding [direction], against the order of the signs. But I do not wish to deal more about these. For you will see the reckoning of our Observations in our Work shortly to be published, God willing. I see, therefore, that nothing further now remains than that I at last finish this Letter. Wherefore fare best and most happily, most Noble and most Excellent Lord Tycho, and to me far dearest; and receive my candour in the best part, and write me back as soon as possible. Again, farewell.

Kassel, the 13th [day before the] Kalends of October [literally; = 13 October per the catalogue], in the Year of salvation 1588. Yours truly, Christoph Rothmann.

Tycho (Uraniborg). This is Rothmann's substantive reply in the great Tycho–Rothmann correspondence, answering Tycho's two letters (the comet-book Tomus II sent via Gellius Sascerides, received 11 July, and a later one) — the natural-philosophical heart of the exchange.
  • Thesis 1 — fluid air-heavens (optics). Rothmann holds the "surrounding matter" of the heavens is pure air, identical in transparency to sublunary pure air (against a distinct aether). His proofs are optical: (a) diverse transparencies must produce refraction (Alhazen 7.2/7.4, Witelo 10.3); so if there were an aether/air boundary, there would be a refraction-"point," contradicting observation; (b) refraction weakens light (Alhazen 7.7, Witelo 10.10), yet zenith-stars are not brighter than others; (c) the decisive demonstration (Euclid I.21): a real aether/air refraction would be greater for the fixed stars (farther) than for the Sun (nearer) at the same altitude — but observation shows the opposite, so the refraction is from vapours near the Earth, not an aether/air boundary. He turns Tycho's own "refraction to the zenith" against him, and notes the warm-window/cold-air experiment. Subsidiary: no sound from stellar motion in pure air (the gross-air wind analogy; the Superi would be deafened); Melanchthon's Physics on the elements' proportion; the 52-Earth-radii lunar distance implying air 1:140,608 the Earth-globe. On the fifth essence (Tycho's chymical point): Rothmann grants it lurks in earthly things but holds it generated by the Sun (the Sol Microcosmi / heart-chambers / vital-spirit analogy), not identical with the planetary "surrounding matter."
  • Thesis 2 — comet tails self-luminous. Reversing his earlier deference to Cardano/Scaliger/Apian, Rothmann now holds the tail is of the head's own (rarer) matter, shining by illumination, not pointing at the Sun — proved by the Landgrave's 1558-comet record (quoted from Cyprian Leovitius's gilded Ephemerides that Wilhelm IV carried with his Bible, self-moving globe, and small quadrant): the comet's tail pointed toward Ursa Major, not the Sun. The tail's curvature (inexplicable on Tycho's straight-ray view) and the great comets of Seneca and Pontanus (one equalling the Milky Way, one of 50°) confirm it. On comet matter, Rothmann's view differs from Aristotle's (tenuous/ray-purged, not gross/burning; in the planetary spheres, not just sublunary); he notes that Tycho, by introducing generation into the heaven, must introduce corruption too.
  • Thesis 3 — the Copernican defence (the historic core). Rothmann reads Tycho's "new hypotheses" as "inverted Copernicus" (geo-heliocentric), and may be diverse only in carrying the planets' homocentric centres on the Sun's eccentric (mixing the solar eccentricity into all planets — which observations would not support). He had himself built such constructions (Homocentrepicyclepicycli) "entirely according to Copernicus" (as did Rheticus and Reinhold); the Landgrave had a 6-inch automaton showing all planetary motions. But Rothmann rejects the geo-heliocentric arrangement: it shows only that, not why, retrogressions occur (the "stain of retrogression"), whereas Copernicus shows why — the retrogressions are the Earth's motion reflected; only proper motions remain (Earth's diurnal + annual, then Moon/Venus/Mercury/Mars/Jupiter/Saturn). The traction objection: the Sun's epicycle-centre cannot "drag" the planets with no connecting matter (the Landgrave's joke that "the Sun's circle must be stronger than bronze"); the system "confuses the spheres," and "God is the author of order, not confusion," and "Nature does not act through more when fewer suffice." He affirms gravity as Copernicus defined it (a natural appetite of parts toward their whole, in all the globes — De rev. I.9), the Earth's fitness to move (the spun-globe analogy), and that Scripture accommodates the vulgar (Genesis 1; the "iron heaven," "windows of heaven," the firmament = "that force by which clouds hang"; the "stretched tent"; Lactantius; Solomon's natural wisdom; Paul, Rom. 1: greater wisdom lies in Nature than is revealed in Scripture). He doubts Tycho really has "geometrical demonstrations" for his system. Yet for teaching he keeps non-Copernican forms (even Maestlin misunderstands the libration), reserving the "Copernican inversion" as a corollary to ease students toward Copernicus.
  • Other matters. The twilight discrepancy (Tycho's morning 16½° vs Rothmann's evening 24° depression); the request for Tycho's chymical-medical teaching (Ramus's "number, weight, measure"; the Sulphur/Salt/Mercury spirits; natural/vital/animal spirits; soul-spirit-body union; the stone and gout); praise of the comet Tomus II (the aethereal course "irrefutably demonstrated"); the lunar max-latitude agreement but the ascending node ("Head of the Dragon") off the Tables by >4°; the minimum solar eccentricity/obliquity referred to Peurbach; Rheticus and Melanchthon/Cardano faulted on solar-eccentricity-and-empires and "the Sun now nearer"; comet-hypotheses "superfluous" (no period); the 1585 comet seen at Bernburg (10 Nov, with Johann Troldenirer, at a Martinmas feast); the Landgrave's 3-hour absorption in the Tomus II and the dispute over whose copy; the request for completed copies for Maurice (the future Moritz the Learned) and Bernhard of Anhalt (tutored by Caspar Gottschalk); and the closing defence of the Venus observations (refraction "separated out," azimuths used) — Rothmann answering for the Landgrave, busy "marrying off his daughter."
