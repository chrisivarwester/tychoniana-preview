CLARISSIMO ERUDITISSIMOQUE VIRO DOMINO IOANNI CRAIGO, PHILOSOPHO ARISTOTELICO ET MEDICINAe DOCTORI, EANDEMQUE EDENBURGI IN SCOTIA EXERCENTI, MATHEMATICO EXCELLENTI, AMICO SUO PLURIMUM DILECTO.

QUID cunctaris, mi CRAIGE, curque tam cito pedem referens nusquam amplius appares? Si caussae et viribus diffidis, veritatisque a nobis propugnatae moenia inexpugnabilia esse, iam tandem expertus es atque concedis, equidem id quod res ipsa exigit, quodque decet, admittis. At cur non id vel verbulo fateri lubet? Neque enim errata agnoscere revocareque usqueadeo inglorium est, quin homini, his per se non parum obnoxio, id conveniat, excusationemque mereatur, cum et a plurimis praestantibus atque apprime eruditis viris etiam in gravioribus negotijs idipsum iam diu multumque laudabiliter factitatum sit. Sin autem nondum omnia tela atque arma, quibus oppugnes, tibi extorta esse praesumis, clypeumque tuum adhuc satis validum nec imbellem esse tibi persuades, quare non denuo in arenam prodis? Nam et defatigatus aut fugitivus miles iterum nonnunquam certare audet. Age igitur, prome si quid residui habes, quo tuum ARISTOTELEM et te tuearis, dum Cometas coelo eliminare citra omnem experientiam iustasque rationes is annixus sit, Posteritatemque Philosophicam tot iam seculis hac falsa opinione imbuerit suisque naenijs dementârit, cuius fistulae sibilum tu patulis auribus tam lubenter recipiens, forte, quod ad hunc a teneris tripudiare (ut mos in Scholis plerumque est) assuefactus sis, illius lusu (si modo non ab hoc delusus) delectaris, et illi tibique nimium applaudis. Imo quod minus excusabile est, in Mathematicorum erudito pulvere simul strenue et industrie exercitatus, non vereris, contra ipsam Mathematicam certitudinem atque Observationibus indubijs superstructas Apodixes Geometricas, omni Herculis robore insuperabiliores figmenta, vel saltem meras coniecturas Peripateticorum, veritati fucum facientes, aut potius hanc offuscantes, quantum in te est, defendere. An hoc est Mathematicum agere et veritatis penetralia, seposito omni praeiudicio, demonstrative enucleare, eidemque compertae constanter assentiri? Facessat Ethnicus iste ἀμαθηματικός (utinam non unâ etiam ἄθεος) ARISTOTELES cum suis discipulis, qui saltem plausibilibus et sophisticis elenchis incrustata facile arripiunt, ijsque acquiescunt. Apud Mathematicos certe non plus fidei promerebitur, quam geometrice comprobârit. Idem de omnibus eius asseclis, sive per imprudentiam sive affectata quadam ignorantia, ipsi suffragantibus, non immerito censendum venit.

Apologiam nostram, qua tuis obiectionibus luculenter respondebam, te iamdudum accepisse, nullum apud me est dubium. Nam et eruditissimus Dn. D. ANDREAS CRAGIUS, Medicus et Physicae in Academia Hafniensi Professor, mihi e Scotia (quo cum serenissima Regina Sponsa eius atque adiunctae nobilitatis Archiatrum agens, quando illac classe Danica deduceretur) reversus significabat, te motae inter nos disputationis, huiusque meae Apologiae mentionem fecisse. Nihil tamen certi referre poterat, an nostris dilutionibus acquiesceres nec ne. Id saltem aiebat, te admodum (si modo non nimium) Aristoteleis placitis mancipatum, peneque omnia ab ipso prolata pro oraculis habere; quae credulitas tibi cum multis alijs Philosophiam profitentibus communis est. Satis itaque, imo plus quam satis, tibi temporis interea, utut alijs negotijs aut Medicinae faciendae occupato, relictum est, quo Apologiam hanc nostram evolveres, disquireres atque si quid contradictionem admittens offenderes, idipsum ulterius disquirendum in medium proponeres. Sesquialter enim annus, ni fallor, iam praeterlapsus est, ex quo hoc scriptum Apologeticum ad te in Scotiam misi, nihilque tam diutino exacto tempore responsi mihi relatum, utut navigatio inter haec regna satis frequens sit et post contractam affinitatem Regiam quam antea commodior et crebrior. Verumenimvero abijce latebras gressumque reflecte et ad nobis respondendum, dummodo quid supererit nervorum, vires recollige, quantumque potes, haec moenia tenta. Sin autem nihil amplius restat, quod in contrarium verisimiliter adduci posse confidis, idipsum saltem indica et ingenue fatere. Tradideram interea nobilissimo cuidam Iuveni Scoto (cuius mihi iam nomen excidit, Secretarium tamen Regis vestri intimum fuisse recordor) cum a Rege suo in Daniam amandatus me quoque hîc inviseret, descriptum nostrarum Observationum atque ratiocinationum de Cometa ultimo, in Martio Anni 1590 conspecto, quale illustrissimo Principi GUILHELMO Hassiae Landgravio concesseram. Atque insuper ab ipso petij, ut te quoque eius redderet participem. Quod et eum fecisse mihi persuadeo. Demonstrâram ibidem admodum expedita et certa ratione et hunc Cometam omnino coelestem fuisse, adeoque nulli sensibili Parallaxi obnoxium, quod et in pluribus alijs, imo omnibus hisce aliquot exactis annis apparentibus, affatim deprehendi. Forte hic ipsus postremo visus Cometes, de quo loquor, te ancipitem et certamen ulterius detrectantem reddidit, cum is tam in 90mo a Meridiano Horizontis gradu, ipso videlicet Verticali principaliore altior existens, et postmodum in Meridiano, infra Polum declivior, interlapsis plurimis horis, declinationem suam nullo minuto aliter variârit, quam motus proprij interea ratio e diurno collecta exigebat, quod nostris Instrumentis erat observatu tam facillimum quam etiam ab omni erroris suspicione semotum. Nec tot anfractus atque Maeandros, ad Parallaxeos insensibilitatem demonstrandam, quemadmodum Cometes anni 77, de quo inter nos mota est quaestio, efflagitabat. Quod si per incuriam aliquam aut ex oblivione eius, cui id concessi, scripti illius copia tibi nondum est facta, apud eundem Secretarium, qui paulo post serenissimi Regis vestri in Regnum suum reditum huc ablegatus erat, uti antea quoque monui, de hoc sciscitare, atque eius communicationem, quod et facile (uti spero) obtinebis, urge, postmodumque attente lege atque expende; et modo tibi hîc quoque aliquid suggeratur dubij, aperte et ingenue indica, mecumque de toto hoc negotio ulterius per literas agere ne supersedeto. Caussa autem, cur id eo enixius desiderem, est haec, quod cum tuas literas, quatenus nostrae de Cometa supradicto Anni 77 lucubrationi nonnulla opponebant, unâ cum nostra Apologetica responsione, quibusdam excellentibus et eruditis in Germania Mathematicis simul inspicienda dijudicandaque transmisissem, illis sane haec amica inter nos veritatis eliciendae confirmandaeque decertatio non displicuit nec indigna iudicabatur, quae publici iuris fieret, ut et alij negotij penetralia eo profundius (hinc allecti) introspicerent atque veritatis involucra commodius eruerent, quidque citra dubium sentiendum foret, rectius constituerent. Proposui itaque hortatu aliorum eam partem literarum tuarum, quae hac de re agit, unâ cum nostra Apologia, tomo secundo de hoc Cometa tractanti propediem subiungere. Quod tibi prius significandum censui, ut si quid in promptu esset, quod hîc ulterius inferre satageres, id mature expedire mihique mittere posses. Nam et hoc addere et sub trutinam vocare non negligam. Cedet id in nullum tuae existimationis aut nominis detrimentum, sed potius erit apud omnes eruditos et cordatos honorificum. Nam et praeceptorum authoritatem tueri atque ab opinione plausibiliter concepta non facile amoveri, sed quoad fieri potest, in ea persistere, generosi semper fuit animi, nec indecorum a candidis et aequis censoribus habitum. Quin et quidam sunt eruditorum in Germania virorum, qui te non serio, sed saltem ut alia et plura eliceres, hanc movisse disceptationem autumant. Inter quos, ut est praecipua dignitate atque authoritate conspicuus, si etiam in rebus Mathematicis scientia et iudicio plurimos alios antecellens, Magnificus et nobilissimus vir Dominus IACOBUS CURTIUS a Senfftenau, Caesareae Maiestatis ab arcanis consilijs atque Imperij Procancellarius pereminentissimus. Is ex quo Apologiam nostram clarissimo viro Domino THADDÆO HAGECIO Medico et Mathematico eximio, veteri meo amico, transmissam forte perlustrasset, posteaque Caesareae Maiestatis amplissimum Privilegium Operibus meis, quo indemnitati sumptuum consulatur, praefigendum mittens, unâque literas proprias ad me dare non gravatus, inter alia et huius nostrae controversiolae non obscure in ijs meminit, nulla tamen tui expressa mentione. Licet vero apographum earum literarum circa frontispicia primi tomi, de Stella Nova et quibusdam alijs luculenter agentis, certa de caussa et rogatu quorundam apposuerim, tamen et hîc eam portionem, quae hoc, de quo loquor, negotium concernit, annotare non pigebit, ut eo citius et certius eius gravissimum hac de re iudicium tibi innotescat. Sonant autem eius verba ex eâdem Epistola, Pragae 28. Iunij Anno 90 ad me datâ, in hunc modum: »De scriptis tuis, quid in genere sentiam, testabitur Caesareae Maiestatis privile gium, quod hisce literis adiunctum tibi mitto, cuius ita conscribendi ego Caesari author et porro, vera in eo Caesarem testari, sponsor fui. In specie vero ea, quae d e Cometis posterioribus, quotquot ipse observasti, a te tradita solidissimisque rationibus confirmata sunt, ta lia esse sentio, ut de ijs ne ambigi quidem amplius ab er uditis viris queat. Itaque non adducor, ut credam, eo s, qui hac in parte dubios sese adhuc ostendunt et contr a demonstratam veritatem ARISTOTELIS authoritati patrocinari videri vo lunt, id ita vere sentire. Sed hoc potius eos agere puto, ut, cum omnem moverint lapidem omniaque undique conquisiverint, quae Aristoteleam sententiam confirmare, tuam vero labefactare aut dubiam reddere videantur, ea tibi proponant, tanquam ad Lydium lapidem examinanda, adeoque caussam tibi praebeant, eruditionis tuae Sole, omnes omnino errorum tenebras non modo, sed vel levissimas nubeculas dissipandi exactissimamque harum rerum doctrinam posteritati perficiendi. Qui si hac mente tecum certant, nae ego illis quam maxime bene cupio, sin aliud agunt, pertinaciam quidem eorum odio dignam existimo. Id tamen, quod pertinacia sua in commune beneficium tibi extorserunt, ipsis valere, vel potius non valere iussis, amplector non secus, ac si bona id mente fecissent, de eoque Reipublicae literariae gratulor.«

Haec CURTIUS. Sed et quid de hypothesibus nostris neotericis idem praestantissimus vir senserit, siquidem tu eas quoque oppugnare non intentatum relinquis, unâ annotare non abs re fuerit. In eadem itaque epistola postmodum de his sic loquitur: »Ad ea, quae novis tuis hypothesibus hactenus obiecta sunt, solide mihi videris respondisse, neque ego quicquam in ijs absurdum, sed omnia invicem pulcherrime congruentia invenio; an vero hypotheses istae, id quod in Copernicianis desideratur, praestiturae sint, ut scilicet exacta siderum loca ad praeterita, praesentia et futura tempora nobis exhibeant, de eo tum demum iudicandum erit, ubi maius illud, quod moliris, opus in lucem prodierit. Neque enim dubito, quin in eo opere omnium aetatum, quotquot extant, exactiores observationes cum tuis, quas exactissimas esse certo mihi persuadeo, summa diligentia contuleris, indeque novam hanc Mundi Machinam, divino sane ingenio, extruxeris.«

Eruditissimus insuper et praestantissimus ille THADDÆUS, de quo dixi, eodem tempore mihi copiose scripserat atque suam de hac Apologia nostra censuram aperuerat, quaedamque alia pro se adversus tuas in eum animadversiones facientia addiderat, quae singula excerpta hîc quoque unâ, ut tibi constent, assignabo: »Quod ad Apologeticam responsionem tuam pertinet, hanc quoque perlegi diligentissime, eaque sane mirifice sum delectatus. Tam solide enim et tam luculente respondes, et obiectiones CRAIGIJ diluis, ut mirum sane videri possit, quid contra tam evidentia vel hiscere CRAIGIUS aut quivis alius possit. Quod si CRAIGIUS perget veritati contradicere in longe maiorem incurret reprehensionem apud veritatis amantes, quam quod ego in Nova Stella parum ex arte suo iudicio praestiterim. Sed sit ita, ut ille iudicat: ego certe illud parum multo gloriosius mihi esse duco, quam si manifeste veritati repugnarem. Primus ego, quid alij senserint, ignorans, libera voce et scripto protuli, Novam Stellam non fuisse Cometam, nec in elementari regione, sed in aethere: licet senserim et protulerim fieri posse, ut Cometae in utraque regione, puta elementari et aetherea, oriantur, qua de re aetas posterior rectius iudicabit. Primus etiam labefactavi opinionem ARISTOTELIS de Cometis, quam CRAIGIUS mordicus retinet, et pro ea velut pro aris et focis depugnat. Sunt, fateor, mea, quae tum attuli, admodum exilia, et, quoad doctrinam parallaxium, plurima in eis desiderari. Sed et haec deinceps aliquantulum recognovi. Tu vero his supremam manum imposuisti. Quamobrem tibi libenter arena cedo. Mihi interea apud bonos et probatos ad veniam, non dico ad gratiam, promerendam sufficiet, quod in negocio profecto difficili (quod nemo unquam, quod sciam, aggredi, nec ab opinione ARISTOTELIS discedere, metu obtrectatorum et invidorum, quibus vix unquam vir bonus ac doctus et veritatis magis quam amicitiae studiosus satisfecerit, ausus est) pro virili laborare studuerim, primusque, ut dixi, in hoc angulo hanc glaciem secuerim, et in incognita via novas orbitas impresserim. Utinam vero CRAIGIUS mea illa exilia saltem umbra veritatis evincere potuisset, habuissem illi gratiam. Sed cum non potuerit, nec poterit unquam, mirum sane non est, quod tua firmissima solidissimaque ne minima quidem in parte labefactârit. Hoc unum egisse video, ut postposita veritate opinionem ARISTOTELIS sartam tectam conservaret constabiliretque. Verum stat pro nobis veritas, licet adhuc deserta et pauper, nec fulta splendido aliquo patrocinio vel veterum vel recentium Philosophorum, efferet tamen suos radios deinceps clarius splendescetque, quantumvis nunc frivolis quibusdam obiectionibus et sophisticis ratiunculis obtenebretur. Certe si quis meliora nostris, quae vel de Nova Stella vel de Cometis protulimus, et abs te solidius multo prolata sunt, afferet, libenter amplectemur. Verum qui nec meliora affert, nec solide aliena refutat, sed tantum certandi studio contradicit, an is candidi et aequi aestimatoris partes agat, alijs dijudicandum relinquo. Miror, cur CRAIGIUS miscet meam Novam Stellam cum meo Cometa, cum egomet ingenue fassus sim, certitudinem mearum observationum Cometae non posse certare cum certitudine observationum Novae Stellae, neque mihi fuisse ad manum ea instrumenta, neque eas commoditates observandi Cometam, quas habui in Nova Stella. Nosti etiam memet et in Nova Stella et in Cometa, agnita veritate, partim sponte correxisse quaedam, partim abs te et a PRÆTORIO alijsque de quibusdam amice monitum nonnulla emendasse. Magis sane miror, CRAIGIUM illam mutationem sententiae in melius, quasi probro, incertitudini et inconstantiae adscribere, quae virtuti et ingenuitati adiudicanda fuit. Turpe enim non errorem, agnita veritate, corrigere. Sed errorem contra veritatem hominis in gratiam tueri, id vero est turpissimum. Quasi vero ipsa experientia non testetur, priores dies studiorum discipulos posteriorum esse solere. Sed vides, quid non audeant isti, qui in verba sui magistri iurârunt. Omnes etiam Mathematicos, qui Cometas aethereos esse asseruerunt, absurditatis arguit, non animadvertens, dum opinionem ARISTOTELIS mordicus tenere et tueri conatur, nec nostra evertere potest, seipsum in longe maiores absurditates implicare, quemadmodum abs te eruditissime evincitur. Quid, quod magna audacia et quidem immerito accusat praeclarum artificem REGIOMONTANUM, quod huc usque nec ab illo nec ab ullo Mathematicorum fuerit demonstratum, quomodo Cometarum remotio a Terra definienda sit, quando nimirum proprio incedunt gressu, et proptere a REGIOMONTANI demonstrationem abire in absurdum? Atqui, optime TYCHO, CRAIGIUS hac in re crasse fallitur, magnaque iniuria afficit optimum artificem REGIOMONTANUM, qui in suo illo praeclaro de Cometa libello problema 2.3.4. diserte monet, in indagatione parallaxeos proprij Cometae motus rationem habendam esse, quemadmodum abs te quoque eius motus ratio habita est. Quare dum ea CRAIGIUS omnibus Mathematicis imputat, tibi quoque non levem facit iniuriam. Ego quidem in eo Cometa et in altero quoque eius motum proprium me neglexisse scientem volentemque, lubens fateor. Sed ne ex eius neglectione notabilis et minime ferendus error exurgeret, meas observationes parvulo interiecto tempore institui, in quo non admodum sensibilis variatio motus proprij Cometae fieret: deinde iudicavi, satis esse, consistere in mediocritate et saltem propius collimare ad veritatem, quam ab alijs hucusque factum est: quemadmodum praeclare dixit REGIOMONTANUS: thesauros naturae nos non evacuare, sed in plerisque scibilibus ipsi veritati propinquum degustandum mortalibus concedi: quasi diceret, satis esse in eo gradu subsistere, qui veritatem penitus non evertat. Haec pro defensione REGIOMONTANI, adeoque veritatis, dicere volui. Neque tamen eo inficias, quin melior aliqua ratio vel abs te, vel ab alijs inveniri possit. Interea certitudinem demostrationum REGIOMONTANI firmam mansuram esse, libere pronuncio, quam neque CRAIGIUS, nec quispiam alius ullius absurditatis convincere poterit. Subolfecit sane REGIOMONTANUS quiddam aliud de Cometis, quam quod scholae ex ARISTOTELE publice docerent. Sed propter eius authoritem non ausus est a recepta sententia discedere: qui si nunc viveret vidissetque Novam Stellam, sine dubio erubuisset, et accuratiores Cometarum considerationes adhibuisset, nec ullum dubium est apud me, quin praeposita veritate nostrae sententiae subscripsisset. Sed quid ego de his apud te, qui haec omnia multo solidius pertractaveris ac CRAIGIJ rationes et obiectiones omnes ita dilueris, ut quid illis amplius ad asserendam veritatem adijci possit, non videam.«

Haec fuit optimi illius THADDÆI de nostra controversia censura. Postquam vero illi ad quaedam dubia in eadem Epistola mota et replicata (circa hypotheses praesertim motuum Cometarum, quas ille non posse constitui tunc existimavit, utut coelestes esse nequaquam inficiatus sit) accurate respondissem, is pro ea qua est animi ingenuitate et candore eximie decoratus, libenter suis quibusdam opinionibus, re rectius perspectâ, cessit, atque nostrae sententiae de toto hoc Cometico (ut sic loquar) negotio omnimodo subscripsit, quod ultimae ipsius ad me literae Praga die 29. Augusti, hoc ipso anno 1591 missae, testantur, dum sic habent: »Etsi vero aliquod otium nunc habuissem, non tamen aliquid nostrae illi disputationi adijcere potuissem vel nunc quoque possem; adeo exacte omnia a te excussa, examinata, meaque argumenta diluta sunt, ut me veritate et evidentia demonstrationum victum fateri necesse sit, nisi manifestae veritati repugnare velim.«

SCULTETUM quoque suarum de Cometa allegato conceptionum paenituisse, easque pro invalidis reputare, vel ijs pauculis ipsius verbis, ex autographo eiusdem excerptis, quas Apologiae inserui, cognoscere liquet. De NOLTHIO non habeo quod dicam. Is enim mihi ignotus est. Nec tamen dubito, quin pro ea, qua pollet Mathematum peritia, dextre et candide nostras animadversiones sit accepturus, seque deviasse, quod et in priore Tomo circa Stellam Novam plenius demonstro, aperte confessurus.

De caeteris in Germania eruditis et iudicio valentibus Mathematicis idem mihi polliceor. Quin et, ut cognoscas excellentia illa Italorum ingenia non secus nostris assertionibus acquievisse easque omnimodo approbasse, excerpam etiam quaedam ex clarissimi atque excellentissimi illius Mathematici IOANNIS MAGINI (cuius celebre nomen proximis annis, ob Tabulas Astronomicas prompta facilitate resolutas et copiose locupletatas, ac Ephemerides ex his subtiliter et luculenter derivatas, undiquaque innotuit) literis, tum ad GELLIUM SASCERIDEM, quondam mihi domesticum et Astronomica atque Medica hîc exercentem, nunc vero in Italia Medicinae studio ulterius operam navantem, perscriptis. Ex quibus facile cognosces, quid magnus ille MAGINUS (ut unum prae multis sumam, consentientibus tamen et aliorum illic Mathematicorum, ut ipsemet innuit, suffragijs) senserit, qui primo in Epistola die 8. Iulij Anno 1590 ad dictum GELLIUM Bononiâ transmissa sic scriptum reliquit: »Ego in TYCHONIS libro perlegendo nihil reperi, quod non maxima laude dignum mihi videretur, communicavique cum amicis eiusdem scientiae professoribus, qui mecum idem sentiunt. Neque gravabor ad ipsum scribere, et officiosissime gratias ipsi de liberali dono ac de praeclaro suo de me iudicio agere, quas maximas potuero. To the most distinguished and most erudite man, Master John Craig, Aristotelian Philosopher and Doctor of Medicine — practising the same at Edinburgh in Scotland — excellent Mathematician, his much-beloved friend.

Why do you delay, my Craig, and why, drawing back your foot so quickly, do you nowhere appear any longer? If you distrust your case and your forces, and have now at last found by experience, and concede, that the walls of the truth defended by us are impregnable, then indeed you admit what the matter itself requires and what is fitting. But why does it not please [you] to confess this even by a little word? For to acknowledge and retract errors is not so inglorious but that it befits a man (in himself not a little liable to these) and merits excuse — since this very thing has now long and greatly been done, in laudable fashion, by very many outstanding and especially erudite men, even in graver matters. But if you presume that not yet have all the darts and arms with which you might assail [me] been wrested from you, and persuade yourself that your shield is still valid enough and not unwarlike, why do you not come forth into the arena again? For even a wearied or fugitive soldier sometimes dares to fight again. Come then, bring forth, if you have anything left, with which to defend your Aristotle and yourself — [Aristotle], who strove to banish the comets from the heaven without any experience and just reasons, and has now for so many centuries imbued Philosophical Posterity with this false opinion and driven [it] mad with his dirges; the whistle of whose pipe you, receiving so gladly with open ears — perhaps because you have been accustomed from your tender [years] to dance to it (as is mostly the custom in the Schools) — delight in his play (if only you are not deluded by him), and applaud both him and yourself too much. Nay — what is less excusable — having been trained at the same time strenuously and industriously in the erudite dust of the Mathematicians, you do not fear to defend, against Mathematical certainty itself and the Geometrical demonstrations [Apodixes] built upon undoubted Observations (figments more insuperable than all the strength of Hercules), or at least the mere conjectures of the Peripatetics, which counterfeit the truth, or rather obscure it, as much as in you lies. Is this to act the Mathematician, and to unfold demonstratively the inner chambers of truth, all prejudice set aside, and constantly to assent to it once found? Away with that heathen, that unmathematical [ἀμαθηματικός] (would that not at the same time also godless [ἄθεος]) Aristotle, with his disciples, who easily snatch up [things] merely crusted over with plausible and sophistical refutations, and rest in them. Among Mathematicians, certainly, he will earn no more credit than he shall have proved geometrically. The same must, not undeservedly, be judged about all his followers, whether through imprudence or through a certain affected ignorance, voting for him.

That you have long since received our Apology [Apologia], in which I amply replied to your objections, I have no doubt. For the most erudite Master Andreas Cragius [Anders Krag], Physician and Professor of Physics in the University of Copenhagen, returned to me from Scotland (whither he had gone, acting as chief physician [Archiater] to the most serene Queen-Bride and the nobility attached [to her], when she was conveyed thither by the Danish fleet), signified that you had made mention of the disputation raised between us, and of this my Apology. He could, however, report nothing certain — whether you acquiesced in our dissolutions [of your objections] or not. He said at least that you are quite (if not too) enslaved to the Aristotelian tenets, and hold almost everything brought forward by him for oracles — a credulity you share with many others who profess Philosophy. Enough time, therefore — nay, more than enough — has meanwhile been left you, occupied though you are with other business and with practising Medicine, in which to unroll this our Apology, to examine [it], and, if you found anything admitting of contradiction, to propose that very thing in the open for further examination. For a year and a half, unless I am mistaken, has now elapsed since I sent this Apologetic writing to you in Scotland, and in so long a time passed no reply has been brought back to me — although the navigation between these kingdoms is frequent enough, and, since the Royal kinship was contracted, more convenient and more frequent than before. But cast off the hiding-places, turn back your step, and, to reply to us, gather your forces — provided any sinews remain — and, as much as you can, assail these walls. But if nothing more remains which you trust can plausibly be adduced to the contrary, at least indicate this and confess [it] frankly. I had meanwhile delivered to a certain most noble young Scot (whose name has now slipped from me, though I recall that he was the intimate Secretary of your King), when, sent off by his King into Denmark, he visited me here too, a copy of our Observations and reasonings about the last Comet, seen in March of the Year 1590, such as I had granted to the most illustrious Prince Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse. And besides, I asked of him that he render you too a sharer of it. Which I am persuaded he did. I had demonstrated there, by a very expeditious and certain method, both that this Comet was altogether celestial, and so subject to no sensible Parallax — which I have also abundantly found in several others, nay in all of those appearing in these some years past. Perhaps this very last-seen Comet of which I speak rendered you doubtful and shrinking from further contest, since it — being so much higher in the 90th degree from the Meridian of the Horizon (namely at the principal Vertical), and afterwards in the Meridian, lower beneath the Pole, very many hours having elapsed — varied its declination by no minute otherwise than the reckoning of its proper motion, meanwhile gathered from the diurnal [motion], required: which with our Instruments was as very easy to observe as it was also removed from all suspicion of error. Nor did it demand so many windings and Maeanders [meanderings], for demonstrating the imperceptibility of parallax, as the Comet of [15]77 — about which the question was raised between us — demanded. But if, through some carelessness or forgetfulness of him to whom I granted it, a copy of that writing has not yet been made [available] to you, inquire about this from the same Secretary (who was sent here a little after the return of your most serene King into his Kingdom), as I advised before too, and press for its communication (which, I hope, you will easily obtain); and afterwards read [it] attentively and weigh [it]; and if any doubt be here too suggested to you, indicate [it] openly and frankly, and do not forbear to deal with me further by letter about this whole business. The reason why I desire this the more earnestly is this: that, when I had transmitted your letter — in so far as it opposed some [things] to our lucubration about the aforesaid Comet of [15]77 — together with our Apologetic response, to certain excellent and erudite Mathematicians in Germany, to be inspected and judged at the same time, this friendly contest between us, for eliciting and confirming the truth, by no means displeased them, nor was judged unworthy to be made public, so that others too might (drawn hence) look more deeply into the inner chambers of the business, and more conveniently dig out the wrappings of truth, and more rightly determine what should be thought beyond doubt. I have therefore proposed, at the exhortation of others, to subjoin shortly that part of your letter which deals with this matter, together with our Apology, to the second volume treating of this Comet. Which I judged should first be signified to you, so that, if anything were ready to hand which you should be eager to bring further here, you might prepare it in good time and send [it] to me. For I shall not neglect to add this too and call [it] to the balance. It will tend to no detriment of your reputation or name, but rather will be honourable among all the erudite and sensible. For to defend the authority of one's teachers, and not easily to be moved from an opinion plausibly conceived, but, as far as can be done, to persist in it, has always been [the mark] of a generous mind, and has been held not unbecoming by candid and fair censors. Nay, there are certain of the erudite men in Germany who suppose that you raised this dispute not seriously, but only that you might elicit other and more [things]. Among whom — as he is conspicuous for pre-eminent dignity and authority, [and] also excelling very many others in knowledge and judgment in Mathematical matters — [is] the Magnificent and most noble man, Lord Jacob Curtius of Senftenau [Jakob Kurz von Senftenau], most eminent privy Councillor of the Imperial Majesty and Vice-Chancellor of the Empire. He, since he had by chance perused our Apology, transmitted to the most distinguished man Lord Thaddaeus Hagecius [Tadeáš Hájek], eminent Physician and Mathematician, my old friend — and afterwards, sending the Imperial Majesty's most ample Privilege, to be prefixed to my Works (whereby provision is made for the indemnity of [my] expenses), and not declining at the same time to give me a letter of his own — among other [things] mentioned, not obscurely, this our little controversy too in it, yet with no express mention of you. And although I have appended a transcript of that letter about the title-pages of the first volume (which treats amply of the New Star and certain other [matters]), for a certain reason and at the request of certain [persons], yet here too it will not irk [me] to note that portion which concerns this business of which I speak, so that the more quickly and surely his most weighty judgment about this matter may become known to you. His words, from the same Letter — given to me at Prague, 28 June in the Year [15]90 — run in this manner: "About your writings, what I think in general, the Imperial Majesty's privilege — which, attached to this letter, I send you — will testify; of whose being so drawn up I was the author to the Emperor, and, further, the surety that the Emperor testifies true [things] in it. But in particular, those [things] which about the later Comets (as many as you yourself observed) have been handed down by you and confirmed by the most solid reasons, I judge to be such that they cannot even be doubted any longer by erudite men. And so I am not brought to believe that those who in this regard still show themselves doubtful, and wish to seem to plead for the authority of Aristotle against the demonstrated truth, truly think so. But I rather think they do this: that, when they have moved every stone and sought out from every side whatever may seem to confirm the Aristotelian opinion and to shake or render doubtful yours, they may propose these things to you, as [things] to be examined at the Lydian touchstone, and so may furnish you occasion of dissipating, by the Sun of your erudition, not only all the shadows of errors, but even the lightest little clouds, and of perfecting for posterity the most exact doctrine of these matters. Who, if they contend with you with this mind, truly I wish them very well indeed; but if they do otherwise, I deem their obstinacy worthy of hatred. Yet that which by their obstinacy they have wrung out for you, to the common benefit — they being bidden farewell, or rather not-farewell — I embrace no otherwise than if they had done it with a good mind, and for it I congratulate the Republic of Letters."

Thus Curtius. But what the same most excellent man thought about our neoteric hypotheses — since you leave these too unattempted [by your] attacks — it will not be amiss to note at the same time. In the same letter, therefore, he afterwards speaks thus about these: "To those [things] which have hitherto been objected to your new hypotheses, you seem to me to have replied solidly; nor do I find anything absurd in them, but everything most beautifully congruent with one another; but whether these hypotheses will deliver that which is wanting in the Copernican [ones] — namely, that they exhibit to us the exact places of the stars for past, present, and future times — about that it will be [time] to judge only then, when that greater work which you are attempting shall have come into the light. For I do not doubt but that in that work you will have collated, with the greatest diligence, the more exact observations of all ages, as many as are extant, with yours (which I am surely persuaded are the most exact), and thence will have built up this new Machine of the World, by a truly divine genius."

Moreover that most erudite and excellent Thaddaeus, of whom I spoke, had at the same time written to me copiously and opened his appraisal of this our Apology, and had added certain other [things] making for himself against your animadversions upon him — which several excerpts I will here too set down at once, that they may be established for you: "As for your Apologetic response, I have read this too most diligently, and I was wondrously delighted with it. For you reply so solidly and so amply, and dissolve the objections of Craig, that it can truly seem a wonder what Craig — or anyone else — could even mutter against things so evident. And if Craig shall persist in contradicting the truth, he will incur a far greater reproach among the lovers of truth than [I incurred], in [the matter of] the New Star, by having performed too little, in his judgment, 'by [the rules of] art.' But be it as he judges: I, certainly, hold that 'too little' to be far more glorious to me than if I manifestly fought against the truth. I was the first — ignorant of what others had thought — who brought forward, with free voice and writing, that the New Star was not a Comet, nor in the elementary region, but in the aether: though I thought and brought forward that it can happen that comets arise in either region — namely the elementary and the aethereal — about which the later age will judge more rightly. I was also the first to shake the opinion of Aristotle about comets, which Craig retains tooth-and-nail, and fights for it as if for altar and hearth. They are, I confess, mine — the things I then brought forward — very slight, and, as to the doctrine of parallaxes, very many things are wanting in them. But these too I afterwards somewhat revised. You, however, have put the finishing hand to them. Wherefore I gladly yield you the field. To me, meanwhile, it will suffice — among the good and approved, for earning pardon (I do not say favour) — that, in a business assuredly difficult (which no one ever, so far as I know, dared to attempt, nor to depart from Aristotle's opinion, from fear of detractors and the envious, whom scarcely ever has a good and learned man, more zealous for truth than for friendship, satisfied), I strove to labour to the best of my power, and was the first (as I said) in this corner to cut this ice, and to impress new tracks on an unknown road. But would that Craig had been able to overthrow those slight [contributions] of mine at least by a shadow of truth — I should have held him grateful. But since he could not, nor ever will be able, it is truly no wonder that he has not shaken your most firm and most solid [arguments] even in the least part. This one thing I see him to have done: that, the truth set aside, he might preserve and establish the opinion of Aristotle safe and sound. But the truth stands for us — though still deserted and poor, and not propped by any splendid patronage of the old or recent Philosophers; yet it will hereafter cast forth its rays more clearly and shine, however much it be now overshadowed by certain frivolous objections and sophistical little reasonings. Surely, if anyone shall bring forward better [things] than ours — which we have brought forward whether about the New Star or about Comets, and [which] have been brought forward far more solidly by you — we shall gladly embrace [them]. But he who neither brings better, nor solidly refutes another's, but only contradicts from a zeal for contending — whether he plays the part of a candid and fair appraiser, I leave to others to judge. I marvel why Craig mixes my New Star with my Comet, since I myself frankly confessed that the certainty of my observations of the Comet cannot vie with the certainty of the observations of the New Star, and that I had neither those instruments at hand, nor those conveniences for observing the Comet, which I had in [the matter of] the New Star. You know too that I myself, both in the New Star and in the Comet — the truth being acknowledged — partly corrected certain things of my own accord, partly emended some, amicably advised about certain [things] by you and by Praetorius and others. I marvel the more that Craig ascribes that change of opinion for the better — as if [it were] a reproach — to uncertainty and inconstancy, [a change] which ought to have been adjudged to virtue and ingenuousness. For it is not shameful to correct an error when the truth is acknowledged. But to defend an error against the truth, to please a man, is indeed most shameful. As if experience itself did not testify that earlier days are wont to be the disciples of later ones! But you see what those men do not dare, who have sworn upon the words of their master. He also accuses of absurdity all the Mathematicians who have asserted comets to be aethereal — not noticing that, while he tries to hold and defend Aristotle's opinion tooth-and-nail, and cannot overthrow ours, he himself becomes entangled in far greater absurdities, as is most learnedly proven by you. What [of this], that with great audacity, and indeed undeservedly, he accuses the splendid artificer Regiomontanus — [claiming] that hitherto it has been demonstrated neither by him nor by any of the Mathematicians how the distance of comets from the Earth is to be defined, since, namely, they advance with their own [proper] step, and that therefore Regiomontanus's demonstration goes off into absurdity? But, best Tycho, Craig in this matter is grossly deceived, and does great injury to the excellent artificer Regiomontanus, who, in that splendid little book of his on the Comet, in problems 2, 3, [and] 4, expressly advises that, in the investigation of parallax, account must be taken of the comet's own [proper] motion — as the reckoning of its motion was also had by you. Wherefore, while Craig imputes these [things] to all Mathematicians, he does no light injury to you too. I, indeed, freely confess that in that comet, and in the other one too, I knowingly and willingly neglected its proper motion. But lest from that neglect a notable and by-no-means-tolerable error should arise, I instituted my observations with a tiny interval of time interposed, in which no very perceptible variation of the comet's proper motion would occur; then I judged it enough to stand in the mean, and at least to take aim nearer to the truth than has hitherto been done by others — as Regiomontanus splendidly said: that we do not empty out the treasures of nature, but that, in most knowable things, it is granted to mortals to taste [something] near to the truth itself — as if he were saying that it is enough to subsist in that degree which does not utterly overthrow the truth. These [things] I wished to say in defence of Regiomontanus, and so of the truth. Nor yet do I deny but that some better reckoning can be found, whether by you or by others. Meanwhile, that the certainty of Regiomontanus's demonstrations will remain firm, I freely pronounce — [a certainty] which neither Craig, nor anyone else, will be able to convict of any absurdity. Regiomontanus assuredly scented something else about comets than what the schools publicly taught from Aristotle. But, on account of his [Aristotle's] authority, he dared not depart from the received opinion: who, if he now lived and had seen the New Star, would doubtless have blushed, and would have applied more accurate considerations of comets — nor is there any doubt with me but that, the truth being put first, he would have subscribed to our opinion. But why [do] I [say] these things to you, who have handled all these matters far more solidly, and have so dissolved all of Craig's reasonings and objections, that I do not see what more could be added to them for asserting the truth?"

This was the appraisal of that most excellent Thaddaeus about our controversy. But after I had accurately replied to certain doubts moved and rejoined in the same Letter (especially about the hypotheses of the motions of comets, which he then thought could not be established — though he by no means denied [the comets] to be celestial), he — exceptionally adorned with that ingenuousness and candour of mind which is his — gladly yielded, the matter being more rightly perceived, [on] certain of his opinions, and subscribed in every way to our view about this whole Cometary (so to speak) business; which his last letter to me, sent from Prague on 29 August of this very year 1591, testifies, since it runs thus: "But even if I had now had some leisure, I could not nevertheless have added anything to that disputation of ours, nor can I even now; so exactly has everything been examined and weighed by you, and my arguments dissolved, that I must confess myself conquered by the truth and the evidence of the demonstrations, unless I should wish to fight against manifest truth."

That Scultetus too repented of his alleged conceptions about the Comet, and reckons them invalid, is clear to be known even from those few words of his — excerpted from his own autograph — which I inserted into the Apology. About Nolthius I have nothing to say. For he is unknown to me. Nor yet do I doubt but that, in keeping with the skill in Mathematics in which he is strong, he will receive our animadversions deftly and candidly, and will openly confess that he went astray — which I also demonstrate more fully in the prior Volume, concerning the New Star.

About the rest of the erudite Mathematicians in Germany, strong in judgment, I promise myself the same. Nay — that you may learn that those excellent talents of the Italians have acquiesced in our assertions no otherwise, and have approved them in every way — I will also excerpt certain [things] from the letters of that most distinguished and most excellent Mathematician Giovanni Magini (whose celebrated name has in recent years become known everywhere, on account of the Astronomical Tables resolved with ready ease and copiously enriched, and the Ephemerides subtly and amply derived from these), written both to Gellius Sascerides — once a member of my household, practising Astronomical and Medical [arts] here, but now devoting his efforts further to the study of Medicine in Italy. From which you will easily learn what that great Magini thought (to take one before many, with the votes of the other Mathematicians there agreeing, however, as he himself hints), who first, in a Letter transmitted to the said Gellius from Bologna on 8 July in the Year 1590, left written thus: "In reading through Tycho's book, I found nothing which did not seem to me worthy of the greatest praise; and I communicated [it] with friends, professors of the same science, who think the same with me. Nor shall I be loath to write to him, and most dutifully to give him the greatest thanks I shall be able, for the liberal gift and for his splendid judgment about me. With whom I shall also wish to confer some [things] which pertain to the correction of the motions. For that promise of emending the motions — in which he affirms that notable lapses of others have hitherto occurred — has, to confess the truth, disturbed me; not but that I desire them to be restored, but because I desire that to be done as soon as possible, if for no other [reason], at least for my own sake."

Afterwards, when he had asked that certain of our animadversions about the Astronomical business be communicated to him, the same Gellius intervening, he subjoins this: "I observed besides, in his book, [something] which threw into me a great desire of knowing the matter thoroughly — namely, by what Method the other Angles and the other side of an oblique-angled Triangle can be hunted out, [with] only one angle given, namely, together with the two sides about it. For Tycho himself signifies that this can be found without a perpendicular, and indeed investigated with great ease, on page 281 [confirmed against De mundi 1588; the transcription's 218 is a transposition error — see Apparatus], saying: 'Since therefore now the angle BHK is established,' etc. Where he also touches up Maestlin — but modestly — who followed the common way in this matter. Indeed I most eagerly await also the first book of our Tycho; nor do I confess that I can obtain a greater benefit from you than if, as soon as it comes out, I be made possessed of it," etc.

From which it is clear that Magini inspected this second book not drowsily, and subscribed to its contents not grudgingly. The same is also gathered, not obscurely, from his letter afterwards given to me myself from Bologna on the Ides of September of the same year [13 September 1590], which, when he had begun thus: "I received your most erudite book, transmitted to me in your name by Gellius Sascerides — than which nothing more welcome could have been presented to me by you, since I am wont most curiously to seek out writings of this kind, and most gladly to read [them] through; for scarcely by another way should I have hoped that it would come to my hands, since very few printed volumes are wont to be imported into Italy thence, on account of the length of the journey and the difficulty of carriage; to which is added that, by this most elegant gift, you have splendidly signified how much you esteem me — on which account indeed I give you the greatest thanks I can," — and after he had intermingled certain other [things], and excused himself for not having written to me before, and sought from me some [things] (about which he had also made mention to Gellius before) concerning the Astronomical restitution, he speaks out in this manner: "But, that you may not lack my sense and judgment about that splendid labour of yours, beheld concerning the Comet of the year 1577 — have it thus, most distinguished Man: since I had perceived your incredible diligence in it, and [your] accurate way of observing, I am persuaded that it will come about that you can emend the motions of the celestial bodies most exactly, and by the fruit of that labour satisfy most fully the zeal and expectation of all, whence eternal glory may be added to your name. I should wish, however, that you would lay yourself out most of all on observing and examining the motion of Mars, since it is commonly persuaded that it cannot be observed exactly."

Then, interweaving certain [things] about correcting Mars's [♂] eccentricities at his own discretion, he continues the letter thus: "I cannot but greatly approve the System of the universe devised by you, although I should wish that the orb of the Sun and of Mars did not at all intersect each other; but if, as I learned from Gellius, it has been observed by you that Mars in the acronychal [position] approaches the Earth more than the Sun [☉], an intersection of this kind must altogether be admitted. I am in great expectation of observations and speculations of this kind of yours, which I shall by no means blush both to approve and to follow — even though, in constructing Ephemerides and resolved tables, I have not deviated a nail's breadth from the Copernican reckoning and the Prutenic Tables. I hope also — which I vehemently desire — to read this year your first book on the Phenomena of the aethereal World, which you promise you will publish."

Soon, certain other [things] being interposed, he has this toward the end: "This year I am going to interpret my sketches [hypotyposes] of the celestial orbs, which will be a good occasion for me to explain far more amply (as I have already begun to do) those [things] which have been published by me in this genre, and to illustrate [them] with Commentaries and Geometrical Demonstrations and computations. Which Commentaries, however, before they are made public, I gladly would wish to learn and understand your lucubrations, so that I might insert and apply your suppositions [hypotheses] too into them."

But I will add no more of these and similar [things]. Hence it becomes sufficiently clear to you, my Craig, that the Philosophers of Ausonia [Italy] — but above all that most eminent Professor of Mathematics of the renowned University of Bologna, Magini — have applaudingly accepted those [things] which our second book comprises, as to the aethereal seats of the Comet (which I chiefly labour to demonstrate there), and him too [accepting] our Neoteric ordering of the celestial Hypotheses — both of which, however, you not only call into doubt, but also, as much as in you lies, strive to shake. The talents of the Italians are certainly sublime and acute, and pre-eminent in the grave dexterity of judgment, and for the greater part bound to the Aristotelian Axioms no less than you [are] in Scotland. For which [axioms] they would even stand firmly and fight strongly, did they not notice that most [of them] are refuted by the certainty of Astronomical observations and by Mathematical Demonstrations. But I will add one [thing] more, that you may be aware [of it]. Since this very Magini exhorts [me] to note diligently the appearances of Mars [♂] — which I have also otherwise, for very many years from now, accomplished not unfruitfully (as I hope) — I, while writing back to him, asked him and Gellius to attend diligently there to Mars, being acronychal this last summer, in the month of June, because there, about the Meridian, being then higher than here, it was less subject to Refractions. And when I had at the same time written to Gellius to procure that a certain Instrument, after the likeness of mine which he saw and handled here, be made for these and certain other Observations to be measured from the heaven there in Italy, Magini came not long after to Padua, and bestowed diligent labour, both in elaborating and in using [it], as regards this Instrument; and among other [things] they there observed, by means of this, Mars several times in the acronychal position of which I spoke; which notations, afterwards transmitted to me, I collated with ours had here in this star at the same time, and scarcely found a difference of one or another minute. Which I thought should be indicated to you, although it is somewhat beside the purpose. It will therefore be yours to aspire heartily to the truth itself, all affection and preoccupied opinion set aside, and to seek, pursue, [and] embrace this alone, and — all by-ways set behind — freely to profess the same. Yet there is no [reason] why you should suppose that I am in any way moved or take it ill that you have shown yourself an antagonist to our assertions in this matter — which indeed you alone, as far as it has hitherto been possible to find out, before all the Mathematicians of Italy, France, and Germany (which I also praise), have dared to undertake. Nay, you have rather performed for me, on this account, a thing very welcome, and by no means displeasing (as you see) to erudite men. And in this way you have furnished a not-inopportune occasion of more fully unravelling and laying open the recesses of inviolate certainty. But that I nonetheless altogether think well of you, and bear a friendly mind toward you, you will perceive not obscurely from this alone: that to your most serene King — when he deigned to visit me in person on this Island, two years ago, having tarried for some while in Denmark with [his] Bride on account of the impediments of winter — I diligently and earnestly commended you to His Majesty, and, promoting [you] to better [things], begged for you submissively and reverently, with what intercession I could, in the hearing of the Magnificent Lord Chancellor and the illustrious Count Lord Keith [Kietzio]; from whom I obtained at the same time that they would promise to be willing to remind the King of the same [matter] when he had returned to his [people]; and if, on account of the incidental distractions of business (as happens) and the unrests of worldly matters, anything in this regard was meanwhile omitted, I have lately faithfully reminded the Lord Chancellor by letter (of which you have the tenor here) of his promise anew; nor do I doubt but that he and others — men of approved nobility and strong in authority at court, stirred by this commendation of mine, such as it is — will strive to wish well to you and your affairs, and to advance them further. You, meanwhile, do not at all cease to write to me more often, and besides to confer with me about Mathematical matters (which is our delight). It will be especially welcome to me, and you will find me not difficult to reply.

Farewell.
Given at Uraniborg, 14 November, in the Year 1591.
Tycho Brahe.

The embedded Hájek quote is additionally corroborated by Hájek's letter of 11 June 1590.
- Sender / recipient. Tycho (Uraniborg). John Craig (Craige, d. 1620), Scottish physician and Aristotelian philosopher, practising medicine at Edinburgh, later physician to James VI/I. He had sent Tycho objections defending Aristotle's sublunary comets; Tycho's Apologetica responsio (= the "Apology" repeatedly named here) answered them. This letter is the second salvo: a year and a half having passed with no reply, Tycho needles Craig to return "to the arena" or concede, and marshals testimonials. The whole exchange would later be printed (Craig's objections + Tycho's Apologia) in the comet volume, as announced here.
- The marshalled testimonials (the documentary core). Tycho quotes, to show the learned consensus:
1. Jacob Curtius (Kurz von Senftenau), Imperial Vice-Chancellor (d. 1594), Prague 28 June 1590 — two excerpts: (a) on the comets (the post-1577 cometary results are beyond doubt among the learned; Aristotle's diehards are merely testing Tycho "at the Lydian touchstone"; Curtius procured the Imperial Privilege for Tycho's Opera and vouched for it to the Emperor); (b) on the neoteric hypotheses (Tycho answered all objections, "everything most beautifully congruent" — final judgment to await the "greater work," the Machina Mundi).
2. Tadeáš Hájek (Thaddaeus), my "old friend" — the whole Craig-paragraph re-quoted from his letter of 11 June 1590; plus a later concession, Prague 29 August 1591, in which Hájek (having first doubted whether comet-motions could be reduced to hypotheses, while never denying them celestial) declares himself "conquered by the truth and the evidence of the demonstrations." This documents the arc from reservation to concession: Hájek's reservation of 11 June 1590 (the comet-motion-law thesis) was answered by Tycho and withdrawn within 14 months.
3. Giovanni Antonio Magini of Bologna — three excerpts: (a) to Gellius Sascerides, Bologna 8 July 1590 (nothing in Tycho's book not "worthy of the greatest praise"); (b) again to Gellius (the oblique-triangle method without a perpendicular, "Cum igitur nunc constet angulus BHK," where Tycho "modestly touches up Maestlin") — the page cross-reference is now resolved: Dreyer's pag. 281 is correct; the working transcription's pag. 218 is a digit transposition. The citation is to Tycho's De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis (1588), where the original 1588 marginal 281 sits at exactly the Cum igitur nunc constet angulus BHK passage (Dreyer, Opera Omnia IV p. 233 / page image PDF p. 247); (c) to Tycho himself, 13 Sept 1590 — the very letter Tycho answers on 1 December 1590: Magini received the comet book via Gellius, praises the 1577-comet labour, urges Tycho onto Mars, approves the Systema universi (while wishing the solar and Martian orbs did not intersect — yet conceding the intersection "must be admitted" given Mars's acronychal approach), confesses he has "not deviated a nail's breadth from the Copernican/Prutenic" in his own tables, and announces his forthcoming hypotyposes commentary. Tycho closes by reporting that, at his and Gellius's prompting, Magini observed Mars at the June-1591 opposition from Padua with a Tychonic-pattern instrument, agreeing with Uraniborg "within one or two minutes."
- The 1590 comet. Tycho's proof-piece against Craig: the comet of March 1590, "altogether celestial," with no sensible parallax — its declination over many hours (at the prime vertical, then on the meridian below the pole) varied by nothing beyond its proper motion. He gave a copy of these observations (the same as sent to Landgrave Wilhelm IV of Hesse) to an unnamed young Scot, the King's intimate Secretary, to pass to Craig.
- The royal visit (datable anchor). "Two years ago" James VI of Scotland visited Tycho on Hven (in fact 20 February 1590 O.S.), during his winter stay in Denmark with his bride Anne of Denmark (married at Kronborg/Oslo, 1589–90). Tycho commended Craig to the King in the hearing of the Lord Chancellor (John Maitland of Thirlestane) and Count Keith (Kietzius — the Earl Marischal George Keith, who had led the embassy to fetch Anne). Andreas Cragius (Anders Krag, Copenhagen professor of physics) had gone to Scotland as the bride-party's chief physician and reported Craig's reaction.
- Persons (others). Gellius Sascerides (Tycho's former assistant, then studying medicine in Italy and acting as his Bologna/Padua agent — cf. the Magini letter of 1 Dec 1590); Bartholomäus Scultetus (Görlitz mathematician, who "repented" his comet conceptions); Nolthius (a mathematician unknown to Tycho — probably Andreas Nolthius, who had written on the 1577 comet); Johannes Praetorius; Michael Maestlin (Mesthlinus); Regiomontanus (the De cometae … problemata, problems 2–4); Aristotle and the Peripatetics.
- The re-quoted Hájek paragraph. Tycho quotes Hájek's Craig-paragraph essentially verbatim from the letter of 11 June 1590, with minor witness-level variants — translated here following this text's wording: veritati contradicere (the 11 June letter: obstrepere); quibus vix unquam (the 11 June letter: nemo unquam); sed tantum certandi studio contradicit, an is candidi et aequi aestimatoris partes agat, alijs dijudicandum relinquo (the 11 June letter ends differently: …rixandi studio contradicit, istud malevoli et invidi hominis est iudicium); monitum (the 11 June letter: monitus); adiudicanda (the 11 June letter: adscribenda); Turpe enim non errorem (the 11 June letter: non est errorem); immerito accusat (the 11 June letter: falso accusat); crasse fallitur (the 11 June letter: turpissime fallitur); imputat (the 11 June letter: impingit). The divergences are stylistic/scribal, not substantive; sense is identical. (Tycho evidently quoted from his retained copy.)
- Greek & Latin tags. ἀμαθηματικός = "unmathematical / ignorant of mathematics"; ἄθεος = "godless, atheist" — Tycho's double jibe at Aristotle. Lydius lapis = the Lydian touchstone (assaying metal; here, a testing-stone for truth). in verba magistri iurare = Horace, Epist. I.1.14. velut pro aris et focis = "as for altar and hearth" (proverbial, for the last ditch). Apodixes = ἀπόδειξις, geometrical demonstration. The Regiomontanus sententia on nature's treasures, and the "earlier days are disciples of later" topos, recur from Hájek's letter of 11 June 1590.
