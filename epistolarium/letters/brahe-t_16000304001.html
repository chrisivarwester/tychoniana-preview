IOHANNES ANTONIUS MAGINUS AD TYCHONEM BRAHE.

TUAS ad me literas 3° Ianuarij datas et mihi opera Clarissimi Domini IOANNIS FRANCISCI SAGREDI Patricij Veneti et amicissimi mei redditas accepi die 22 Februarij. Eae mirum in modum, et quantum dici vix potest, gratae fuere; sed ijs respondere statim non potui ob praematurum tabellarij discessum; nactusque otium integrae hebdomadis ad te pluribus quam umquam antea scribendum putavi. Tuum illud praecipuum negotium mihi tanta fiducia mandatum pro eo ac debeo curabo, nihilque praetermittam, quod ad dignitatem tuam pertinere videatur. Res est maximi ponderis, proinde haud negligenter tractanda, sed consideranda accuratissime. Huc usque, ut fatear ingenue, ut etiam tu ipse affirmas, non aliam tibi operam dedi, nisi ut vertendas Latine curarem epistolas illas Germanice scriptas, quae ad octo sunt, quod mihi praestitit Germanus quidam medicinae studiosus, idque in gratiam Transylvani cuiusdam mei auditoris: ubi diu laborassem in inveniendo eius rei interprete, propterea quod nullum familiariter nossem eorum, qui Bononiae sunt e natione Germanica, qui perpauci sunt. Ac certe tum domesticis, tum scholasticis et publicis et privatis negotijs, tum valetudine parum firma impeditus, (cum distillatione laborarim dies complures huiusce hiemis culpa; quae omnium quas umquam meminerim inclementissima huc usque fuit ob nivium incredibilem vim omnia obsidentium, ut vix aliud agere, quam ad ignem pellendo frigori sedere potuerim, libellum aliquem ibi incommode legendo) etsi maxime voluissem, non multum tamen in rebus Dominationis tuae promovere potuissem. Sed deinceps mitescente caelo, spero me non poenitendam operam tibi navaturum. Primum etenim ex omnibus hisce scriptis excerpam brevia aliquot capita et quasi summam conficiam ad facile alios edocendos idoneam, cuius plura exempla ubi fecero, ad varios amicos transmittam.

Assentior tibi de narratione rerum tuarum non in dialogo facienda sed occasione panegyrici. Sed venit mihi tamen in mentem quod rationi nostrae multo sit conducibilius atque aptius. Intelligo BERNARDINUM BALDUM Urbinatem Guastallae Abbatem, qui olim edidit HERONIS opus de machinis se movendis e Graeco a se ipso translatum, multis abhinc annis provinciam suscepisse scribendarum vitarum praestantium Mathematicorum. Hominem quidem de facie non novi, sed tamen scio, cum aliquando Bononia transiret, domum ad me venisse, ut me de facie nosset, ut mihi postea dictum, nam me non invenit. Ex hoc spero, si quid ab eo petam, tamquam a mei studioso, facile impetraturum. Itaque scribere ad eum cogito et cum eo agere, ut vitam tuam diligenter conscribat, et ad me eius historiae exemplum mittat: illud postea cum encomijs tui poëticis coniungere licebit, et qui encomia illa collegerit, idem sibi ab illo eius historiae copiam factam dicere poterit. Sed is tamen prius diligenter de omnibus rebus, quae argumenti eius erunt, edocendus est. Ego quaecumque huc spectantia colligere potero, non praetermittam; sed bimestre spatium pusillum est, quando ij, quorum opera indigemus, suas quoque habent occupationes, quibus distinentur. Hoc mihi in huiusmodi negotio optimum consilium videbatur: quod meum tamen iudicium tuae prudentiae permitto.

Eclipsin Solarem anni 1598 instrumentis mathematicis non observavi, cum hîc prae manibus non haberem idoneum instrumentum. Horologio tamen ex pulvere, quod quadrantes horarum indicabat, accomodato ad horam exortus Solis, percepi eclipsin ipsam tardiorem fuisse hora pene una, quam prodat calculus Prutenicus, et eius magnitudo novem puncta non excessit, quamvis expectaretur hîc maior obscuratio. Hoc idem et Romae et Patavij observatum fuit, scilicet tardiorem fuisse ad integram fere horam et minorem fuisse obscurationem. Exemplar epistolae Illustrissimi Cardinalis SS. QUATTUOR, nepotis felicis memoriae INNOCENTIJ octavi Summi pontificis, in qua mihi de illa eclipsi, prout Romae contingit, historiam narrat, ad te mitto, ut videas, si ex illa quidquam haurire potes, et si tibi bona occasio de illo honorifice loqui patere poterit, mihi gratissimum erit; est enim patronus meus humanissimus, cui maxime debeo ob innumera beneficia in me collata. Nullas praeteritis temporibus feci observationes ob varia impedimenta, cum semper integras epistolas conscripserim apud aliquos Cardinales et Principes, quando scilicet hîc erant a scholis vacationes. Quod tempus maxime huic negotio erat idoneum.

Laboravi non parum, ut perciperem tuam rationem supputandi Lunarem cursum ex quinternione mihi relicto a D. FRANCISCO TENGNALIO, a pag. 113 incipiente. Notandum vero quod haec omnia, quae de revolutionibus etc. usque ad pag. 120, sed non sine magno labore rem tandem assequutus sum, cum nullum extet exemplum dictae computationis, et cum apud me non sint, quae de octavae sphaerae ac de Solis motu exarasti, tamquam praecedentia hunc Lunarem motum. Unde pro habendo Solis motu ex tuis fundamentis confugi ad Ephemeridem tuam motus Solis ad annum 1591 mihi a te olim liberaliter communicatam, notavique in ea Solem attigisse locum Apogaei, nempe gr. 5. 32' ♋ die 17 Iunij, h. 13,44 p. m., hîcque nulla est differentia inter verum motum et medium: excerpsi deinde motum Solis medium ex meis tabulis congruentem dicto temporis momento et inveni quidem ipsum scr. 2' 52'' minorem, dempta prius a loco ☉ tuo aequinoctiorum praecessione, quam gr. 28 17' 42'' tunc te praesupposuisse arbitratus fui. Quocirca mihi videtur his temporibus posse elicere medium Solis motum ex meis tabulis addendo eidem 2' 52'', ut cum tuis radicibus concordet. Posthaec varia ratione supputavi locum ☾ ex tuis tabulis, idque ad diem 9 Ianuarij anni 1587 atque etiam ad 15m et 16m eiusdem, quibus observasti verum ☾ locum per instrumenta, ut recenses pag. 56 tuarum Epistolarum astronomicarum, cuius libri reliquit mihi folium D. GELLIUS tuus a pag. 49 usque ad 56. At veram supputationis ☾ formam secundum tua placita non recte prius cognovi, quam confugissem ad triangulorum operationes, quibus certior factus fui, te aequationes illas exactissime colligere non curasse, facturum postea id meliori occasione, scilicet in tuo Theatro absolutissimo, cum ex angulo maximae 2i epicicli aequationis gr. 7 41' 15'' a te per observationes deprehenso colligatur recta AD a centro Terrae ad centrum primi Circelli par. 98677 qualium DE datur 1100. Sed ex angulo minimae aequationis perigeae elicitur recta AD 101312. Forsan lato modo pro dictis aequationibus definiendis sumpsisti dictam rectam AD tanquam sinum integrum, quod etiam mihi confirmat variatio centri maxima a te posita m. 45' 20'', cum recta AB semidimetiens scilicet parvi circelli ad mundi centrum sit 1320, qui numerus sinus est m. 45' 20'' vel 45' 24''. Experiri volui, quantam efficeret varietatem semidiameter CD parvi Circelli, super quo moveri statuis centrum primi epicicli, et inveni angulum maximae aequationis primi epicicli gr. 7 27' 43'', centro primi epicicli versante in Apogaeo parvi illius Circelli, statuendo scilicet rectam AD particularum 101640 iuxta tuos numeros. Idem angulus maximae aequationis in Perigaeo illiusmet Circelli prodit gr. 7 30' 35''; different, a priore m. 2' 52''. Sed uterque horum angulorum sensibili differentia ab angulo maximae aequationis a te definito gr. 7. 41¼ discrepat, ut suspicer commensurationes orbium Lunarium a te positas aliqua correctione indigere, praesertim quod videam etiam calculum ex tuis tabulis prodeuntem ad unguem tuismet observationibus non congruere, quod in bonam accipias partem velim, tanquam a sincero et ingenuo viro prodiens, qui zelotypia tui honoris laborat. Ab initio variationem orbis non solum subducebam a medio motu ☾ a Sole, vel eidem addebam iuxta tabulam, sed etiam a medio argumento vel anomalia: sed postea ex calculo triangulorum cognovi te aequationem anomaliae ex duplici illa variatione, quam argumentum medium subit, composuisse, quod mihi valde placuit. Suppositio haec tua Lunaris mirum arguit tuum ingenium et praestantiam: sed maxime praecavendum est, ne numeri ex ipsa derivantes ab observationibus vitio tuorum supputatorum discrepent. Non me latet magnum ac permolestum esse laborem in supputandis hisce aequationum tabulis. Ego tamen, cum compendia quaedam mira in computationibus triangulorum possideam a me nuper excogitata, exiguam persentirem molestiam in his tabulis de novo ad 2a scrupula exactissime construendis. Quod libenter tibi significare volui, ut si mea opera posthac indigueris, libere possis uti, praesertim pro reliquis quinque planetis, quorum unamquamque tabulam in decem diebus perfecte absolverem. Quod tibi incredibile videri non debet. Tabulam enim primi mobilis universalem habeo a me ad singula decem minuta (ad instar illius REGIOMONTANI, quae est ad singulos gradus) supputatam, et alias insuper tabulas, quibus calculationes triangulorum sphaericorum per ingressus laterales absolvo. Et in planis triangulis quando anguli inquirendi sunt, eademmet tabula utor artificio quodam a me invento, quod etiam locum habet in triangulo obliquangulo, in quo, datis duobus lateribus cum angulo ab ijs comprehenso, inquirendi veniunt anguli, qua operatione indigemus tantopere in aequationibus planetarum definiendis.

Scias profecto me adeo tui esse studiosum et observantissimum, ut nihil aliud cogitem, quam ut tibi me gratum probem. Quin et hoc te non latere volo, tantopere mihi esse in optatis, ut me adhuc vivo prodeat in lucem correctio caelestium motuum, ut nulli parcendum labori existimem, quo id assequi liceret, etsi nihil ex eo ad gloriam profici putem, sed satis habeam fructum illum, ut scilicet mihi tantum usui sint in exercenda Astrologia iudiciaria vulgo dicta, qua mirum in modum delector.

Ephemeridum mearum unum tantum tomum ab anno 1598 incipientem et in annum 1610 desinentem denuo impressum evulgavi, in quem nihil novi reposui, praeter auctuaria quaedam in Isagogicis, cum compendio directionum secundum viam rationalem, et Quadrante Directorio ad mentem PTOLEMÆI. Exemplar ad te libenter mitterem, si congrua mihi esset occasio; aestate hac proxima alter tomus, viginti feto quentes annos complectens, excudetur; et tunc enixe curabo, ut ad te utriusque tomi Exemplar deferatur.

Vidi nuper quae contra me inutiliter effutivit DAVID ORIGANUS in editione suarum Ephemeridum, et exactissime cognovi illum unum esse ex illis, qui ex insectandis et censurandis alijs gloriam sibi quaerere conantur; sed poenitebit aliquando se de me quicquam protulisse, ubi ostendero ipsum gravius in suis Ephemeridibus a calculo Prutenico aberrasse, quam a me factum sit, praesertim in postremis illis annis, ultra scilicet 1620, cum meas ibi desinentes Ephemerides non habuerit prae oculis, quod maximo argumento erit, et aperte satis demonstrabit, ipsum plurima ex meis desumpsisse. Pro emersionibus et occultationibus Planetarum ac Fixarum mutuavi arcus visionum a PTOLEMÆO definitos, quos parum a vero recedere in fixis puto, sed in Planetis credo cum STADIO esse varios, penes differentiam ipsorum Planetarum a Terra. Si quid notatu dignum ex tuismet observationibus circa hosce arcus habes, illud mihi humanissime communicare ne graveris; quod te subnixe oro ac deprecor: polliceor enim ingenue, quidquid illud erit, a te recepisse testaturum.

Vale, vir praeclarissime, et me, ut soles, ama ac addictissimum habe.
Bononia 4 Martij MDC.
Perillustri Dominationi tuae addictissimus
IO. ANTONIUS MAGINUS, Patavinus.

JOHANNES ANTONIUS MAGINUS TO TYCHO BRAHE.

Your letter to me, dated the 3rd of January and delivered me by the agency of the most renowned Lord Giovanni Francesco Sagredo, a Venetian patrician and my dearest friend, I received on the 22nd of February. It was wonderfully — and beyond what can scarcely be said — welcome; but I could not answer it at once, on account of the courier's premature departure; and, having got the leisure of a whole week, I thought I should write you more [fully] than ever before. That chief business of yours, committed to me with such trust, I will care for as I ought, and will omit nothing that may seem to pertain to your dignity. It is a matter of the greatest weight, and so to be treated not negligently, but considered most accurately. Hitherto, to confess frankly (as you yourself also affirm), I have given you no other work than to see those letters written in German — there are about eight — translated into Latin; which a certain German student of medicine performed for me, and that for the sake of a certain Transylvanian hearer of mine — after I had long laboured to find a translator for the matter, because I knew familiarly none of those who are at Bologna of the German nation, who are very few. And certainly, hindered both by domestic and by scholastic and public and private business, and by health none too firm (for I have laboured with a catarrh [distillatio] for many days, by the fault of this winter — which has hitherto been the most inclement of all I can remember, for the incredible force of the snows besieging everything, so that I could scarcely do anything but sit by the fire driving off the cold, inconveniently reading some little book there) — even had I most wished it, I could not have advanced much in Your Lordship's affairs. But hereafter, the weather growing milder, I hope to do you work not to be regretted. For first I will excerpt some brief chapters from all these writings, and make, as it were, a summary fit for easily instructing others; of which, when I have made several copies, I will send them to various friends.

I agree with you that the narration of your affairs should be made not in dialogue, but on the occasion of a panegyric. But there comes to mind, nonetheless, something much more conducive and apt to our plan. I understand that Bernardino Baldi of Urbino, Abbot of Guastalla — who once published Hero's work On Self-Moving Machines, translated by himself from the Greek — undertook, many years ago, the province of writing the Lives of distinguished Mathematicians. I do not indeed know the man by face; but yet I know that, when he once passed through Bologna, he came to my house, to know me by face (as was told me afterward, for he did not find me). From this I hope that, if I ask anything of him, as of a well-wisher of mine, I shall easily obtain it. And so I think to write to him, and to deal with him, that he diligently write your Life, and send me a copy of that history; it can afterward be joined with the poetic encomia of you, and whoever shall have collected those encomia can say that the copy of that history was made for him by [Baldi]. But he must first be diligently informed of all the matters which will be his argument. Whatever pertaining hither I can collect, I will not omit; but a two-month space is little, when those whose work we need have their own occupations too, by which they are detained. This seemed to me the best counsel in a business of this kind — which judgement of mine, however, I leave to your prudence.

The Solar Eclipse of the year 1598 I did not observe with mathematical instruments, since I had not here at hand a suitable instrument. Yet, by a sand-clock [horologium ex pulvere] which marked the quarters of hours, adjusted to the hour of the Sun's rising, I perceived that the eclipse itself was later by nearly a whole hour than the Prutenic calculation gives, and its magnitude did not exceed nine points [digits], although a greater obscuration was expected here. This same thing was observed also at Rome and at Padua — namely, that it was later by nearly a whole hour, and the obscuration smaller. A copy of the letter of the most illustrious Cardinal of the Santi Quattro — nephew of Innocent VIII, the Supreme Pontiff of happy memory — in which he narrates to me the history of that eclipse, as it happened at Rome, I send to you, that you may see whether you can draw anything from it; and if a good occasion to speak honourably of him may lie open to you, it will be most welcome to me — for he is my most humane patron, to whom I owe much, for innumerable benefits conferred on me. I made no observations in past times, on account of various impediments, since I always wrote whole letters [stayed?] with certain Cardinals and Princes — namely, when there were here the vacations from the schools; which time was most fit for this business.

I laboured not a little to perceive your method of computing the Lunar course, from the quinternion left me by Lord Franz Tengnagel, beginning at p. 113 — and be it noted, all these things which [you wrote] about the revolutions etc., up to p. 120; but not without great labour did I at last attain the matter, since no example of the said computation exists, and since I do not have what you wrote about the eighth sphere and the Sun's motion, as preceding this Lunar motion. Whence, in order to have the Sun's motion from your foundations, I resorted to your Ephemeris of the Sun's motion for the year 1591, formerly liberally communicated to me by you; and I noted in it that the Sun reached the place of the Apogee — namely 5° 32′ Cancer [♋], on 17 June, at 13h 44′ p.m. — and here there is no difference between the true motion and the mean. I then excerpted from my tables the Sun's mean motion agreeing with the said moment of time, and found it indeed 2′ 52″ less, having first subtracted from your Sun's place the precession of the equinoxes — which I reckoned you then presupposed [to be] 28° 17′ 42″. Wherefore it seems to me that for these times I can elicit the Sun's mean motion from my tables by adding 2′ 52″ to it, that it may agree with your roots. After this I computed, by various methods, the Moon's place from your tables — and that for the 9th of January 1587, and also for the 15th and 16th of the same, on which you observed the Moon's true place by instruments, as you recount on p. 56 of your Astronomical Letters, of which book your [Gellius Sascerides] left me a leaf, from p. 49 to 56. But the true form of the Moon's computation, according to your principles, I did not rightly know before I had resorted to triangle-operations — by which I was made the more certain that you did not take care to collect those equations most exactly, [intending] to do that afterward on a better occasion, namely in your most absolute Theatre [the Mechanica/Progymnasmata]; since, from the angle of the maximum equation of the second epicycle — 7° 41′ 15″, found by you through observations — there is collected the straight line AD, from the centre of the Earth to the centre of the first little circle, [as] 98677 parts, of which DE is given [as] 1100; but from the angle of the minimum perigeal equation there is elicited the straight line AD [as] 101312. Perhaps, broadly, for defining the said equations, you took the said straight line AD as the whole sine — which the maximum variation of the centre, posited by you [as] 45′ 20″, also confirms to me, since the straight line AB (the semidiameter, namely, of the small circle to the world's centre) is 1320, which number is the sine of 45′ 20″ or 45′ 24″. I wished to test how great a variation the semidiameter CD of the small circle — on which you posit the centre of the first epicycle to move — would make; and I found the maximum-equation angle of the first epicycle [to be] 7° 27′ 43″, the centre of the first epicycle being at the Apogee of that small circle (positing, namely, the straight line AD of 101640 parts, according to your numbers); the same maximum-equation angle, at the Perigee of that same circle, comes out [as] 7° 30′ 35″ — differing from the prior by 2′ 52″. But each of these angles differs, by a sensible difference, from the maximum-equation angle defined by you [as] 7° 41¼′ — so that I suspect that the commensurations of the Lunar orbs posited by you need some correction, especially since I see that even the calculation coming out of your tables does not agree to the nail with your own observations — which I would have you take in good part, as proceeding from a sincere and ingenuous man, who labours with jealousy [zeal] for your honour. From the beginning I subtracted the orb-variation not only from the Moon's mean motion from the Sun (or added it, according to the table), but also from the mean argument or anomaly; but afterward, from the triangle-calculation, I learned that you composed the anomaly-equation from that double variation which the mean argument undergoes — which pleased me much. This Lunar supposition of yours wonderfully argues your genius and excellence; but it must most be guarded lest the numbers deriving from it differ from the observations, by the fault of your computers. It does not escape me that it is a great and most troublesome labour to compute these tables of equations. Yet I, since I possess certain wonderful compendia in the computations of triangles, lately devised by me, should feel little trouble in constructing these tables anew, to seconds, most exactly. Which I gladly wished to signify to you, that, if you need my work hereafter, you may freely use it — especially for the remaining five planets, each table of which I would perfectly complete in ten days. Which ought not to seem incredible to you. For I have a universal Table of the primum mobile, computed by me to single ten-minutes (after the likeness of that of Regiomontanus, which is to single degrees), and besides other tables, by which I complete the calculations of spherical triangles by lateral entries; and in plane triangles, when the angles are to be sought, I use the same table, by a certain artifice invented by me — which also holds place in the oblique triangle, in which, two sides being given with the angle comprehended by them, the angles come to be sought — the operation we so greatly need in defining the equations of the planets.

Know assuredly that I am so zealous and observant of you that I think of nothing else than to prove myself grateful to you. Nay, I would not have this hidden from you: that it is so much in my wishes that the correction of the celestial motions come to light while I am yet alive, that I think no labour is to be spared, by which it might be attained — even though I think nothing is profited from it toward glory, but have fruit enough [in] this: namely, that they be of so much use to me in practising the judicial Astrology, as it is commonly called, in which I wonderfully delight.

Of my Ephemerides I have published anew only one volume, beginning from the year 1598 and ending in the year 1610, into which I put nothing new, except certain augmentations in the Introductory [parts], with a compendium of directions according to the rational way, and a Directory Quadrant after the mind of Ptolemy. I would gladly send you a copy, were a fit occasion mine; this coming summer the second volume, comprising the twenty following years, will be printed; and then I will earnestly take care that a copy of both volumes be delivered to you.

I saw lately what David Origanus uselessly blurted against me in the edition of his Ephemerides, and I have most exactly recognised that he is one of those who try to seek glory for themselves by attacking and censuring others; but he will someday repent of having put forth anything about me, when I shall show that he erred more gravely, in his Ephemerides, from the Prutenic calculation, than was done by me — especially in those last years, beyond 1620, namely, when he did not have my Ephemerides (ending there) before his eyes; which will be the greatest argument, and will clearly enough demonstrate that he took very much from mine. For the emersions and occultations [risings and settings] of the Planets and Fixed [stars], I have borrowed the vision-arcs defined by Ptolemy, which I think recede little from the truth in the fixed [stars]; but in the Planets I believe, with Stadius, that they are various, according to the difference of the Planets themselves from the Earth. If you have anything worthy of note, from your own observations, about these arcs, do not find it a burden to communicate it to me most humanely — which I earnestly beg and entreat of you; for I frankly promise that I will testify to have received whatever it be from you.

Farewell, most distinguished man, and love me, as you are wont, and hold [me] most devoted.
Bologna, 4 March 1600.
Most devoted to Your most illustrious Lordship,
Giovanni Antonio Magini, of Padua.

Apparatus

  • Direction & correspondent. Incoming, Magini (Bologna) → Tycho — the reply to Tycho's letter of 3 January 1600 (brahe-t_16000103002); survives in Magini's printed Tabulae primi mobilis.
  • Significance — the origin of the Baldi plan, the 1598 eclipse, and a detailed lunar-theory analysis.
  • ★ The Bernardino Baldi proposal (origin). Magini, agreeing that Tycho's Life should come "on the occasion of a panegyric, not in dialogue," proposes commissioning Bernardino Baldi of Urbino (Abbot of Guastalla, translator of Hero's On Self-Moving Machines) to write Tycho's biography for his Lives of the Mathematicians, then attaching the poetic encomia — with a cover story that the Life supplied the encomia. This is the proposal Tycho approves in brahe-t_16010124001 (24 January 1601) — the Italian self-monument scheme. Magini has had eight German letters translated into Latin (via a German medical student) toward it; the harsh Bologna winter and his catarrh delayed the rest.
  • The 1598 solar eclipse. Magini observed it only crudely (a sand-clock, no proper instrument): nearly an hour later than the Prutenic tables, magnitude c. 9 digits (as at Rome and Padua). He encloses the Cardinal of the Santi Quattro's account (the Cardinal being "nephew of Innocent VIII" and Magini's patron), asking Tycho to speak honourably of him — the thread Tycho takes up in his reply of 24 January 1601.
  • ★ The lunar-theory analysis (the technical core). Magini works painstakingly through Tycho's multi-epicycle lunar model (from the Tengnagel quinternion, pp. 113–120; plus a leaf of the Epistolae left by Gellius Sascerides), reconstructing the solar mean-motion offset (+2′52″; precession 28°17′42″) and the lunar geometry (lines AD, DE, AB, CD; the small circle; the first/second epicycle). He finds the maximum second-epicycle equation 7°41′15″ implied by Tycho's observations, but his own re-derivation gives only 7°27′43″–7°30′35″ — so "the commensurations of the lunar orbs need some correction," the calculation "not agreeing to the nail with your own observations." He praises the model ("wonderfully argues your genius") but warns of computers' errors, and offers to recompute all the planetary equation-tables (each in ten days) using his own prosthaphaeretic compendia and his 10-minute primum mobile table (cf. brahe-t_16010220001).
  • Astrology, ephemerides, Origanus, heliacal arcs. Magini wants Tycho's corrected motions chiefly for judicial astrology, "in which I wonderfully delight" — not for glory.
