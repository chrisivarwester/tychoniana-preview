To the most noble man, pre-eminent as much in the brightness of his birth as of his erudition, Lord Holger Rosenkrantz of Rosenholm, my friend, relation-by-marriage, and kinsman, by far the dearest.

Heartiest greeting.

Your most longed-for and long-desired letter, most loving Holger, I have now at last received — in which at the outset you excuse your slowness in writing back, and that by reasons which easily find a place with me, who otherwise think candidly of you. Afterward you recount many things about the condition of my affairs, which Divine Clemency has granted me here with the most august Emperor Rudolf II — and how, related by you to others, it pleased them, but was to you and your intimate friends especially most delightful to hear — which abundantly attest your sincere affection toward me (of which I never doubted before too); for which constant love toward me I give you the greatest thanks I can, proceeding from a heart most zealous of you in turn. My affairs are still in the same state in which they were when I last wrote you; for on account of the absence of the Emperor — who still tarries at Pilsen, a city 15 miles distant hence — they could meanwhile take no increase of any moment, which however I hope will come hereafter. For the Imperial Majesty remits nothing of his most clement favour toward me (which I count as accepted by God), but daily increases it; the leading men of this realm too wish me very well, so much that they have of their own accord offered to adopt me as a native of the Kingdom, that I might enjoy all their Privileges. But of these I now add no more, to indicate such things more amply perhaps in their time.

Yesterday, at last, Friedrich Rosenkrantz departed from me — your cousin and my kinsman — who came hither a month ago. I grieve from my heart — and who would not grieve at that excellent young man's most unhappy lot, and the less-expected calamity of most inconstant fortune? I — not only because he was so carefully commended to me by you (though this too must always weigh much with me), but also for the bond of blood between us — laboured diligently, as far as was in me and as he himself wished, to add at least some relief to his adversities, if I could not wholly cure them. That I might do this the more readily, I advised him to betake himself to Pilsen, to the Emperor's court, and gave him a letter to the most ample Imperial Vice-Chancellor, the illustrious and magnificent Lord Coraduz, asking him to obtain for this Rosenkrantz letters of intercession from the Imperial Majesty to the most serene Archduke Matthias, the [Emperor's] Brother, who now presides over the supreme command of the Hungarian war — which was obtained sooner than our expectation, Lord Coraduz himself (who greatly favours me, and does not decline to expedite anything for my sake) disposing and promoting this business. He also of his own accord added privately another letter to a certain noble Baron at the Archduke's court, familiarly known to him, likely to profit Friedrich not a little; and I too, that I might add my own vote of whatever kind, wrote humbly to the most serene Archduke himself, and left nothing untried to aid our kinsman's wishes with His Highness according to my small measure, intimating in a few words his misfortune in his fatherland — for so it seemed advisable, not only to me but to Friedrich, that we might forestall others, who by carping might perhaps exasperate the evil more odiously, and so render the matter more suspect. At the same time I sent His Highness two of my books, in all respects like those presented to your King [Christian IV]; and together with them a certain globe, made with skill at Alkmaar in Holland, from our verification of the stars — that by these little gifts I might the more readily catch the Prince's clement benevolence, by which he might the more gladly assent to our Friedrich's wishes. Copies of all these letters of which I have spoken, and at the same time Friedrich's own report, which on returning hither from Pilsen he set down in his own hand, and likewise his letter to you, I have placed here, that from reading them you may the more readily draw the circumstances of the matter done. I conceive no doubtful hope that our Friedrich, supported by these means, will accomplish his purpose with the renowned Archduke happily enough — especially as to those external and Political wishes, which I leave to his favour; each is drawn by his own pleasure; sometimes necessity urges us to accept what otherwise were less to be cared for.

But one scruple still lurks within, which is of no small moment, and could delay all the rest — or even render it vain, or cut it off entirely, and quite deliver him from so many adversities and troubles of this wretched life. For he labours — and more gravely than he himself seems to perceive — under the afflictions of a not lightly damaged lung, so that he is turning asthmatic, and gradually wasting with consumption [phthisis], unless, by God's grace, he shall in good time recover health by suitable medicines (which, presently relieving such great evils, are not easily found) — for the obtaining of which the manner of life, and the condition of region and diet which he now affects, are to be feared to hinder rather than help. But in this part too I not only forewarned him, but was at hand to him with counsel and the help of medicines, so far as I now had them ready, and prescribed a method how more are to be prepared hereafter, which may, in my opinion, so far as God shall grant, relieve this affliction of health to which he is subject. And so, as I said, I sent him off yesterday, making for Vienna, praying all auspicious things for him — there being joined to him a certain Master, called Johannes Kepler, excellently skilled in Mathematics, who, coming from Styria, was here with me for a third of a year [four months], and has resolved to return, his affairs there being settled. Since he must, on his return, pass through Vienna, and is acquainted with the men and the roads on this journey, he can be a useful companion to Friedrich. For the rest, just as you began your letter with a thing welcome and pleasing — about the change, by divine help, of my affairs for the better — not without joyful applause, and then in the middle interposed certain sad and turbulent and less smiling things about the calamity of our common kinsman, so at last at the end you bring on a glad catastrophe, especially delightful to hear, so that you seem to represent a kind of comedy. For what you report last (to leave the earlier things, of which enough is now said): about your dearest Sophia, my niece — that God blessed her with the fruit of the womb, and that she was then near her delivery; whom Friedrich reported also to have happily given birth — these things, I say, affected me with great delight, and were most welcome to hear. May God, Best and Greatest, deign of His goodness to preserve this little daughter He has given you safe, and guard her from dangers, that, growing in age, she may be a joy to both Parents. To which everlasting and triune Godhead I hold you, and your wife, together with your female offspring, commended with my whole heart.

Farewell.
Given from the imperial castle of Benátky, the 3rd day of June, the year 1600.
Yours, Tycho Brahe, in his own hand.

[Postscript:] POSTSCRIPTS. I have added certain poems, sent me these days by Paul Melissus, the standard-bearer of the Poets of Germany, together with a copy of his letter, that you may know on what occasion he set them down. And because near the foot he makes mention of the Ode of your Cramer, I have appended this too. If leisure permit the transcribing, I shall add also a copy of the letter of Lord Joseph Scaliger, which has likewise very lately been delivered to me. When you have read these through, I should wish you to share them with Lord Doctor Nicolaus, our common friend. / Sir, beloved kinsman, since after the victory won and the trophy taken up you press on with a less valid insistence — according to that saying, "there will be none willing to yield to genius" — I have nothing else to answer than that there is gold with me which not even Hercules could have wrested away with his club, far less that it should yield to such feeble arguments. Farewell, and beware lest hereafter you lay a wager with those who measure the matter geometrically. Given as above in your letter. T. B. / When I had already written this letter to you, a little after Friedrich Rosenkrantz had departed from me, two months having now elapsed, I kept it by me meanwhile until a sure and safe occasion of sending should be at hand; which I have now conveniently found through Master Christiernus Longomontanus, who for several years was a member of my household and is returning to Denmark (and whom, should he have need of your help, I beg you to hold commended on my behalf and to advance to better things), and I judged that I should keep it by me no longer, together with the enclosures of which they make mention. I am now at Prague, whither I was summoned a month ago by the Sacred Imperial Majesty, our most clement Lord, as soon as he had returned from Pilsen — where for three quarters of a year he was absent on account of the plague — to the castle of his usual residence; for His Majesty deigned to speak with me in secret about certain particular matters, and to commit to me certain things to be done for him, and likewise to learn from me whether the things he had long since most benignly ordered to be done for the advancement of my studies and the convenience of my living were so succeeding — which was done more often than once, for whole hours, privately, in His Majesty's chamber, and all the things I could most humbly desire were most clemently granted me. But since I do not know whether His Majesty wishes to speak with me at greater length and to command anything further beyond this, I do not dare to depart hence before I am sufficiently certain of this matter. The Legates of great Princes are arriving, and now there is here [the Legate] of the King of France (who has invited me to him tomorrow) with a most splendid retinue, and great is the mass of business to which His Majesty must needs direct his cares, to one thing and another as they fall out. As far as I have understood from the Magnificent Lord Coraduz, the Imperial Vice-Chancellor, His Majesty would wish that I obtain a dwelling nearer to himself, where I might equally pursue my studies (which is even now being worked at); with what success this may be, you will learn another time. There is also a certain other business, less welcome yet necessary, which keeps me here of my own will. For that man of Dithmarschen, the Author of that infamous writing against me, lies mortally ill — partly worn out by graver diseases, partly ill at ease in conscience over that deed, and fearing punishment; him I have long since wished, having been summoned to law for those atrocious injuries and calumnies which you know, to be lawfully judged; but because of the plague, with the Emperor's Court dispersed for almost a whole year, this could not be done before. I therefore now press the legal process, since the action is personal and there is danger in delay; and I have now obtained from the Imperial Majesty that certain Commissioners be deputed from the Councillors, to take cognizance of everything in this difficult case and to pronounce a definitive sentence; what this is to be, you will learn at last. When in these past days I was walking in the Court of the Castle in the morning hours among Gentle and Noble Men, there was pointed out to me that Baron Octavius Caurianus, to whom Lord Coraduz had written on behalf of our Rosenkrantz; wherefore, having caught a convenient occasion, I conversed diligently with him about Rosenkrantz — where he might be (for he had written nothing to me since he departed hence), and in what state his affairs stood. He said that Rosenkrantz had been most clemently received by Archduke Matthias, and had soon set out, with certain horsemen, as he himself wished, into Hungary, and was there in the army of our Emperor, which is now occupied in the siege of the Castle of Pápa — where we have heard that that vigorous commander of the war, Schwarzenburg, was lately struck by a musket-ball and fell, the same Octavius likewise confirming the report. But that I might be the more fully informed about Rosenkrantz's affairs (particularly since I said that these days I would be writing into Denmark to certain of his kinsmen and friends), he shared with me this paper, subscribed in his own hand, which I have placed here. When I have learned more things concerning him, I will make you further partaker of them. Farewell, and greet again most carefully on my behalf your wife, my dearest niece; I had resolved to write something to her myself, but these courtly interruptions so call me away (for from this very moment I am about to go up to the castle), and I was unwilling to detain Master Christiernus, of whom I spoke, already girt for his journey, any longer. For I could scarcely attend to my own affairs meanwhile; that will be done, God willing, another time, when better leisure is granted. Greet also dutifully on my behalf your mother, the most noble and most honourable Lady, and likewise your other friends, whom you know to be upright and sincere, and persevering in love of us. Again, farewell. Given at Prague, in haste, the 3rd day of August, New Style, in the year 1600. T. T. B.

Apparatus

- Recipient. Holger Rosenkrantz of Rosenholm — Tycho's kinsman (and, via his wife Sophie Brahe, nephew-in-law); cf. brahe-t_15980925001.
- Significance — a multi-thread letter that resolves the Friedrich-Rosenkrantz / Kepler / globe episode.
1. Imperial favour. Tycho's standing under Rudolf II grows daily (the Emperor at Pilsen); the Bohemian nobility has offered to adopt Tycho as a native of the realm with full privileges.
2. Friedrich Rosenkrantz — the family side of brahe-t_16000502001. Holger's cousin Friedrich Rosenkrantz (the disgraced kinsman of the Archduke Matthias letter of 2 May 1600) came to Tycho "a month ago" and left yesterday (2 June) for Vienna. Tycho recounts the whole effort: sending him to the court at Pilsen with a letter to Coraduz, who obtained the Emperor's intercession to Archduke Matthias (head of the Hungarian war) "sooner than expected," plus a private letter to a court Baron; and Tycho's own humble letter to Matthias (the letter of 2 May 1600) — discreetly "intimating his misfortune in his fatherland" to forestall worse rumour.
3. The gift to Matthias — the globe "made at Alkmaar." Confirms and specifies the gift announced on 2 May 1600: two books (matching those given to Christian IV) and "a globe, made with skill at Alkmaar in Holland, from our verification of the stars." A precise provenance for the celestial globe sent to Matthias — made/finished at Alkmaar in Holland from Tycho's star-catalogue — a notable datum for celestial-globe history (cf. the Dutch globe-makers, brahe-t_15990417001, and the Fischer letter brahe-t_16000516002). Flagged for the globe-history layer.
4. Friedrich's fatal illness. A striking medical passage: Friedrich suffers a "not lightly damaged lung," turning asthmatic, wasting with consumption (phthisis) — worse than he realises; Tycho gave counsel, medicines, and a regimen, fearing his chosen "manner of life and diet" will harm him. (Friedrich died in the Hungarian war in 1602.)
5. Kepler travels with Friedrich — resolving the departure. Tycho sends, as Friedrich's travelling companion to Vienna, "a certain Master, Johannes Kepler, excellently skilled in Mathematics, who came from Styria and was here with me a third of a year and now resolves to return." This is the execution of the very arrangement of the Danish Rindschat letter (brahe-t_16000000001) — the Rosenkrantz kinsman taking Kepler to Vienna at no cost — and fixes the departure to 2 June 1600. The April-1600 Kepler letters — the angry one of 8 April (brahe-t_16000408001) and the warm Danish one (brahe-t_16000000001) — were the prelude; this is the event. (Note Kepler's residence is here "a third of a year" ≈ 4 months → arrival ~Feb 1600.)
6. Holger's newborn daughter. The "glad catastrophe": Sophie Brahe (Tycho's niece, Holger's wife) had given birth to a daughter — reported by Friedrich; Tycho rejoices and blesses the child.
- Cross-references. Binds together brahe-t_16000000001 (Rindschat), brahe-t_16000502001 (Matthias), and this letter as one episode (Friedrich Rosenkrantz + Kepler → Vienna, June 1600); refines the Rindschadt letter's date to ~May 1600; adds the Alkmaar globe-provenance to the globe-distribution record.
- Persons & places named. Holger Rosenkrantz; Rudolf II; Friedrich Rosenkrantz; Coraduz (Vice-Chancellor); Archduke Matthias; the unnamed court Baron; Christian IV ("your King"); Johannes Kepler; Sophie Brahe (Holger's wife, Tycho's niece) and the newborn daughter. Places: Benátky, Pilsen, Vienna, Styria, Alkmaar (Holland), Hungary, Denmark.
- Date (the edition's convention /). Source dateline die 3. Iunij Anno 1600 → 3 June 1600 [N.S.] (led with, by the edition's convention). The catalogue label's alternative "3 August" (and the identifier's 0803) is an error — contradicted by the text and by the internal chronology (Kepler "here a third of a year"; Friedrich "came a month ago," i.e. ~early May → departed 2 June). Flagged for the label-date audit.
