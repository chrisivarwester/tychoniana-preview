English translation machine translation, Probable

To the Honourable, Our trusty and well-beloved, Tycho Brahe, Lord of Knudstrup.
Moritz, by the grace of God, Landgrave of Hesse, Count of Katzenelnbogen, etc.

Our gracious greeting beforehand, Our trusty and well-beloved. We have received your letter sent to us, dated Uraniborg the 20th of February, [and] read it, and from it understood at length what it is you desire of us. That we should now be extolled as so lofty and excellent a poet, and that our poetry should be so highly commended to you — in this indeed too much is done to us, for we know we cannot make good such a stately commendation, [nor live up to] the trust you place in us, in the [a] work; yet it is not untrue that in our early studies we did exercise ourselves somewhat in poetry — though for a good while now we have not employed that exercise, on account of other matters more pressing upon us.

Nonetheless, in accordance with your request, we have made ready a commendatory poem [Carmen commendatorium], as well as we were able to make it, for your astronomical works [Opera Astronomica] which you intend to bring out in print; and we have written that same [poem] in our own hand, on the page before the Imperial privilege [Diplomate Caesareo], where you desired it [to be]. Should our commendatory poem now be acceptable to you, we should take a gracious pleasure in it.

This we would not, in turn, graciously withhold from you; and we are well-disposed toward you with gracious goodwill.

Given at Kassel, the 2nd of April, in the year 1592.
Moritz, Landgrave of Hesse.

MORITZ, LANDGRAVE OF HESSE, TO TYCHO BRAHE, A NOBLE AND MOST ILLUSTRIOUS MAN.

Divine Urania surpasses the rest of her Sisters,
and is called the foremost glory of the sacred Choir.

Her the Fathers of old cultivated, and mighty Kings,
who by the power of mind touched the gods more nearly.

Her the wise man chooses to set as the goal of his studies,
that he may bear away the worthy rewards of famed renown.

For by as much as the lofty Ether rises above the Earth,
by so much does the knowledge of it stand first in praise.

She herself teaches the motions of the heavens, and God himself
bids us look upon heaven with faces raised.

Let us not fear the fates that evils bring, but, forewarned
by the Stars, let us take refuge in the help of those above.

Nor let us seek our fodder on the ground, but let him be borne up by praise
who, as Creator, built so great a Work as the heavens.

Therefore, rejoicing in your merits, go on cherishing
with your studies so great a Goddess; she will grant bountiful gifts —

seeing that she has long marvelled that all things which before lay hidden
have become open to your instruments.

Hence, for you as you search out the laws of lofty Olympus,
she prepares an honoured place among the Stars.

Apparatus

  • Issuer. Moritz "the Learned," Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel (1572–1632), who had succeeded his father Wilhelm IV on the latter's death (25 August 1592 — so Moritz writes here still as the young heir, in his nineteenth year, though already styling himself Landgraf; his father was alive at the date of writing). A polymath, composer, and Latinist, Moritz inherited his father's astronomical patronage and the Kassel observatory circle (Rothmann, Bürgi). The full chancery titulature — Landgraf zu Hessen, Graf zu Katzenelnbogen — and the princely first-person plural (Wir / euch / unser) are preserved by the edition's convention
  • Recipient address. Dem Vesten = "To the Honourable/Steadfast" (the conventional honorific for a nobleman below princely rank); unserm lieben Besondern = "Our trusty and well-beloved"; Herrn zu Knudstrup = "Lord of Knudstrup" (the Brahe family seat in Scania).
  • Occasion. Tycho's Epistolarum astronomicarum libri (printed at Uraniborg; Liber I appeared 1596), the very volume that prints this exchange. Tycho had written on 20 February 1592 (cited de dato Uraniburg, den 20. Februarij) requesting that Moritz contribute a commendatory poem to be placed before the work, next to the imperial privilege. The letter is Moritz's reply enclosing/announcing the poem.
  • Latin code-switches (kept in the source, italicised on first use): de dato "dated"; Poëta / Poëtica / Poësi "poet / poetry / poetry"; Commendation / commendiret / Carmen commendatorium "commendation / commended / commendatory poem"; Exercitium "exercise (practice)"; Opera Astronomica "astronomical works"; Diplomate Caesareo "the imperial privilege" — i.e. the printing privilegium / Privileg granted by the Holy Roman Emperor (here Rudolf II), conventionally printed at the front of a book; ut-style chancery diction. The mixing of Latin technical/legal nouns into a German princely body is the expected Kassel-court register.
  • Orthography (chancery German, c. 1592). ewer / ewerm = euer / euerm "your"; jr = ihr "you (pl./formal)"; gethan = getan; darauß = daraus; gerümbt = gerühmt "extolled"; statliche = stattliche "stately, splendid"; zutrawet = zutraut "entrust/credit"; numehr = nunmehr "now, by now"; außgehen = ausgehen "bring/go out"; außgehen lassen "have published"; annemlich = annehmlich "acceptable"; seyndt = seind/sind "are." The v/u convention (Vnsern, vns, vnser) and ß / -tz spellings are period-standard.
  • Textual crux — "im a Werck". The source reads "… wie jr vns auch zutrawet/ im a Werck nicht zu erweisen …". The stray lower-case a between im and Werck corresponds to no German word in context; it is almost certainly an editorial/printer's reference-mark (a marginal-note or sidenote signal of the kind the 1596 Epistolae uses) that has been pulled inline in the working transcription, the intended reading being im Werck = "in [the/its] working-out, in deed/in practice" (i.e. Moritz cannot in practice live up to the praise). The printed a is retained verbatim in the source block and bracketed [a] / [the] in the translation rather than silently deleted; the sense-reading im Werck "in deed/in practice" is adopted but flagged. (Cf. the parallel printer's-mark interruption.)
  • Spacing artefacts of the transcription. "Commendation,vnd" (missing space after the comma) and "DatumCassel" (run-together Datum Cassel) are reproduced as in the working transcription; both are typesetting/transcription spacing slips, not substantive. Datum Cassel = the dating formula "Given at Kassel."
  • Negations. Three are present and all reproduced: (1) im [a] Werck nicht zu erweisen "not able to make good in practice"; (2) Ohn ist es aber nicht, daß … — the period idiom es ist nicht ohne, daß = "it is not untrue / not without foundation that," i.e. an affirmative concession ("yet it is true that …"), here rendered "yet it is not untrue that"; (3) Wolten wir euch hinwider gnediglich nicht verhalten "this we would not graciously withhold from you in turn" (the standard chancery closing affirming the favourable communication). The double-negative idiom in (2) is the only point where a literal word-count could mislead an English reader; it is rendered as the affirmative concession the German idiom carries, and flagged here.
  • Calendar / place. Datum Cassel, am 2. Aprilis, Anno 1592 = "Given at Kassel, 2 April 1592." Hesse-Kassel kept the Julian (Old Style) calendar at this date (Protestant Germany did not adopt the Gregorian reform until 1700), so [O.S.]; this agrees with the catalogue label (1592. 2. april. Kassel). No in-letter date discrepancy. The cited incoming date den 20. Februarij (of Tycho's letter from Uraniborg) is likewise O.S.
  • The enclosed carmen commendatorium. This is the Latin dedicatory poem itself that Landgrave Moritz of Hesse-Kassel promises in the already-published body of the letter ("we have written that same poem in our own hand, on the page before the Imperial privilege...") — composed by Moritz personally to be printed before Rudolf II's imperial privilege (Diplomate Caesareo) in Tycho's forthcoming Opera Astronomica, i.e. the Astronomiae Instauratae Mechanica (Wandsbek, 1598). Letter and enclosure belong to the well-documented Hven-Kassel correspondence — Wilhelm IV, then his son and successor Moritz, corresponding with Tycho and his court astronomer Christoph Rothmann — which Mosley's Bearing the Heavens identifies as the single most-studied portion of Tycho's surviving correspondence.
  • Content of the poem. Fourteen lines of elegiac couplets celebrating Urania, muse of astronomy, as chief among the Muses and the pursuit of "ancient Fathers and mighty kings," closing with direct praise of Tycho: Urania "has long marvelled" that his "Fabricis" (instruments/constructions — Tycho's own term, as in the Mechanica's subject matter) have made accessible what before lay hidden, and the poem promises him "an honoured place among the Stars" for searching out "the laws of lofty Olympus."
  • Duplication in the recovered segment. The recovered text repeats the full heading ("MAVRITIVS HASSIAE LANDTGRAVIVS AD TYCHONEM BRAHE...") and the entire fourteen-line poem twice, verbatim. This reads as a duplication artifact of the recovery process rather than a feature of the original enclosure — the letter's own published German prose refers to "ein Carmen commendatorium" in the singular, with no hint that two copies were sent. Translated once here per house rule against padding; the duplication should be checked against the underlying manuscript/print exemplar (the Rothmann-Kassel correspondence materials are held at the Hessian State Archive, Marburg, and the Kassel university library) before being treated as meaningful.
  • Register. Rendered as close, line-for-line English prose, preserving the couplet breaks of the Latin elegiacs rather than attempting an English metrical equivalent.
