The original

TYCHO BRAHE AD MARTINUM BRASCHIUM.

NULLA tui oblivione nec mea negligentia accidit, eruditissime Domine BRASCHI, quod Mag. Dominus GÖDELLMANNUS, Legatum hîc agens Saxonicum, sine meo ad te responso Rostochium venerit. Iam enim illud inchoatum erat, sed is mea opinione citius abijt, ut vix eas, quæ magni momenti erant, et pecunias meas istic hærentes concernebant, complere licuerit, ideoque oretenus illi quædam dixi tibi insinuanda, quæ etiam literis ad Dominum D. BACKMEISTERUM, quas tum præ manibus habui, quatenus tempus tulit, subiunxi. Mitto autem nunc ea, quæ per quendam meorum de Astronomiæ historia e quodam libro descripta sunt. Plura invenies sub initium Astronomici Cæsarei APPIANI, tum quoque nomina quorundam Astronomorum, in priori parte tabularum primi mobilis REINHOLDI, quemadmodum etiam in Sphæra PEUCERI, quod tuæ industriæ relinquo, mihi enim non suppetit otium talia inquirendi. Carmen quomodo disponendum et concinnandum, tuæ discretioni et venæ Poëticæ liberum relinquo. Principium paulo aliter ordinandum censerem, nec volo, ut mei in primis carminibus fiat ulla mentio, sed omnia conveniente methodo per mundi ætates successive deducantur ad hunc Cæsarem RUDOLPHUM Secundum, postea, si tibi sic placuerit, poteris migrationem meam e Dania, quod ad hoc studium ab Augustissimo Imperatore huc clementissime vocatus sim, paucis attingere, sicque in Cæsaris laudem, hoc etiam nomine, discurrere, ita tamen ut optimum illum Regem FRIDERICUM Secundum, laudatissimæ memoriæ, qui hæc studia in suo regno, quoad vixit, liberaliter fovit, sub quo etiam Uraniburgum exædificatum est, non prætereas. Si quid aliud inciderit, de quo te monendum duxerim, in quadam charta adiungam, neque enim nunc tantum suppetit temporis, ut carmen tuum relegere atque omnes circumstantias dispicere valeam. Ubi integre tuas conceptiones ad colophonem deduxeris, mitte mihi exemplar, antequam imprimantur, et si liberum reliqueris, meam interponere sententiam, ingenue id faciam, daboque postea operam, quantum in me est, ut Cæsarea Mtas liberalem vicissim erga te clementer praebeat. Plura adderem, modo aliæ interpellationes aliaque negotia id paterentur. Tu itaque, mi optime BRASCHI, rectius quam nuper vale, et pleniores per meliorem oportunitatem a me expecta. Dabantur Pragæ ex ædibus olim Cæsaris, nunc novo Uraniburgo Cæsareo 14 Aprilis Anno 1601.

HISTORIA ILLUSTRIUM ASTRONOMORUM INDE A MUNDI EXORDIO AD NOSTRUM ÆVUM HEROICO CARMINE CONCINNATA.

NON placet in primo carmine radioque potentes, nam licet apud VIRGILIUM sit Descripsit radio, tamen hoc verbum radius non habet hîc emphasin in ipso carminis frontispicio, sed potius alicubi in medio. Videtur etiam principium nimis humile, quare sic mallem:

Artifices summos Divina scientia cœli
Quos celebres genuit, quæque incrementa Mathesis
Docta habuit, pedetentim Mundi ab origine prima
Ætatem in nostram, certis deducere fastis
Mens amet et clausos Heliconis inire recessus,
Arduum opus, atque alijs res intentata Poëtis,
Res preciosa licet et Musis digna canoris etc.

vel in eandem ferme sententiam per teipsum melius. Hæc saltem admonitionis causa assignare lubuit, prout nunc in buccam venerunt.

Postea inferre, cur hoc potius Argumentum arripueris, quam alias res ludicras huius inferioris scenæ mundanæ, quales sunt bella, victoriæ, triumphi, amores, Bucolica, et similia a prioribus Poëtis sæpe decantata, sed potius sublimia illa et præstantiora, quæ varijs fluctuationibus et vanitati non ita sunt obnoxia, quæque in tanto cœtera illa dignitate antecellunt, quantum arduus æther declivis terræ superat loca.

Huc addatur invocatio ad verum DEUM, totius cœli et universitatis creatorem, non Deastros licet fictitios, quos gentiles Poëtæ implorant, et tandem narratio exordium habeat ab ijs, quæ a MOISE primo capite Geneseos de cœli et siderum creatione referuntur. Deinde ex IOSEPHO antiquitatum Iudaicarum scriptore, quod ADAM docuerit suos filios et nepotes Astrorum scientiam, et duabus columnis excitatis in Siria ab ijsdem, et de NOAH, quem BEROSUS ait aliqua etiam ex parte Diluvium prævidisse ex Astrorum dispositione, et de ABRAHAMO, quod insignis Astronomus et Geometra fuerit, atque artes Ægyptios docuerit, quodque ex ijs, quæ circa Solem, Lunam et Sidera, terrasque et maria apparent, cognoverit unum saltem esse DEUM boni autorem in Natura, quod prædictus habet IOSEPHUS, et quod ABRAM appellatus sit, dum ex sola Astrorum scientia claruit et DEUM hinc aliquatenus agnovit, postquam vero divinitus est plenius edoctus et illuminatus, ABRAHAM, sic volente DEO, est dictus, ut PHILO Iudæus infert, quod etiam a MOISIS relatione non videtur alienum.

Deinceps poteris ordine recensere, quæ e profanis etiam scriptoribus colligi poterunt, quorum etiam aliquam indicationem suppeditabit liber Domini HENRICI RANZOVIJ de Regibus et Principibus, qui Astronomiam foverunt. Omnia autem ordine se sequantur, prout tempora ætatum mundi requirunt, sed decenti methodo, ut principaliora præcedant ea, quæ minus ad rem faciunt; et narretur etiam, quomodo Astronomia peregrinata sit ex Siria et Mesopotamia in Ægyptum, hinc ad Chaldæos, Arabes, Æthiopes, Indos, ex Ægypto etiam venisse in Græciam, hinc in Italiam et Hispaniam ac Mauritaniam, postea in Galliam et Germaniam, atque ibi præcipua lumina, quæ in hac arte apud Germanos viguerunt, commemorare, usque ad COPERNICUM et REINHOLDUM, et quod tandem in Daniam transfretârit, ubi regnante optimo Rege FRIDERICO Secundo quietem et portum aliquandiu invenit, exædificata arce Uraniburgo in Insula celebris illius Porthmo Danici Huenna, et fabricatis magnis et idoneis Instrumentis, qualia non alibi reperiuntur, ubi etiam per 21 annos strenue exercebatur, quamdiu idem Rex vixit, tum quoque in octennali interregno post ipsum. Verum cum sequentibus annis firmam sedem ibi habere nequierit, in Germaniam redijt, et in Holsatia feudoque Producis HENRICI RANZOVIJ aliquandiu quievit, et ibi laudare RANZOVIUM, tam ob hanc quam alias virtutes, et quod aliæ circumiacentes regiones, ut Gallia, Anglia et Batavia, interea eam ambiverint, sed vocata ab Augustissimo Imperatore RUDOLPHO II Austriaco, in Bohemiam ad ipsum concedere prætulerit, ubi ab eodem munifice non sine aliorum invidia sustentata est, et primum de arce Bennatika, ubi per annuum spacium mansit, postea de Praga, ubi est residentia Cæsaris et Regis Bohemici, hisque ædibus Curtianis in eos usus liberaliter a Cæsare coemptis. Hoc etiam loco quædam de earundem structore, Domino IACOBO CURTIO, priore Procancellario, inserenda, siquidem is Mathesi et Astronomicis studijs impense addictus fuerit, atque ea de causa hasce ædes in maxime ædito civitatis loco magnis impensis exstruxerit, quodque, dum adhuc in vivis esset, mihi etiamnum in Dania commoranti inhabitandas obtulerit, si huc Pragam venire vellem, quod, vivente tunc Rege FRIDERICO, non concedebatur, et nunc post fata eiusdem Domini CURTIJ non sine numine et Divino provisu ex liberalitate Cæsaris RUDOLPHI easdem ædes mihi cessisse, pro Astronomia hîc excolenda. Postmodum elogia domus Austriacæ subiungere, atque ad hunc RUDOLPHUM omnia deducere, atque in eius excurrere laudes easque ampliare, atque victorias et præclare gesta commemorare, inter quæ hoc quoque non postremum, quod Astronomiam foveat, qua in re non cedat ALPHONSO Regi, cuius fama per Tabulas et liberalitatem in eas construendas cœlo coæva durabit, cumque id numeri ipsi cœlo non per omnia correspondentes tribuere potuerint, quid tibi, Cæsar Austriace, ubi Tabulæ Rudolpheæ sub auspicijs tuis cœlestes motus exactissime æmulantes lucem viderint? etc.

TYCHO BRAHE TO MARTIN BRASCH.

It is by no forgetfulness of you, nor negligence of mine, most learned Lord Brasch, that the magnificent Lord Gödelmann, acting here as Saxon Legate, came [back] to Rostock without my answer to you. For it was already begun; but he left sooner than I thought, so that I could scarcely complete those [letters] which were of great moment and concerned my monies stuck there; and so I told him by word of mouth certain things to convey to you, which I also subjoined — so far as time allowed — to the letter to Lord Doctor Backmeister that I then had in hand. But I now send what one of my men copied out, from a certain book, about the history of Astronomy. You will find more at the beginning of Apian's Astronomicum Caesareum, and also the names of certain Astronomers in the former part of Reinhold's tables of the primum mobile, as likewise in Peucer's Sphaera — which I leave to your industry, for I have no leisure to inquire into such things. How the poem is to be arranged and composed, I leave free to your discretion and poetic vein.

The beginning I should think is to be ordered a little otherwise; and I do not wish that any mention of me be made in the first verses, but that everything be deduced, by a fitting method, through the ages of the world successively, down to this Emperor Rudolf II; afterward, if it so please you, you may touch briefly on my migration from Denmark — that I was most clemently called hither for this study by the most august Emperor — and so discourse, on this account too, to the Emperor's praise; yet so that you do not pass over that excellent King Frederick II, of most praised memory, who liberally fostered these studies in his realm while he lived, under whom too Uraniborg was built. If anything else occur of which I judge you should be advised, I will add it on a sheet; for I have not now so much time as to re-read your poem and consider all the circumstances. When you have brought your conceptions entire to the colophon, send me a copy before they are printed; and if you leave me free to interpose my opinion, I will do it frankly; and I will afterward take pains, so far as in me lies, that the Imperial Majesty in turn show himself clemently liberal toward you. I would add more, if other interruptions and other business allowed it. So fare better than of late, my excellent Brasch, and await fuller [letters] from me by a better opportunity. Given at Prague, from the house once the Emperor's, now the new Imperial Uraniborg, 14 April, the year 1601.

HISTORY OF ILLUSTRIOUS ASTRONOMERS, FROM THE BEGINNING OF THE WORLD TO OUR AGE, COMPOSED IN HEROIC VERSE.

I do not like, in the first verse, "radioque potentes"; for although in Virgil there is "Descripsit radio," yet this word radius has no emphasis here, at the very frontispiece of the poem, but rather somewhere in the middle. The beginning, too, seems too humble; wherefore I should prefer thus:

> Let my mind love to bring forth, step by step — from the world's first origin into our age, by sure annals — the supreme craftsmen whom the divine science of heaven begot famous, and what increments learned Mathematics has had; and to enter the closed recesses of Helicon — an arduous work, a thing untried by other Poets, yet a precious thing, and worthy of the tuneful Muses, etc.

— or to nearly the same sense, better, by yourself. These I was pleased to set down only by way of admonition, as they now came to the mouth.

Afterward, bring in why you have seized this Argument rather than the other frivolous things of this lower worldly scene — such as wars, victories, triumphs, loves, Bucolics, and the like, often sung by earlier Poets — but rather those sublime and more excellent things, not so subject to various fluctuations and to vanity, and which excel the rest in dignity as much as the high aether surpasses the low places of the sloping earth.

To this let there be added an invocation to the true GOD, creator of all heaven and the universe — not the fictitious godlings whom the gentile Poets implore — and at last let the narration take its beginning from what is reported by Moses, in the first chapter of Genesis, on the creation of heaven and the stars. Then from Josephus, writer of the Jewish Antiquities: that Adam taught his sons and grandsons the science of the Stars, and of the two columns raised in Syria by them; and of Noah, whom Berosus says foresaw the Flood partly from the disposition of the Stars; and of Abraham, that he was a notable Astronomer and Geometer, and taught the arts to the Egyptians, and from what appears about the Sun, Moon, Stars, lands, and seas knew that there is but one GOD, author of good in Nature (which the aforesaid Josephus has) — and that he was called Abram while he was famed from the science of the stars alone and thence somewhat knew God, but, after he was more fully taught and illuminated divinely, was called Abraham (God so willing), as Philo the Jew infers — which too does not seem alien from Moses's account.

Thereafter you may recount, in order, what can be gathered from the profane writers too — of which some indication will be supplied by the book of Lord Henry Rantzau, On the Kings and Princes who fostered Astronomy. But let all follow in order, as the times of the world's ages require, yet by a decent method, so that the more principal things precede those that make less to the matter. And let it be narrated how Astronomy travelled from Syria and Mesopotamia into Egypt, thence to the Chaldeans, Arabs, Ethiopians, Indians; from Egypt came also into Greece, thence into Italy and Spain and Mauritania, afterward into Gaul and Germany — and there commemorate the chief lights who flourished in this art among the Germans, up to Copernicus and Reinhold; and that at last it crossed over into Denmark, where, under the reign of the excellent King Frederick II, it found rest and a port for a while — the castle Uraniborg built on the Island, in that famous Danish Sound, Hven, and great and suitable Instruments fabricated, such as are found nowhere else, where also [Tycho] was strenuously occupied for 21 years, as long as that King lived, and also in the eight-year interregnum after him. But since in the following years he could not have a firm seat there, he returned into Germany, and rested a while in Holstein and the fief of the Pro-duke Henry Rantzau — and there praise Rantzau, both for this and for his other virtues; and that other surrounding regions — Gaul, England, and Holland — meanwhile courted her [Astronomy], but, called by the most august Emperor Rudolf II of Austria, she preferred to go to him into Bohemia, where by him she was munificently sustained (not without others' envy): first at the castle Benátky (where she stayed for a year's space), then at Prague, the residence of the Emperor and King of Bohemia, in the Curtius house, liberally bought for these uses by the Emperor.

In this place, too, something is to be inserted about the builder of that house, Lord Jacob Curtius [Kurz], the former Vice-Chancellor — since he was deeply devoted to Mathematics and Astronomical studies, and for that cause built this house, at great cost, in the very highest place of the city; and that, while he was yet alive, he offered it to me — still dwelling then in Denmark — to inhabit, if I would come hither to Prague; which, King Frederick then living, was not permitted; and now, after the death of that same Lord Curtius, not without [divine] will and providence, the same house was ceded to me by the liberality of the Emperor Rudolf, for cultivating Astronomy here. Afterward subjoin the elogia of the House of Austria, and deduce all things to this Rudolf, and run out into his praises and amplify them, and commemorate his victories and glorious deeds — among which this too [is] not the least, that he fosters Astronomy, in which matter he yields not to King Alfonso, whose fame, through the Tables and his liberality in constructing them, will endure coeval with heaven; and since [those Alfonsine] numbers could not correspond to heaven in all things — what [praise] for you, Austrian Caesar, when the Rudolphine Tables, under your auspices, most exactly emulating the celestial motions, shall have seen the light? etc.

Apparatus

  • Recipient. Martin Brasch / Martinus Braschius (1550–1601), the Rostock poet (cf. brahe-t_16000915001; the "Nicolaus" name-error in brahe-t_16000808001). Written from the Curtius house, "the new Imperial Uraniborg" (one day before brahe-t_16010415001 to Bacmeister, dated from the same place).
  • Significance — Tycho's outline for a verse History of Astronomy, and the naming of the Rudolphine Tables. A remarkable document: Tycho commissions and minutely plans an epic poem, the History of Illustrious Astronomers from the Beginning of the World to Our Age, to culminate in Rudolf II — and supplies:
  • Sources for Brasch: a copied extract on the history of astronomy, plus Apian's Astronomicum Caesareum, Reinhold's tables of the primum mobile, Peucer's Sphaera, and Heinrich Rantzau's De regibus et principibus qui astronomiam foverunt.
  • Tycho's own hexameter verse — seven lines he prefers to Brasch's "too humble" opening (rejecting "radioque potentes" with a point about Virgil's "Descripsit radio"). A rare specimen of Tycho composing Latin poetry.
  • The structural plan: a recusatio (why astronomy, not wars/loves/Bucolics); an invocation to the true God (not "fictitious godlings"); a Biblical/patristic prologue (Moses/Genesis; Josephus on Adam's star-science and the two Syrian columns; Noah/Berosus and the Flood; Abraham the astronomer-geometer, the Abram→Abraham etymology per Philo); then the migration of astronomy Syria→Egypt→Chaldeans/Arabs/Ethiopians/Indians→Greece→Italy/Spain/Mauritania→Gaul/Germany→Copernicus & Reinhold→Denmark.
  • Tycho's autobiographical self-presentation (in the third person, for the poem): 21 years at Uraniborg/Hven under Frederick II + the 8-year interregnum; the return to Holstein under Heinrich Rantzau; the courting by Gaul, England, Holland; the imperial call; Benátky (one year) → Prague / the Curtius house.
  • The Curtius-house backstory (new detail). Jacob Curtius (Kurz von Senftenau), the mathematically-devoted former Vice-Chancellor, built the house and had offered it to Tycho years earlier (declined while Frederick II lived); after Curtius's death it passed to Tycho by Rudolf's gift — "the new Imperial Uraniborg."
  • The climax — the Rudolphine Tables. Casting Rudolf as surpassing Alfonso X (whose Alfonsine Tables "could not correspond to heaven in all things"), Tycho gives Brasch the poem's intended peroration: "what [praise] for you, Austrian Caesar, when the Tabulae Rudolpheae, under your auspices, most exactly emulating the celestial motions, shall have seen the light?" — the earliest naming of the Rudolphine Tables in this corpus, by Tycho himself, months before his death (the work Kepler would complete and publish in 1627).
  • Cross-references. A keystone for the Rudolphine Tables project, the Curtius-house / "new Uraniborg" identity (brahe-t_16010415001, brahe-t_16000103001), the exile/autobiography thread, and the Alfonsine comparison (brahe-t_16000915001).
  • Persons & works named. Martin Brasch; Gödelmann; Lucas Backmeister; Apian (Astronomicum Caesareum), Erasmus Reinhold, Caspar Peucer (Sphaera); Virgil; Moses, Josephus, Adam, Noah, Berosus, Abraham, Philo Judaeus; Heinrich Rantzau (De regibus … qui astronomiam foverunt); Copernicus; Jacob Curtius (Kurz); Alfonso X; Rudolf II, Frederick II of Denmark; the Rudolphine Tables. Places: Prague (Curtius house), Benátky, Hven/Uraniborg, Holstein, Denmark, the whole translatio of astronomy (Syria→…→Germany→Denmark).
  • Calendar. 14 Aprilis Anno 1601 at Prague (imperial reckoning) → 14 April 1601 [N.S.]; header and label agree.
