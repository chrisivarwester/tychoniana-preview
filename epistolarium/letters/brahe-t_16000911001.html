The original

NOBILISSIMO DOCTISSIMOQUE VIRO DOMINO TYCHONI BRAHE, DOMINO A KNUDSTRUP ET URANIBURGO, MATHEMATICORUM OMNIUM FACILE PRINCIPI, DOMINO ET AMICO SUO SINGULARI OBSERVANTIA COLENDO. PRAGAe.

CUM praeteritis diebus, vir nobilissime atque Mathematicorum omnium facile princeps, Augusta transirem, ipse consignavi Theatrum tuum Mechanicum Generoso viro, D. PHILIPPO EDUARDO FUGGERO. Egit ille tum gratias ingentes, et pollicitus est, se tibi in scriptis quoque gratias acturum. Hac ipsa die domum reversus reperi litteras tuas die 28. Augusti stylo novo Pragae exaratas, quibus inter alia desideras quantocitius ea tibi transmitti, quae M. IOANNES KEPLERUS de hypothesibus tuis antehac ad me scripserit. Est is sane Mathematicus insignis, mihique tam eo nomine, quam et ob candorem suum perquam acceptus: quare nollem eum quavis ratione vel occasione offendere. Sed cum scribas fato iam functum illum, qui sibi hypotheses illas arrogaverat, et non modo pro re nata, verum etiam pro benevolentia tua, qua me complecteris, plane mihi persuasum sit, hanc communicationem amico meo D. KEPLERO non fore ingratam, idcirco morem gerere tibi statui. Scripsit autem is 30. die Maij anno proxime elapso 1599 haec verba:

»Fatetur URSUS, se hypotheses illas suas non a seipso habere nisi, quatenus aliquid differt a TYCHONE. Contendit vicissim TYCHO, quas profiteatur hypotheses (qua parte discrepant a Copernicanis) eas se suo Marte invenisse nec aliquid simile uspiam vidisse. Contra URSUS, et suas et TYCHONIS deductas esse APOLLONIJ PERGÆI iam pridem circa tempora CHRISTI publicatis hypothesibus. Huius sui pronunciati caussam reddit loca aliqua COPERNICI, in quibus partim ipsa hypothesis TYCHONIS expresse ponatur ab ipso COPERNICO, partim vero APOLLONIO PERGÆO ascribatur. Atqui nihil minus ijs locis COPERNICUS dicit quam hoc ipsum quod vult URSUS. Neque enim TYCHONIS hypothesin exprimit (fallitur ex allusione URSUS) nec eam APOLLONIO PERGÆO ascribit. Cum ergo fateatur URSUS 1. se hausisse suam hypothesin aliunde, 2. invenisse expressam in COPERNICO et attributam PERGÆO, 3. eandemque esse quam habet TYCHO: fatetur ergo se descripsisse illam ab aliquo, qui idem dicit quod TYCHO.

Notetur hoc, quod qui suas URSO monstrarit hypotheses (dempto exiguo quod mutatu facile est) is eadem plane ὑποτιθεῖ cum TYCHONE. Quis autem is est? URSUS dicit, COPERNICUM illum esse, et in eo PERGÆUM praetereaque neminem. Nemo itaque est Author Ursinarum praeter aut PERGÆUM, in COPERNICO, aut TYCHONEM. At ego dico, PERGÆUM non esse Authorem Ursinarum, nec COPERNICUM. Ergo solus TYCHO praetereaque nemo fuit URSO commonstrator ad suas hypotheses. Firmamentum caussae in eo est, ut genuinum sensum COPERNICI habeamus. Caetera confessione sua concinnavit URSUS ipse. Ergo lib. 3. cap. 25. Revolutionum, ubi Tychonias URSUS a COPERNICO putat exprimi, largitur quidem COPERNICUS aliquos ex motibus, quos ipse Terrae tribuit, Soli transcribi posse: sed non id largitur de motu annuo, nec de motibus 5 Planetarum capitalibus ibi agit: nec id fert argumentum libri, quod versatur in explicatione solius motus Solaris (sive terrestris) sine respectu motuum caeterarum stellarum. Ad evidentiam rei describam duo schemata mentem COPERNICI explicantia:

[Figura] Haec est hypothesis COPERNICI, circa unum orbem Terrae vel Solis.

[Figura] Haec est mutatio, quam libro 3. cap. 25. etiam possibilem ponit, et in dubio relinquit.

In priore schemate A centrum et mundi et corporis Solaris. B centrum Epicycli, quod salvat eccentricum (COPERNICUS ex epicyclis et magno orbe facit eccentricum, idem esse ipse ait). Et B illud aequaliter per anni spacium circa A circumit. C centrum Epicyclij quod mutationem praestat eccentricitatis. Illud C itidem annuo motu circumit circa B, in partem contrariam. D Terra est, quae intra annos 3434 circa C circumagitur.

In altero schemate F est centrum mundi et motus Terrae H, quae annuo motu adhuc aequaliter circumit. E est centrum corporis Solaris, quod circumit circa G centrum in annis 3434. G est centrum epicycli, qui Solem E fert, praestans apogaeo Solis progressum, nam in annis 17188 secundum COPERNICUM et Prutenicas circa F circumit, sub fixis. Itaque movebitur quidem loco suo Sol duobus motibus, sed tardissime, nec multum. Sunt enim circelli valde angusti ad comparationem eius circuli, in quo, in utroque schemate, Terra currit. At URSUS, seu inscitia, seu stultitia (qui haec neminem intellecturum speravit) putat COPERNICUM de tali motu Solis loqui, qui sit similis motui Terrae annuo Copernicano: quique aliquid in parallaxibus 5 Planetarum inferat. Nam hi duo, soli secundo loco concessi a COPERNICO, in consideratione caeterorum Planetarum negliguntur, sive Terrae sive Soli attribuantur: quamvis postulante id demonstratione exiguum aliquid inferant, quod videtur sentire in observationibus TYCHO. Haec de primo loco.

In reliquis locis ex COPERNICO allegatis lib. 5. c. 3, 35 adhuc multo peiorem causam habet URSUS. COPERNICUS mentionem APOLLONIJ PERGÆI eiusque lemmatij ex PTOLOMÆO transtulit, quod miror non legisse URSUM in PTOLOMÆO. Sententia PERGÆI toto fere genere alia est. Non enim versatur ille in consideratione systematis 5 Planetarum, quorum centrum in corpore Solis sit, ut TYCHO atque COPERNICUS et quidam antiqui: sed solum in eo ut dicat, qualem proportionem habere debeat Epicyclus cuiuslibet Planetae, seorsim ad suum orbem proprium et horum ad motum anomaliae commutationis et motum aequalem periodicum, ut ex tali proportione sequatur Planetae illius statio et regressio. Sic etiam defectus eius hypotheseos in eo fuit, quod PERGÆUS opinatus est, quemlibet Planetam una tantum inaequalitate variari, quae a PTOLOMÆO salvatur per epicyclum, nesciens ipsum etiam epicycli centrum inaequaliter incedere, sive secundum PTOLOMÆUM, in Eccentrico. Hunc allegatorum locorum sensum esse omnes professores dicent. At URSUS aut caecus aut vafer, interpositione parenthetica vocis (mobilis) persuadere vult, PERGÆUM vel pro eo COPERNICUM loqui de communi illo motu omnium 5 Planetarum, propter Solem, centrum illorum, annuo motu circumactum: de quo motu TYCHONIS hypotheses sonant, quique a COPERNICO Terrae tribuitur. Atque hoc est firmamentum causae. Qui haec perpendit, illi iam ultro suspicio oritur ex omnibus circumstantijs. Fatetur se furti philosophici reum, se Magdeburgi sententiam TYCHONIS de mundi habitu publicam fecisse, sed iuxta suam, natam scilicet ex quadam matre, quae nuspiam est, ut modo quaerendo comperimus.«

Hactenus illa, quae M. KEPLERUS ad me scripsit. Ad alia quae scribis, responsum habebis, cum primum mihi fuerit tantum otij, atque hisce tibi officia et studia mea offero promptissime.

Datae Monachij die 11. Sept. Anno 1600.
Nobilissimae T. Dni Addictissimus
IOAN. GEORG. HERWART AB HOHENBURG, manu propria.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the most noble and most learned man, Lord Tycho Brahe, Lord of Knudstrup and Uraniborg, easily the Prince of all Mathematicians, his Lord and friend to be revered with singular respect. At Prague.

When in past days, most noble man and easily Prince of all Mathematicians, I was passing through Augsburg, I myself delivered your Theatrum Mechanicum [the Mechanica] to the noble Lord Philipp Eduard Fugger. He then gave great thanks, and promised that he would thank you in writing too. This very day, returned home, I found your letter written at Prague on the 28th of August, New Style, in which, among other things, you desire that there be transmitted to you as quickly as possible what Master Johannes Kepler had earlier written to me about your hypotheses. He is indeed a distinguished Mathematician, and most welcome to me both on that account and for his candour; wherefore I should not wish to offend him by any means or occasion. But since you write that the one who had arrogated those hypotheses to himself [Ursus] is now dead, and since I am fully persuaded — both from the case as it stands and from your benevolence, with which you embrace me — that this communication will not be unwelcome to my friend Lord Kepler, I have therefore resolved to comply with you. Now he wrote, on the 30th day of May of the year just past, 1599, these words:

"Ursus admits that he does not have those hypotheses of his from himself, except so far as something differs from Tycho. Tycho in turn contends that the hypotheses he professes (in the part where they differ from the Copernican) he found by his own effort [suo Marte*], and saw nothing similar anywhere. Ursus, on the contrary, [contends] that both his and Tycho's are derived from the hypotheses of Apollonius of Perga, published long ago around the times of Christ. The cause of this his assertion he gives as certain passages of Copernicus, in which partly Tycho's hypothesis itself is expressly set down by Copernicus, partly is ascribed to Apollonius of Perga. But Copernicus says nothing less in those passages than this very thing which Ursus wants. For he neither expresses Tycho's hypothesis (Ursus is deceived by the allusion) nor ascribes it to Apollonius of Perga. Since, then, Ursus admits: (1) that he drew his hypothesis from elsewhere; (2) that he found it expressed in Copernicus and attributed to Perga; (3) that it is the same as Tycho's — he therefore admits that he copied it from someone who says the same as Tycho.

Let this be noted: that whoever showed Ursus his hypotheses (apart from a trifle easily changed) posits [ὑποτιθεῖ] plainly the same as Tycho. But who is that? Ursus says it is Copernicus, and in him Perga, and no one else. There is therefore no author of Ursus's [hypotheses] except either Perga (in Copernicus) or Tycho. But I say that Perga is not the author of Ursus's, nor Copernicus. Therefore Tycho alone, and no one else, was Ursus's informant for his hypotheses. The foundation of the case lies in this: that we have the genuine sense of Copernicus. The rest Ursus has framed by his own confession. So, in Book 3, ch. 25 of the Revolutions, where Ursus thinks the Tychonic [hypotheses] are expressed by Copernicus: Copernicus does indeed grant that some of the motions which he attributes to the Earth can be transcribed to the Sun — but he does not grant this of the annual motion, nor does he there treat the principal motions of the 5 Planets; nor does the book's argument bear it, which is engaged in explaining the solar (or terrestrial) motion alone, without respect to the motions of the other stars. For evidence of the matter I will describe two diagrams explaining Copernicus's mind:

[Figure] This is Copernicus's hypothesis, about the single orb of the Earth or Sun.

[Figure] This is the alteration which in Book 3, ch. 25 he sets down as also possible, and leaves in doubt.

In the former diagram, A [is] the centre both of the world and of the Solar body. B [is] the centre of the Epicycle which saves the eccentric (Copernicus makes the eccentric out of epicycles and the great orb, [and] says it is the same thing). And that B circles uniformly about A through the space of a year. C [is] the centre of the Epicycle which produces the variation of the eccentricity. That C likewise circles about B by an annual motion, in the contrary direction. D is the Earth, which is carried about C within 3434 years.

In the other diagram, F is the centre of the world and of the motion of the Earth H, which still circles uniformly by an annual motion. E is the centre of the Solar body, which circles about the centre G in 3434 years. G is the centre of the epicycle which carries the Sun E, producing the progress of the Sun's apogee — for in 17188 years, according to Copernicus and the Prutenic [Tables], it circles about F, beneath the fixed [stars]. So the Sun will indeed be moved from its place by two motions, but most slowly, and not much; for the little circles are very narrow in comparison with the circle in which, in both diagrams, the Earth runs. But Ursus, whether from ignorance or from folly (who hoped no one would understand these things), thinks Copernicus speaks of such a motion of the Sun as is similar to the Copernican annual motion of the Earth, and which would introduce something into the parallaxes of the 5 Planets. For these two [motions], granted to the Sun [only] in the second place by Copernicus, are neglected in the consideration of the other Planets, whether they be attributed to Earth or to Sun — although, the demonstration requiring it, they introduce some little thing, which Tycho seems to perceive in [his] observations. So much for the first passage.

In the remaining passages alleged from Copernicus, Book 5, ch. 3 [and] 35, Ursus has a still much worse case. Copernicus transferred the mention of Apollonius of Perga and his little lemma from Ptolemy — which I wonder Ursus did not read in Ptolemy. Perga's view is of an almost wholly different kind. For he is not engaged in the consideration of the system of the 5 Planets whose centre is in the Sun's body (as Tycho, and Copernicus, and certain of the ancients), but only in this: to state what proportion the Epicycle of each Planet ought to have, severally, to its own proper orb, and these to the motion of the anomaly of commutation and the equal periodic motion — so that from such proportion the station and regression of that Planet may follow. So too the defect of his hypothesis was this, that Perga supposed each Planet to be varied by only one inequality (which by Ptolemy is saved by an epicycle), not knowing that the centre of the epicycle itself also proceeds unequally — that is, according to Ptolemy, on the Eccentric. That this is the sense of the cited passages, all the professors will say. But Ursus, either blind or crafty, by the parenthetic insertion of the word "(mobile)," wants to persuade [us] that Perga, or in his place Copernicus, speaks of that common motion of all 5 Planets — on account of the Sun, their centre, carried round by an annual motion — of which motion Tycho's hypotheses speak, and which by Copernicus is attributed to the Earth. And this is the foundation of the case. Whoever weighs these things, to him suspicion now arises of its own accord from all the circumstances. [Ursus] confesses himself guilty of philosophical theft: that at Magdeburg he made public Tycho's view of the structure of the world, but according to his own [version] — born, namely, of a certain "mother" that exists nowhere, as we have just now found by inquiry."

Thus far what Master Kepler wrote to me. To the other things you write, you shall have an answer as soon as I have so much leisure; and herewith I offer you my services and zeal most readily.

Given at Munich, the 11th day of September, the year 1600.
Most devoted to Your most noble Lordship,
Johann Georg Herwart von Hohenburg, in his own hand.

Apparatus

  • Direction & correspondents. Incoming, Johann Georg Herwart von Hohenburg (1553–1622) — Bavarian chancellor, learned correspondent of Kepler — to Tycho. The body is a verbatim transcript of Kepler's letter to Herwart of 30 May 1599, sent at Tycho's request (Tycho's letter of 28 Aug 1600).
  • Significance — Kepler's brief in the Tycho-v-Ursus plagiarism case (a keystone of the dispute). This document carries, in Kepler's own words, his logical and technical demolition of Ursus's plagiarism defence:
  • The logical core. Ursus claimed both his and Tycho's world-systems derive from Apollonius of Perga (via passages of Copernicus). Kepler shows Copernicus neither states Tycho's hypothesis nor ascribes it to Apollonius; therefore, from Ursus's own three admissions, "Tycho alone, and no one else, was Ursus's informant" — Ursus copied the geo-heliocentric scheme from someone "who says the same as Tycho," and that someone can only be Tycho. (The pivot: "the foundation of the case lies in having the genuine sense of Copernicus.")
  • The technical refutation. Kepler reads the cited Copernican passages (De revolutionibus III.25; V.3, V.35) in detail — with two diagrams of Copernicus's solar/terrestrial models (centres A–D; F–H; the 3434-year and 17188-year cycles, per the Prutenic Tables) — to show Ursus misreads Copernicus's minor solar motions as the annual motion affecting planetary parallaxes, and misreads Apollonius's lemma (transmitted from Ptolemy), which concerns only single-inequality epicycle proportions, not a heliocentric five-planet system. Ursus's case rests on a dishonest parenthetic insertion of "(mobile)."
  • The verdict. Kepler brands Ursus "guilty of philosophical theft," who published Tycho's world-structure at Magdeburg as his own, dressed up as born "of a mother that exists nowhere."
  • Context. This is the analysis that underlies Kepler's Apologia Tychonis contra Ursum (commissioned by Tycho). Its transmission here (Sept 1600), with Tycho noting Ursus "is now dead" (Ursus died 1600), marks the dispute's late stage. (Cf. the Ursus thread: brahe-t_16000401001, brahe-t_15990922001, brahe-t_16000408001.)
  • A Mechanica distribution event. Herwart delivered Tycho's Mechanica ("Theatrum Mechanicum") to Philipp Eduard Fugger at Augsburg — a datable gift-distribution event to register with the Mechanica distribution layer.
  • Persons & works named. Herwart von Hohenburg; Johannes Kepler (the quoted author); Ursus (Nicolaus Reimers Bär); Tycho; Philipp Eduard Fugger; Copernicus (De revolutionibus), Apollonius of Perga, Ptolemy; the Prutenic Tables. Places: Munich, Augsburg, Prague, Magdeburg.
  • Text note. The two diagrams Kepler drew are not reproduced in the source (marked [Figure]); the lettered descriptions (A–D; E–H) are translated as written. The Greek ὑποτιθεῖ ("posits/hypothesises") is reproduced.
