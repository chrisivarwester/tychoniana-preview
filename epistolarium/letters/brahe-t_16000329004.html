The original

TYCHO BRAHE AD MARTINUM BRASCHIUM

S.

ACCEPTISSIMÆ mihi fuerunt tuæ literæ, Eruditissime et amantissime Domine Magister MARTINE BRASCHI, quas circa initia huius Anni filius meus e Daniâ rediens inter alias ab Amicis Rostochiensibus profectas attulit: ac pro benevolâ et candidâ congratulatione, quam de successu rerum nostrarum apud Augustissimum Imperatorem RUDOLPHUM Secundum, Dominum nostrum Clementissimum, a principio refers, tibi peramanter gratias habeo.

Probâsti etiam tuum erga me sincerum affectum carmine illo Elegiaco unâ transmisso, ubi Uraniam Imperatorem meâ de caussâ adloquentem eique gratias agentem introducis. Adiungam quidem id cœteris tuis mecum antea communicatis, quæ a multis probantur, sed cum in hoc non ita selectis per omnia utaris verbis, et tali concinnitate, quam requirit hoc genus carminis, ad imitationem venæ Ovidianæ aut aliorum Poëtarum, qui in hac facilitate excelluerunt, adeo ut respectu antecedentium carminum te ipso (liceat enim mihi quod censeo ingenue profiteri) minor videaris: et sint præterea in ipsâ Historicâ relatione, quam pressius sequi voluisti, nonnulla, quæ nimis diffusa, quoad particularia, nec satis decore prolata existimem; idcirco Elegiam hanc superioribus versibus, Heroicis et Lyricis, necdum adiunxi aut alijs monstrare lubuit. Sed si tuâ bonâ veniâ id fieri poterit, uti fieri posse mihi polliceor, quædam prius in eadem recognoscam et paulo aliter mutabo, quædam vero prorsus omittam, prout in cœteris quoque hanc mihi libertatem sumpsi, quæ etiam tibi non displicuit; nemini enim res meæ rectius perspectæ esse poterint quam mihi ipsi. Perplacet alias inventio et ductus carminis, sed bonus interdum dormitat HOMERUS. Vides, quantâ erga te utor libertate et confidentiâ: ubi vero aliquale mihi supererit otium, remittam carmen illud, iuxta meam intentionem paulo aliter formatum; vel saltem loca indicabo, quæ a te ipso alterari vellem. Satius itaque erit, ut interea tibi ipsi hoc reservans, nemini exemplum impertiaris, multo minus ut publici iuris facias.

Excellentissimus ille MELISSUS necdum mihi quidpiam misit, spero tamen ipsum, pro eâ quâ præditus est humanitate et erga me affectione, id officij mihi non denegaturum. Si Cæsæ Mati scriptum aliquod, uti innuis, aliquando humillime dedicare constituisti, de quo meum exquiris consilium, cuperem prius scire, quale id esset, et quâ de re, ac Exemplum, si fieri possit, introspicere, tum demum, an gratum sit futurum, partim ex aliorum, qui ipsius Mati ab intimis sunt, partim meâ ipsius æstimatione, coniecturam faciam, tibique id, quicquid erit, solitâ ingenuitate pandam.

Vale.
Dabantur properanter ex Arce Cæsarea Benatica Die 29 Martij Anno 1600 stylo novo.

TYCHO BRAHE TO MARTIN BRASCH

Greeting.

Most welcome to me were your letters, most learned and most loving Lord Master Martin Brasch, which about the beginning of this year my son, returning from Denmark, brought among others sent by [my] friends at Rostock; and for the kindly and candid congratulation which from the first you offer on the success of our affairs with the most august Emperor Rudolf II, our most clement Lord, I give you most affectionate thanks.

You have proved your sincere affection toward me, too, by that Elegiac poem sent along [with the letter], in which you bring on Urania addressing the Emperor on my behalf and rendering him thanks. I will indeed add it to your other [poems] earlier shared with me, which are approved by many; but since in this one you do not throughout use such choice words, and such polish as this kind of poem requires — in imitation of the Ovidian vein, or of the other Poets who have excelled in this fluency — so much so that, set beside your preceding poems, you seem (let me be allowed to declare frankly what I judge) lesser than your own self; and since, besides, there are in the Historical narration itself, which you chose to follow rather closely, some things which I judge too diffuse in their particulars, and not set forth becomingly enough — for that reason I have not yet joined this Elegy to the earlier verses, the Heroic and the Lyric, nor cared to show it to others. But if it may be done with your good leave — as I promise myself it may — I will first revise certain things in it, and alter them a little otherwise, and some I will leave out entirely, just as in the others too I took this liberty for myself, which did not displease you either; for to no one can my own affairs be more rightly known than to myself. Otherwise the invention and the conduct of the poem please me well — but good Homer sometimes nods. You see with how great freedom and confidence I deal with you; and when some leisure is left to me, I will send that poem back, shaped a little otherwise according to my intention, or at least I will mark the passages I should wish you yourself to alter. It will therefore be better that, keeping this to yourself meanwhile, you impart a copy to no one — much less make it public.

That most excellent Melissus has not yet sent me anything; yet I hope that he, by that humanity with which he is endowed and his affection toward me, will not deny me that service. If you have resolved, as you hint, to dedicate some writing at some time, most humbly, to His Imperial Majesty — concerning which you ask my counsel — I should wish first to know what it would be, and on what subject, and, if it could be done, to look over a copy; then at last I shall make a conjecture whether it will be welcome, partly from [the judgement] of others who are among His Majesty's intimates, partly from my own estimation, and will lay it open to you, whatever it be, with my wonted candour.

Farewell.
Given in haste from the imperial castle of Benátky, the 29th day of March, in the year 1600, New Style.

Apparatus

  • Recipient. Martin Brasch (Martinus Braschius, 1565–1601), Rostock humanist and Poeta laureatus — the same poet named in Paul Melissus's letter to Tycho of 5 March 1600 (brahe-t_16000305001) among those who had prompted verses in Tychen tuam. Tycho is on the most familiar terms with the Rostock literary circle (Amici Rostochienses), which routed letters to him via his son's return from Denmark "about the beginning of this year."
  • The occasion — a verse-portrait of Tycho's imperial fortune. Brasch had sent an Elegiac poem in which Urania (the Muse of astronomy) addresses Emperor Rudolf II on Tycho's behalf and thanks him — a poetic celebration of Tycho's new imperial patronage. It was meant to join Tycho's growing collection of friends' commendatory verses (the Heroic and Lyric pieces already gathered).
  • Tycho as candid literary critic (the heart of the letter). Unusually, Tycho returns a frank, unflattering critique: the diction is not uniformly selecta (choice), the concinnitas (polished arrangement) falls short of what elegiac verse demands "in imitation of the Ovidian vein," and the closely-followed Historica relatio (the narrative of events) is in places too diffuse and "not becomingly enough set forth" — so that Brasch here seems te ipso minor, "lesser than his own self." Tycho therefore withholds the poem from the collection and from other eyes, proposes to revise it himself (as he had done, with Brasch's prior consent, to the others — "for to no one can my affairs be more rightly known than to myself"), and counsels Brasch not to circulate or publish it meanwhile. The whole is softened by the Horatian tag.
  • Classical tag. bonus interdum dormitat Homerus — Horace, Ars Poetica 359 (quandoque bonus dormitat Homerus, "sometimes even good Homer nods"): the polite formula for a fine poet's lapse. Kept as the idiom and identified.
  • Cross-links & persons named.
  • Melissus = Paul Melissus (Schede) (1539–1602), the Heidelberg poet, Comes Palatinus and laureate. Tycho notes Melissus "has not yet sent me anything" — i.e. as of 29 March 1600 the verses that Melissus's letter of 5 March 1600 (brahe-t_16000305001) says he was enclosing had not yet reached Benátky. The two letters thus bracket the same in-transit packet of poems.
  • Rudolf II (Augustissimus Imperator Rudolphus Secundus); Tycho's son (the one returning from Denmark, unnamed here).
  • A possible dedication to the Emperor. Brasch had hinted (innuis) at dedicating some writing to Rudolf II and sought Tycho's advice; Tycho, well placed at court, offers to gauge its likely reception "partly from His Majesty's intimates, partly from my own estimation" before Brasch commits — a glimpse of how dedications to the Emperor were vetted through courtier-intermediaries.
  • Place / calendar. Arx Caesarea Benatica = the imperial castle of Benátky nad Jizerou; dateline explicitly stylo novo — New Style, per Tycho's imperial-service usage. Reproduced, not converted.
