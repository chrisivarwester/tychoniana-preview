The original

TYCHO BRAHE AD THADDÆUM HAGECIUM.

Post scripta ad D. D. Thaddaeum Hagecium

IN alteris tuis, optime mi THADDÆE, facis mentionem istius calumniatoris Ditmarsici, quodque eius scriptum maledicum in Daniam ad me mitti, antequam in Moraviam abires, procuraveris, mihi significas, quo maturius id obtinerem, pro qua tua in me benevolentia gratias habeo. Famosum istum libellum non quidem inde accepi, nescio, an eo pervenerit, literae enim solae absque libro mihi redditae sunt hîc Wandesburgi, e Dania transmissae; attamen ante paucos dies istum a quodam studioso Helmstadiensi mihi missum accepi. Extimas tu quidem, me ad omnia percommode responsurum, quod etiam mihi foret quam facilimum. Verum ego cum tam impudente conviciatore, qui omnes verecundiae limites transilijt, et totus calumnijs et mendacijs scatet, verba commutare mihi probrosum duco, neque enim dignus est, ut in scriptis meis nominetur, nedum refutetur. Erit forte alius, qui pro me tum quoque ROTHMANNO, bonae mem. id faciet, eiusque effrenem maledicentiam retundet. Ego vero eum non verbis sed verberibus dignum censeo, et iuxta leges puniendum, quod contra Imperij constitutiones, tam famosum, impudens et meris nugis, mendacijs et malitijs refertum scriptum in publicum edere non sit veritus, et nihilominus Caesaris Mathematicum in frontispicio se profitetur, nec tamen Typographi nomen subiungere audet, uti fit in famosis libellis. Constitui itaque, modo cum bona Caesaris venia, uti spero, id fieri queat, istum pessimum blateronem in ius vocari curare, et publice plagij iniuriarum et mendaciorum accusatum et convictum legibus coerceri et puniri, tum quoque Typographum, qui tam scurrilia impressit citra ordinationes Imperij, mulctari, totumque illud scriptum uti meretur irritum reddi, auctorem vero, unâ cum Typographo, quem tu facile nosti, istic re et corpore in arresto detineri, donec res iure decisa sit. Interim ad praecipuas Germaniae Academias tam Pontificias quam alias scriptum istud mittam, et earum censuram, adiuncta mea declaratione, exquiram, quae omnia postea in ius proponam. Quid Helmstadiensis Academia de hoc iudicet, ex ijs, quae studiosus ille unâ cum libro transmisso ad me perscripsit, videre est, quae est eiusmodi: »Rerum novarum hic nihil est, nisi verum et ferum istum Ursum non hominem (cuius librum hîc quidem non venalem, sed a magno quodam viro acceptum, vobis mitto) pro suo ingenio in vos debacchatum esse, qui, quod vobis gratulamur, sui ipsius ore suam prodidit inscitiam, et innata verborum virulentia ac canina calumniandi rabie vestram probe ultus est iniuriam, utque doctissimus noster CASELIUS, caeteraque Professorum cohors attestatur, quod impuro suo scripto de doctorum virorum coetu plane se exemit, nec dignus est, ut Caesaris amplius vocetur Mathematicus, nedum ut sit. Sed nae ego desipio, qui γλαῦκας εἰς Ἀθήνας. Introspiciatis! Brunsvicensem Cancellarium Pragae fuisse, vere ad vos delatum est, URSUMque exemplar sui operis illi obtulisse, quo pravi ingenij scurrili specimine statuit hîc professionem ambire atque impetrare. Sed ut Cancellarij humanitatis erat, oblatum librum non renuere, ita sapientiae, tam impurum scriptum plane improbare et irridere, quod etiam ab Illmo nostro (uti ab aulico mihi coniunctissimo certo comperi) prorsus damnatum est, adeo ut eius indignitas muneris alicuius illi apud nos deferendi Illustrissimo cogitationem plane ademit. Nos illius torsa in se tela laetamur, vestramque virtutem a tali gregario milite invictam, ut novum quiddam non miramur, qui, si vos isthic agere intellexisset, haud dubie ut in alijs omnibus discessum e Dania exilij convitio calumniose perstrinxisset. Sed huius monstri calumnias nunquam melius quam silentio retundetis, non enim dignus est ira Caesaris.« Haec ille, et postea in postscriptis quibusdam de DUNCANO LIDDELIO Scoto Professore isthic agente, quem meas sibi publice arrogasse Hypotheses, quas tamen a me, cum in Dania semel atque iterum mihi adesset, didicerat, fama percrebuit, ideoque scire avebam, an res ita se haberet, nec ne, sic proloquitur: »Maxima diligentia inquisivi de vestris hypothesibus, an DUNCANUS eas sibi arrogaret, nec potui intelligere ex ijs, qui quotidie cum illo conversantur, eum nunquam ausum fuisse eas appellare suas hypotheses, quod etiam in publicis eius praelectionibus nunquam audivi. Scit bene se obtinere non posse, ut fides ei adhibeatur, sed aliquoties dixit de suo conterraneo CRAIGO, licet saltem privatim apud suos familiares, quem suum vocat Praeceptorem, quod vos aliquando scripsistis quaedam contra illum, in quibus illum mancipium ARISTOTELIS appellatis, quod eum male habet. Ille, cum Rostochij privatim doceret, vestras Hypotheses Tychonianas appellavit, nec pro suis venditavit, qui satis est conscius a nemine esse excogitatas, antequam vos eas invenissetis, quia in Dania eas a vobis intellexerat, diu antequam URSI istius Ditmarsici plagiarij vestri Fundamentum Astronomicum (si dijs placet) evulgatum est, ubi iste perfrictae frontis URSUS hanc inventionem, quam tamen non satis capere videtur, sibi attribuere non erubescit, quam procul dubio apud vos habuit, cum ante multos annos Nobiliss. viro ERICO LANGIO serviens, Uraniburgi cum dicto ERICO per multos dies mansisset. De hoc URSO, cum DUNCANUS maledicum in vos scriptum vidisset, licet vobis ob suum conterraneum et praeceptorem CRAIGUM non valde faveret, sic dixit: Se satis mirari non posse istius URSI impudentem audaciam, et quod nulla honestas, nulla modestia, nullum virtutis vestigium in eo reperiatur, quod omnes etiam docti coguntur fateri, et aiunt: ipsum evacuasse omnis impudentiae poculum, ipsumque valde, uti et meritus est, execrantur, neque potest honestus animus cordati cuiusvis viri aliud in posterum sentire de isto impudenti URSO, quam ipsum omnes verecundiae limites supra modum excessisse, neque dignum, ut in consortio honestorum virorum amplius versetur.«

Haec nimirum est Helmstadiensium Professorum de URSO isto Ditmarsico sententia, cui plures etiam Academiae non difficulter adstipulabantur, et quotquot sunt Hamburgi viri docti, ad quos infamis iste liber pervenit, non meliori Elogio auctorem ornant. Haec vero ob id tibi paulo prolixius refero, dilectissime THADDÆE, quod sciam tibi etiam tam inverecundum et scelestum facinus displicere, rogoque ut rationem ineas, mihique tuum consilium aperias, quomodo is absque Caesaris offensa de iure castigandus sit, neque enim ad tot tantaque convitia atque calumnias salvo honore meo prorsus silere decet, cum non saltem me, sed et domum meam honestissimam, de qua fama hactenus verita est aliquid improbi loqui aut sentire, scurrilibus mendacijs et infamationibus proscindere non erubescat, multaque alia, quae piget referre, singula tamen suo tempore, in ipsum, prout meretur, retorquenda; sed haec aliquantisper dissimulanda censeo, donec in altero negotio, quod longe maioris est momenti, ex voto, uti confido, omnia transacta fuerint. Ista alia suam bene invenient occasionem, praesertim ubi Caesar, cum rectius noverit et clam apud Ducem Brunsvicensem Professionem in Academia Helmstadiensi ambivisse, aut repulsam passum esse resciverit. Nec etiam ea, quae in eodem scripto circa calcem habet, admodum honorifice de Caesare, cuius se iactat Mathematicum, prolata videntur, ubi suam penuriam, sibique nihil dari, et nescio quae alia in Reges et Principes scommata seu potius convitia exaggerat. Os enim ex eo abundat quo cor repletum est. Velim itaque, mi THADDÆE, ut in hoc quoque negotio tuum mihi significes cum consilio iudicium, et quomodo Eruditi atque honesti viri istic tam in aula quam urbe scriptum illud aestiment, et an Caesari tale quid innotuerit, atque utrum placeat vel displiceat, tum quoque ut de Typographo, quae scire opus est, cognoscas. Feceris mihi hac etiam in parte rem imprimis acceptam, et nihil addubito, quin tuo hîc non sis defuturus officio, cum sciam te non minus aegre ferre ea, quae in me evomuntur, maledicta verba, quam si eiuscemodi quid tibi ipsi contingeret, velut et ego in te semper pari affectus sum animo. Vale.

Haec Mathematum quidam Professor Wittebergensis, M. MELCHIOR IŒSTELIUS scripsit ad CHRISTIANUM SEVERINI LONGOMONTANUM, meum antea Studiosum, qui sic habet:

De RAYMARI nuperrime in te impudentia et debacchatione per librum quendam suum recens, ut intelligo, editum sparsa, idem IŒSTELIUS literis suis ultimis ad me missis haec subiungit:

»Pervenit ante paucos dies ad manus meas libellus quidam RAIMARI, in quo toto non tam sophistice quam scurriliter in Generosum Dominum TYCHONEM invehitur. Etsi autem certum est, neminem sanum aut cordatum hoc probaturum, Generosum item equum non curare latratum canum, tamen minime dubito, quin saltem coquus Domini TYCHONIS futilitatem et virulentiam ipsius retundet.«

Tycho Brahe to Thaddaeus Hagecius.

Postscript to Master Doctor Thaddaeus Hagecius

In your second letter, my excellent Thaddaeus, you make mention of that Dithmarschen slanderer, and you inform me that, before you departed for Moravia, you arranged for his scurrilous writing to be sent to me in Denmark, so that I might obtain it the sooner — for which goodwill of yours toward me I render thanks. That defamatory little book I did not, indeed, receive from there; I do not know whether it [ever] arrived there, for it was the letters alone, without the book, that were delivered to me here at Wandsbek, forwarded from Denmark; nevertheless, a few days ago I received that [book], sent to me by a certain student at Helmstedt. You reckon, to be sure, that I shall reply to everything quite handily, which would indeed be most easy for me. But I judge it shameful to bandy words with so impudent a reviler, who has overleapt all the bounds of decency and teems wholly with slanders and lies; for he is not worthy to be named in my writings, far less refuted. There will perhaps be someone else who will do this on behalf of myself and also of Rothmann, of good memory, and will blunt his unbridled scurrility. I, for my part, judge him worthy not of words but of whippings, and to be punished according to the laws, in that, against the constitutions of the Empire, he has not feared to publish a writing so defamatory, so impudent, and stuffed with mere trifles, lies, and malice — and nonetheless professes himself His Imperial Majesty's Mathematician on the title-page, yet does not dare to append the printer's name, as is done in defamatory pamphlets. I have therefore resolved — provided it can be done, as I hope, with His Imperial Majesty's good leave — to see that this vilest babbler be summoned to law, and, publicly accused and convicted of plagiarism, of injuries, and of lies, be restrained and punished by the laws; and likewise that the printer, who printed such scurrilous matter contrary to the ordinances of the Empire, be fined; and that that whole writing be, as it deserves, declared void; and that the author, together with the printer — whom you readily know — be there detained in arrest, in goods and in person, until the matter has been decided at law. Meanwhile I shall send that writing to the principal universities of Germany, both the Catholic ones and the others, and shall seek out their censure, with my own declaration appended — all of which I shall afterward lay before the court. What the University of Helmstedt judges of this may be seen from what that student wrote out to me along with the book he forwarded, which is of this sort: »There is no news here, except that that true and savage Bear [Ursus], no man, (whose book — not, indeed, on sale here, but received from a certain great man — I send you) has, after his fashion, raved against you; who, as we congratulate you, has by his own mouth betrayed his own ignorance, and by his inborn virulence of words and his canine madness for slandering has well avenged your wrong; and, as our most learned Caselius and the rest of the company of Professors attest, by his foul writing he has plainly removed himself from the company of learned men, and is not worthy any longer to be called His Imperial Majesty's Mathematician, far less to be one. But truly I am playing the fool, [carrying] owls to Athens [γλαῦκας εἰς Ἀθήνας]. Look closely! That the Chancellor of Brunswick was at Prague has been truly reported to you, and that Ursus offered him a copy of his work, by which scurrilous specimen of his crooked wit he resolved to seek and obtain a professorship here. But as it was a mark of the Chancellor's courtesy not to refuse the offered book, so it was a mark of his wisdom plainly to disapprove and deride so foul a writing — which was also wholly condemned by our Most Illustrious [Lord] (as I have learned for certain from a courtier most closely bound to me), to such a degree that its unworthiness plainly took from the Most Illustrious [Lord] the thought of conferring any office on him among us. We rejoice at his weapons turned back upon himself, and that your virtue is unconquered by such a common soldier — so that we do not wonder at it as anything new; for he, had he understood that you were active there, would doubtless, as in all other things, have slanderously carped at your departure from Denmark with the reproach of exile. But you will never blunt this monster's slanders better than by silence; for he is not worthy of His Imperial Majesty's anger.« Thus he; and afterward, in certain postscripts, concerning Duncan Liddel, the Scottish Professor active there — of whom a report had spread that he had publicly claimed as his own my Hypotheses, which yet he had learned from me when he was with me in Denmark once and again, and so I was eager to know whether the matter was so, or not — he speaks thus: »With the greatest diligence I inquired about your hypotheses, whether Duncan claimed them as his own, and I could not gather, from those who converse with him daily, that he had ever dared to call them his hypotheses; which I have never heard in his public lectures either. He well knows that he cannot bring it about that credence be given him; but he said several times of his fellow-countryman Craig — though only privately, among his intimates — whom he calls his Preceptor, that you once wrote certain things against him, in which you call him a bond-slave of Aristotle, which galls him. He, when he was teaching privately at Rostock, called your Hypotheses 'Tychonic,' and did not peddle them as his own, being well enough aware that they were devised by no one before you discovered them, because he had learned them from you in Denmark, long before that Dithmarschen plagiarist Ursus's Astronomical Foundation (if it please the gods) was published abroad — wherein that brazen-browed Ursus does not blush to attribute to himself this invention, which, however, he does not seem sufficiently to grasp, [and] which without doubt he got from you, when, serving years ago the most noble Erik Lange, he had stayed with the said Erik at Uraniborg for many days. Of this Ursus, when Duncan had seen the scurrilous writing against you, although he did not much favour you on account of his fellow-countryman and preceptor Craig, he spoke thus: that he could not sufficiently marvel at the impudent audacity of that Ursus, and that no honesty, no modesty, no trace of virtue is found in him — which even all the learned are compelled to admit, and they say: that he has drained the whole cup of impudence, and they greatly execrate him, as indeed he has deserved; nor can the honest mind of any sensible man henceforth feel otherwise about that impudent Ursus than that he has beyond measure exceeded all the bounds of decency, and is not worthy to consort any longer in the company of honest men.«

This, then, is the verdict of the Helmstedt Professors about that Dithmarschen Ursus — to which several other universities also assented without difficulty; and however many learned men there are at Hamburg, to whom that infamous book has come, they adorn its author with no better eulogy. But I report these things to you at somewhat greater length, most beloved Thaddaeus, for this reason: because I know that so shameless and wicked a deed displeases you also; and I ask that you devise a plan and disclose your counsel to me, [as to] how he may lawfully be chastised without offence to His Imperial Majesty — for it does not befit my honour to keep wholly silent at so many and so great revilings and slanders, since he does not blush to rend with scurrilous lies and defamations not only me but also my most honourable house, of which rumour has hitherto feared to speak or think anything dishonourable, and many other things which it irks me to relate — each of them, however, in its own time, to be turned back upon him as he deserves; but I judge that these things should be dissembled for a while, until in the other affair, which is by far of greater moment, everything shall have been transacted according to [my] wish, as I trust. Those other matters will well find their own occasion, especially once His Imperial Majesty — when he comes to know more correctly, and learns that [Ursus] secretly sought a professorship at the University of Helmstedt with the Duke of Brunswick, or suffered a rebuff — [shall be informed of it]. Nor, too, do the things he has near the end of the same writing seem to be uttered very honourably about His Imperial Majesty, whose Mathematician he boasts himself to be, where he magnifies his own want, and that nothing is given him, and I know not what other gibes — or rather revilings — against Kings and Princes. For the mouth overflows from that with which the heart is filled. I should wish, therefore, my Thaddaeus, that in this matter too you signify to me your judgement together with your counsel, and how learned and honest men there, both at court and in the city, esteem that writing, and whether anything of the kind has come to His Imperial Majesty's notice, and whether it pleases or displeases [him]; and likewise that you find out, concerning the printer, what it is needful to know. You will have done me, in this part also, a thing especially welcome; and I do not doubt at all that you will not fail in your good office here, since I know that you bear the abusive words spewed out against me no less hard than if something of the kind befell you yourself — just as I too have always been disposed toward you with an equal feeling. Farewell.

These things a certain Professor of Mathematics at Wittenberg, Master Melchior Jöstel, wrote to Christian Sørensen Longomontanus, formerly my student, which run thus:

Concerning Reimarus's impudence and raving lately spread abroad against you through a certain book of his, recently, as I understand, published, the same Jöstel adds these things in his last letter sent to me:

»A few days ago a certain little book of Reimarus's came into my hands, in the whole of which he inveighs against the Noble Master Tycho not so much sophistically as scurrilously. But although it is certain that no sane or sensible person will approve this, [and] that a noble horse likewise heeds not the barking of dogs, yet I do not doubt in the least that Master Tycho's cook, at any rate, will blunt his futility and virulence.«

Apparatus

  • Sender / recipient. Tycho Brahe to Thaddaeus Hagecius (Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku, 1525–1600), the Bohemian court physician and astronomer in Prague, Tycho's long-time friend and his principal channel to the imperial court of Rudolf II. The letter is an appendix ("Post scripta" / postscript) to a fuller letter, gathering the documentary evidence Tycho wished Hagecius to use against Ursus.
  • Date / place. The edition label is s.l. ("no place [given]"); no dateline survives in the transcript. The body locates Tycho hîc Wandesburgi — "here at Wandsbek" (Wandesburgum, now a district of Hamburg, where Tycho lived 1597–98 after leaving Denmark) — and the events (receipt of Ursus's De astronomicis hypothesibus of 1597, Tycho's plans to prosecute) fix it to 1598 (O.S.), the year given in the source header and the identifier. The header therefore carries the bracketed place and the year; the apparatus notes that neither a day nor month is in the text. No date is silently asserted beyond the year the source supplies.
  • "istius calumniatoris Ditmarsici" / "URSUS" — Nicolaus Reimarus Ursus (Reimers Bär, d. 1600), of Dithmarschen (Ditmarsicus), then Imperial Mathematician at Prague. His De astronomicis hypothesibus … tractatus (Prague 1597) attacked Tycho and claimed the geo-heliocentric world-system; Tycho regarded it as plagiarism of his own system, learned during Ursus's 1584 visit to Uraniborg in the service of Erik Lange. The pun on his name — Ursus = "Bear" — is exploited in the Helmstedt letter ("verum et ferum istum Ursum non hominem", "that true and savage Bear, no man"); kept and glossed.
  • "ROTHMANNO, bonae mem." — Christoph Rothmann, the Hesse-Kassel mathematician; bonae memoriae, "of good [i.e. blessed] memory," indicates Tycho here believed Rothmann dead (his date of death is in fact uncertain). Ursus's tract had also attacked Rothmann.
  • "Caesar / Caesaris Mathematicum" — Caesar throughout = Emperor Rudolf II; Caesaris Mathematicus = "the Emperor's Mathematician," the imperial post Ursus held and styled himself by on his title-page (in frontispicio). Rendered "His Imperial Majesty / His Imperial Majesty's Mathematician."
  • "contra Imperij constitutiones … citra ordinationes Imperij" — the imperial press laws (the Reichspolizeiordnung provisions against unsigned defamatory libels); the absence of the printer's name is precisely the statutory offence Tycho points to.
  • Helmstedt letter (the inset quotation). Sent to Tycho by an unnamed studiosus Helmstadiensis (a student at the University of Helmstedt, the Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel university). "CASELIUS" = Johannes Caselius (1533–1613), the eminent Helmstedt humanist. "Brunsvicensem Cancellarium" = the Chancellor of Brunswick(-Wolfenbüttel); "Illmo nostro … Illustrissimo" = "our Most Illustrious [Lord]," i.e. the reigning Duke (Heinrich Julius of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel), at whose university Ursus had reportedly angled for a chair.
  • Greek. γλαῦκας εἰς Ἀθήνας — "owls to Athens," the proverb (cf. Aristophanes, Birds 301; the owl being Athena's bird and Athens' civic emblem) for redundant effort, our "coals to Newcastle." Transcribed verbatim (with the printed final-sigma forms) in the Source block and translated with the proverb flagged, by this chapter's convention
  • "DUNCANO LIDDELIO Scoto" / "CRAIGO" — Duncan Liddel (1561–1613), the Scottish physician-astronomer then professor at Helmstedt, reported (the report is here being checked and not confirmed) to have claimed Tycho's planetary hypotheses; he is reported rather to have taught them honestly as Hypotheses Tychonianae ("Tychonic hypotheses") while teaching privately at Rostock. "CRAIGO" / CRAIGUS = John Craig (d. 1620), Liddel's Scottish fellow-countryman and (so Liddel called him) Praeceptor, "preceptor," with whom Tycho had a separate dispute; "mancipium Aristotelis" = "a bond-slave of Aristotle," Tycho's reported jibe at Craig.
  • "ERICO LANGIO" — Erik Lange (1553–1613), the Danish nobleman, alchemist, and Tycho's friend (later brother-in-law's circle); Ursus had been in his service and so came to Uraniborg.
  • "Fundamentum Astronomicum (si dijs placet)" — Ursus's work is named ironically; Fundamentum Astronomicum is in fact the title of an earlier Ursus work (Strasbourg 1588). The parenthetical si dijs placet, "if it please the gods," is a sarcastic period idiom (≈ "if you please / God help us"); kept and glossed, the irony preserved rather than smoothed.
  • "plagij" / "plagiarij" — plagium, the legal offence of man-stealing/literary theft; plagiarius, "plagiarist." Rendered "plagiarism / plagiarist"; this is among the earliest technical legal uses in the Tycho dossier.
  • Closing inset. M. Melchior Jöstel (1559–1611), Master (Magister) and professor of mathematics at Wittenberg, wrote to Christian Sørensen Longomontanus (Christen Sørensen, 1562–1647), Tycho's senior assistant ("formerly my student"); "RAYMARI / RAIMARI" is again Ursus (Reimarus). The sentence "Generosum … equum non curare latratum canum" ("a noble horse heeds not the barking of dogs") is a proverb, kept as such. The closing jest — that even Master Tycho's cook could refute Ursus — is an insult-by-belittlement (Ursus is so feeble that the lowliest of Tycho's household would suffice); rendered straight.
  • Scriptural allusion. "Os enim ex eo abundat quo cor repletum est" — "For the mouth overflows from that with which the heart is filled," a paraphrase of Matthew 12:34 / Luke 6:45 (ex abundantia cordis os loquitur); rendered as the proverb-allusion it is.
  • Negation check. The legal-force clauses were counted: "non sit veritus" ("has not feared," i.e. did dare), "nec … audet" ("does not dare"), "non … nominetur, nedum refutetur" ("not to be named, far less refuted"), "non verbis sed verberibus" ("not of words but of whippings"), "non … prorsus silere decet" ("it does not befit … to keep wholly silent"), "non erubescit" ("does not blush"), "nunquam … nedum", and the closing "non … defuturus officio" ("will not fail in your office"). Each negation is reproduced one-for-one; none added or dropped.
  • Correction. Latin corrected against Dreyer, TBOO VIII pp.57-59 (page images): rejoined the place-name 'Uraniburgi' and the following word 'cum', which the working transcription had fused across a page-break catchword into 'Uraniburgicum' (p.57-58); and restored the dropped phrase 'et debacchatione' ("and raving") describing Ursus/Reimarus's pamphlet, with the English updated to match (p.59).

- text error (low). Dreyer characterizes Ursus/Reimarus's book as spreading both 'impudentia et debacchatione' (impudence AND raving/frenzy). The transcribed original silently drops 'et debacchatione', and the English follows the truncated source, losing one of the two descriptive terms characterizing the pamphlet. Dreyer prints: De RAYMARI nuperrime in te impudentia et debacchatione per librum quendam suum recens... editum sparsa (p.59, line 3-4).
