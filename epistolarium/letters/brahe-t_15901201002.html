The original

TYCHO BRAHE AD IACOBUM CURTIUM.

RESCRIPSI tuis humanissimis pariterque eruditissimis hac praeterlapsa aestate ad me datis literis, vir amplissime et nobilissime, amice in primis honorande, idque per eundem quem tunc temporis ad me ablegâras Tabellarium, nec dubito, quin literae meae tibi satis certo redditae sint; quare de ijs, quae tunc ultro citroque inter nos agebantur, nunc ulterius quidpiam movere, minus duco operaepretium. Unum saltem vel alterum est, de quo te denuo hisce compellandum censui. Cum Geographiae exacta cognitio Astronomiae penitius excolendae non parum (uti scis) subsidij adferat, haec vero ab antecessoribus nostris non minus quam altera nequaquam satis accurate et solide exculta est: decrevi itaque proprijs Instrumentis missis hincinde per Europam idoneis observatoribus quorundam praecipuorum locorum latitudines atque longitudines indagari curare, et primo quoque tempore cum portatili quodam Sextante, cochleis ad dissolutionem et compositionem hincinde dispositis, compagniatum quendam ex meis in Germaniam amandabo, qui Rostochij, Vitebergae, Dresdae elevationes polares exactius, quam antea factum est, scrutetur, indeque Pragam et ad vos transiens idem illic scrutabitur. Tum instrumenti huius fabricam concinnam atque usum apprime expeditum coram perspicies, atque si ita lubuerit, imitari facies. Postea Noribergam translato Organo, ibi etiam poli sublimitatem exactissime observari in votis habeo, idque eam potissimum ob caussam, ut ex observationibus quibusdam BERNHARDI GUALTERI discipuli REGIOMONTANI, illic ante unum proxime seculum habitis, ijsdemque inter se invicem collatis atque expensis, de Apogaeo atque Eccentricitate Solari, augeanturne an vero minuantur, aliquid certi et indubitati concludere queam, quod absque eius loci polari elevatione scrupulosissime cognita fieri haud posse tute non ignoras. At hanc e Solaribus altitudinibus etiam in utroque Solstitio diligenter exploratis, rimari non satis tutum est, ut diutina nos docuit experientia, caussaeque, cur id fiat, alibi a nobis exponuntur. Peractis autem ijs, quae Noribergae intermediante hoc instrumento denotanda habeo, optarem quidem illud in Italiam, ut et illic polares quaedam designarentur altitudines, transportari. Verum cum Alpium longinquae et praeruptae salebrae non parum impedimento sint, quo minus Organum hoc in Ausoniam sartum tectum absque ulla laesione perferatur, suo etiam pondere itineris difficultates respuente, vix id praestari posse mihi persuadeo. De alia igitur ratione, qua nihilominus in praecipuis quibusdam Italiae civitatibus nostris desiderijs satisfiat, mecum constitui. Degit enim nunc Patavij iuvenis quidam nostras GELLIUS SASCERIDES nomine, qui mihi paulo antea octennium domesticus fuit, atque tam in exercitijs Astronomicis quam Pyronomicis fidelem navavit operam; huic per literas iam nuper significavi, ut illic Sextantem quendam ad instar cuiusdam meorum, quos hîc vidit atque tractavit, meis sumptibus confieri procuret atque eius beneficio quorundam Italiae locorum latitudines ratas inquirat, meque de his postea certiorem reddat. At cum ne hîc quidem consistere animus sit, sed usque in Aegiptum ad septemplicis Nili ostia observationes extendere, Alexandriam videlicet in locum vetustissimae habitationis Ptolemaicae, partim ut Poli altitudo mihi usitato more illic accuratissime explorata cum Ptolemaica consignatione conferri queat, et an rite satisque exacte, de quo etiam certis de caussis non parum dubito, ab eo constituta sit, aut etiam, ut nonnulli, sed frustra, uti opinor, suspicantur, mutationem aliquam tot interlapsis seculis senserit, indubitate dignoscatur, partim ut per ☾am, cum circa 90am ab horizonte Eclipticae partem versatur, si modo per eclipsationes non liceat, longitudo eius loci, qua Geographi autoritati Ptolemaicae confisi praecipue utuntur, et de Meridianorum respectu huius atque aliorum Europae locorum differentijs aliquid rati et ambiguitati non obnoxium decernere liceat. Verumenimvero, cum instrumentum, quod in Italia parari iussi, non commode in Aegiptum, ob itineris nimiam intercapedinem aliasque difficultates transvehi operâ unius alicuius hominis possit, nec etiam unicum tanto labori atque indubiae pervestigationi sufficiat, et is etiam, de quo dixi, GELLIUS meus ex his Arctois locis oriundus, Maris Adriatici atque Mediterranei aestuationes, tum etiam aëris Aegiptiaci subtilitatem atque fervorem absque valetudinis iactura aut vitae periculo vix sustinuerit, et res haec per se alias sit magni momenti, plurimumque ad Geographiae adeoque ipsius Astronomiae plenariam instaurationem conducat, omnem idcirco movendum esse duco lapidem, ut etiam hîc votis nostris respondeat successus. Hîc vero tuum quam maxime desidero expetoque unâ cum consilio auxilium. Si enim ad Magnificentiss. Coesareae Maiestatis Legatum, qui Venetijs moratur, interposita etiam, si opus fuerit, eiusdem Caes. Maiestatis auctoritate, literas dare non gravatus fueris, quibus is rogetur admoneaturque, ut ab Illustrissimo Venetorum Principe atque Senatu amplissimo impetret, ut duo vel tria eiuscemodi Instrumenta per idoneos artifices adamussim fabrefieri mandent, et duos vel tres harum rerum capaces et industrios iuvenes locorum insuper et viarum non ignaros deputent sumtusque et necessaria, quod illis perfacile est, largiantur, quo Alexandriae non unico saltem, [sed] si id forte in tanto itinere laesionem aliquam admiserit, duobus vel tribus loci eius tam longitudo quam latitudo diligentissime indagetur: si inquam tua intercessione atque ope hoc ipsum Venetijs mature procuratum fuerit, equidem non dubiam concipio spem, omnia exoptatationi nostrae feliciter et commode satisfactura. Instrumenta autem eiuscemodi affabre et absque vitio elaborandi rationem artificibus Venetis praescribet antedictus GELLIUS, tum etiam eos, qui observandi gratia Alexandriam amandandi venient, de eorum usu atque tractatione, caeterisque ad coelestem denotationem rite et citra errorem perficiendis sufficienter instruet, stellarumque quarundam declinationes atque distantias meis hîc organis antea conquisitas, quibus uti et instrumentorum certitudinem examinare comprobareque poterint, suppeditabit. De quibus etiam omnibus illum ante paucas septimanas affatim per literas admonui. Nec dubito, quin is in diligenter exequendis ijs, quae illi a me iniuncta sunt, suo officio non sit defuturus, cum ipsius fidem et solertiam pari integritate coniunctam iamdudum diu multumque exploratam habeam. Quapropter te praestantissime et spectatissime vir non solum per eam, quae nobis intercedit, amicitiam, sed ulterius quoque per divinae illius Astronomiae incrementa atque restaurationem, quam te prae multis alijs summis votis exoptare expectareque non ignoro, et quibus insuper possum precibus, obnixe rogatum habeo, ut nostris desiderijs hac in parte subvenire, illisque tua autoritate atque suasu adeoque manuductione et auxilio praesto esse non molestum ducas.

Cedit illud, ut spero, non solum in antiquissimae et praestantissimae syderalis scientiae atque Cosmographiae promotionem et commodum, verum etiam in inclytissimae Caesareae Maiestatis atque amplissimae Reipub. Venetae, tuique ipsius, qui haec procuras atque promoves, laudem atque apud posteros, si qua restabit benemeritorum gratia, memoriam et perpetuam celebrationem. Cumque nihil diffidam te sponte tua ad rem Astronomicam sublevandam locupletandamque et meis in his conatibus adiutandis (cuius et iam antea eximium specimen in procurando illo honorifico atque illustri Diplomate Caesareano exhibuisti) propensum esse, nolo te pluribus rogando diutius hac de re interpellare, cum ultro currenti equo calcaria (ut est in proverbio) addere non deceat. Alterum adhuc saltem superest, de quo tuum consilium capere satago. Cuperem Caesareae Maiestati ipsemet scribere, et tum ob Diploma illud ab eius Maiestate mihi gratiose et clementer concessum submisse gratias agere, tum etiam quaedam alia, quae ipsius Maiestati forte non ingrata atque incommoda sint futura, humiliter atque secreto indicare. Vereor autem ne ipsius Maiestatem serenissimam atque augustissimam meis tenuioribus literis interpellando eius Maiestati aut etiam alijs dignitate atque auctoritate in aula praepollentibus offensioni sim futurus, ita ut in minus dextram partem mihi res haec vertatur. Te itaque iterum atque iterum peramanter rogatum habeo, ut quid a me hac in parte fieri liceat et deceat, ingenue commonefacere velis, et per primam quamque opportunitatem, etiam, si fieri possit ante Maris Balthici navigationes impedientem congelationem, mihi de singulis rescribere, atque quid sperandum faciendumque sit, significare non graveris. Vale vir amplissime, meque tui studiosissimum et perpetua amicitia tibi devinctum retinere (quod et ultro per me fiet) non intermitte. Communi amico nostro THADDÆO seorsim scripsi, cui etiam quam optime cupio omniaque ex animi sententia opto.

Iterum quam diutissime felicissimeque vale.
Datae Calendis Decembris Anni veteris Iuliani 1590.

Tycho Brahe to Jacob Curtius.

I wrote back, most ample and most noble sir, friend above all to be honoured, to the most courteous and likewise most learned letter you sent me this past summer, and that by the very courier you had then dispatched to me; nor do I doubt that my letter was delivered to you securely enough. Therefore I judge it less worth the trouble now to raise anything further about those matters that were then debated back and forth between us. There is, at least, one thing or another about which I have thought you should be addressed anew on these matters. Since an exact knowledge of geography brings no small aid (as you know) to the deeper cultivation of astronomy, but this [geography] has been by our predecessors no less than the other [astronomy] in no way cultivated accurately and soundly enough, I have therefore resolved to have the latitudes and longitudes of certain principal places investigated, by sending my own instruments here and there through Europe to suitable observers; and at the very first opportunity I shall send off one of my men, in company [compagniatum], into Germany, with a certain portable sextant [Sextante], [fitted] with screws set here and there for taking apart and putting together, that he may scrutinize at Rostock, Wittenberg, and Dresden the polar elevations more exactly than has been done before, and thence, passing to Prague and to you, will scrutinize the same there. Then you will see in person the neat construction of this instrument and its altogether ready use, and, if it so please you, you will have it imitated. Afterwards, the instrument [Organo] having been carried to Nuremberg, I have it among my wishes that the height of the pole be there too observed most exactly, and that chiefly for this reason: that from certain observations of Bernhard Walther, the pupil of Regiomontanus, made there nearly a century ago, and these compared with one another and weighed, I may be able to conclude something certain and indubitable concerning the solar apogee and eccentricity — whether they are increasing or in fact decreasing — which you are well aware cannot be done without that place's polar elevation being most scrupulously known. But to search this out from solar altitudes, even though diligently observed at both solstices, is not safe enough, as long experience has taught us, and the reasons why this is so are set out by us elsewhere. And when those things are accomplished which I have to mark down at Nuremberg by means of this instrument, I should indeed wish it to be transported into Italy, so that there too certain polar altitudes might be determined. But since the far-off and sheer ruggedness of the Alps is no small hindrance to this instrument's being carried into Ausonia [Italy] sound and whole without any damage — its very weight, too, refusing the difficulties of the road — I can scarcely persuade myself that this can be brought off. I have therefore settled with myself on another plan, by which our desires may nonetheless be satisfied in certain principal cities of Italy. For there is now living at Padua a certain young countryman of ours, Gellius Sascerides by name, who a little while ago was for eight years a member of my household, and bestowed faithful service both in astronomical and in pyronomic [chemical] exercises. To him I have lately signified by letter that he should there see to it that a certain sextant be made at my expense, after the pattern of one of mine which he saw and handled here, and that by its use he investigate the fixed latitudes of certain places of Italy, and afterwards make me more certain of these. But since my mind is not to halt even here, but to extend the observations as far as Egypt, to the mouths of the sevenfold Nile — namely to Alexandria, the site of Ptolemy's most ancient dwelling — partly so that the height of the pole, most accurately explored there in my usual manner, may be compared with Ptolemy's record, and that it may be discerned beyond doubt whether it was duly and exactly enough established by him (whereof I also, for certain reasons, no little doubt), or whether even, as some — but in vain, as I think — suspect, it has undergone some change in so many intervening centuries; and partly so that, by the Moon [☾], when it is situated about the 90th part of the ecliptic from the horizon, if only it not be permitted by eclipses [eclipsationes], the longitude of that place — which geographers, trusting in Ptolemy's authority, chiefly use — and the differences in respect of the meridians of this and of other places of Europe, may be settled in something fixed and not liable to ambiguity. But in truth, since the instrument which I have ordered to be prepared in Italy cannot conveniently be conveyed into Egypt by the effort of any single man, on account of the journey's excessive length and other difficulties — nor would a single one suffice for so great a labour and an indubitable investigation — and since that Gellius of mine of whom I spoke, born in these arctic regions, could scarcely endure the swells of the Adriatic and Mediterranean seas, and then too the thinness and heat of the Egyptian air, without harm to his health or peril of his life; and since this matter is otherwise in itself of great moment, and contributes very much to the full restoration [instaurationem] of geography and indeed of astronomy itself: I therefore judge that every stone must be moved, so that here too success may answer to our wishes. And here I most especially long for and seek your help together with your counsel. For if you would not find it burdensome to give a letter to the most magnificent Legate of His Imperial Majesty who resides at Venice — with the authority of the same Imperial Majesty interposed too, if there be need — by which he may be asked and admonished to obtain from the Most Illustrious Prince of the Venetians and the most ample Senate that they command two or three instruments of this kind to be skilfully made to the line by suitable craftsmen, and that they appoint two or three young men capable of these matters and industrious, and moreover not ignorant of the regions and the routes, and bestow the expenses and necessaries (which is very easy for them), so that at Alexandria — not by one instrument only, [but,] should that perchance suffer some damage on so great a journey, by two or three — both the longitude and the latitude of that place may be most diligently investigated: if, I say, this very thing be procured in good time at Venice by your intercession and aid, then for my part I conceive no doubtful hope that all will turn out happily and conveniently to our longing. The aforesaid Gellius will prescribe to the Venetian craftsmen the method of working instruments of this kind skilfully and without fault, and will also sufficiently instruct those who shall come to be sent off to Alexandria for the sake of observing in their use and handling, and in the other things to be carried out duly and without error for celestial measurement; and he will supply the declinations and distances of certain stars previously ascertained here with my instruments [organis], by which they will be able both to make use of them and to examine and verify the trustworthiness of the instruments. Of all which things, too, I have fully admonished him by letter a few weeks ago. Nor do I doubt that he will not fall short of his duty in diligently carrying out those things which have been enjoined upon him by me, since I have long ago thoroughly and at length tested his fidelity and skill joined with equal integrity. Wherefore, most excellent and most esteemed sir, I hold you earnestly entreated — not only by that friendship which subsists between us, but further also by the advancement and restoration [restaurationem] of that divine astronomy, which I am well aware that you, beyond many others, long for and await with the highest wishes, and by whatever prayers I can besides — that you would not deem it troublesome to come to the aid of our desires in this part, and to be ready for them by your authority and persuasion, and indeed by your guidance and help.

This will redound, as I hope, not only to the promotion and advantage of that most ancient and most excellent sidereal science and of cosmography, but also to the praise of the most renowned Imperial Majesty and of the most ample Venetian Republic, and of yourself, who procure and promote these things, and — among posterity, if any gratitude for those who have deserved well shall remain — to your memory and perpetual celebration. And since I in no way distrust that you are of your own accord inclined to relieve and enrich the astronomical enterprise and to assist me in these endeavours of mine (of which you have already before now displayed a signal token in procuring that honourable and illustrious Imperial Diploma), I do not wish to importune you longer about this matter by asking more, since (as the proverb has it) it does not become one to add spurs to a horse already running of its own accord. There remains still at least one other thing, about which I am eager to take your counsel. I should wish to write to His Imperial Majesty myself, and both to give humble thanks for that Diploma graciously and clemently granted me by His Majesty, and also to indicate humbly and in secret certain other things which will perhaps be neither unwelcome nor disadvantageous to His Majesty. But I fear lest, by importuning His most serene and most august Majesty with my too-slight letter, I should give offence to His Majesty or even to others prevailing in dignity and authority at court, so that this matter should turn out to my less favourable side. I therefore hold you, again and again, most lovingly entreated, that you would be willing frankly to advise me what may be lawful and fitting for me to do in this part, and that you would not find it grievous, at the very first opportunity — even, if it can be done, before the freezing-over that impedes the navigations of the Baltic Sea — to write me back about each point, and to signify what is to be hoped and to be done. Farewell, most ample sir, and do not cease to keep me as most devoted to you and bound to you by perpetual friendship (which will also be done by me of my own accord). To our common friend Thaddaeus I have written separately, for whom too I wish all that is best and desire all things according to the mind's intent.

Again, farewell as long and as happily as may be.
Given on the Kalends of December [1 December] of the old Julian year 1590.

Apparatus

  • Sender / recipient. Tycho Brahe, writing from Uraniborg on Hven (place not named in the dateline; supplied in brackets), to Jacob Kurz von Senftenau (Iacobus Curtius, d. 1594), Imperial Pro-Chancellor (Reichsvizekanzler) at the court of Emperor Rudolf II in Prague. Kurz was the patron who in 1590 procured for Tycho the imperial privilege ("Diploma") protecting his publications in the Habsburg lands — the Diploma Caesareum referred to twice below — and a correspondent of Tycho's through the intermediacy of Thaddaeus Hagecius.
  • MS witness. A = Vienna, ÖNB cod. Vindob. lat. 10686.5, the Kladde (Tycho's retained draft); hence the sole authority is a draft, not the dispatched original.
  • Tabellarium — "courier" (the letter-carrier Kurz had sent).
  • The portable sextant project. portatili quodam Sextante, cochleis ad dissolutionem et compositionem … dispositis — a sextant (per glossary) made demountable by screws for transport; Tycho's plan to fix the latitudes/longitudes of principal European cities by circulating his own instruments. compagniatum quendam ex meis — "one of my men, in company"; compagniatum is a non-classical (Italianate/mercantile) coinage for an associate or travelling companion, kept literal.
  • Itinerary of the instrument. Rostochij, Vitebergae, Dresdae = Rostock, Wittenberg, Dresden; thence Pragam et ad vos = Prague and "to you" (i.e. to Kurz at the Imperial court); then Noribergam = Nuremberg. elationes polares / poli sublimitatem / polari elevatione — all rendered consistently "polar elevation / height of the pole" (= geographic latitude).
  • Bernhard Walther. BERNHARDI GUALTERI discipuli REGIOMONTANI = Bernhard Walther (c. 1430–1504), the wealthy Nuremberg patrician and observer, pupil and collaborator of Regiomontanus (Johannes Müller, 1436–76); his late-15th-century Nuremberg solar observations are Tycho's reason for re-determining Nuremberg's latitude. ante unum proxime seculum rendered "nearly a century ago."
  • Astronomy — concepts. Apogaeo atque Eccentricitate Solari = "the solar apogee and eccentricity"; augeanturne an vero minuantur — Tycho's open question whether they are increasing or decreasing, the disjunction preserved. in utroque Solstitio = "at both solstices"; Eclipticae … 90am … partem … ab horizonte — the Moon at the point of the ecliptic 90° from the horizon (the nonagesimal), used for a longitude determination by lunar method if eclipses are not available (si modo per eclipsationes non liceat); the conditional hedge is kept. Meridianorum … differentijs = differences of meridians (= longitude differences).
  • instauratio / restauratio — rendered "restoration" throughout (plenariam instaurationem … Geographiae adeoque ipsius Astronomiae; divinae illius Astronomiae … restaurationem), Tycho's programmatic term by this chapter's convention (astronomia instaurata).
  • Gellius Sascerides. GELLIUS SASCERIDES (1562–1612), Danish scholar, eight years a member of Tycho's Uraniborg household, then (from 1589) at Padua (Patavij); later briefly engaged to Tycho's daughter Magdalene. in exercitijs Astronomicis quam Pyronomicis — "astronomical and pyronomic exercises"; pyronomic = chemical/alchemical (the art of fire), kept as the period term and glossed.
  • The Alexandria / Egypt scheme. septemplicis Nili ostia … Alexandriam … in locum vetustissimae habitationis Ptolemaicae — the sevenfold mouths of the Nile, and Alexandria as Ptolemy's observing-site; Tycho proposes to re-measure Alexandria's latitude against Ptolemaica consignatio (Ptolemy's record) and to fix its longitude by lunar observation, testing whether Ptolemy's value was exact or has changed over the centuries. Ausoniam = poetic for Italy (Ausonia), glossed in the translation.
  • The Venetian request. Magnificentiss. Coesareae Maiestatis Legatum, qui Venetijs moratur = the Imperial Legate (ambassador) resident at Venice; Illustrissimo Venetorum Principe atque Senatu = the Doge ("Prince of the Venetians") and Senate of the Venetian Republic (Reipub. Venetae). adamussim = "to the line, exactly" (kept). The bracketed [sed] at "non unico saltem, [sed] si id forte…" follows Dreyer's supplement; the printed draft omits the conjunction, recorded here rather than silently absorbed.
  • The Imperial Diploma. Diplomate Caesareano / Diploma illud — the imperial privilege (printing privilege/copyright for the Habsburg realms) Kurz had procured, later printed at the front of Tycho's Astronomiae instauratae progymnasmata. Caesareae Maiestati = His Imperial Majesty (Rudolf II); Tycho's wish to write to the Emperor directly, and his fear of giving offence to courtiers, are stated as his own hedged apprehension and kept hedged.
  • Proverb. ultro currenti equo calcaria … addere — "to add spurs to a horse already running of its own accord," kept as the proverb Tycho flags (ut est in proverbio).
  • Thaddaeus. THADDAeO = Thaddaeus Hagecius (Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku, 1525–1600), Imperial physician at Prague and Tycho's chief Bohemian correspondent and intermediary with Kurz; here the communis amicus.
  • Symbol. per ☾am = per Lunam, "by the Moon" — the Moon glyph ☾ kept in the Source block and named in the translation by this chapter's convention
  • Calendar / dateline. Datae Calendis Decembris Anni veteris Iuliani 1590 = "Given on the Kalends of December (1 December) of the old Julian year 1590" — Old Style, and explicitly so labelled by Tycho (Anni veteris Iuliani), a deliberate flag of the O.S./N.S. distinction toward an Imperial (Gregorian-calendar) correspondent. The header date agrees with both the dateline and the catalogue label (1 December 1590). Maris Balthici … congelationem — the freezing of the Baltic Sea that would halt shipping, Tycho's seasonal deadline for a reply.
  • Back-translation fix. non ingrata atque incommoda — the single non governs both adjectives; rendered "neither unwelcome nor disadvantageous" (the earlier "not … and disadvantageous" risked leaving incommoda un-negated).
  • Correction. Latin corrected against Dreyer, TBOO VII p.285: restored the dropped syllable in 'elevationes polares' (the working transcription read 'elationes'); the English translation already read 'elevations' correctly and needed no change.

- text error (low).
