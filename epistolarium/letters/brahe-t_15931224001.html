The original

NOBILITATE GENERIS AVITA, VIRTUTE ET DOCTRINA PRÆSTANTI D. TYCHONI BRAHE DOMINO IN KNUDSTORFF ET INSULÆ URANIÆ, SUO DOMINO ET AMICO OBSERVANDO. S.

ANNUS integer præterijt die 2 Decemb. nobiliss. D. TYCHO et amice observande, cum ad te scripsi et respondi ad tuas, quas 28. Septemb. anni præteriti scripseras ad me. Sic totus annus literarum silentio nobis elapsus est, quod accidit, non oblivione amicitiæ nostræ, quæ, quod ad me attinet, sancte observatur et colitur, sed ob labores meæ functionis et occupationes rei familiaris varias, valetudinem item parum firmam et constantem, et, ut uno verbo dicam, ob senium. Ago enim annum 68, paulo post, iuxta proverbium ponte deijciendus. Memoria certe ita me deficit, ut, nisi meam viderem et agnoscerem manum, nunquam me ea legisse aut tractasse dicerem. Deum oro, ne mihi penitus memoriam aboleri sinat, quod TRAPEZUNTIO in senio evenisse legimus, sed aliquam saltem concedat, ne divinarum promissionum ulla me capiat oblivio. Astronomica studia nunc plane conticuerunt apud me, nec est, mi amantiss. TYCHO, quod in illis aliquid a me expectes. Tu perge in his, quæ cœpisti, et perfice, dum vires ingenij et corporis vividæ sunt, et temet æternitati conserva. Solam Medicinam tracto, sustentandæ familiæ domesticæ gratia. Illi adiungo Theologica et meditationem mortis, quæ mors iam ostium nostri tabernaculi sæpe pulsat. Tractaveram etiam Chymica per annum integrum et amplius, sed cum vana omnia esse deprehendissem, deserui.

Privilegium Cæsareum a SAGERO Medico Lubecensi te accepisse existimo. Nam die 5 Maij huius anni significavit mihi id ad te transmisisse. Eius reditum ad nos aut Misenam expectabam. Cometam præterito autumno effulsisse, communis erat fama. Aliunde etiam de eo perscribebatur. A me tamen studiose lustrante cœlum nuspiam conspici potuit. Si quid tibi de eo constat, ad me perscribas, oro. Die 12 Decemb. quæ de eo Cometa D. PEUCERUS ad me perscripserit, ex eius literis, quarum exemplum ad te mitto, cognosces. Hodie qui est dies 22 Decemb. pransus sum cum D. CURTIO. Monstravit mihi suum Sextantem. Confectus est a quodam insigni artifice Lantgraviano, et nuper admodum allatus. Si iam Quadrantem haberemus paratum, primo omnium hîc altitudinem poli observaremus, deinde distantias stellarum aliquot, et si quid aliud emergeret, haberes nos collegas, me quidem seniculum imbellem, at promptum ad labores ætati convenientes. Curat sibi quoque D. CURTIUS confici globum compactum ex parvis assulis ligneis simul conglutinatis. Eius diameter est 6 pedum longitudine. In eum globum imponere cupit stellas tuæ observationis. Iussit te salvere plurimum. Scripsisset libentissime ipse ad te, sed testari possum, ob gravissimas occupationes et negocia aulica non potuisse, petit ut eum habeas excusatum. Invitat te ad Comitia Imperij Ratisbonam. Quod si te eo venturum certo intellexero, ipsemet tui videndi et te salutandi gratia accurram, modo valetudo et vires corpusculi non impedierint. Sin venire non poteris, remitte, quæso, libellum APIANI de Cometa, cui opuscula quædam Bohemica alligata sunt. Item Commentaria TRAPEZUNTIJ, quibus te iam fortasse non egere existimo. Ea opuscula ad D. CURTIUM mitte. B. V. Datæ Pragæ die 24 Decembris Anno 1593.

Per D. VINSHEMIUM hæc ad te scribo.

T. studiosiss.
THAD.

CASPARUS PEUCERUS AD THADDÆUM HAGECIUM. S. D.

EXPECTAVI avide abs te responsum ad postremas meas, quas non dubito te ab inclyto et generoso D. D. FERDINANDO Comite a Schlick accepisse. Illud cum differatur opinione mea diutius, censui eliciendum esse, sed nulla re meliore me perficere id posse sum ratus, quam si explorem tuas de Cometa nuper viso παρατηρήσεις, quem ego me fateor bis tantum conspexisse, circiter horam tertiam matutinam in asterismo Ursæ maioris, et quidem eius capite, corpore parvo, colore lurido prorsus et obscuro, cauda oblonga et sub finem tenui, a Septentrione supra ortum solstitialem porrecta versus occasum brumalem. Mihi organis omnibus et operis destituto, locorum etiam opportunitate, aliud nihil observare licuit, quam quod visus deprehendit. De tempore exortus cognovi serius. Post conspectum autem bidui spe mea citius evanuit. Si quid igitur tibi de illo exploratum est, et de eventu quid præsagias, ut aperias mihi oro, missis literis per hunc virum nobilem ALBERTUM a Conttenau, qui isthuc in aulam Cæsaream ab Illmo meo Principe ablegatur. In quod Zodiaci δωδεκατημόριον et quam eius partem inciderit ignoro. Plus non observavi, quam quod a Capellæ et Hædorum sidere non procul constiterit, de latitudine, altitudine et parallaxi, nihil comperi, de his ergo tua peto et expecto observata. Nobilem hunc virum tibi commendo.

Bene et feliciter vale.
21 Novemb. Iul. Anno 93.
CASPAR PEUCERUS.
D.

To Master Tycho Brahe, lord in Knudstrup and on the Isle of Urania, eminent in ancestral nobility of birth, in virtue and in learning, his lord and friend to be regarded with honour — greeting.

A full year went by on 2 December, most noble Master Tycho and friend to be honoured, since I wrote to you and replied to your letter — the one you had written to me on 28 September of the previous year. So a whole year has slipped past for us in epistolary silence, which has come about not through forgetfulness of our friendship — which, for my part, is observed and cherished as something sacred — but through the labours of my office and the various preoccupations of my household, likewise through health none too firm or settled, and, to say it in one word, through old age. For I am now in my sixty-eighth year, soon, as the proverb has it, to be thrown off the bridge. My memory is indeed failing me so badly that, did I not see and recognise my own hand, I should never say that I had read or handled these matters. I pray to God that he may not let my memory be utterly abolished in me — which we read befell Trapezuntius in his old age — but grant me at least some, that no forgetfulness of the divine promises may take hold of me. My astronomical studies have now fallen quite silent with me, and there is nothing, my most beloved Tycho, that you should expect from me in that line. You, press on in those things you have begun, and bring them to completion, while the powers of mind and body are still vigorous, and preserve yourself for eternity. I deal with medicine alone, for the sake of supporting my household family. To it I join theological matters and meditation upon death — which death now knocks often at the door of our tabernacle. I had also dealt with chymical matters for a full year and more, but when I had found them all to be vain, I gave them up.

I suppose that you have received the Imperial privilege from Sager, the physician of Lübeck. For on 5 May of this year he informed me that he had sent it on to you. I was awaiting his return to us, or to Meissen. That a comet shone forth last autumn was common report. It was written about from elsewhere too. By me, however, though I scanned the heavens diligently, it could nowhere be sighted. If anything about it is established for you, write it out to me, I pray. On 12 December — what Master Peucer wrote out to me concerning that comet, you will learn from his letter, a copy of which I am sending you. Today, which is the 22nd day of December, I dined with Master Curtius. He showed me his sextant. It was made by a certain distinguished Landgravian craftsman, and brought here only very recently. If only we now had a quadrant ready, we would first of all observe here the altitude of the pole, then the distances of some stars, and, if anything else should arise, you would have us as colleagues — me indeed a feeble little old man, but ready for labours suited to my age. Master Curtius is also having made for himself a globe built up from little wooden slats glued together. Its diameter is six feet in length. Onto that globe he desires to set the stars of your observation. He bade me give you his very warmest greetings. He would most gladly have written to you himself, but I can testify that, on account of his most weighty occupations and court business, he was unable to; he asks that you hold him excused. He invites you to the Diet of the Empire at Regensburg. And if I learn for certain that you are coming there, I will myself hasten up for the sake of seeing you and greeting you, provided that my health and the strength of my poor little body do not hinder it. But if you cannot come, then send back, I beg, the little book of Apian on the comet, to which certain Bohemian opuscula are bound; likewise the Commentaries of Trapezuntius, which I suppose you now perhaps no longer need. Send those tracts to Master Curtius. Fare well. Given at Prague, 24 December, in the year 1593.

I write this to you by way of Master Vinshemius.

Most devoted to you,
Thad[daeus].

Caspar Peucer to Thaddaeus Hagecius — heartiest greeting.

I have eagerly awaited a reply from you to my last letter, which I do not doubt you received from the renowned and noble Lord Ferdinand, Count of Schlick. Since that [reply] is, in my opinion, being delayed rather long, I judged it should be drawn out; but I reckoned I could accomplish this by no better means than if I were to enquire into your observations [παρατηρήσεις] of the comet lately seen — which I confess I myself caught sight of only twice, at about the third hour of the morning, in the asterism of the Great Bear, and indeed at its head; small in body, of a wholly wan and dim colour, with an oblong tail, slender toward its end, stretched out from the north above the solstitial point of rising [the summer sunrise] toward the winter point of setting. Being myself destitute of all instruments and assistants, and of opportunity of place as well, I was able to observe nothing other than what sight grasped. Of the time of its rising I learned too late. And after I had observed it for two days it vanished, sooner than I had hoped. If, then, anything about it has been ascertained by you, and if you presage anything about its outcome, I beg you to disclose it to me, by a letter sent through this noble man, Albert von Conttenau, who is being dispatched thither to the Imperial court by my Most Illustrious Prince. Into which dodecatemorion [δωδεκατημόριον] of the Zodiac, and into what part of it, [the comet] fell, I do not know. I observed no more than that it stood not far from the star of the She-goat and the Kids; of its latitude, altitude, and parallax I found out nothing — about these, therefore, I seek and await your observations. I commend this noble man to you.

Fare well, and happily.
21 November, Julian, in the year [15]93.
Caspar Peucer.

Apparatus

  • Sender. Thaddaeus Hagecius = Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku (Thaddaeus Hagecius ab Hayck, 1525–1600), Prague physician, astronomer, and Imperial medicus; a long-standing correspondent of Tycho's and the leading Bohemian advocate of Tycho's super-lunar interpretation of the 1572 nova and the 1577 comet. He signs THAD. He states he is "in his sixty-eighth year," consistent with a 1525 birth.
  • Recipient / address. Domino in Knudstorff et Insulae Uraniae — "lord in Knudstrup [Tycho's ancestral seat in Scania] and on the Isle of Urania" (i.e. Hven, with Uraniborg).
  • Enclosed letter. The second letter is the enclosure Hagecius mentions ("a copy of which I am sending you"): Caspar Peucer (1525–1602), the Wittenberg/Melanchthonian physician-astronomer (Melanchthon's son-in-law), writing to Hagecius on the same comet. S. D. = salutem dicit, "sends greeting". The closing D. is the chancery Datae/Datum ("given"), rendered bracketed.
  • annus integer praeterijt die 2 Decemb. — "a full year went by on 2 December": Hagecius's previous letter to Tycho was of c. 2 December (1592), replying to Tycho's of 28 September (1592). A relative dating internal to the letter, not the dateline; left as written.
  • iuxta proverbium ponte deijciendus — "to be thrown off the bridge, as the proverb has it": the Roman saying sexagenarios de ponte (the old were proverbially "cast from the bridge"); a self-deprecating reference to his age. Kept as proverb.
  • TRAPEZUNTIO … Commentaria TRAPEZUNTIJ — George of Trebizond (Georgius Trapezuntius, 1395–1486), the humanist translator/commentator of Ptolemy's Almagest; his loss of memory in old age was a topos. The Commentaria Trapezuntij Hagecius asks Tycho to return are Trebizond's commentary on Ptolemy. ("Trapezuntius" kept as the period name, glossed.)
  • Chymica … vana omnia — "chymical matters … all vain": Hagecius reports abandoning alchemy after more than a year as fruitless. Chymica kept as "chymical" (period term), not modernised to "chemistry."
  • SAGERO Medico Lubecensi — "Sager, physician of Lübeck": a Lübeck medical man serving as courier of an Imperial privilege (Privilegium Caesareum, a printing/publishing privilege) to Tycho. Misenam = Meissen.
  • The comet. The Cometa of "last autumn" is the comet of autumn 1593. Hagecius, despite diligent search, never saw it; Peucer (writing 21 Nov.) reports two pre-dawn sightings near the head of Ursa Major, close to Capella and the Kids (Capellae et Haedorum sidere — the star Capella with the Haedi in Auriga), wholly without instruments. The request for latitude, altitude, and parallax (latitudo, altitudo, parallaxis,) reflects the Tychonic programme of deciding a comet's super-lunar position by parallax.
  • CURTIO / CURTIUS — Jacob Kurtz von Senftenau (Jacobus Curtius, d. 1594), Imperial Vice-Chancellor at Prague, astronomy patron; the Curtius whose Prague house Tycho would later use. He owns a sextant by "a distinguished Landgravian craftsman" (i.e. from the Hesse-Kassel workshop of Landgrave Wilhelm IV — likely Joost Bürgi's circle) and is commissioning a wooden globe six feet in diameter onto which he wishes to plot "the stars of your observation" (Tycho's star catalogue). The conditional "if only we had a quadrant" (Quadrantem … altitudinem poli … distantias stellarum) is Hagecius's wistful offer of collaboration.
  • VINSHEMIUM — Vinshemius (Vögelin / Veit Winsheim line), the courier by whom the letter is conveyed; named as bearer.
  • FERDINANDO Comite a Schlick — Ferdinand, Count of Schlick (Šlik), Bohemian magnate, named in Peucer's letter as the conduit of his earlier letter.
  • ALBERTUM a Conttenau — Albert von Conttenau (so spelled), a nobleman being sent by Peucer's prince to the Imperial court, proposed as the return courier. Illmo meo Principe = "my Most Illustrious Prince" (Peucer was then in the service of the Electors of Saxony / Anhalt at Dessau).
  • APIANI … de Cometa — a comet tract of Apian (Peter Apian, or his son Philipp), bound with "certain Bohemian opuscula"; to be returned to Curtius.
  • Comitia Imperij Ratisbonam — the Diet of the Empire at Regensburg (the Imperial Diet held there); Curtius's invitation to Tycho.
  • Greek. παρατηρήσεις — paratērḗseis, "observations" (transliterated paratereseis); transcribed in the source block and translated, by the edition's convention δωδεκατημόριον — dōdekatēmórion, literally "twelfth-part," the technical term for a sign / dodecatemorion of the Zodiac; transcribed and glossed (kept as "dodecatemorion" with the gloss "of the Zodiac").
  • ortum solstitialem … occasum brumalem — the ancient wind-rose/horizon idiom: the comet's tail stretched from the north "above the summer sunrise point toward the winter sunset point" (i.e. roughly NE-toward-SW on the horizon). Rendered with the idiom and glossed, not converted to azimuths.
  • Calendar / dating crux. Hagecius's letter is dated Pragae die 24 Decembris Anno 1593 — Prague, at the Catholic Imperial court, so New Style (Gregorian); the header carries this as written. Within his letter he says "Today, which is the 22nd day of December" yet subscribes "24 December": a two-day gap between the body's "today" and the dateline (composition begun 22 Dec., completed/dispatched 24 Dec.) — flagged, not reconciled by the edition's convention. Peucer, by contrast, subscribes "21 November, Julian [Iul.], in the year [15]93" — he explicitly marks his date Old Style, the Protestant Saxon usage; the ten-day Julian/Gregorian gap is thus visible across the two enclosed letters and is preserved exactly as each writer dated his own. No date is silently converted.
  • Honorifics. S. = salutem (dicit), "greeting"; T. studiosiss. = Tui studiosissimus, "Most devoted to you"; B. V. = Bene Vale, "Fare well."
