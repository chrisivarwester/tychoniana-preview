NOBILISSIME ET CLARISSIME VIR, AMICE HONORANDE.S.

P.SUB praeteritae hyemis initium, vir magnificus, D. GUILHELMUS STUARTUS, mihi literas tuas et librum, quem misisti, reddidit, quibus, ut in manus meas venêre, avide perlectis, delectatus sum plurimum; nam non solum erudita intricatae quaestionis tractatio, in qua eximiam doctrinam et singularem industriam perspexi, mirum in modum placuit, sed etiam gratissimum fuit, tam amicam erga me voluntatem in te viro praestanti cognoscere. Pro ista tua humanitate et libro tuo, quo me honorare libuit, ago quas possum gratias, cumque simili studiorum fructu respondere nequeam, animum tui peramantem tuaeque amicitiae studiosum profiteor. Quod eam LIDDELIUS noster mei apud te fecerit mentionem, ex amore quodam id suo egit; quod vero te ad amicitiam mutuam invitaverit, mihi rem gratam, et quam multis ante annis expetitam a me fuisse scierat, fecit. Nam a quo doctissimus WITTICHIUS ex Dania redierat, tua ille tantopere praedicavit, ut ab eo tempore te semper in amore et honore habuerim, et ante quinquennium, cum in patriam per Porthmum vestrum navigarem, quae ille commendaverat, coram intueri cupieram et constitueram; sed ventis contra afflantibus et pro tua Urania Norvegicis scopulis ad mensem obiectis, aegre votis frustratus sum. Ab eo tempore accessit honorifica nostratium Legatorum de admirandis tuis instrumentis (nam de alijs iudicare vix poterant) commendatio; quae illud amicitiae tuae desiderium renovavit et auxit, adeo ut superioribus annis saepenumero in animo habuerim meis literis initium eius facere; quod non acciderit, vitae nostrae ratione eiusmodi cogitationes subinde excutiente factum est; quod tu nunc prior id egeris, et comitatem agnosco, et benevolentiam grato animo complector, meque vicissim teneri statuo, ut quod a te bene coeptum est, a me, quo poterit studio, in posterum colatur et conservetur. Quod quidem non illibenter faciam; nam quamvis exiguum sit, quod ipse polliceri potero, in tanta earum rerum oblivione, in quibus tu excellis, iucundum tamen erit de ijs nonnunquam tecum conferre, a quo summa quaeque expectare licebit. Nam et sic liber tuus, licet ab undecim hinc annis eiusmodi vix attigerim, tam fuit acceptus, ut accuratas eius observationes et ingeniosas demonstrationes summa cum voluptate legerim; ac pro veteri nostra erga has disciplinas affectione, studiosis earum plurimum gratulor, esse eiusmodi Artifices, quibus nec ad perfectam scientiam nec ad absolutam operationem quicquam deesse videtur: tantumque de illis studijs te meritum esse statuo, ut omni commendatione dignissimus sis.

Sed ad literas tuas nunc veniam, quibus citius respondissem, nisi continuae peregrinationes nostrae ab initio Februarij vix unum diem domi esse concessissent. Ex Anglia ante paucos dies domum reversus, nonnihil otij sum nactus ad tua relegenda et iterum expendenda; nec tamen ut cuperem. Nam si reliqua suppeterent, experiri vellem, an Cometas iterum numeris possem coelo deducere in nativum suum locum inter ARISTOTELIS fumositates. Sed hoc alterius erit otij et maioris earum rerum recordationis. Nunc pro praesenti occasione, quantum occupationes permittent, paucis rescribam.

Literis tuis a me petis, ut sententiam meam candide significem. Quod ad id attinet, mihi tua vehementer placent, et inprimis illud nobile studium in corrigendis stellarum locis et motibus et exactioribus numeris subijciendis supra modum commendare oportet; quo ut pergas nomen tuum inter illos aeternos numeros consecrare hortari vix audeo, cum summo conatu eo te ferri omnibus necessarijs instructum iam palam constet; si ullam operam in tam nobili conatu praestare tibi possem, non deessem; sed nobis nec tam accurata instrumenta hîc sunt, nec his coelestibus a caducis corporibus vacare licet. Nunc enim magis Rhabarbarum et Sene similiaque expenduntur, quam omnes Planetarum gyri. Verum, nobilissime vir, cum ad eam contemplationem te ille aeternus omnium Creator vocaverit, ijsque, quibus oportuit, instruxerit, macte hac felicitate et virtute utere, ut talento tuo posteritati lucrifacias. At non Paradoxis; nam ingenue loquor, veritatis causa, sed acceptioribus disciplinis. Hoc etenim tuum, in quo CICERONEM te Mathematice imitari voluisse mihi persuadeo, minime arridet. Non enim placet, sapientissimi Architecti perfectissimum ordinem obscurari, et mutabilibus cum immutabilibus confusis, αταξίας Chaos introduci. Nec latet, alios ante te in ea fuisse opinione, contra quos aliquando meditabar in Cometometria nostra disputationem. Verum cum illi nullis rationibus eo fuerint inducti, evidentius est, quam ut ostendi debeat. Nemo tam multis id conatus est persuadere, quam tu. Quod an effeceris, ego quidem affirmare nequeo; mihi enim Physicorum coniecturae ex rerum naturis sumtae magis consentaneae adhuc videntur. Et ne absque ratione ita sentire me existimes, paucis tua, si non ingrata fuerit veritatis inquisitio, excutiam.

Primis quinque capitibus, quae de apparente loco et motu Cometae demonstrasti, praeclare a te constituta videntur, nec si numeri constent, contradictioni locus est. Sed sexto capite plus ultra progrederis, coelum novis corporibus aperturus, ubi nihil ijs loci esse ARISTOTELES demonstravit. Neque tuae rationes eius authoritati hîc praevalere censendae sunt, si diligenter examinentur. Nam illae tres Maximae, quas prima comprobatione affers, probabiliter duntaxat, ut ipse fateris, pro sententia tua faciunt, sed quaeso, quorum ante authoritate fuerint approbatae, ut probabilem concludendi vim habeant? Sed sint tales: ut arcum maximi circuli describere, sit in coelo esse; quod Phaenomenon tuum talem descripsisse tibi visum sit, demonstras; sed an alijs similiter moveri apparuerit, dubito. Tibi videtur materiae caussa circulum magnum describi non posse, quod vagabundius hincinde ab eiusmodi orbita potius expatiaretur, ac ob id ingenitam ipsi motus sui scientiam asscribis. Mihi tam intelligens sui fuisse nequaquam consentaneum existimatur; quin potius illum motum materiae caussa accidisse, quippe aliâs non fuit, quam a primi motus numero retardatio, quae maior circa initia extitit, cum moles maior plus subsisteret, subinde et quasi proportionaliter decreverit, flamma aequalibus temporibus materiam lychni instar depascente, quod non in minorem, sed maiorem abierit circulum, argumentum est, variasse a Terra remotionem, materia nimirum indies magis ac magis extenuata, et flamma ad superiora praevalente, quae Physica ipsius motus ratio magis consentanea est, quam statuere animal fuisse logicum, seu αὐτοκίνητον a sua motrice mente corpus.

Secunda Maxima coelo Cometas potius eliminas et longe exterminas: ideo cuius moles, lux et motus continuo decreverunt, donec prorsus evanuerint, in illo immutabili loco fuit? quo concesso, ut alio praeteream, praecipua Astronomiae hypothesis evertetur, qua invariabilis coelestium conditio ponitur. Planetae a celeriore ad * terram et hoc ad illud certis sub periodis remeant, at Cometis a suo interitu non est regressio, nec quicquam hîc magis simile, quam circularis est rectae. Tertia, quod motum Luna celeriorem habuerit, supra eam evehis: sic progrediendo, ad Saturnum usque sub finem evolabit; neque enim si inter coelestia talis motuum Harmonia sit, quicquid speciem aliquam eiusmodi motus habet, pro suo numero inter ea reponetur? Coelestibus ingenitum est circularis motus principium: mutabilibus adventitium est tantum, cumque toto naturae genere haec ab illis differant, ut asymmetra, secundum magis et minus comparari non debent. Itaque his Topicis tuis nusquam adhuc locantur Cometae, quod paucis indicare volui. Videamus iam an Mathematicae dimensiones coelum ipsis patefaciant, quod maxime quidem secunda comprobatione tua conaris, ubi assumptis duabus ab ore Pegasi observatis Cometae distantijs inquiris, an collectae ex calculo iuxta Hypothesin distantiae congruant. Primum quidem ex ijsdem distantijs, cognita declinatione et a Meridiano remotione, colligis ad data tempora apparentes altitudines. Et posito Cometa in orbe lunari inquiris sectionis angulum, quem facit verticalis cum circulo apparentis motus: cuius anguli beneficio parallaxes in longum et latum elicis, unde vicissim distantias ab ore Pegasi ratiocinaris: quarum posterior cum maior priori obveniat, et quo inferius ponetur Cometa, eo ab observatione magis discrepabit, concludis necessario supra Lunam esse. Ponemus in hoc successivo rationum ductu nihil requiri, an ideo supra Lunam erit, quia sic collectae distantiae ab observatis differunt: at hac ratione supra Lunam non erit, quia nunquam congruent, quantumvis enim removeris, semper posterior collectarum maior erit posteriori observatarum. Et ubi Parallaxis nulla est, posterior erit duntaxat aequalis priori, coëuntibus apparentis et veri loci lineis: nunquam minor fiet, ut vult observatio. Deinde hae collectae fiunt, Phaenomeno a priore ad posteriorem situm motu duntaxat primo translato, nulla proprij motus habita ratione: quomodo posterior distantia necessario maior evadet priori, si quicquid ex Parallaxi accesserit; si nihil, erit aequalis: nunquam minor, nisi ex proprio id motu fiat: quo interveniente, ut si versus stellam supra se positam proprio motu feratur, quod ex Parallaxi accederet, iterum subtrahetur vel etiam amplius: ac tum Cometa etiam longe infra Lunam posito, non modo nulla erit aspectus diversitas, sed posterior quoque distantia a Fixa minor evadet priori. Ut sit Cometae sublunaris remotio h b, ad quod quidem punctum b primo motu in secunda observatione perveniret, sed proprio retrahatur in d, sitque retractio in Instrumento ad a insensibilis fere ad 3.' vel 4.' c e, fiat tamen per eam in illa immensa octavi orbis remotione, ut abs situ b supra Lunam in f inter coelestia ad g usque attollatur. Ubi ex Parallaxi, quae haberi vix potest, remotio Cometae a Terra definiri nequit, quod nemo hactenus animadvertit.

Sed repeto nunc illum ductum, qui admodum interruptus est: unde data tempora habes; non ex altitudinibus Fixarum : Si ex Horologijs, cur non id alijs concedis? Deinde ille sectionis angulus d c e pag. 114. non est repertus, neque idem est cum angulo g c k pag. 112. quia circulus d c e pag. 112. per apparentem locum transit, et apparentis est motus, at f c g, pag. 114. alius est et transit per verum ex Hypothesi locum: proinde d c e pag. 114. non est inventus, quo Parallaxes exquiris: ac ideo hae in longum et latum deductae Parallaxes alienae sunt, nec aliae sunt visae longitudines, quam quas veras appellas pag. 115: ut hae distantiae ne quidem iuxta Hypothesin rite sint collectae, unde apparet, hanc demonstrationem omni modo infirmam esse ad id quod intendis. Cum collimatius rem agis, proprium ad stellam motum consideras, et easdem omnino reperis distantias, ut omnia exempla docent, primo excepto pag. 119. in quo illa tria scrupula prima, quorum causa tot centenis Semid. Terrae vis Cometam removeri, non fiant ulla retardatione respectu Parallaxeos, sed calculo tantum obrepunt: nam in alijs omnibus non occurrunt, nec mirum si ex ijsdem eadem proveniant, cum nihil alieni miscetur: ac in illa immensa remotione, cum Parallaxis evanescat, nullam pariet differentiam, illa quam affers cum observatione convenientia nihil concludat.

Tertia comprobatio ad quaestionis huius veritatis eruendam accommodari fortasse poterit, sed pro tua sententia nihil facit: nam concessa illa ad idem momentum reductione, quamvis in locis longitudine et latitudine differentibus eadem reperiatur Cometae a Vulturis stella distantia, non sequitur locorum intercapedinem a b pag. 125. respectu remotionis b c insensibilis esse quantitatis: nam negligis differentiam longitudinis, et in eodem plano statuis lineas visuales a c f et b c e, cum in diversis sint, quod loca longitudine differant, et in diversis planis existentes. Licet in corpore Cometae ad idem momentum coëant, in octavo tamen orbe in contrarias dehiscunt partes e et f: a quibus ad stellam d aequales sint distantiae arcus d e d f, ac triangulum fiat isosceles d e f, idque Cometa longe infra Lunam posito: quare hac probatione non efficietur coelestem habuisse situm.

In longitudine Uraniburgi et Pragae ab alijs differre videris, quod Pragam facias Uraniburgo orientaliorem, cum, ut de alijs non dicam, in ORTELIJ Mappis longitudo Pragae sit circiter 32, Uraniburgi vero 35; quaeso, ad me perscribas exactas, quas habere poteris tui loci, Pragae et Cassiliae longitudines.

In quarta probatione, quas appellas respectu centri altitudines, non sunt aliae, quam apparentes, respectu nimirum superficiei, quia ex declinatione et azimutho apparentibus deducis: quod in orbe Lunae differentia verarum altitudinum minor sit differentia apparentium ad 9.' nihil aliud arguit, quam in hoc supposito situ Cometam admittere aspectus diversitatem: nec dubium est, quin quo magis removeris, differentia verarum et apparentium altitudinum sit minor futura, donec prorsus nulla evadat. REGIOMONTANI demonstratio satis accommodata est Phaenomeno immobili, quantumvis elevetur, si modo ulla sit Parallaxis: at mobili nequaquam inservit, nec tua adhibita correctione accommodatior fit, quia in correcto Azimutho Cometa non apparuit, ut Parallaxis in eo non sit quaerenda: talis adhibenda esset correctio, qua proprius ad stellam motus, qui Parallaxin exhaurit, determinetur, verum ne hac ratione remotio eius a Terra erui poterit, quod per Parallaxin haec indagari nequeat. Atque hae sunt rationes, quibus Cometas inter coelestia locare conaris, quam vero deficiant ex ijs quae attuli, manifestum esse arbitror.

Quae octavo Capite affers, cum his nitantur, cum ijs consistent, nec id pluribus exquirere libet. Quod ad illam mundani Systematis Hypotyposin attinet, cum ut penetrationum dimensionem evites, Planetas per liquidum Aethera progressivo motu ferri statues, ut alia praeteream, circularis motus Hypothesin evertes, et apparens anomalia per helices indemonstrabilis et inexplicabilis reddetur. Sic est Systema constituendum, ut habeat Mathematicus, in quo acquiescat, nec Physico sit, quod obmurmuret; nam nequeunt horum Philosophorum demonstrationes contradicere. Illustrissimi D. Landgravij observationes valde placent, nec dubito, quin ex ijs possit quaestionis veritas elici. Rudes MÆSTLINI et CORNELIJ notationes licet ad apparentem motum ostendendum aliquo modo faciant, ad Cometae situm demonstrandum nihil conferunt. Et illum Philosophia Tubingensis melius potuit instituere, nisi phantasiae ostentandae causa eiusmodi figmenta publicare voluisset. HAGGECIUS, quae in Nova Stella parum ex arte praestiterit, me non latet, ut mirum non sit, in hoc Cometa tam fuisse incertum. Imo mirum non est hos Mathematicos in tam absurdam abijsse sententiam, cum nec sufficientia ad inquisitionem propositam data sumpserint, nec certam haberent demonstrationem, quam sequerentur: Non enim adhuc constat, nec demonstratum est ab ullo, quantum scio, qua ratione possit Cometarum remotio definiri, si proprium habeant motum. REGIOMONTANI demonstratio in absurdum abit. DIGESSÆI alae non pertingunt eo, et illa doctissimi WITTICHIJ (quem ad coelestia migrasse, ad quae summo studio semper aspiravit, vehementer doleo, vel Mathematum caussa) quam sub SCULTETI nomine reprehendis, quamvis ei concedantur observationes, nihilo magis rem expediet. Repetatur libri tui figura pag. 38. Statuit in illa verticalium prioris et posterioris observationis sectione B. verum Cometae locum fuisse, et Parallaxes utrique tempori respondentes facit b a, b k: at in illo b puncto, si Cometa proprium habuit motum, vere non fuit, ut ab eo Parallaxis numerari non debeat, ac ideo demonstratio haec duntaxat inservit Phaenomeno immobili. Cum itaque dimensionibus Mathematicis ad locum Cometarum nondum sit deventum, quid in hac quaestione statuemus? Ipse nondum a praeceptore ARISTOTELE discedo, qui mutabilia ab immutabilibus, ut naturis, sic locis discernere docuit, illaque orbis Lunaris complexu comprehensa nulla naturae vi supra posse efferri demonstravit. Si Mathematicas requiris demonstrationes, cum aliae pro tempore non suppetant, D. WITTICHIJ assumam. Observationem tu refellis, quod sub eadem Verticali simul stellae illae cum Cometa esse non potuerint. Licet hoc demonstrationem non infringat (nam sub diversis concludit) tamen non video, quî tua ratiocinatio plus fidei merebitur, quam illius doctissimi artificis de sensu suo affirmatio: mihi constat fuisse illius, nec illi vel peritiam observandi vel Instrumenta idonea defuisse, ut si indiligentiam probare nequeamus, illas sensu notas observationes, ut principia concedere oporteat. Demonstratio vero, quamvis iam reieceram, multum tamen fidei meretur, quo circa illud tempus accommodata fuit, quo Cometa a numero primi motus insensibiliter deficiebat, et maxime inter binas observationes nulla fuit in intermedio tempore variatio, ut sectio B. pag. 38. fuerit, cum ille observaret, verus locus, quare cum reliqua succedant, licet WITTICHIJ probatio magnam fidem meretur. Sic si NOLTHIO suae sensiones non denegentur (nec intelligo, quî refelli possint, cum circa idem fere tempus observatio fuerit) nec operatio in absurdum abeat, admodum probabiliter concludit, sublunarem fuisse Cometam. Ipse eundem observavi, idoneis Instrumentis iustae magnitudinis, Radio, Quadrante et Parallatico, ac aliqua adhibita diligentia sumpsi qualibet observatione apparentes altitudines Cometae et Aquilae vel alterius stellae: deinde distantiam apparentem Cometae et stellae: Ex quibus azimutha et tempora per demonstrationem reperi: ac certe prima apparitione in absurdas incidi operationes, sed postea plus minus 5° gradibus Parallaxin habere inveni. Motum proprium habuisse animadverti, quo Parallaxis exhauriebatur: et nonnulla occurrebant ἀγεωμέτρητα, nec tum exactissima eius rei demonstratio fuit nota, unde factum est, ut iudicium suspenderem. Ab eo tempore ita illa omnia manibus nostris excussa sunt, ut nunquam ea resumere licuerit: minusque iam ad eiusmodi calculum aptus sum: Si tamen aliquando otium expetitum fuerit, ea nonnihil repetam, tecumque, si libuerit, de ijs conferam. Rogo te ut ad me mittas omnes praestantium artificum et maxime tuas observationes, quas habes de nova Stella et Cometis, qui superioribus annis illuxerunt.

Atque haec sunt, quae ad Paradoxon tuum afferre volui; quae quidem non alio modo allata sunt, quam ut ostendam, quibus ipse adducar, cur ab eruditis Physicorum coniecturis nequeam adhuc discedere; ut in bonam partem ea accipias, vehementer peto. Audio, clariss. vir, te in Chymicis excellere; si quid singulare in hoc artificij genere habes, sive ad extractiones faciendas, sive ad aquas purgantes, vel tale quid pertineat, quaeso mihi communices. Habent Germani, ut accepi, nunc inventum, quo distillatis corpus expurgant, quo licet non indigeamus, tamen ignorare non cupio; saepius id experiri cogitavi, sed cum non suaderet ratio, ne operam et oleum, ut non semel mihi accidit, perderem, omisi. Verum charta deficiente finem faciam, et haec quae rescripta sunt, boni consule; nam diligentius ista tractare, aegri non patiuntur. Vale. Datae Edenburgi in Scotia, in Martio primum, postea sub Maij initium.

Tui amantiss.
IOANNES CRAIGUS,
Philosophiae et medicinae doctor.

Most noble and most renowned Sir, friend to be honoured — greeting.

About the beginning of last winter the magnificent gentleman Master William Stuart delivered to me your letter and the book you sent; and when these came into my hands, I read them through eagerly and was delighted in the highest degree. For not only did the learned treatment of an intricate question — in which I discerned outstanding doctrine and singular diligence — please me wonderfully, but it was also most gratifying to recognise in you, an excellent man, so friendly a disposition towards me. For this kindness of yours, and for your book, with which it pleased you to honour me, I give what thanks I can; and since I cannot answer with a like fruit of studies, I profess a mind most loving of you and zealous for your friendship. That our friend Liddel made mention of me to you, he did out of a certain affection of his own; but that he invited you to a mutual friendship, he did me a welcome thing, and one he knew I had sought for many years past. For ever since the most learned Wittich returned from Denmark, he so extolled your work that from that time I have always held you in love and honour; and five years ago, when I was sailing home through your Sound, I had desired and resolved to behold in person what he had commended — but, with the winds blowing against me and (in lieu of your Urania) the Norwegian rocks set before me for a month, I was, to my regret, cheated of my wishes. Since that time there was added the honourable commendation of our envoys concerning your admirable instruments (for of other matters they could scarcely judge); which renewed and increased that longing for your friendship, so much so that in recent years I have often had it in mind to take the first step by a letter of mine — and that this did not come to pass was owing to the manner of our life, which keeps shaking out such thoughts. That you have now done it first, I both acknowledge your courtesy and embrace your goodwill with a grateful mind, and judge myself bound in turn, so that what has been well begun by you may henceforth be cultivated and preserved by me with what zeal I can. And this indeed I shall do not unwillingly; for although what I myself shall be able to promise is slight, amid so great a forgetfulness of those matters in which you excel, it will nonetheless be pleasant now and then to confer with you about them — from whom one may expect all that is highest. For thus too your book, though for eleven years past I have scarcely touched anything of the kind, was so welcome that I read its accurate observations and ingenious demonstrations with the greatest pleasure; and, out of our old affection towards these disciplines, I most heartily congratulate their students that there are such craftsmen, in whom nothing seems lacking either for perfect knowledge or for finished practice; and I judge you to have deserved so much of those studies that you are most worthy of every commendation.

But I shall now come to your letter, to which I would have replied sooner had not our continual travels, from the beginning of February, scarcely allowed me to be at home a single day. Having returned home from England a few days ago, I have got some little leisure to re-read and re-weigh your matters — yet not as much as I could wish. For if the rest were at hand, I should like to test whether I could once again, by the numbers, lead the comets down from the sky into their native place among Aristotle's exhalations [fumositates]. But this will require another leisure and a fuller recollection of those matters. For now, on the present occasion, so far as my occupations permit, I shall write back briefly.

In your letter you ask me to signify my opinion candidly. As to that, your work pleases me greatly, and above all that noble pursuit of correcting the places and motions of the stars and of subjecting them to more exact numbers I must commend beyond measure; and to exhort you to go on consecrating your name among those eternal numbers I scarcely dare, since it is already plainly evident that you are borne thither with the utmost effort, furnished with all that is needful. If I could render any service in so noble an endeavour, I should not be wanting; but we here have neither such accurate instruments, nor are we permitted to have leisure for these celestial matters [apart] from perishable bodies. For now rhubarb and senna and the like are weighed out more [often] than all the circlings of the planets. Yet, most noble Sir, since that eternal Creator of all things has called you to that contemplation, and has equipped you with the things that were needful, go on, blessed [macte], and use this happiness and this excellence so that you may turn your talent to gain for posterity. But not by paradoxes — for I speak frankly, for the sake of truth — rather by more acceptable doctrines. For this [conclusion] of yours, in which I am persuaded you wished to imitate Cicero mathematically, by no means smiles upon me. For it does not please [me] that the most perfect order of the most wise Architect should be obscured, and that, with the mutable confounded with the immutable, a Chaos of disorder [αταξίας] should be introduced. Nor is it hidden [from me] that others before you held that opinion, against whom I once meditated a disputation in our Cometometria. But since they were led to it by no reasons, it is too evident [to need] that it should be shown [against them]. No one has tried to persuade [people] of it with so many [arguments] as you. Whether you have accomplished it, I for my part cannot affirm; for to me the conjectures of the natural philosophers, drawn from the natures of things, still seem more consonant [with reason]. And lest you should think I hold this view without reason, I shall, in a few [words] — if the inquiry into truth shall not be unwelcome — sift your arguments.

In the first five chapters, where you demonstrated [matters] concerning the apparent place and motion of the comet, things seem to me admirably established by you, nor — if the numbers hold — is there room for contradiction. But in the sixth chapter you go further beyond [plus ultra], about to open the heaven to new bodies, where Aristotle demonstrated there is no place for them. Nor are your reasonings here to be reckoned to prevail over his authority, if they be carefully examined. For those three Maxims which you bring forward in your first proof make for your opinion only probably, as you yourself admit; but I ask, by whose prior authority were they approved, that they should have a probable force of concluding? But let them be such: that to describe the arc of a great circle is to be in the heaven; that your phenomenon seemed to you to have described such [an arc], you demonstrate — but whether it appeared to others to move likewise, I doubt. To you it seems that a great circle cannot be described for a material cause, because [the body] would rather wander more erratically hither and thither, off such an orbit; and on that account you ascribe to it an innate knowledge of its own motion. To me it is judged by no means consonant that it should have been so intelligent of itself; but rather that that motion came about from a material cause, since [otherwise] it was nothing but a retardation from the rate of the first motion [a primi motus numero retardatio] — which was greater about the beginnings, when the greater mass offered more resistance, and thereafter decreased, as it were proportionally, the flame feeding on the matter, lamp-wise, in equal times. That it went off into a greater, not a smaller, circle is an argument that its distance from the Earth varied, the matter, namely, being day by day more and more attenuated and the flame prevailing toward the upper [regions]; which physical account of its motion is more consonant [with reason] than to maintain that it was a logical animal, or a body self-moving [αὐτοκίνητον] by its own moving mind.

By the second Maxim you rather eliminate comets from the heaven and banish them far off; [for] was that, whose mass, light, and motion continually decreased until they utterly vanished, in that immutable place? Granting which — to pass over the rest — the chief hypothesis of astronomy will be overthrown, by which the unchangeable condition of the celestial [bodies] is posited. The planets return, from the swifter to the [slower], [and] this to that, under fixed periods; but for comets there is no return from their extinction, nor is anything here more alike [to the celestial motions] than a straight line is to a circle. The third [Maxim], that it had a motion swifter than the Moon, [whereby] you raise it above her — proceeding so, it will fly off, by the end, even as far as Saturn; for if there is among the celestials such a Harmony of motions, will not whatever has some appearance of such a motion be set, according to its [own] rate, among them? To the celestials the principle of circular motion is innate; to the mutable it is only adventitious; and since these differ from those in their whole kind of nature, as incommensurables [asymmetra], they ought not to be compared according to more and less. And so by these Topics of yours comets are nowhere yet located — which I wished to indicate briefly. Let us now see whether mathematical measurements open the heaven to them, which indeed you chiefly attempt in your second proof, where, taking two observed distances of the comet from the mouth of Pegasus, you inquire whether the distances gathered from the calculation according to the Hypothesis agree. First, indeed, from those same distances, the declination and the distance from the Meridian being known, you gather the apparent altitudes for the given times. And, the comet being placed in the lunar orb, you inquire the angle of section which the vertical makes with the circle of apparent motion; by the benefit of which angle you elicit the parallaxes in longitude and latitude, whence in turn you reason out the distances from the mouth of Pegasus; and since the later [distance] comes out greater than the former, and the lower the comet is placed the more it will differ from observation, you conclude that it is necessarily above the Moon. We shall grant that in this successive course of reasonings nothing is wanting — [yet] will it on that account be above the Moon, because the distances so gathered differ from the observed [ones]? But by this reasoning it will not be above the Moon, because they will never agree; for however far you remove it, the later of the gathered [distances] will always be greater than the later of the observed [ones]. And where there is no parallax, the later will be merely equal to the former, the lines of the apparent and the true place coinciding; it will never become smaller, as the observation requires. Next, these [distances] are gathered with the phenomenon carried from the former to the later position by the first motion only, no account being taken of its proper motion; [so] how shall the later distance necessarily come out greater than the former? If anything will have accrued from parallax [it will be greater]; if nothing, it will be equal; never smaller, unless it come about from the proper motion — which, intervening (as if it be carried by its proper motion toward a star placed above it), [then] what would accrue from parallax is again subtracted, or even more; and then, the comet being placed even far below the Moon, not only will there be no diversity of aspect, but the later distance from the fixed [star] too will come out smaller than the former. Let the sublunary distance of the comet be h b, to which point b it would arrive by the first motion at the second observation, but let it be drawn back by its proper [motion] to d; and let the retraction on the instrument at a be almost insensible, to about 3' or 4', c e; yet let it come about, through that, in that immense distance of the eighth orb, that from the position b above the Moon it be raised in f up to g among the celestials. Where[upon], from parallax — which can scarcely be had — the comet's distance from the Earth cannot be determined: which no one has hitherto noticed.

But I now resume that course [of argument], which has been much interrupted: whence have you the given times? Not from the altitudes of the fixed [stars]. If from clocks, why do you not concede the same to others? Next, that angle of section d c e on page 114 is not found, nor is it the same as the angle g c k on page 112; because the circle d c e on page 112 passes through the apparent place, and is [the circle] of the apparent motion, but f c g on page 114 is another and passes through the true place [taken] from the Hypothesis. Accordingly d c e on page 114, by which you seek out the parallaxes, is not found; and therefore these parallaxes deduced in longitude and latitude are alien [to the case], nor are the visual longitudes other than those you call true on page 115 — so that these distances are not even rightly gathered according to the Hypothesis, whence it appears that this demonstration is in every way infirm for that which you intend. When you handle the matter more sightingly [collimatius], you consider the proper motion toward the star, and you find altogether the same distances, as all the examples teach, the first on page 119 excepted — in which those three first minutes [tria scrupula prima], for the sake of which you would have the comet removed by so many hundreds of Earth-semidiameters, do not arise from any retardation in respect of parallax, but creep in by the calculation only; for in all the others they do not occur, nor is it any wonder if the same things come out of the same, since nothing alien is mixed in. And in that immense distance, when parallax vanishes, it [the calculation] will produce no difference, [so that] that agreement with observation which you bring forward proves nothing.

The third proof can perhaps be adapted to drawing out the truth of this question, but it does nothing for your opinion; for, granting that reduction to the same moment, although in places differing in longitude and latitude the same distance of the comet from the star of Vultur [the Eagle, Aquila] be found, it does not follow that the interval of the places a b on page 125 is, in respect of the distance b c, of insensible quantity; for you neglect the difference of longitude, and set the visual lines a c f and b c e in the same plane, when they are in different [planes], because the places differ in longitude and [the lines] exist in different planes. Although they coincide in the body of the comet at the same moment, yet in the eighth orb they gape apart into contrary parts, e and f; from which, to the star d, let the distances of the arcs d e, d f be equal, and let an isosceles triangle d e f be made — and that with the comet placed far below the Moon; wherefore by this proof it will not be brought about that it had a celestial position.

In the longitude of Uraniborg and of Prague you seem to differ from others, in that you make Prague more easterly than Uraniborg, whereas — not to speak of others — in Ortelius's Maps the longitude of Prague is about 32, but of Uraniborg 35. I beg you, write out to me the exact longitudes that you will be able to have, of your [own] place, of Prague, and of Kassel.

In the fourth proof, the [altitudes] which you call altitudes in respect of the centre are no other than the apparent ones — in respect, that is, of the surface — because you deduce them from the apparent declination and azimuth. That in the lunar orb the difference of the true altitudes should be less than the difference of the apparent ones by 9' argues nothing other than that, in this supposed position, the comet admits a diversity of aspect; nor is there any doubt that the more you remove it, the smaller the difference of the true and apparent altitudes will be, until it becomes utterly none. Regiomontanus's demonstration is sufficiently adapted to an immobile phenomenon, however high it be raised, provided there be any parallax at all; but to a mobile [one] it in no way serves, nor is it made more apt by the correction you applied, because in the corrected Azimuth the comet did not appear, so that parallax is not to be sought in it: such a correction would have to be applied as would determine the proper motion toward the star, which exhausts the parallax — yet not even by this reasoning will its distance from the Earth be drawn out, because by parallax this cannot be investigated. And these are the reasonings by which you try to locate comets among the celestials; how truly they fall short, I judge to be manifest from the things I have brought forward.

What you bring forward in the eighth chapter, since it rests on these [reasonings], will stand or fall with them, nor does it please [me] to examine that at greater length. As to that sketch [hypotyposis] of the world-System: since, in order to avoid the dimension of interpenetrations [of the orbs], you will lay it down that the planets are carried through the liquid Aether by a progressive motion — to pass over other matters — you will overthrow the Hypothesis of circular motion, and the apparent anomaly will be rendered indemonstrable and inexplicable by spirals [helices]. A System is to be so constituted that the mathematician have [something] in which he may rest, nor be there [anything] for the physicist to murmur against; for the demonstrations of these [two kinds of] philosophers cannot contradict [one another]. The observations of the Most Illustrious Lord Landgrave please [me] greatly, nor do I doubt that from them the truth of the question can be drawn out. The rough notations of Maestlin and of Cornelius, although they make in some way for showing the apparent motion, contribute nothing toward demonstrating the comet's position. And the Philosophy of Tübingen could have instructed him better, had he not, for the sake of showing off his fancy, wished to publish such fabrications. It is not hidden from me how little Hagecius performed by art in [the matter of] the New Star, so that it is no wonder that in this comet he was so uncertain. Nay, it is no wonder that these mathematicians have lapsed into so absurd an opinion, since they neither took data sufficient for the inquiry proposed, nor had a sure demonstration which they might follow; for it is not yet established, nor has it been demonstrated by anyone, so far as I know, by what reasoning the distance of comets can be determined, if they have a proper motion. Regiomontanus's demonstration goes off into the absurd. Digges's wings do not reach so far; and that [demonstration] of the most learned Wittich — whom I deeply grieve to have migrated to the celestial [regions], to which with the utmost zeal he always aspired, [if only] for the sake of the Mathematics — which you reprehend under the name of Scultetus, although his observations be granted, will no more dispatch the matter. Let the figure of your book on page 38 be taken up again. He laid down that, in that section B of the verticals of the former and the latter observation, the true place of the comet was, and he makes the parallaxes answering to each time b a, b k; but at that point b, if the comet had a proper motion, it truly was not, so that parallax ought not to be reckoned from it; and therefore this demonstration serves an immobile phenomenon only. Since, then, by mathematical measurements the place of comets has not yet been arrived at, what shall we determine in this question? I for my part do not yet depart from my teacher Aristotle, who taught us to distinguish the mutable from the immutable in their places as in their natures, and demonstrated that those [mutable bodies], comprehended within the embrace of the lunar orb, can by no force of nature be raised above [it]. If you require mathematical demonstrations, since others are not for the present at hand, I shall take up those of Master Wittich. You refute the observation, [on the ground] that those stars could not have been under the same vertical at the same time as the comet. Although this does not break the demonstration (for it concludes under different [verticals]), yet I do not see how your reasoning will merit more credit than the affirmation of that most learned craftsman about his own perception. It is established for me that it was his, and that he lacked neither skill in observing nor suitable instruments; so that, if we cannot prove negligence, we ought to concede those observations, noted by the senses, as principles. And the demonstration, although I had now rejected it, yet merits much credit, in that it was adapted to that time at which the comet was insensibly failing from the rate of the first motion, and especially [in that] between the two observations there was no variation in the intervening time, so that the section B on page 38 was, when he observed, the true place; wherefore, since the rest follow, the proof of Wittich merits great credit. So too, if to Noltius his perceptions be not denied (nor do I understand how they can be refuted, since the observation was made at about the same time), and the operation does not go off into the absurd, [the matter] concludes very probably that the comet was sublunary. I myself observed the same [comet], with suitable instruments of just size — a cross-staff, a quadrant, and a parallactic [instrument] — and, with some diligence applied, took at each observation the apparent altitudes of the comet and of Aquila or of another star, then the apparent distance of the comet and the star; from which I found the azimuths and times by demonstration. And at the first apparition I certainly fell into absurd operations, but afterwards I found it to have a parallax of more or less 5 degrees. I noticed that it had a proper motion, by which the parallax was exhausted; and some things came up that were not amenable to geometry [ἀγεωμέτρητα], nor was the most exact demonstration of that matter then known [to me], whence it came about that I suspended judgement. Since that time all those [papers] have been so shaken out of our hands that it has never been possible to take them up again, and I am now less fit for such calculation; yet if some longed-for leisure shall ever come, I shall take them up again somewhat, and, if it please [you], confer with you about them. I ask you to send me all the observations of excellent craftsmen, and above all your own, which you have concerning the New Star and the comets that have shone in years past.

And these are the things I wished to bring forward against your Paradox; which indeed have been brought forward in no other way than to show by what [considerations] I myself am led [to see] why I cannot yet depart from the learned conjectures of the natural philosophers; and I earnestly beg that you take them in good part. I hear, most renowned Sir, that you excel in chemistry; if you have anything singular in this kind of craft — whether it pertain to making extractions, or to purging waters, or anything of the sort — I beg you to communicate it to me. The Germans, as I have heard, now have a discovery by which they purge the body with distillates; which, though we do not need it, I do not wish to be ignorant of. I have often thought to try it, but since reason did not counsel it, lest I lose my labour and oil [operam et oleum] — as has happened to me more than once — I left it off. But, my paper failing, I shall make an end; and take these [things] that have been written back in good part, for being sick I am not allowed to treat the matter more diligently. Farewell. Given at Edinburgh in Scotland, first in March, afterwards about the beginning of May.

Most loving of you,
John Craig,
Doctor of Philosophy and Medicine.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Ioannes Craigus = John Craig (d. 1620), Scottish physician and natural philosopher, trained at Frankfurt-an-der-Oder and Königsberg, an Aristotelian opponent of the new cosmology; later physician to King James VI/I. This is the opening salvo of the Craig–Brahe cometary controversy: a point-by-point critique of Tycho's De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis liber secundus (Uraniborg, 1588), Tycho's treatise on the comet of 1577 — the book ("liber") and accompanying letter that William Stuart had delivered. Craig defends the orthodox Aristotelian doctrine of a sublunary, exhalation-fed comet against Tycho's parallax-based demonstration of a supralunary, celestial comet.
  • D. Guilhelmus Stuartus = William Stuart (Colonel William Stewart), the Scottish diplomat/soldier who carried Tycho's letter and book to Scotland; vir magnificus = "magnificent gentleman."
  • Liddelius noster = Duncan Liddel (1561–1613), Scottish mathematician-physician then at Rostock/Helmstedt, who had visited Tycho and acted as the intermediary recommending Craig to him.
  • Wittichius = Paul Wittich (c. 1546–1586), the Silesian mathematician who worked with Tycho on Hven in 1580 and whose cometary demonstration Tycho had published (Craig says Tycho criticised it "under the name of Scultetus"). Craig had known Wittich and laments his recent death (ad coelestia migrasse — "migrated to the celestial regions," a deliberate pun given the subject). Per the house Brahe profile, Tycho's relationship with Wittich is contested (the Copernicus-marginalia attribution dispute); Craig here treats Wittich's observations as authoritative principia.
  • Scultetus = Bartholomäus Scultetus (Schultz, 1540–1614), Görlitz mathematician; Tycho discussed a cometary demonstration under his name which Craig identifies as actually Wittich's.
  • Maestlinus = Michael Maestlin (1550–1631), Tübingen astronomer (Kepler's teacher), author of an Observatio et demonstratio cometae (1578); Cornelius = Cornelius Gemma (1535–1578), Louvain physician-astronomer who also wrote on the 1577 comet. Philosophia Tubingensis = "the Philosophy of Tübingen," i.e. Maestlin's school; rudes … notationes = "rough notations."
  • Haggecius = Tadeáš Hájek (Thaddaeus Hagecius, 1525–1600), Bohemian imperial physician-astronomer who wrote on both the 1572 nova stella and the 1577 comet; Craig disparages his artistry.
  • Digessaei alae = "Digges's wings" — Thomas Digges (c. 1546–1595), whose Alae seu scalae mathematicae (1573, on the new star) is meant; the phrase puns on the title (alae = "wings"). Rendered literally with the title-pun flagged.
  • Regiomontani demonstratio = the parallax method for comets in Regiomontanus (Johann Müller von Königsberg, 1436–1476), De cometae magnitudine longitudineque; Craig argues it holds only for an immobile object.
  • Nolthius = Andreas Nolth(ius) / Noltius, a German observer of the 1577 comet whose sublunary result Craig credits; identification only probable — flagged.
  • Landgravius = Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel; his Kassel observations Craig praises.
  • Ortelii Mappae = the maps of Abraham Ortelius (Theatrum orbis terrarum), cited for the longitudes of Prague ( c. 32°) and Uraniborg ( c. 35°); Craig challenges Tycho's making Prague easterly of Uraniborg.
  • Places. Uraniburgum → Uraniborg; Praga → Prague; Cassilia → Kassel; Edenburgum in Scotia → Edinburgh, Scotland; Porthmus vester = "your Sound" (the Øresund); Norvegici scopuli = "the Norwegian rocks."
  • Greek (transcribed and translated by this chapter's convention). (1) αταξίας (printed without breathing/accent in the source; classical ἀταξίας, gen. of ἀταξία) = "of disorder/disarray" — αταξίας Chaos = "a Chaos of disorder." (2) αὐτοκίνητον = "self-moving" — Craig mocks the idea that the comet is a self-moving body endowed with mind. (3) ἀγεωμέτρητα = "things not amenable to geometry / un-geometrical," echoing the Academy's ἀγεωμέτρητος; rendered "things not amenable to geometry."
  • Technical terms (editorial glossary). parallaxis → parallax; declinatio → declination; altitudo → altitude; azimuthum → azimuth (kept); Meridianus → Meridian; anomalia (apparens) → (apparent) anomaly; Hypothesis / Systema mundanum → Hypothesis / world-System; octavus orbis → the eighth orb (the sphere of fixed stars); orbis lunaris / orbis Lunae → the lunar orb; liquidus Aether → the liquid Aether. Semid. Terrae = semidiametri Terrae, "Earth-semidiameters" (Earth radii). aspectus diversitas = "diversity of aspect," i.e. parallax in the period sense; kept as the author's phrase.
  • Instruments. Radius → cross-staff (radius astronomicus); Quadrans → quadrant; Parallaticum → parallactic [instrument] (triquetrum); Instrumentum → instrument.
  • Astrology/chemistry. Chymica → chemistry; extractiones → extractions; aquae purgantes → purging waters; distillata → distillates; Rhabarbarum et Sene → rhubarb and senna (purgative drugs — Craig's physician's idiom contrasting medical practice with planetary theory). remedium-type modernisation avoided by the audit's rule.
  • Symbols. A single asterisk survives in the source at "a celeriore ad terram" (page 89 of the source text): the edition prints * where a word (most plausibly tardiorem, "slower," to complete the antithesis a celeriore ad [tardiorem]) is missing or illegible. The translation supplies "[slower]" in brackets and the asterisk is flagged as a textual lacuna — see crux below. The string "5°"* is read as "5 degrees."
  • Allusions & idiom. plus ultra — "further beyond" (the Habsburg/Pillars-of-Hercules motto, here a barb that Tycho oversteps the bounds Aristotle set); kept. fumositates (Aristotle's) → "exhalations" (the dry/hot vapours of Meteorologica I from which comets were held to form); rendered "exhalations" with the literal "fumes/smoky stuff" sense noted. macte (hac felicitate et virtute utere) → "go on, blessed; use…" (the encouragement-formula macte virtute). operam et oleum perdere → "lose labour and oil" (proverbial: waste effort, lit. lamp-oil); kept literal with gloss. Cometometria nostra = "our Cometometria," Craig's own (unpublished/projected) treatise on comet-measurement. Topica tua = "these Topics of yours" — Craig sarcastically calls Tycho's three Maxims mere dialectical topoi (places of argument), not demonstrations; rendered "Topics" with the Aristotelian-dialectic sense flagged. Hypotyposis = "sketch/outline" (the Tychonic world-system sketch in ch. 8). lychni instar → "lamp-wise," the candle/lamp simile for the comet's matter being consumed by its flame.
  • Page references. Craig cites Tycho's De mundi … phaenomenis by page (pp. 38, 112, 114, 115, 119, 125) and by lettered points in the diagrams (B; a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h, k); these are reproduced verbatim, as they key to the 1588 figures and are essential to the argument.
  • Dateline (crux of dating — handled by this chapter's convention and the header rule). The subscription reads "Datae Edenburgi in Scotia, in Martio primum, postea sub Maij initium" — "Given at Edinburgh, first in March, afterwards about the beginning of May." The letter was thus begun in March and finished about the beginning of May 1589 (O.S.; Scotland was on the Julian calendar). The edition label ("1589. maj. Edinburgh") records only the completion. The header therefore gives the as-written span "March / beginning of May 1589 [O.S.]" rather than asserting a single day the text does not substantiate; no day-of-month is stated in the source for either terminus, so none is invented. This is a genuine in-letter dating notice (a letter composed over ~two months), preserved, not reconciled to a single date.
  • Edition of record & witness. Per the source header, the text-of-record is the Commercium litterarium clarorum virorum (as carried into Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII); the sole MS witness cited is A = Berlin, "a manuscript copy in a copy of Tycho Brahe's astronomical writings" (shelfmark not given in the source — recorded as cited).
