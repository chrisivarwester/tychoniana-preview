The original

CLARISSIMO ET DOCTISSIMO VIRO, DOMINO TOBIÆ FISCHERO, PHILOSOPHIÆ ET MEDICINÆ DOCTORI EXIMIO, AMICO SUO INPRIMIS DILECTO.

CLARISSIME et præstantissime vir, accepi quidem literas tuas unâ cum carminibus in mei gratiam a te eleganter et erudite concinnatis, antecedente anno, cum primum Pragam venissem, a Domino Doctore SWALBIO, communi nostro amico, mihi redditas, tum quoque alteras hoc ipso anno Mense Martio ad me datas. Verum quod hactenus neutris responderim, velim occupationum mearum multitudini, quodque varie interea partim locorum mutationibus, partim alijs impedimentis distractus fuerim, tribuas, meque pro tuo singulari candore excusatum habeas. Fuerunt sane utræque illæ literæ mihi longe acceptissimæ, nam præter insignem humanitatem pari eruditioni coniunctam, quæ inde satis superque elucebat, eximium etiam et sincerum erga me, licet de facie tibi ignotum, amorem et animum candoris integerrimi refertissimum affatim ostendebant, pro qua tua in me sincera, licet antea nihil tale a te promeritum, benevolentia tibi gratias habeo quam maximas, et te vicissim intime diligere, atque in amicorum, quos selectiori albo inscribo, annumerare non desinam, daboque operam, ne te certâsse priorem pœniteat.

Cœterum quod ad carmina illa tua, quæ etiam mihi longe gratissima erant, attinet, velim, ut in meliorem partem accipias, quod paucula quædam in ijs alterârim, prout metri illius genus postulare videbatur secundum eos criticos, qui libertatem illam, qua tu nonnunquam usus es, curiositate quadam non facile admittunt. Quid vero pauculis in locis mutatum sit, carmen illud denuo exaratum et excellentiss. Domino MONAVIO (si recte memini) transmissum patefaciet. Nescio tamen, an ultimus versus isthic ita transformatus sit, prout postea (ni fallor) incidit, qui sic habet:

Gloria vana rudis vulgi, laus una placere
Bonis, malorum negligere calumnias.

Laudo ingenium tuum, laudo venam mellifluam et delicatam, et si plura eiuscemodi aut etiam alio carminis modo per otium luseris mecumque communicaveris, erit id mihi gratum, eaque prioribus adiungam. Coacervo multorum excellentium Poëtarum mea de causa, postquam patria excessi, exarata Poëmata, donec libellum aliquem mediocrem adimpleverim; videbis suo tempore sylvulam.

Opera mea Astronomica tibi impertiri necdum licet, ea enim ad manus non habeo, nam sunt inter res meas, quæ adhuc Magdeburgi reservantur, brevi tamen ex clementissima voluntate Cæsaris huc advehenda, neque etiam omnia, quæ typis prius inchoata habeo, plane ad Colophonem deducta sunt, ob eam, quæ mihi in Patria evenit, studiorum conturbationem. Spero tamen, me, favente divino Numine, cuius provisu Cæsarea Maiestas e tanto naufragio hunc portum, tantisque turbulentijs hoc otium benignissime mihi indulsit, ea, quæ desiderantur, propediem absoluturum, et plura additurum; tunc te voti compotem haud invite reddam, interea morulam intervenientem pro tuo candore in meliorem partem accipe.

Globi MERCATORIS, de quibus scribis, iam ferme antiquati sunt, vixque, quod sciam, reperiuntur exemplaria. Duo talium parandorum artifices Batavi, quorum unus Amsterodami IACOBUS FLORENTIUS, alter Alcmariæ WILHELMUS IANUS nominatus, globos secundum nostram in fixis sideribus restitutionem diligenter elaborârunt. Quorum posterior dimidium annum mecum in Insula nostra Uraniburgica fuit, atque ibidem stellas suo, quod attulit, globoso corpori, me circiter millenas illi concedente, insignivit. Si aliquam ex his nancisci posses reformationem, diceres MERCATORIS vel aliorum globos, ex erroneis stellarum canonibus, non autem cœlitus verificatis locis, conformatos, ijs non æquiparari posse. Nescio, an Francofurtum hi neoterici advehantur, sed si Bohemiam aliquando ingressus me inviseris, exemplum monstrabo, tunc simul alia, quæ penes me elaborata habeo, siquidem te etiam his studijs affici videam, lubens ostendam, tecumque de talibus iucunde conferam.

Vale.
Dabantur ex arce Cæsarea Benatika 16 Maij A. 1600.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the most renowned and most learned man, Lord Tobias Fischer, distinguished Doctor of Philosophy and Medicine, his friend especially beloved.

Most renowned and most excellent man, I did indeed receive your letter, with the poems elegantly and learnedly composed by you in my honour — delivered to me last year, when I had first come to Prague, by Lord Doctor Schwalb, our common friend — and also others, sent me this very year in the month of March. But that I have hitherto answered neither, I would have you attribute to the multitude of my occupations, and to my being meanwhile variously distracted, partly by changes of place, partly by other impediments; and hold me excused, after your singular candour. Both those letters were indeed far most welcome to me: for besides the remarkable humanity joined to equal erudition, which shone forth from them more than enough, they abundantly showed an exceptional and sincere love toward me — though unknown to you by face — and a mind most full of the most upright candour; for which sincere benevolence of yours toward me (though I had previously deserved nothing such from you) I give you the greatest thanks, and will not cease, in turn, to love you intimately and to count you among the friends I inscribe on the more select roll; and I will take pains that you do not regret having striven first [in friendship].

For the rest, as concerns those poems of yours — also far most pleasing to me — I would have you take in good part that I altered a very few things in them, as the kind of that metre seemed to demand, according to those critics who do not easily admit, with a certain fastidiousness, that liberty which you have sometimes used. What has been changed in a very few places, that poem — written out anew and sent to the most excellent Lord Monau (if I rightly remember) — will reveal. Yet I know not whether the last verse there was so transformed as it afterward (unless I am mistaken) occurred [to me], which runs thus:

"The glory of the rude crowd is vain; the one [true] praise is to please
the good, [and] to disregard the calumnies of the wicked."

I praise your genius, I praise your mellifluous and delicate vein; and if you compose more of this kind — or even in another manner of poem — at leisure, and share them with me, it will be welcome, and I will add them to the earlier ones. I am heaping up the Poems composed by many excellent Poets on my account, since I left my fatherland, until I shall have filled some moderate little book; you will see in time a little sylva [collection].

My Astronomical works I cannot yet impart to you, for I do not have them at hand: they are among my things still kept at Magdeburg, soon however to be brought hither by the Emperor's most clement will; nor are all the things I had earlier begun in print quite brought to the colophon, on account of that disturbance of my studies which befell me in my fatherland. Yet I hope — by the favour of the divine Power, by whose provision the Imperial Majesty most kindly granted me this harbour out of so great a shipwreck, and this leisure out of so great turbulences — soon to complete what is wanting, and to add more; then I will, not unwillingly, make you master of your wish; meanwhile take the intervening little delay in good part, after your candour.

The globes of Mercator, about which you write, are now almost antiquated, and scarcely, so far as I know, are copies to be found. Two Dutch craftsmen for making such [globes] — one at Amsterdam, named Jacobus Florentius, the other at Alkmaar, named Wilhelm Janus — have diligently elaborated globes according to our restitution in the fixed stars. Of these, the latter was with me half a year on our island of Uraniborg, and there marked the stars upon the globe-body he had brought — I granting him about a thousand. If you could obtain one of these renewed [globes], you would say that Mercator's globes, or others' — fashioned from erroneous star-catalogues, not from positions verified from the sky — cannot be matched with them. I know not whether these new ones are brought to Frankfurt; but if, having entered Bohemia at some time, you visit me, I will show you a specimen; then at the same time I will gladly show you other things I have elaborated, if I see you too taken with these studies, and will pleasantly confer with you about such matters.

Farewell.
Given from the imperial castle of Benátky, 16 May, the year 1600.

Apparatus

- Recipient & occasion. Tycho's reply to Tobias Fischer (the Silesian poet-physician of Schweidnitz; cf. his letter brahe-t_16000318001), written the same day as the Monau letter (brahe-t_16000516001). Tycho admits Fischer to his friendship and thanks him for two sets of verses (delivered via Doctor Schwalb, Swalbius, in 1599 and March 1600).
- The poems and the sylvula. Tycho explains his emendation of Fischer's verses (tightening a metrical "liberty" the strict critics dislike), the corrected copy sent to Monau, and quotes the revised closing distich — "Gloria vana rudis vulgi, laus una placere / Bonis, malorum negligere calumnias" ("the glory of the rude crowd is vain; the one praise is to please the good and ignore the wicked's calumnies"). He is collecting friends' commendatory poems "since I left my fatherland" into a planned little sylva (verse-anthology in his honour).
- His works still at Magdeburg. Tycho cannot yet send his astronomical works (still stored at Magdeburg, awaiting the imperial transport; the Progymnasmata not yet "brought to the colophon" because of "the disturbance … in my fatherland"). The "harbour out of so great a shipwreck … this leisure out of so great turbulences" is his standard figure for the imperial refuge.
- Significance — the Tychonic celestial globes and Willem Jansz (Blaeu) at Uraniborg (cornerstone of cartographic history). Asked about Mercator's globes ("now almost antiquated"), Tycho names two Dutch globe-makers who built globes on his restored star-positions:
- Jacobus Florentius of Amsterdam = Jacob Floris van Langren (of the Van Langren globe-making house);
- Wilhelm Janus of Alkmaar = Willem Janszoon (Blaeu) (1571–1638), the future great Amsterdam cartographer.
The latter "was with me half a year on the island of Uraniborg, and there marked the stars on the globe-body he had brought, I granting him about a thousand." This is Tycho's own testimony to Blaeu's ~half-year stay at Hven (1595–96) and the c. 1,000 Tychonic star-positions he set on his celestial globe — the documentary basis for the Blaeu globes carrying Tycho's catalogue, far superior to globes "from erroneous star-catalogues, not positions verified from the sky." (This resolves the "Dutch globe-maker" item flagged from brahe-t_16000506001, where Tycho's gift-globe to Elector Christian II was made by "a skilled Dutchman at Uraniborg" — i.e. Blaeu.)
- Persons named. Tobias Fischer; Bartholomäus Schwalb (Swalbius); Jacob Monau; Mercator (Gerardus); Jacob Floris van Langren (Jacobus Florentius); Willem Janszoon Blaeu (Wilhelm Janus of Alkmaar); Rudolf II.
- Calendar. Dateline Benatika 16 Maij A. 1600; New Style (imperial). Header agrees with the label.
