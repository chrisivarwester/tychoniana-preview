The original

EXEMPLAR EPISTOLÆ NOBILISSIMI ET AMPLISSIMI VIRI, DOMINI HENRICI RANZOVII DE BREDENBERGA, VICARII REGII IN HOLSATIA ET ADIUNCTIS PROUINCIJS AD TYCHONEM BRAHE DATÆ, QUA SEQUENTIUM EDITIONEM EXPETIT ET IMPETRAT.

PERLEGI vir Clariss. et Nobiliss. diligenter literas Illustrissimi Principis LANDTGRAVII ad te transmissas, vna cum responsione tua semel atque iterum rescripta, quarum lectio mihi accidit iucundissima, tum quod plurima cognitu vtilia, et arti Astronomicæ plenius excolendæ idonea in ijs contineri percepi, tum quod a Principe, quo nemo inter reliquos hoc tempore Astronomiam magis colit, atque a te nostri æui præstantissimo Mathematico vltro citroque sunt conscriptæ. Ac sane non possum non gratulari nostro Seculo, quod DEVS optimus maximus non solum Viros Nobiles ac Plebeios, sed etiam aliquot Principes excitet, qui huius scientiæ Studio delectentur. Non enim vt vulgus opinatur, huius Artis cognitio omni certitudine caret, sed certis fundamentis et Obseruationibus nititur, humanoque generi imprimis est necessaria. Quo circa toto etiam Cœlo errant, qui hanc ab otiosis ingenijs excogitatam esse arbitrantur. Si quis enim eius prima indagabit initia, depræhendet a Patribus eam profectam, ac deinde ad posteros propagatam, longoque rerum vsu ac Obseruationibus crebris et diuturnis tandem confirmatam esse. Ne autem huius Scientiæ vtilitates longius persequar, tantummodo huius Doctrinæ contemtores ob oculos sibi ponere velint, quantis in tenebris versaremur, si Scientia de motibus Solis, Lunæ et Stellarum, item de Æquinoctijs ac alijs, careremus. Huius enim Doctrinæ, si expertes essemus, præstantissima Corpora Cælestia non secus ac bruta animalia aspiceremus. Quod ad Doctrinam de prædictionibus attinet, etsi ingenue quidem fatendum est, non semper euentus prædictionibus respondere: Tamen eius rei culpa Scientiæ Astrologiæ minime imputanda, sed potius imperfectæ eius cognitioni. Tum etiam considerandum est, quod DEVS totius Orbis arbiter ac moderator non semper alligatus sit causis secundis, sed multa pro sua Diuina Sapientia ita disponat, vt alios, atque nos putaramus, euentus consequantur. Neque tamen propterea tota Ars adspernanda est. Proinde inprimis huius Artis tam vtilis et necessariæ cultores laude digni sunt, tum quod Doctrinam conseruent, ex cuius traditione, vt ad cæterarum Scientiarum Doctores, nulla ad eos non solum redundat vtilitas, siue lucri alicuius perceptio, sed etiam vilis vulgi contemptus. At tu longe aliter de hac sublimi Scientia; præsertim quo ad motuum conseruationem iudicas. Nactus enim in eum vsum peculiarem et idoneam Insulam, cursus Cœlestium Corporum maxima diligentia obseruas, Instrumentis in eum vsum excogitatis et confectis quamplurimis, magnitudine forma et artificio inæstimabilibus. Quin et ex illa Insula quam incolis, aliqui Libri Astronomici tuo Labore et Industria iam publicam lucem viderunt, speramusque indies plures tanquam ex Equo Troiano prodituros, qui insigne Doctrinæ tuæ testimonium toti posteritati patefacient. Nam et ij, qui editi sunt, non solum iam a Viris Principibus et Doctis, magno cum applausu excipiuntur ac adprobantur, sed et illi venturæ posteritati erunt gratissimi, nominisque tui prædicationem in omnia æui Secula propagabunt. Subsequuturos itaque idipsum luculentius facturos non ambigo. Sed vt ad institutum Vir Doctiss. reuertar, cum te iterum quoddam opus Mathematicum sub prælo habere cognouerim, non possum non te adhortari, vt Illustrissimi Principis LANDTGRAVII ad te scriptas Literas, tuasque ad eum, tum ad Excellentiss. suum Mathematicum, et si quas præterea habes (vt te habere non dubito) Clarissimorum et Doctissimorum Virorum tecum de hac re conferentes Literas, vna cum tua ad singulas responsione, Operi quod præ manibus habes subiungere velis. Id enim mihi inprimis conueniens videtur, non solum, quia ad Astronomiæ penitiorem culturam, sed etiam quia proprie ad eam materiam, quam in eo Opere tractas, plurimum istæ Literæ faciant, magnamque cum ea non solum affinitatem habeant, sed illam plenius etiam illustret. Hoc si feceris, non tantum mihi, sed reliquis etiam huius Artis studiosis rem præstabis longe gratissimam. Et si tandem etiam in calce adieceris resolutionem Quæstionis eius, quam non ita diu tibi per literas proposui, de contracta Solari, vt a plurimis putatur, Sphæra, feceris et hac in parte cum mihi, tum alijs rem pergratam, neque vti spero inutilem. Hæc breuibus a te petere volui, nec diffido quin votis nostris et aliorum sis hac in parte satisfacturus.

Vale et Amicitiæ nostræ memoriam, vt sedulo conserues, peto. Iterum vale.
Datæ ex Arce Regia Segeberga 17 Ianuarij. Anno Salutiferi partus 1587.

English translation machine translation, Probable

A COPY OF A LETTER FROM THE MOST NOBLE AND MOST EMINENT MAN, MASTER HEINRICH RANTZAU OF BREDENBERG, ROYAL VICAR IN HOLSTEIN AND THE ADJOINING PROVINCES, ADDRESSED TO TYCHO BRAHE, IN WHICH HE ASKS FOR — AND OBTAINS — THE PUBLICATION OF WHAT FOLLOWS.

I have read through carefully, most renowned and most noble Sir, the letters of the Most Illustrious Prince the Landgrave that were sent to you, together with your reply written back time and again; and the reading of them proved most delightful to me — both because I perceived that very many things useful to know, and apt for the fuller cultivation of the art of Astronomy, were contained in them, and because they were composed back and forth by a Prince than whom no one else at this time cultivates Astronomy more, and by you, the most outstanding Mathematician of our age. And indeed I cannot but congratulate our Century, that God Best and Greatest raises up not only Noblemen and commoners, but even several Princes, to take delight in the study of this science. For the knowledge of this Art does not, as the common crowd supposes, lack all certainty, but rests upon sure foundations and Observations, and is in the first place necessary to the human race. Wherefore those too wander by a whole Heaven [astray] who judge that it was thought up by idle wits. For if anyone shall search out its first beginnings, he will discover that it set out from the Fathers, and was thereafter handed on to posterity, and at last confirmed by long practice of affairs and by frequent and long-continued Observations. But that I may not pursue the uses of this Science any further, let only the despisers of this Doctrine set before their own eyes in what great darkness we should be living, were we to lack the Science of the motions of the Sun, the Moon, and the Stars, and likewise of the Equinoxes and the rest. For if we were without this Doctrine, we should look upon the most excellent Heavenly Bodies no otherwise than the brute beasts do. As for the Doctrine concerning predictions, although it must indeed be candidly admitted that outcomes do not always answer to the predictions, yet the blame for this is by no means to be charged to the Science of Astrology, but rather to its imperfect knowledge. And then it must also be considered that God, the arbiter and governor of the whole World, is not always bound to second causes, but disposes many things, according to His Divine Wisdom, in such a way that outcomes follow other than we had supposed. Nor on that account, however, is the whole Art to be despised. Accordingly the cultivators of this Art, so useful and so necessary, are above all worthy of praise — both because they preserve a Doctrine from the handing-down of which (as happens to the teachers of the other Sciences) there redounds to them not only no benefit, nor the gaining of any profit, but even the contempt of the base crowd. But you judge far otherwise of this sublime Science — especially as regards the preservation of the [celestial] motions. For having obtained an Island peculiarly fitted for that use, you observe the courses of the Heavenly Bodies with the greatest diligence, with very many Instruments devised and made for that use, of inestimable size, form, and craftsmanship. Indeed, from that Island which you inhabit, certain Astronomical Books have by your Labour and Industry now seen the public light, and we hope that day by day more will come forth, as it were out of the Trojan Horse, which will disclose to all posterity a signal testimony of your Doctrine. For even those that have been published are not only received and approved already, with great applause, by Princely and Learned Men, but they will also be most welcome to the posterity to come, and will carry the proclamation of your name forward into all the ages of time. I do not doubt, then, that those to follow will do the very same more brilliantly still. But, that I may return to my purpose, most learned Sir: since I have learned that you once again have a certain Mathematical work under the press, I cannot but urge you to be willing to append, to the Work you have in hand, the Letters written to you by the Most Illustrious Prince the Landgrave, and yours to him, and to his most excellent Mathematician, and any others besides that you have (as I do not doubt you have) — the Letters of most renowned and most learned Men conferring with you on this matter, together with your reply to each. For this seems to me especially fitting, not only because those Letters bear very greatly upon the deeper cultivation of Astronomy, but also because they bear properly upon the very subject which you treat in that Work, and have a great kinship with it, and indeed illustrate it more fully. If you shall do this, you will render a most welcome service not only to me, but also to the rest who are devoted to this Art. And if at last you shall also add at the close the resolution of that Question which not long ago I put to you by letter — concerning the contracted Solar Sphere, as it is held by very many — you will in this part too do a thing most gratifying both to me and to others, and, as I hope, not unprofitable. These few things I wished to ask of you, and I do not distrust that in this part you will satisfy our wishes and those of others.

Farewell, and I ask that you diligently keep the memory of our Friendship. Again, farewell.
Given from the Royal Castle of Segeberg, 17 January. In the Year of the Salvation-bringing Birth 1587.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Dominus Henricus Ranzovius de Bredenberga, Vicarius Regius in Holsatia = Heinrich Rantzau (Henricus Ranzovius, 1526–1598), the Danish crown's Statthalter (royal governor / Vicarius Regius) in the duchies of Holstein and Schleswig, lord of Breitenburg (Bredenberga), and the foremost German-Danish humanist patron of the age — bibliophile, prolific author, and a notable promoter of astrology. He wrote from his castle at Segeberg. The identification is secure from the rubric's own titulature.
  • Document type & function. This is the prefatory / dedicatory letter that opens Tycho's Epistolarum astronomicarum libri (Uraniborg, 1596) — the volume of the Tycho–Hesse-Kassel correspondence. The rubric (QUA SEQUENTIUM EDITIONEM EXPETIT ET IMPETRAT, "in which he asks for — and obtains — the publication of what follows") frames the whole letter as the public request that the printed book purports to answer: Rantzau urges Tycho to publish the Landgrave correspondence, and the book is the granting of that request. The rubric is the printed edition's own editorial heading, not part of the letter body; preserved verbatim by the edition's convention.
  • Illustrissimus Princeps Landtgravius — Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel (1532–1592), Tycho's princely correspondent and fellow observer. Excellentiss. suus Mathematicus = Christoph Rothmann, the Landgrave's mathematician at Kassel.
  • Places. Segeberga / Arx Regia Segeberga = Segeberg (Bad Segeberg, Holstein), the royal castle; Holsatia = Holstein; Bredenberga = Breitenburg.
  • Astronomy — instruments & programme. Instrumentis … quamplurimis, magnitudine forma et artificio inaestimabilibus = "very many Instruments … of inestimable size, form, and craftsmanship" (instrumenta → "instruments,"); peculiarem et idoneam Insulam = the island of Hven. motuum conseruatio — rendered "the preservation of the [celestial] motions"; motus → "motion(s)" by the edition's convention "[celestial]" is an editorial gloss (bracketed) supplied because the genitive is unqualified in the source.
  • Astrology / theology of prediction. Rantzau's central apologetic passage defends Astrologia (predictive astronomy) against the charge of uncertainty: failed predictions are blamed not on the Scientia but on imperfecta eius cognitio ("its imperfect knowledge"), and on God's freedom not to be alligatus … causis secundis ("bound to second causes"). The hedges are preserved exactly: non semper euentus praedictionibus respondere ("outcomes do not always answer to the predictions"), ingenue … fatendum est ("it must … be candidly admitted") — no sharpening.
  • Idiom. toto … Coelo errant — "they wander by a whole Heaven [astray]," the proverbial toto caelo errare ("to be utterly mistaken"); kept literal with a bracketed gloss, since Rantzau is punning on the astronomical subject. a Patribus eam profectam — "it set out from the Fathers," i.e. the ancients/forefathers (not specifically the Church Fathers); rendered "the Fathers" with the ambiguity left as in the source.
  • Trojan Horse. tanquam ex Equo Troiano prodituros = "as it were coming forth out of the Trojan Horse" — a complimentary metaphor for the succession of books Tycho's island will yet disgorge; kept as metaphor.
  • The crux of the request. resolutionem Quaestionis … de contracta Solari, vt a plurimis putatur, Sphaera = "the resolution of that Question … concerning the contracted Solar Sphere, as it is held by very many." The phrase concerns a debated point about the sphere (orb) of the Sun and whether it is contracta ("contracted," i.e. drawn in / reduced) — most plausibly the contemporary controversy over the size and ordering of the solar orb (bound up with the absence of solar parallax and the dimensions of the world-system), which Tycho took up. Sphaera → "Sphere/orb". Flagged: the exact technical referent of contracta Solari … Sphaera is not made explicit in the letter itself (it points back to an earlier letter from Rantzau "not long ago"); the rendering keeps Rantzau's own words and the parenthetical hedge vt a plurimis putatur rather than resolving the doctrine.
  • Greek / symbols. None present.
  • Calendar. 17 Ianuarij. Anno Salutiferi partus 1587 = "17 January … In the Year of the Salvation-bringing Birth 1587" (Anno Salutiferi partus = anno Domini, a humanist flourish; kept literal). Rantzau, in Danish/Holstein royal service, wrote Old Style (Denmark retained the Julian calendar until 1700); no N.S. equivalent is asserted. The dateline date (17 January 1587) agrees with the edition label ("1587. 17. januar. Segeberg"); no discrepancy.
  • Honorifics. vir Clariss. / Nobiliss. / Doctiss. → "most renowned / most noble / most learned Sir"; closing Vale … Iterum vale → "Farewell … Again, farewell".
