TYCHO BRAHE AD HENRICUM BRUCÆUM.

RUPISTI tandem altum illud silentium, quo per totam aestatem aliquid ad me literarum dare supersedisti, amantissime BRUCÆE, quod autem nunc demum quasi e profundo somno expergefactus me tuis eruditis literis reviseris, est quod moram hanc hactenus commissam condonem, teque excusatum habeam. Agis autem in his mecum veluti ex professo de ijs, quae continentur in libro meo secundo Aethereorum Phaenomenωn, tibi praeterito vere per quendam e meis domesticis hinc in Germaniam proficiscentem transmisso, quaeque in priore eius parte inveniuntur, te sedulo perlegisse ostendis, posteriore leviter attacta. Visum itaque est tibi ad ea, quae de his ad me perscripsisti, in hunc modum respondere. Quod in priore eius voluminis portione contenta ex observationum et demonstrationum certitudine accomodationeque approbas et commendas, inprimis vero neotericae meae Hypotheseos circa dispositionem revolutionum coelestium inventionem tibi admodum placere asseveras, mihi pergratum est. Equidem quantum ad Hypotheseos eius constitutionem attinet, suspicabar eam tuo Genio non arrisuram, siquidem alias tam vehemens et constans sis autoritatis antiquorum conservator et propugnator, adeo ut nonnunquam nimium his tribuere et certioribus rationibus ipsique experientiae non parum derogare videaris; sed aliter tamen hîc accidit quam putabam: Hanc enim nostram mundani systematis Hypotyposin a veteribus nunquam perspectam, nec etiam a recentioribus ullis prius excogitatam non solum tuo suffragio comprobas, sed etiam ceteris, quod eiuscemodi, quae illae a PTOLEMÆO et COPERNICO propositae, non admittat absurda, anteferendam censes. Ut autem eo rectius intelliges, qua ratione in huius Hypotheseos inventionem ante aliquot annos pervenerim, mitto tibi exemplum literarum, nuper ad clarissimum et doctissimum virum D. CASPAREM PEUCERUM a me datarum, quibus eius quibusdam dubijs (quae ad nobilissimum virum Dominum HENRICUM RANZOVIUM, qui illi eius Hypotheseos nudam delineationem e libro meo excerptam communicârat, perscripsit, cuius etiam verborum unâ cum alterius cuiusdam Mathematici de eadem re ambigua sententia copiam facio) respondendum duxi, ut hac occasione plenius de hoc toto Astronomico negotio cum tanto Philosopho et Mathematico conferrem. Ex his plurima non saltem [ad] Hypotheseos huius rationes et uberiorem declarationem, sed etiam ad totam rem Astronomicam peculiariter facientia percipies tuo Genio, uti opinor, non displicitura. Ubi vero has ipsas literas perlegeris, vel etiam, si lubet, transcribi curâris, velim eas mihi remitti, siquidem exemplar hoc reservare cupiam, neque otium nunc suppetat, ut id denuo exarari faciam. Quae circa Astronomiae instaurationem desideras, ut omnia huic Hypothesi fundata eque certis observationibus Geometricis in numeros redacta Phaenomenis exacte correspondeant, etiam in his literis ad Dominum PEUCERUM datis, quo ordine et quanto conamine rem tantam aggredi molirique, favente Coeli ipsius et Universi Opifice, proposuerim, invenies. Nec est, ut pertimescas, quod laboris immensitas, rei tam arduae difficultas, temporis requisiti diuturnitas aut sumtuum magnitudo ullave alia molestia, eodem supremo Numine conatibus nostris aspirante, me absterrebunt, quo minus gratae, uti spero, posteritati tam divinae et sublimis artis restitutionem ea praecisione (absit invidia dicto) qua hactenus a nemine exantlata est, consecrem, omnibusque suis numeris, quantum quidem ab uno homine praestari potest, absolutam relinquam.

Sed de his hoc loco satis. Est tamen, quod tibi hac in re conquerar. Quidam nimis perfrictae frontis, quem indignum censeo, ut hîc nominetur, qui etiam, ubicunque fuit aut est, seipsum, qualis sit, prodit, hanc meam Hypothesium inventionem sibi arrogare, idque publico scripto, Argentinae hoc anno edito, asserere non erubuit. An et hanc cum reliquis, quae apud me clam descripsit, dum nobilissimo viro ERICO LANGE inserviens, in eius comitatu per aliquot dies fuit, subtraxit, aut qua alias ratione eius dispositionem hîc hauserit, noverit ipse; et Studiosi mei similem quandam inter segestria tunc iacentem, quae minus apposite delineata erat, monstrârunt. Certe quam fraudulenter et subdole hic egerit, reliqua, quae hîc, me inscio, ex meis descripsit, quorum aliquam partem recepi, ipsius manu exarata, satis convincunt, utut Hypothesin illam inter eas schedas non reperio; sed poterat hanc alibi abscondisse aut in memoria retinuisse, cum forte non suppeteret delineandi commoditas. Norunt domestici mei Studiosi illam Hypothesin iam aliquot annis a me fuisse constitutam atque illis ostensam: quin et liber hic de Cometa Anni 77, etsi prae se ferat editionem hoc Anno 88 factam, tamen norunt omnes, qui mecum sunt, eundem Anno 87 in mea Typographia excusam esse, data opera autem uno anno tardiorem notatam, quo esset ob novitatem acceptior. In hoc autem libro eandem mecum Hypothesin generaliter explicari, te non ignorare, ex iam dictis liquet. Sed egregie prodidit iste, qui sibi eam nuper vendicaret, se illam non probe intelligere, nedum ut eius autor sit, dum Martium orbem Solarem totaliter ambientem pingit et fingit, quod impossibile esse vel ex solo Copernico saltem obiter introspecto discere potuisset. Et quî, quaeso, is novas unquam condet Hypotheses, coelestibus revolutionibus analogas, qui multorum annorum exquisitis observationibus non est instructus, ut inde, quid in hactenus usitatis Hypothesibus desideretur, et quomodo idipsum restitui possit, solerter pervestiget? Sine his adminiculis novam aliquam inventionem iactare frivolum est planeque ridiculum, cum non habeat, unde probet per ipsas apparentias, cur prioribus relictis sic innovare [oporteat] et quomodo per multiplices observationes hanc apparentijs coelestibus rectius congruere confirmet. Sed facessant istae ineptiae, quibus nemo, nisi rem non intelligens, fidem adhibuerit.

Ad epistolam tuam redeo, ea, quae postmodum continet, ordine, ut constitui, disquisiturus. Emendationem locorum affixarum stellarum cap. 2 a me propositam ex declinatione stellae et transitu per meridianum aliquantulum in dubium vocare videris, eo quod exquisita temporis notitia Solisque locus praecise vix constare possint; id licet aliquo modo non abs re dicatur, et a me etiam circa finem eiusdem capitis in Additione fol. 31 incipiente idem fere asseveretur, nihilominus tamen adhibita competenti diligentia, et medijs quibusdam temporis minimas particulas aequali ductu, quoad fieri potest, exhibentibus, qualia nobis non pauca in promtu sunt, etiam haec ratio absque admodum sensibili errore locum meretur, praesertim si crebra reiteratione examinetur, donec obtenta non admodum sensibiliter differant, ut patet ex observationibus prioribus eo modo institutis, quae pag. 30 oculis subijciuntur, si conferantur cum alijs reiteratis et diverso modo per Solem intermediante Veneris stella ad fixa sidera diductis, quae pag. 33 exhibentur. Differentia enim est perexigua, utpote quae decimam unius gradus partem, ubi maxima est, non excedat, et in sex vel septem stellis tria minuta vix attingat; quae sane praecisio a veteribus non est extricata, ut mirari satis non possim, quod in ea, quibus tunc usus sum, instrumentorum parvitate et penuria vel per hanc ipsam tam lubricam ad deflectendum viam adeo prope tamen ad scopum collimârim. Verum exactiorem et nulli ambiguitati obnoxiam stellarum fixarum loca verificandi rationem in eadem Additione breviter indicavi, per distantias videlicet a Sole ad Venerem interdiu apparentem factas et noctu ab hac ad stellas relatas. Qua de re in antecedenti libro plenius ago, et per aliquot eo modo accurate factas animadversiones, easque tam Eoas quam vespertinas, stellarum inerrantium loca in veram et praecisam ab Aequinoctio vernali distantiam repono; idque ex ipsis observationibus ita et non aliter se habere posse demonstro, annotatis etiam earum veris latitudinibus, in quibus non minus quam longitudinibus nimium hactenus aberratum est. Solaris etiam cursus ad nostra tempora exactissima restitutio ibidem praemittitur, siquidem ex eo cetera omnia dependeant. Ubi Apogei locus eccentricitatisque vera quantitas e certis aliquot praecedentium annorum observationibus pluribus et non fallacibus instrumentis coelitus diligenter habitis Geometrice demonstrantur, adiecta aequalium motuum etiam correctorum et prostaphaereseωn e reperta eccentricitate extracta Tabula, ut de Solis motu apparentijs praecise congruente nullum amplius restet apud me dubium. Subiungam forsan de Lunari curriculo etiam subtiliter restituto nonnulla, quando per plurium annorum observationes eius perplexam semitam tam quoad longum quam latum plenius extricabo. Video te adhuc ruminare ea, quae doctissimus GEMMA FRISIUS in libello de Radio Astronomico quoad fixarum in Globum aliquem denotationem et postea Aequatoris Eclipticaeque in eodem designationem molitus est. Verum ego convenientius certis de causis fieri iudico, ut Aequator et Ecliptica prius in Globo ordinentur et in minimas particulas distribuantur, postea secundum harum exigentiam impositis affixarum locis et varia ratione hinc inde comprobatis, ita ut in unum et idem punctum recidant. Per distantias tamen beneficio Radij Astronomici factas, prout GEMMA tradidit et postea a multis tentatum est, negotium hoc nunquam dextre absolvetur. Impossibile enim est, per Radium, quantacunque longitudine praeditus sit, et qualicunque subtilitate divisus, etiam saepissime reiterando observationem exactas in ipso scrupulo stellarum rimari intercapedines, ut alibi per suas causas ostendemus. Ideoque ego primus Sextantes huic officio apprime idoneos et ubique veras stellarum remotiones in eodem minuto aut etiam huius aliquota parte, quotiescunque repetatur observatio, praebentes excogitavi, quibus etiam aliquot iam annis praecisa subtilitate distantias siderum inquirendo feliciter usus sum. Quin et frustra in Globum reponuntur stellae, quocunque tandem instrumento coelitus deriventur, nisi is admodum magnus sit, qualem ego sex pedum in diametro possideo, qui undiquaque orichalcicus est et divisionem graduum in singula sexagena minuta per ductum transversalem, more nobis usitato, discriminatim exhibet, absolutaque insuper rotunditate praeditus est. In hoc omnium affixarum loca instrumentis varijs ex ipso coelo deducta contignare decrevi, quorum etiam magna et praecipua pars iam antea a me in promtu habetur. Quare non est, quod dubites, me sponte mea ea, quae ad fixarum stellarum praecisam restitutionem faciunt, omni possibili diligentia moliri, ut nulla admonitione, vel eorum quae cap. 2 a me proponuntur, recapitulatione, opus fuisset.

Praeterea latitudinem Lunae PTOLEMÆI placitis quarta parte gradus unius maiorem a me adinventam, suspectam habes, eamque per eclipsium Lunarium magnitudinem et calculum illi correspondentem impugnas. Autoritatem insuper REGIOMONTANI, COPERNICI, WERNERI et aliorum insignium Mathematicorum convocas, quod illi Ptolemaicam Lunae latitudinem maximam praecise 5 partium perpetuo retinuerint, quos non dubitas magna diligentia eam coelitus ita se habere deprehendisse. Ptolemaicam insuper latitudinem omni carere vitio existimas ob loci suae observationis opportunitatem, quae Lunam iuxta Tropicum aestivum in maxima latitudine borea exigentem vertici quam proxime adunivit, semotis ob id parallaxium et refractionum impedimentis etc. De his ut tecum plenius agam, veritas ipsa postulat. Scias itaque, Lunae latitudinem maximam non temere aut sine crebra exactaque observatione a me Ptolemaica 15 scrupulis maiorem constitutam. Eam enim ita revera se habere, praesertim iuxta quadraturas, multis experimentis minimeque fallacibus observationibus, ex ipso coelo edoctus sum, nisi quod aliquando uno vel altero scrupulo maiorem, interdum paululum minorem adinvenerim; et si quae alia subest, uti non negavero, inaequalitas, eam penitius enodabo. Nec eclipsium animadversiones meae assertioni quippiam, quod in sensus cadere possit, derogant. Nam licet maxima latitudo 15 scrupulis ab ea, quae usitata et diu recepta est, maior statuatur, tamen haec differentia circa limites illos, ubi eclipses prope nodos fieri solent, nihil, quod animadverti possit, importat. Siquidem in distantia a nodis 12 graduum, intra quem proxime terminum eclipses Lunares fieri necessum est, discrimen latitudinis Ptolemaicae et nostrae evadit saltem trium scrupulorum primorum, quae unicum tantummodo digitum in Lunae obscuratione causantur, qui etiam vix est perceptibilis ob umbrae Terrenae extremitatem praecise in sensus non incurrentem, et ipsa eclipsium tempora non adeo certa hactenus innotuerunt, ut haec res nostrae inventioni quippiam officere queat, quod nos plurima et quam diligentissime aliquot praeteritis annis observata docuerunt deliquia Lunaria iuxta sextum gradum distantia a nodis; differentia latitudinum utroque modo assumtarum provenit 1½ minuti, quod quoad quantitatem obscurationis Lunae est insensibile. Circa hunc vero locum crebriores conspiciuntur eclipses, qui et medius est inter nodos et limites extremos. Prope vero ipsos nodos, quando Luna eclipsatur, Ptolemaica et nostra latitudo maxima nihil ingerunt discriminis, ut tute haec omnia facile colligere potes, re melius quam antea considerata, velut etiam ex ascripta delineatione in numeros resoluta perspicuum evadit. Accedit et hoc, quod incertus sim, an latitudo Lunae eodem modo se habeat iuxta novilunia et plenilunia quemadmodum prope quadraturas, ubi hanc divagationem potissimum animadverti; qua de re alias, volente Numine, quae plenior dederit experientia, patefaciam. Frustra itaque eclipses Lunares nobis obtrudis. Videris autem per incuriam aliquam existimasse, etiam circa terminos eclipsium quartam gradus partem in latitudine Lunae a Ptolemaica abundare, non perpendens, id saltem iuxta maximam ab Ecliptica divagationem, quae quadrante circuli a nodis distat, locum habere, et postea versus intersectionem attenuari paulatim, adeo ut iuxta ipsos nodos in unum concursus fiat, et in paucorum graduum distantia, iuxta quos eclipses Lunae contingere possunt, perexiguam vixque perceptibilem subesse differentiam. Autoritas REGIOMONTANI, COPERNICI, WERNERI et aliorum, si qui sunt, hac in parte me non movet, siquidem Astronomiae redintegratio non autoritatibus hominum, sed e certis observationibus et his innixis demonstrationibus derivanda veniat. Neque REGIOMONTANUS unquam latitudinem Lunae maximam coelitus observavit, aut ulla correctione indigere suspicatus est. Nec illa, quae alias in Sole et reliquis sideribus ab ipso et discipulo eius BERNHARDO GUALTERO observata literis produntur, recte se habent, adeo ut sibijpsis non consentiant. COPERNICUS latitudinem Ptolemaicam in Luna maximam invariatam retinuit, neque ipsemet quicquam in hac, an ita se habeat nec ne, coelitus attentare aggressus est, excusans hoc, quod non eam occasionem experiendi sibi fortuna contulerit quam CL. PTOLEMÆO, ob commutationum Lunarium impedimenta in altiori poli situ, quae in Alexandria commode praecaveri poterant, ut patet ex ipsiusmet COPERNICI verbis cap. 15 libri 4 Revolutionum. Nec WERNERUS quippiam circa hanc rem attentavit, quod ex ullo eius scripto probari possit. Sed et is Ptolemaicae animadversioni acquievit, nec ab ullis alijs, quod sciam, quippiam certi hac in parte elaboratum est. Frustra igitur allegatur aliorum authoritas in re nunquam ab illis explorata, licet haec etiam per se in Mathematicis locum non mereatur. PTOLEMÆUM longe commodiorem habuisse opportunitatem latitudinem Lunae maximam perscrutandi, non equidem eo inficias, cum prope verticem et parallaxis (quam COPERNICUS commutationem vocat) et etiam refractionis vitio non laborârit, ideoque ipsemet in eisdem Annotationibus, ubi de Lunae latitudine maxima ago pag. 40 et seq. Ptolemaicam observationem nullatenus improbo, adeo ut eam tunc temporis se recte habuisse iudicem, postea vero usque ad nostram aetatem quarta gradus parte mutatam, quemadmodum Solaris etiam orbitae ab Aequatore digressio interea non invariata permansit. Nolo etiam Parallaticum ipsius instrumentum, quo in hac observatione usus est, in dubium vocare, licet id forte non citra rationem facere possem, ut duobus similibus instrumentis maiori cura et diligentia, quam forsan a PTOLEMÆO praestitum est, fabrefactis, crebra nos docuit experientia. Taceo, quod divisionem partium subtilissimam non adhibuerit, et, quod maxime refert, Lunares radios per foramina transmiserit, ubi in pauculis scrupulis proclivis est aberratio. Accedit et hoc, quod in eo Regulae longioris loco prope perpendicularis contactum, ubi observatio verticem proxime respicit, minima et paene insensibilis anguli per Regulam minorem mobilem mutatio plures divisiones in oblongiori pertranseat, quam alias in remotiore a vertice instrumenti dispositione, quemadmodum ijs, qui haec tractare ipsa experientia edocti sunt, in propatulo est. Neque tamen instrumenti vel observationis vitio ipsum per quartam gradus partem aberrasse propterea dixerim, sed potius revera ad nostra usque tempora adauctam fuisse Lunarem ab Ecliptica digressionem, quae an in omni ad Solem dispositione aeque magna esse possit, necdum tuto affirmârim. Reclamitant etenim nonnullae circa plenilunia observationes, quae Ptolemaicae divagationi quam proxime accedunt, ut ob id inaequalitatem aliquam hîc latere suspicer, de qua tamen nihil certi pronunciare volo, antequam plenius, uti dixi, rem omnem expendero. Licet vero in nostro Horizonte parallaxis Lunae et refractio non parum difficultatis ingerant, quo minus negotium hoc tam expedite, atque in Alexandria a PTOLEMÆO, absolvi queat, tamen haec impedimenta satis praecavere iamdudum crebra observationum praxi didici, ita ut fidem tuto interponere ausim, unum vel alterum ad summum minutum in mea Lunaris latitudinis limitatione nullatenus desiderari.

Vin’ autem aliorum suffragium? siquidem meis solius observationibus non penitus standum arbitreris (quod facile ab ijs admittitur, qui ipsimet sidera non observant, nec quanta diligentia et assiduitate nos huic labori incumbamus norunt) en tibi dabo praestantis Mathematici CHRISTOPHORI ROTHMANNI, Illustriss. Principi GULIELMO Hassiae Landgravio in exercitijs Astronomicis operam navantis, testimonium non obscurum, satisque firmum, quod in literis ante paucas hebdomadas ad me perscripsit. Asserit enim is, se eandem fere latitudinem Lunae maximam instrumentis Principis, quam et ego meis, deprehendisse. Et insuper addit, caput Draconis abesse a Tabulis plus quatuor gradibus. Id vero qua ratione adinvenerit et quomodo comprobare possit, non video. Nam hîc satis evidenter reclamitant eclipses Lunares, cum 4 gradus iuxta nodos ipsos latitudinem Lunae per 21 minuta, id est 2/3 corporis Lunaris partes varient, quod sane in eclipsibus Lunae admodum esset perceptibile, a me tamen, ita se habere, hactenus non compertum. Verum ut eo rectius mentem ROTHMANNI capias, curavi describi particulam quandam literarum ipsius nuper ad me datarum, in qua haec, quae refero de latitudine Lunae maxima et nodis, inter quaedam alia continentur. Misissem tibi integrum literarum exemplar, sed Amanuensis meus illud adeo festinanter, alijs occupatus, transcribere nequivit. Sunt enim prolixiores et aliquot quaterniones adimplent. Atque haec de latitudine Lunae maxima a me constituta sit replicasse satis, quibus si tibi adhuc non satisfactum est, tute instrumenta idonea nullique errori obnoxia construi cura, et coelum ipsum inspicere examinareque disce, priusquam aliorum inventa crebro vigilatis noctibus ex ipsa coelesti observatione et non in vaporario ex chartis depromta sub dubitationem vocare praesumseris.

Postea recapitulatione quadam uteris eorum, quae sequentibus capitibus prioris partis comprehendo; quod autem ibidem oblique insinuas, me asseverare, Cometam circulum descripsisse, quamvis interdum quinque aut paucioribus scrupulis ab exacta peripheria observationes dissentiant, id vero quid est aliud, quam nodum in scirpo quaerere? Nam ista perexigua differentia, quae quina scrupula non excedit, imo rarissime ea attingit et utplurimum in uno vel altero scrupulo sibi constat, non est digna, ut in mentionem veniat, cum sit pene imperceptibilis; et aëris diversitas applicatioque Cometae ad Horizontem tempore quarundam observationum propior, quarundam vero remotior, hanc tantillulam diversitatem facilime ingerere potuit, ut nihil dicam paucula scrupula in locis fixarum, quibus usus sum, aliter se habere, quam a nobis iuxta veterem animadversionem assumta sunt, velut patet ex Additamento, fini secundi capitis apposito, de quo etiam prius dixi. Hanc etiam non magni momenti discrepantiam in ductu circuli Cometae in ipso libro suo loco satis superque excusavi, ut non opus fuerit tam exilia et ad rei scopum non pertinentia neque quicquam nostrae asseverationi apud candidos et peritos derogantia inculcare. Si quanto labore et industria ad eam ipsam, quam nactus sum, praecisionem opus fuerit, ipsa experientia satis perspectum haberes, utique de minimis et sensum pene omnem effugientibus non otiose disputares.

Dehinc ambiguitatem quandam moves in eo, quod aliquoties asseverem, omnes Cometas in aetherea regione supra Lunam versari, et LYCOSTHENEM mihi obijcis, eumque refers in quodam loco sui libri de Ostentis prodidisse, quosdam Cometas conflagrantes incendium in subiecta Terra excitasse, atque inde aliquos sublunares fuisse, et seeundum mentem Peripateticorum ignes in supremo aëre accensos extitisse, tacite inferre conaris. At ego, perlustrato toto illo LYCOSTHENIS libello, nihil eiuscemodi in eo invenire potui. Imo etiamsi tale quid illic reperiretur, attendendum nihilominus esset, quod isti autores Historici non Mathematice rem tractent, sed generaliter de Ostentis scribentes saepenumero discrimen inter Cometas revera in coelo constitutos et faces atque lampadas ignesque volantes similiaque aëris infralunaris ignita meteora minime faciant. Fieri itaque quam facilime potuit, ut ex horum ignitorum et sublunarium meteororum alicubi in Terram delapsis incendium quoddam in competenti materia olim excitatum sit, quod tamen Cometae alicuius conflagrationi vel flammarum excitatam inconvenienter attribuitur. Hi enim non ardent, neque ignem edunt, verum non multum dissimili ratione a reliquis stellis lumen sibi insitum vibrant, sive id a Sole, sive aliunde obtineant. Quod autem materiam omnium Cometarum aetheream ex eadem, in qua versantur, mundi regione constantem asseverem, non caret suis certis rationibus, velut in Epilogo totius operis patebit, ubi ex professo de materia et procreatione Cometarum tum aliorum opiniones sub disquisitionem iustam revocabo, tum etiam meam sententiam aperte exponam, siquidem in hoc libro et reliquis etiam duobus leviter hac de re ago, eo quod ista Physica consideratio non competenter proponitur, priusquam omnia, quae iuxta Astronomicam demonstrationem tum in Nova Stella, tum etiam aliquibus Cometis postea visis, a me pertractata fuerint. Illic etiam certis rationibus ostendam, quam absurda sit haec tua et aliorum quorundam opinio, Cometas, etsi in coelo ipso curriculum suum absolvant, nihilominus e materia elementari sursum in aethera vi siderum sublata constare posse. Quot enim et quanta huic opinioni tum ex ipsis Cometarum apparentijs, tum e veriore Physica repugnent, ibidem manifestum reddam. Nec quod intereant Cometae, huic tuae sententiae suffragium praebet. Nam quae post mundi primam creationem miraculose aliquando in natura, praesertim coelesti, eveniunt, ea etiam ante universalem rerum creatarum abolitionem interire consentaneum evadit. Tycho Brahe to Heinrich Brucaeus.

You have at last broken that deep silence in which, all summer long, you forbore to send me any letter, most loving Brucaeus; and since now finally, as though roused from a deep sleep, you have visited me again with your learned letter, this is reason for me to forgive the delay hitherto committed and to hold you excused. In it you treat with me, as it were professedly, of those matters contained in the second book of my Aetherial Phenomena [Aethereorum Phaenomenωn], which was conveyed to you last spring by one of my household who was setting out from here into Germany; and you show that you read carefully through what is found in its earlier part, the later part being lightly touched upon. It seemed good to you, therefore, to reply in this manner to the things you wrote to me about these matters. That you approve and commend what is contained in the earlier portion of that volume, for the certainty and fit of its observations and demonstrations — and especially that you affirm that the invention of my novel hypothesis [neotericae meae Hypotheseos] concerning the arrangement of the celestial revolutions pleases you greatly — is most gratifying to me. Indeed, as regards the constitution of that hypothesis, I had suspected that it would not appeal to your temperament [Genio], seeing that you are otherwise so vehement and steadfast a conserver and champion of the authority of the ancients — so much so that you sometimes seem to attribute too much to them, and to detract not a little from surer reasonings and from experience itself; but here it has turned out otherwise than I supposed. For this sketch [Hypotyposin] of ours of the world-system — never discerned by the ancients, nor even devised before by any of the moderns — you not only approve with your vote, but you also judge it to be preferred above the rest, because, unlike those proposed by Ptolemy and Copernicus, it admits no such absurdities. But that you may the more rightly understand by what reasoning I arrived, some years ago, at the invention of this hypothesis, I send you a copy of a letter recently written by me to that most renowned and learned man, Master Caspar Peucer — a letter in which I thought I should reply to certain doubts of his (which he had written to that most noble man, Master Heinrich Rantzau, who had communicated to him a bare outline of his hypothesis excerpted from my book — and of whose words, together with the ambiguous opinion of another certain mathematician on the same matter, I here furnish you a copy) — so that on this occasion I might confer more fully with so great a philosopher and mathematician about this whole astronomical business. From these things you will perceive very many matters bearing peculiarly not only [upon] the reasonings and fuller explanation of this hypothesis, but also upon the whole astronomical enterprise, which, I think, will not displease your temperament. But once you have read this very letter through — or even, if it please you, had it transcribed — I should wish it sent back to me, since I desire to keep this copy, and I have not now the leisure to have it written out again. As to what you desire concerning the restoration [instauratio] of astronomy — that everything founded upon this hypothesis and reduced to numbers from sure geometrical observations should correspond exactly to the phenomena — you will also find, in this letter sent to Master Peucer, in what order and with how great an effort I have proposed to approach and undertake so great a matter, with the favour of the Maker of Heaven itself and of the Universe. Nor have you any reason to fear that the immensity of the labour, the difficulty of so arduous a matter, the long duration of time required, the magnitude of the expense, or any other trouble will, with that same supreme Deity breathing upon our endeavours, deter me from consecrating to a grateful posterity (as I hope) the restoration of so divine and sublime an art with that precision (let it be said without envy) with which it has hitherto been drained dry [i.e., accomplished] by no one, and from leaving it, complete in all its parts [omnibus suis numeris], so far indeed as can be performed by one man.

But enough of these things in this place. There is, however, one thing about which I must complain to you in this matter. A certain man of all-too-brazen brow — whom I judge unworthy to be named here, and who moreover, wherever he has been or is, betrays himself for what he is — has not blushed to arrogate to himself this invention of my hypotheses, and to assert this in a public writing published at Strasbourg [Argentinae] this year. Whether he filched this too, along with the rest that he secretly copied at my house — while, in the service of that most noble man Erik Lange, he was for some days in his retinue — or by what other means he drew off its arrangement here, let himself know; and my students showed [me] a certain similar [drawing] then lying among the waste wrappings [inter segestria; see apparatus], which had been less aptly delineated. Certainly how fraudulently and craftily this man acted, the rest of what he copied here from my [papers], without my knowledge — some part of which I have recovered, written out in his own hand — sufficiently prove, even though I do not find that hypothesis among those sheets; but he could have hidden it elsewhere or retained it in memory, since perhaps the convenience of drawing was not at hand. My household students know that I had established that hypothesis several years ago now and shown it to them. Nay more: this book on the Comet of the year [15]77, although it bears on its face an edition made in this year [15]88, yet all who are with me know that it was printed in my own press in the year [15]87, but deliberately marked one year later, that on account of its novelty it might be the more welcome. And that in this book the same hypothesis as mine is generally explained, you are not unaware, as is clear from what has already been said. But that man, who lately would claim it for himself, signally betrayed that he does not properly understand it — far less that he is its author — when he depicts and feigns the Martian orb as totally encircling the Solar [orb], which he could have learned to be impossible even from Copernicus alone, merely glanced at in passing. And how, I ask, will he ever construct new hypotheses analogous to the celestial revolutions, who is not furnished with exquisite observations of many years, so as thence to investigate skilfully what is wanting in the hypotheses hitherto in use, and how that very thing may be restored? Without these supports, to boast of some new invention is frivolous and downright ridiculous, since he has nothing whereby to prove, by the appearances themselves, why — leaving the earlier ones aside — one [ought] thus to innovate, and how, by manifold observations, he may confirm that this [innovation] more rightly agrees with the celestial appearances. But away with those follies, to which no one, unless he does not understand the matter, will lend credence.

I return to your letter, intending to examine the things it contains thereafter, in order, as I have determined. You seem to call somewhat into doubt the correction of the places of the fixed stars proposed by me in chapter 2 from the declination of a star and its transit across the meridian, on the ground that an exquisite knowledge of time and the Sun's place can scarcely be established precisely. Although this is in some way not said without reason — and is asserted by me too, nearly so, towards the end of that same chapter, in the Addition beginning at folio 31 — nevertheless, when due diligence is applied, and certain means that exhibit the smallest particles of time by an even movement, as far as can be done, of which we have not a few ready to hand, this method too deserves a place without any very sensible error, especially if it be examined by frequent reiteration until the results obtained differ not very sensibly, as is plain from the earlier observations made in that manner, which are set before the eyes on page 30, if they be compared with others, reiterated and obtained by a different method, by way of the Sun, with the star Venus intermediating, [referred] to the fixed stars, which are shown on page 33. For the difference is very small — inasmuch as, where it is greatest, it does not exceed the tenth part of one degree, and in six or seven stars scarcely reaches three minutes; a precision which truly was not extracted by the ancients, so that I cannot sufficiently marvel that, with that smallness and scarcity of instruments which I then used, even along this very road so slippery for going astray, I nevertheless aimed so close to the mark. But a more exact method, liable to no ambiguity, of verifying the places of the fixed stars, I briefly indicated in the same Addition: namely, by distances taken from the Sun to Venus appearing by day, and at night related from her to the [fixed] stars. About which matter I treat more fully in the preceding book, and by several observations accurately made in that manner — both morning and evening ones — I restore the places of the fixed stars to their true and precise distance from the vernal equinox; and I demonstrate that this can be so, and not otherwise, from the observations themselves, having noted also their true latitudes, in which no less than in the longitudes there has hitherto been too much error. There too is set first the most exact restoration of the Sun's course to our times, since upon it all else depends. There the place of the apogee and the true quantity of the eccentricity are geometrically demonstrated from several sure observations of preceding years, diligently obtained from the heavens with several and not-fallacious instruments, with a Table appended of the mean motions, corrected too, and of the prosthaphaereses [prostaphaereseωn] extracted from the eccentricity found — so that concerning the Sun's motion precisely agreeing with the appearances no further doubt remains with me. I shall perhaps subjoin some things also about the lunar course, likewise subtly restored, when through the observations of several years I shall more fully unravel its perplexed path, both as to length [longitude] and breadth [latitude]. I see that you are still ruminating on those things which the most learned Gemma Frisius attempted, in his little book on the cross-staff [de Radio Astronomico], as to the marking of the fixed stars upon some globe, and afterwards the designation of the equator and ecliptic upon the same. But I, for certain reasons, judge it more fitting to be done thus: that the equator and ecliptic be first arranged on the globe and distributed into the smallest particles, and afterwards, according to their requirement, the places of the fixed stars be set down and verified in various ways from here and there, so that they fall back into one and the same point. By distances taken by means of the cross-staff, however, as Gemma handed down and as it was afterwards attempted by many, this business will never be rightly accomplished. For it is impossible, by the cross-staff [Radium] — with however great a length it be endowed and with whatever subtlety divided, and even reiterating the observation very often — to discover the exact intervals of the stars to the very second [in ipso scrupulo; here read as arc-second — see apparatus], as we shall show elsewhere by its causes. And therefore I was the first to devise sextants supremely fit for this office, and everywhere yielding the true distances of the stars within the same minute, or even within an aliquot part of this, however often the observation be repeated; with which, for several years now, I have happily made use, with precise subtlety, in investigating the distances of the heavenly bodies. Moreover, the stars are set down on a globe in vain — by whatever instrument they be at last derived from the heavens — unless it be very large, such as the one I possess, six feet in diameter, which is everywhere of brass [orichalcicus], and which exhibits distinctly the division of the degrees into single sixty-minute [intervals] by transversal line-rulings, in the manner customary with us, and which is moreover endowed with perfect roundness. On this I have resolved to record the places of all the fixed stars, derived from the heaven itself by various instruments, of which a great and chief part is already held by me ready beforehand. Wherefore you have no reason to doubt that I am of my own accord working, with all possible diligence, at those things which conduce to the precise restoration of the fixed stars — so that there would have been no need of any admonition, or of a recapitulation of those things which are proposed by me in chapter 2.

Furthermore, you hold suspect the maximum latitude of the Moon, which I have found to be greater by a quarter of one degree than the dicta of Ptolemy, and you assail it by means of the magnitude of lunar eclipses and the calculation corresponding to it. You summon up moreover the authority of Regiomontanus, Copernicus, Werner, and other distinguished mathematicians — that they perpetually retained the Ptolemaic maximum latitude of the Moon at precisely 5 degrees — whom you do not doubt to have ascertained with great diligence, from the heavens, that it is so. You reckon, moreover, that the Ptolemaic latitude is free from every fault on account of the opportuneness of the place of his observation, which conjoined the Moon — requiring [being at] its maximum northern latitude near the summer tropic — as close as possible to the vertex [zenith], the impediments of parallaxes and refractions being on that account removed, etc. That I may treat with you more fully of these things, the truth itself demands it. Know then that the maximum latitude of the Moon was established by me as greater by 15 minutes than the Ptolemaic, not rashly nor without frequent and exact observation. For that it really is so, especially near the quadratures, I have been taught from the heaven itself by many trials and by observations in no wise fallacious — except that I have sometimes found it greater by one or another minute [scrupulo], and at times a little smaller; and if some other inequality underlies [it], as I shall not deny, I shall unravel it more thoroughly. Nor do the observations of eclipses detract anything from my assertion that can fall under the senses. For although the maximum latitude be established as greater by 15 minutes than that which is in use and has long been received, yet this difference, around those limits where eclipses are wont to occur near the nodes, imports nothing that can be perceived. For at a distance of 12 degrees from the nodes — within which limit, most nearly, lunar eclipses must occur — the difference between the Ptolemaic latitude and ours comes to only three first-minutes [arc-minutes], which cause only a single digit [digitum] in the obscuration of the Moon — and that scarcely perceptible, on account of the extremity of the Earth's shadow not precisely falling under the senses; and the very times of eclipses have hitherto not become so certain that this matter could in any way harm our discovery — as very many lunar eclipses observed by us most diligently in some past years, near the sixth degree of distance from the nodes, have taught us: the difference of the latitudes assumed in both ways comes out at 1½ minutes, which, as regards the quantity of the Moon's obscuration, is insensible. But around this place, which is also the midpoint between the nodes and the extreme limits, the more frequent eclipses are observed. Near the very nodes, however, when the Moon is eclipsed, the Ptolemaic maximum latitude and ours bring in no difference, as you yourself can easily gather all this, the matter being better considered than before — as is also plain from the appended delineation, resolved into numbers. There is added this too, that I am uncertain whether the latitude of the Moon behaves the same way near the new and full moons as near the quadratures, where I have chiefly observed this wandering [divagationem]; about which matter, God willing, I shall disclose elsewhere what fuller experience shall have given. In vain, therefore, do you thrust lunar eclipses upon me. But you seem, through some carelessness, to have reckoned that even around the limits of eclipses the Moon's latitude exceeds the Ptolemaic by a quarter of a degree — not weighing that this holds only near the maximum wandering from the ecliptic, which is distant a quadrant of the circle from the nodes, and afterwards is gradually attenuated towards the intersection, so much so that near the very nodes there comes a coincidence into one [value], and that at the distance of a few degrees — near which lunar eclipses can occur — there underlies a very small and scarcely perceptible difference. The authority of Regiomontanus, Copernicus, Werner, and others (if there are any) does not in this part move me, since the renewal [redintegratio] of astronomy is to be derived not from the authorities of men, but from sure observations and the demonstrations resting upon these. Nor did Regiomontanus ever observe the maximum latitude of the Moon from the heavens, or suspect that it needed any correction. Nor are those things which are reported in letters as observed elsewhere, on the Sun and the rest of the heavenly bodies, by him and his disciple Bernhard Walther, correct — so much so that they do not agree with themselves. Copernicus retained the Ptolemaic maximum latitude in the Moon unaltered, nor did he himself attempt to test from the heavens whether this is so or not, excusing this on the ground that fortune did not grant him the same opportunity of testing as it did to the renowned Ptolemy — because of the impediments of the lunar parallaxes [commutationum] in the higher situation of the pole, which at Alexandria could be conveniently guarded against — as is plain from Copernicus's own words, chapter 15 of book 4 of the Revolutions. Nor did Werner attempt anything about this matter that can be proved from any writing of his. But he too acquiesced in the Ptolemaic observation, nor, so far as I know, has anything certain been worked out in this part by any others. In vain, therefore, is the authority of others alleged in a matter never explored by them — although this [authority], even of itself, does not deserve a place in mathematics. That Ptolemy had a far more convenient opportunity of scrutinizing the maximum latitude of the Moon I do not for my part deny, since near the vertex he did not labour under the fault either of parallax (which Copernicus calls commutatio) or of refraction; and therefore I myself, in those same Annotations where I treat of the Moon's maximum latitude, page 40 and following, in no way disapprove the Ptolemaic observation — so much so that I judge it to have been correct at that time, but afterwards, down to our age, to have changed by a quarter of a degree, just as the departure of the solar orbit from the equator has likewise not remained unaltered in the meantime. Nor do I wish to call into doubt his Parallactic instrument [Parallaticum instrumentum], which he used in this observation — although I could perhaps do so not without reason, as frequent experience has taught us, with two similar instruments built with greater care and diligence than was perhaps furnished by Ptolemy. I pass over that he did not employ the subtlest division of the parts, and — what matters most — that he transmitted the lunar rays through apertures [foramina], where in a few minutes [scrupulis] aberration is inclined [to occur]. There is added this too: that in the place of the longer Rule near the perpendicular contact, where the observation looks most nearly toward the vertex, the smallest and almost insensible change of the angle by the smaller movable Rule passes through more divisions on the longer [scale] than otherwise in a disposition of the instrument more remote from the vertex — as is manifest to those who have been taught by experience itself to handle these things. Nor yet should I on that account say that he erred by a quarter of a degree through a fault of the instrument or of the observation, but rather that the lunar departure from the ecliptic has in truth been increased down to our times — whether it can be equally great in every disposition relative to the Sun, I should not yet safely affirm. For some observations near the full moons cry out against it, ones which approach the Ptolemaic wandering very closely, so that on that account I suspect some inequality lurks here — about which, however, I am unwilling to pronounce anything certain before I have, as I said, more fully weighed the whole matter. Although in our horizon the Moon's parallax and refraction bring in not a little difficulty, so that this business cannot be accomplished so readily as at Alexandria by Ptolemy, yet I have long since learned, by frequent practice of observations, to guard sufficiently against these impediments — so that I would dare safely to pledge my faith that in my determination of the Moon's latitude one minute, or at most two [unum vel alterum ad summum minutum], is in no way wanting [i.e., the error is no more than a minute or two].

But do you want the suffrage of others? — since you would think one should not rely wholly on my observations alone (which is readily granted by those who do not themselves observe the heavenly bodies, nor know with what diligence and assiduity we apply ourselves to this labour) — lo, I will give you the testimony, neither obscure nor insufficiently firm, of that excellent mathematician Christoph Rothmann — who devotes his effort to astronomical exercises for the Most Illustrious Prince Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse — which he wrote to me in a letter a few weeks ago. For he asserts that he detected almost the same maximum latitude of the Moon with the Prince's instruments as I [did] with mine. And he adds, moreover, that the Head of the Dragon [the ascending node] departs from the Tables by more than four degrees. But by what reasoning he found this out, and how he can prove it, I do not see. For here the lunar eclipses cry out clearly enough, since 4 degrees near the very nodes vary the Moon's latitude by 21 minutes — that is, 2/3 parts of the lunar body — which truly would be quite perceptible in lunar eclipses, yet has not hitherto been found by me to be so. But that you may the more rightly grasp Rothmann's mind, I have had a certain portion of his letter recently sent to me transcribed, in which these things which I report concerning the Moon's maximum latitude and the nodes are contained among certain others. I would have sent you the entire copy of the letter, but my amanuensis, occupied with other things, could not transcribe it in such haste. For they are rather long-winded and fill several gatherings [quaterniones]. And let it suffice to have replied this much concerning the maximum latitude of the Moon as established by me; with which, if you are still not satisfied, do you yourself take care to construct suitable instruments liable to no error, and learn to inspect and examine the heaven itself, before you presume to call into doubt the discoveries of others — [discoveries] drawn from celestial observation itself, on many a wakeful night, and not produced out of papers in a steam-room [in vaporario].

Afterwards you make use of a certain recapitulation of the things which I comprehend in the following chapters of the earlier part; but as for what you there obliquely insinuate — that I assert the Comet to have described a circle, although the observations sometimes disagree by five or fewer minutes from the exact circumference — what is that but to look for a knot in a bulrush [nodum in scirpo quaerere; i.e., to make difficulties where there are none]? For that very small difference, which does not exceed five minutes — nay, very rarely reaches it, and for the most part holds steady within one or another minute — is not worthy to come into mention, since it is almost imperceptible; and the diversity of the air, and the application [position] of the Comet relative to the Horizon — nearer at the time of some observations, more remote at others — could most easily have brought in this tiny diversity; to say nothing of the fact that a few minutes in the places of the fixed stars which I used stand otherwise than they were assumed by us according to the old observation, as is plain from the Addendum attached to the end of the second chapter, of which I also spoke before. This discrepancy too, of no great moment, in the tracing of the Comet's circle, I excused fully and more than enough in its proper place in the book itself — so that there was no need to dwell upon things so slight and not pertaining to the matter's aim, nor in any way detracting, among the candid and skilled, from our assertion. If you had thoroughly perceived, by experience itself, with how much labour and industry there was need to reach that very precision which I attained, you would surely not idly dispute about the smallest things, and ones that escape almost all the senses.

Next you raise a certain ambiguity in this, that I several times assert all comets to dwell in the aetherial region above the Moon; and you object Lycosthenes against me, and you report that he, in a certain place of his book On Portents [de Ostentis], set down that certain comets, blazing, kindled a conflagration on the Earth below — and thence you try tacitly to infer that some were sublunar, and that, according to the mind of the Peripatetics, they were fires kindled in the uppermost air. But I, having surveyed that whole little book of Lycosthenes, could find nothing of the kind in it. Nay, even if some such thing were found there, it would nonetheless have to be attended to that those Historical authors do not treat the matter mathematically, but, writing generally about portents, very often make no distinction at all between comets really established in the heaven and torches and lamps and flying fires and similar kindled meteors of the infralunar air. It could therefore most easily have happened that, from some of these kindled and sublunar meteors fallen somewhere onto the Earth, some conflagration was once kindled in suitable material — which, however, is unsuitably attributed to the burning of some comet, or to flames kindled [by it]. For these [comets] do not burn, nor do they emit fire, but in a manner not much unlike the rest of the stars they dart forth a light implanted in themselves, whether they obtain it from the Sun or from elsewhere. But that I assert the matter of all comets to be aetherial, consisting of the same [region] of the world in which they dwell, is not without its sure reasons, as will be plain in the Epilogue of the whole work, where I shall professedly recall both the opinions of others about the matter and procreation of comets to a just inquiry, and also openly set forth my own opinion — since in this book and in the other two as well I treat of this matter only lightly, because that physical consideration is not fittingly proposed before all the things which, according to astronomical demonstration, both in the New Star and also in some comets seen afterwards, have been treated by me. There too I shall show by sure reasons how absurd is this opinion of yours and of certain others — that comets, even though they complete their course in the heaven itself, can nevertheless consist of elementary matter lifted up into the aether by the power of the stars. For how many and how great [arguments] oppose this opinion, both from the very appearances of comets and from a truer physics, I shall there make manifest. Nor does the fact that comets perish lend any suffrage to this opinion of yours. For those things which sometimes come about miraculously in nature, especially the celestial, after the first creation of the world, it follows fittingly that they should also perish before the universal abolition of created things. But why the New Star and comets, once arisen, do not endure perpetually, nor always retain the same magnitude or colour, I shall bring forth the reasons in that same Epilogue of the whole work. But that which the ancient Epicureans and certain other philosophers held — that the heavenly bodies are nourished by humours [vapours] — is in itself plainly false and ridiculous, far less that anything certain about comets can thence be proved. You seem to me to be about shortly to degenerate, without your own knowledge, into a Paracelsist. For he too maintained the same error of the ancients — although he thought that the stars are nourished not entirely from vapours, but from a certain celestial matter — so much so that he did not fear to assert that all meteors are nothing other than the excrements of the stars. For it seemed fitting that those things which need fodder and nourishment should also have their excretions; but this speculation is alien to all truth. That different comets arise and are destroyed from time to time, and that some of them stay in one place while others are mobile, and that among those that move, some direct their course in the order [following the signs], others against the order of the signs, and that some of them seek the North, others the South — all this proves nothing, [namely] that they consist of elementary matter. For these very things, and more, can belong to them as established in the heaven itself out of celestial matter, as we shall discuss more amply in that same Epilogue. But what you add, that some keep a certain order in their motion, while others wander without order, is excessive. For I judge that all comets have kept a sure order, and have not slipped suddenly from a slower motion into a faster, or from this into that, nor coursed by a wandering, inconstant, or tumultuous (as Seneca calls it) leading. For in all the comets that have been noted by me I have detected a constant and proportional tenor of motion, just as also in the rest that were diligently observed from the heavens by our predecessor mathematicians. For as for the fact that certain Historians have perhaps attributed a wandering and inconstant course to some [comets], we make nothing of it. For the same thing could appear to them, and to others like them not understanding the astronomical matter, in the other planets too, nay, in the fixed stars themselves. Nor is a ratified truth gathered from uncertain testimonies. Nay, even mathematicians not of the common sort attributed a certain motion to the New Star, although it stood perpetually in the same place, and they did not hesitate to assert that comets seen afterwards completed their course by a wandering and disordered leading — as both Cornelius Gemma and Thaddaeus, both excellent mathematicians, inconsiderately admitted in this very comet (not to speak now of the rest), as is indicated by us in its proper place in the book itself. You see, therefore — and you will see more fully elsewhere — that from all those little reasonings which you adduce, what you intend does not follow: that comets are generated from elementary matter, whether above or below the Moon.

You subjoin afterwards a still further unsuitable hesitation, or rather a less-than-deliberate disapproval of our hypothesis concerning the comet of the year [15]77, of which this book sent [to you] treats. For you reckon, from the fact that I posit the Comet to be moved around the Sun, so that it could depart from it by at most a sixth of a circle [60°], that it is a consequence that, if ever it were in the part of its circle nearest to the earth, it would then touch the Earth — since the semidiameter subtends a sixth of a circle, as is plain from the elements of geometry. But here you seem to me to have weighed the whole matter too drowsily. For although the Comet wandered from the Sun, the centre of its orb, by 60 parts [degrees] of a great circle in the heaven — as regards our view of it from the earth — yet that imaginary orb was not so great that it could in any way touch the Earth; nay, its lowest part stood nearly three times more remote from here than the lunar abodes. Which is most easily plain from the appended figure, in which that orb which the Comet described around the Sun is represented by DBEG, whose centre C is near the Sun, but let A be the Earth, on which we dwell, and whence we behold the celestial things. The maximum digression of the Comet from the Sun is at B, to which place let CB and AB be drawn, meeting at the tangent of the circle. There is therefore, in the triangle ABC, a right angle at B (as you know). But the angle CAB is of 60 parts [degrees] according to observation. But let the side CA be presupposed, according to Copernicus, at the Sun's mean distance from the Earth, of 1142 earth-semidiameters. Therefore, in a right-angled rectilinear triangle, one [angle] and one side being given, the side BC will not be unknown — of 988 earth-semidiameters — which is equal to CE itself, since both are from the centre of the same circle. And therefore, CE of 988 earth-semidiameters being subtracted from CA of 1142 of the same, as I just said, there will remain EA of 154 semidiameters. But EA is the nearest distance by which the Comet could approach the earth, even if it had completed the whole circumference of its circle several times. And since the nearest distance of the Moon from the earth is not unsuitably set by Copernicus at about 52 earth-semidiameters, it appears that the Comet, even when most closely approaching the Earth, was nearly three times more remote from it than is the nearest approach of the Moon to the same; and that according to Copernican reckonings, which are truer than the Ptolemaic (by which the Moon comes out still nearer to us). But if it please [anyone] to resolve this approach of the Comet into German miles — taking for each earth-semidiameter 860 of our own or German miles (which is not very far from the truth) — there will leap forth 132,440 miles, by which the Comet, in its nearest situation, could be brought near to us. Is this to touch the Earth? Although, however, I have not here scrutinized everything quite so scrupulously — as indeed there was no need — since I took no account of the apogee and the eccentricity (because this matters little in this place), yet a greater nearness to the Earth can scarcely be given, as is indicated on page 297 in the book itself — although there, by the carelessness of the Printer, 145 is found instead of 154, the two latter characters being transposed; which error, however, is detected among the typographical faults at the end of the book.

It appears, therefore, how inconsiderately you detracted from our hypothesis, by asserting that the Comet's circle touched the earth — which error indeed would have been far too gross, since I was unwilling even to admit its sublunar course, and demonstrated the contrary throughout the whole book. The very distances from the Earth in the penultimate column of its Table — which derives the numbers in various respects from the hypothesis — could have warned you that the Comet, by the presupposed hypothesis, was never made so low as you suspect; since on 9 November its remoteness is set at 173 earth-semidiameters, and indeed at that time it was not so far from its nearest approach to us, so that it could scarcely have been brought nearer by 20 semidiameters. Do you yourself, then, ponder with yourself how undeservedly you cast so great an absurdity upon us. But you make use of a certain skirmishing and hypothetical caution, saying: If I rightly grasp your opinion. Yet certainly my opinion is not so obscure, nor so sluggishly and intricately declared, that it cannot be perceived by you, so great a mathematician — especially since you had my book with you for nearly half a year; and if, owing to other occupations and cares, any residue of doubt had remained in your mind, it would have been better to have suspended judgement, rather than to have disapproved our [findings] so enormously and beyond all reason. But I easily see whence this not-light error crept upon you. For you unsuitably supposed that the digression of the Comet from the Sun occurs in that circuit which passes through the centre of its circle and is at so great a distance from us as the Sun itself — and that at F — whereas the tangent, at which the maximum apparent digression occurs to us, falls far lower, near B. In a like manner too Girolamo Cardano [Hieronymus Caroanus] was deluded, when at the beginning of the second book On the Variety of Things [de varietate rerum] he tries to demonstrate that the epicycle of Venus, by the ancients' hypothesis, is so large that it touches the Earth, if Venus [♀] near the Perigee be understood to be distant from the Sun, by reckoning of the epicycle, by 45 parts [degrees]. For there too he takes no account of that place in the epicycle where the tangent is made by the line going out from the earth, but inconsiderately weaves through his demonstration by way of the very diameter of the epicycle — not weighing that proportion of the epicycle to the eccentric which was explored by the ancients according to this hypothesis, which, namely, is about 43 to 60. But great men too are sometimes liable to great errors.

You assert that you have not yet unrolled [read through] the second part of my book, and have read very little in it — so much so that you have only cursorily run through the animadversions against Maestlin. Nay, as far as I can perceive from your letter, you reckon that effort to have been useless or less necessary, by which in the second portion of that book I called the dicta of others about this comet to the anvil [sub incudem revocavi; i.e., subjected to scrutiny] — since the things demonstrated by me geometrically in the earlier part can be torn down by no one. But I judge that one should think far otherwise. For the observations and demonstrations of others, if they are correct, lend great suffrage to the truth — especially when administered in different places by different persons. For although the things which are demonstrated geometrically stand correct, yet, since observations made by mechanical means are not everywhere rightly performed, it comes about that the conclusions themselves, however much they make a pretext of geometrical demonstrations, are very often vain. Thus you do not fear to call into doubt our maximum latitude of the Moon (as also the change of the latitude of the fixed stars according to the varied departure of the ecliptic from the equator, in certain other letters previously sent to me). But when you have understood, from Rothmann's observations in a remote place and verified with other instruments, that the matter behaves the same way, perhaps you will more easily acquiesce in my discoveries — although they are of themselves so ratified that they need no external testimony. And so, both for this and for other necessary reasons, in the first member [section] of the second part I weighed their writings, by which the fact that this Comet was aetherial is fittingly proved together with me. Lest anyone suspect that the animadversions of all the others were wholly alien from my discoveries — which, however, of itself I did not much care about, provided only the truth itself be safe, which is indeed assailed by the opinions of men, but not stormed [conquered], and which also lies hidden, beyond the grasp of most, in the very inner recesses and marrow of things. But in the latter member I overturned the reasonings of the rest, who held the contrary, so that, their contradictions removed and refuted, the free truth might shine forth of itself. And it was by no light fallacies [paralogisms] that four men of great authority, from different places, agreed in this — to prove the Comet so near to the earth that it would produce a parallax of 5 or 6 degrees, and therefore [to be] far below the Moon, scarcely 8 or 9 earth-semidiameters remote from here — as they also pretended demonstrations from sure geometrical observations, by which they would persuade even some learned mathematician, if he had not circumspectly weighed everything, that the matter is so; so much so that your Aristotelian Philosopher Erastus did not hesitate to cite the suffrages of the mathematicians on this comet, asserting that, according to the dogmas of the Peripatetics, it was concreted in the elementary world from kindled fumes — because certain of them tried to demonstrate its parallax greater than that which is in the Moon. Thus does truth often suffer violence, unless the things that are false be skilfully removed. Furthermore, many things about this whole business are contained in the second part, of which there was no occasion to discourse in the earlier. Read through, therefore, at your leisure, this latter section of the book diligently, and then at last bring forth your judgement about it. You say that you made rough observations of that same Comet, by which, according to Regiomontanus's dicta, you found it to have been by no means sublunar, and that you had earlier indicated this to Wittich as he was setting out to me. But when he was here, he held no such view, but was thoroughly imbued with the opinion of the Peripatetics — that all comets wander in the air below the Moon, and there dry out [exhale] from fumes drawn up from the Earth. And therefore he disapproved the contrary dicta of Maestlin and others — which opinion, however, he changed before he returned from here into Germany, because from my observations — even on that comet which appeared in the year [15]80, at which time he was with me — he was instructed that the matter stands otherwise. Yet I recall that you yourself once told me, when I was passing through Rostock on my travels, that you could find no parallax in that Comet — imitating the mind of Regiomontanus, and exploring the whole matter by azimuths and altitudes with intervening time — and at that time you showed me the instrument with which you undertook this observation; which, however, seemed to me to be such that, if by another [instrument] you had found a parallax of 10 or 15 degrees, it would not on that account follow that this Comet was elementary — for greater diligence is required in taking azimuths and altitudes together than has hitherto been perceived by many, especially in so subtle a business, where the greatest things are drawn out from the smallest, and an exquisite knowledge of time within five or ten seconds, such as was necessary, could by no means have been sufficiently perceived by you. For it is not investigated precisely enough by the altitudes of the stars, as I have advised elsewhere. And on that account it came about that you could in no way complete the operation, as you yourself then admitted, according to Regiomontanus's prescription.

At last you conclude that there was no need to undertake so much labour about a matter never recurring, so as to demonstrate so accurately all the circumstances of the motions — provided only it be established to us at least whether comets dwell in the aetherial or in the elementary region, since about this matter the chief controversy is moved among philosophers. And by the instability and evanescence of haloes [Halonum] and similar meteors, you try to infer that comets too need no very diligent observation — so much so that you do not hesitate to subjoin that writings of that kind are extinguished together with the meteor itself. But how sincerely and rightly these things are brought forth by you, let it be the judgement of others. Certainly Rothmann thinks far otherwise about my labours in accurately unravelling this business of the comets, as may be seen even from the portion of his letter recently sent to me, which I said I was sending along [with this]. Otherwise too Scultetus (as is plain from a particle of his epistle written to me not long ago, which I have also had transcribed for you) judges about this matter; and he reports that a certain Monau — a learned man in Silesia, but unknown to me — wrote to him, that he [Scultetus, or perhaps Monau himself — ambiguous; see apparatus] might be willing to urge the publication of the other volumes at my [press], although he likewise complains of the ingratitude of this age — that it is not worthy that solid truth be communicated to it in such matters. For scarcely anything is now so well said or done that it is not depraved by cavilling. But come, let us see whether our labours in this part are deservedly so disparaged by you or by anyone else — as though, on account of the evanescence of the subject of which they treat, they were useless and of no great or lasting moment. This question about the situation of comets — whether it be aetherial or elementary, and how they are generated — is neither new nor slight, but for many centuries past has been agitated long and much, with great effort and the subtlest argumentations, by the most ancient philosophers long before Aristotle (most of whom also thought more rightly than he about this matter), as is sufficiently plain even from Seneca's book which he left On Comets. Nor yet, down to these times, has it been thoroughly investigated what comets are and where they are procreated, because the ancients, and very many of the moderns too, undertook to resolve this more-than-Gordian knot not from sure observations through mathematical proofs [apodixes], but presumed to settle the whole matter merely by certain subtleties and conjectures and computational niceties — assuming as conceded most of the things which first needed to be inquired into and demonstrated, [namely] whether they were so or not. On that account I judged it worth the effort to inquire, from ratified observations, into the exact truth in this part, and to demonstrate it geometrically, lest the subtleties of the Peripatetics and others should any longer cast a deceptive gloss [fucum facere] upon the truth, as has been done for so many centuries now. But that this must be investigated from the parallaxes of comets, no one skilled in mathematics is ignorant; yet since those parallaxes need subtle inquiry — so much so that some error easily creeps in tacitly, unless everything be very circumspectly administered — nay, since it is even impossible to extricate certain parallaxes unless the precise reckoning of the comet's proper motion be also applied: it is in any case necessarily required that its apparent course be marked from the heavens as diligently as possible, as often as can be done through clear weather; which truly is not effected unless it be inquired what disposition it has relative to the ecliptic, in respect of longitude and latitude, and to the equator, as to right ascension and declination — and hence it be at once established what proper track it designates, and how it moves in it, and by what reckoning it varies its course day by day. For without these things one would try in vain to scrutinize the parallax. For the proper motion affects the comet's appearances more than that diversity which arises by reason of the parallaxes, if perchance they had any. These two things, therefore, must be skilfully discerned and diligently compared with one another. Hence too those things which were variously and learnedly devised by that supreme Regiomontanus and certain other moderns about investigating the parallaxes of comets — even if as regards the demonstration they are correct — yet, since they presuppose that to happen in comets which very rarely or never occurs (namely, that they are destitute of all proper motion and are revolved by no other [motion] than that universal diurnal circuit of the primum mobile) — are in practice itself useless and altogether superfluous. It is plain, therefore, that if that which is controverted deserves a just inquiry — namely, whether comets are aetherial or not — and this cannot be done without the investigation of the parallaxes, while the parallaxes cannot in any way be discerned without a diligent disposition as to the chief circles of the heaven and the limits of the proper track and the alteration of the diurnal motion — the consequence is that it is not to be judged a useless and superfluous labour to demonstrate, from sure observations, the longitudes, latitudes, and the tracing of the circle under which comets move, and with what tenor they complete their course, [and] how they augment it or even check it by a sure law — since without these things the other [question], which principally comes to be inquired and is called into doubt, cannot be decided. Nay, after it has been established that they dwell in the aether itself, this very greatly confirms and illustrates the business, if their course be demonstrated, after the manner of the planets, by a suitable hypothesis, and resolved into numbers. For thus it is more fully and quickly persuaded to others that there is altogether a celestial nature in them, not very alien from the properties of the other planets — except that it is not perpetual and so exalted as in the rest of the wandering stars. But it is easier to carp at and disparage the indefatigable labours of others than to imitate or even surpass them.

Further, that comets disappear like meteors, and do not always endure, proves nothing — [namely, it does not prove] that their motion and conditions are not to be diligently investigated. For although different ones are seen at a certain time and at length gradually consumed, yet others return again. Nor was there ever an age that did not behold some comets — which men have always greatly marvelled at, as something great, new, and unusual — so much so that these hairy [crinitae] stars have always moved their minds more than the rest of the heavenly bodies coeval with the world. But although comets sometimes seen disappear not so long after, nor do others return within a long time — so much so that sometimes in 20 years scarcely any has appeared — on that very account it is the more worth the effort to apply diligence in investigating their appearances while they last and are present, that thereby the controversy about the situation, motion, and nature of such phenomena, so long variously disputed, may the better be settled; since such an occasion is not always offered, as in the rest of the heavenly bodies, which on every clear night — all those that have surmounted the Horizon — it is permitted to behold and observe, if only that care drew the minds of mortals. For these reasons, therefore, and many others, it is necessary and useful to apply a most assiduous description to the situation and motion of comets. Moreover, although they might of themselves seem not worthy enough that for their sake one should drain off [exhaust] such great labours, yet there is something else why it is so expedient that this very thing be done — for the sake of more thoroughly investigating those perpetual heavenly bodies and the nature and disposition of the whole heaven — so that the same can scarcely be established from elsewhere. And therefore I, from this accurate consideration of comets in past years, have detected many things conducing to the whole astronomical matter, which are of no small moment; such as: that the heaven does not consist of any real, hard, and impervious orbs, as all Antiquity believed, and very many of the moderns still [do]; that there are no epicycles; that the Earth does not really go round [the Sun] yearly; and very many other things, of which I treat more copiously toward the end of the whole work. And thus, by this exquisite observation of the adventitious and new heavenly bodies, I prepare the way — nay, I lay the foundations — for those things which, in that whole astronomical work, I have proposed to be re-integrated and, as it were, renewed afresh [de novo instauranda], with the favour of divine aid, concerning the appearances and revolutions of the perennial heavenly bodies coeval with the world — at which I also labour day by day with arduous and indefatigable effort. Nay, in these books too, on the recent phenomena of the heaven, many things long vainly desired are proposed, for establishing the astronomical matter more rightly than hitherto — such as the exact restoration of the solar course, the emendation of the lunar latitude, the verification of the fixed stars, and very many others, which, on the occasion of these secondary heavenly bodies, I communicate to the cultivators of astronomy, sought out by me with no small labour and study. Thus too I would have reserved this new hypothesis to myself, until the publication of the whole astronomical work, had not that Comet of the year 1577, revolved around the Sun, invited me to admit its disclosure in that book; so many and so great things, even from this sole occasion of the comets, hitherto less perceived, have we brought into the light, and, with the favour of the Deity, we shall hereafter bring forth more abundantly. But if such writings, together with the phenomena of which they treat, are evanescent, surely they will deserve sometime to survive — at least on account of the consideration of the perennial bodies, of which they treat in many places at the same time — nor will they be so easily abolished as you forebode. And perhaps, while that song-and-dance about the primum mobile and the spherical rudiments and the common theories of the planets — so long and so often repeated to the point of nausea (all which find or establish nothing new and previously desired) — [the others] will remain among the skilled and the grateful. Or are you ignorant that that most lauded and most ancient astronomer Hipparchus would never have left us a canonical description of all the fixed stars, so assiduous and laborious, had he not been incited to surmount so immense a labour by a New Star arisen in his time, which doubtless disappeared a little afterwards, as Pliny testifies of him? And indeed, after him nothing similar was performed by any of mankind, some 2000 years having elapsed. For Ptolemy and his successors acquiesced in the Hipparchian notation, taking account at least of the advancement [precession] of the whole eighth sphere. And therefore Regiomontanus calls this catalogue [abacum] of the fixed stars a work more divine than human, and a treasure not to be profaned or entrusted to the common crowd. Nor does he value it without reason. Remember too what great things Archimedes left us, even on the occasion of the most worthless sands, at the urging of Gelon, king of the Sicilians. Thus too on other occasions a cause has often been offered to men, from the smallest and not-long-enduring things, of building up and investigating things far greater and more stable — about which it is now not fitting to say more. For I see that I have already been too [lengthy] in writing back, and have transgressed not a little the laws of a letter; which lingering may you take in good and fair part, and ascribe it to the fact that I confer with you on this subject with a willing mind. Farewell — and take care that I shortly have that book of Master Camerarius on this same Comet, published (I think) at Frankfurt-on-the-Oder. I should also wish to inspect the writing of Simon van der Eycke [Simonis a Quercu] on the squaring of the circle. For it has not yet been brought to me. And if you procure it for me along [with the other], you will do me a most welcome service.

Again, fare you most happily, and write back before the winter's ice.
Given at Uraniborg, amid very many other occupations, with a hurrying pen, [the letter] however long-winded, on the 4th of November, the year [15]88.

But if your most illustrious Prince has by now sent to Rostock any paper suited to my workshop, take care that I receive it as quickly as possible, kept safe from the harm of winter and of rains. I also ask most affectionately that you be willing, now, straightaway, to send to me some craftsman who is excellently skilled in the art of engraving in wood. For I have need of such a man through the winter season, and perhaps even through the whole summer, on account of various figures — both of instruments and of other designs — to be engraved in wood, which are afterwards to be printed. If, therefore, you procure such a man in good time and send him here at the first opportunity, you will do a thing greatly welcome to me and befitting our friendship. As to his wages, once he has arrived, we shall easily agree between ourselves. Whatever you lay out for his travelling expenses, I shall repay.

Quidam nimis perfrictae frontis … publico scripto, Argentinae hoc anno edito — the man Tycho refuses to name is Nicolaus Reymers Bär ("Ursus", Raimarus Ursus, c. 1551–1600), whose Fundamentum astronomicum (Strasbourg [Argentina], 1588) published a world-system Tycho regarded as plagiarized from his own, learned during Ursus's days in 1584 in the retinue of Erik Lange (Erico Lange, Danish nobleman and alchemist) at Uraniborg.   • What it is. The recovered text is the opening of the postscript-like close of a letter from Tycho to Heinrich Brucaeus, professor of medicine and mathematics at Rostock — the surviving published text picks up only after this point, mid-request about a book by Camerarius.
  • The Prince. "Princeps vester illustrissimus" is almost certainly Duke Ulrich III of Mecklenburg, sovereign of Rostock; Dreyer records that in December 1587 Tycho had already asked, via Heinrich Below, for Ulrich's help securing paper from the ducal paper mills, to be forwarded through Brucaeus [Dreyer, Tycho Brahe (1890)]. This letter shows the arrangement still running a year later.
  • The engraver. Tycho is recruiting a woodcut engraver for his Uraniborg press ("meæ officinæ"), needed specifically to cut figures of instruments and other designs for printing — plausibly connected to the instrument plates later published in the Astronomiae Instauratae Mechanica (1598). Christianson confirms Tycho kept a substantial stock of woodcut printing blocks alongside his presses [Christianson, On Tycho's Island (2000)].
  • Financial detail. Tycho offers to negotiate wages once the craftsman arrives and to reimburse travel costs — ordinary workshop-recruitment business, otherwise undocumented for this specific hire.
  • Correction. Two Latin transcription errors corrected against TBOO VII: p.151 restores the dropped numeral "15" before "scrupulis" (Dreyer: "a me Ptolemaica 15 scrupulis maiorem constitutam"), with the English hedge "[some] minutes" replaced by "15 minutes" accordingly; p.160 restores the dropped trailing digit in "anno 80" (Dreyer: "qui anno 80 apparuit", i.e. the comet of 1580), with the English's hedged "[15]8[0] (printed \"8\"; see apparatus)" simplified to "[15]80" per the letter's ordinary year convention.
