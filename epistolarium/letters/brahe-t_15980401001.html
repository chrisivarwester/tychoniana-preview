The original

CLARISSIMO ET PRÆSTANTISSIMO VIRO Μ. IOHANNI KEPLERO ILLUSTRIUM STYRIAe PROVINCIALIUM IN GYMNASIO GRÆCENSI MATHEMATICO EXIMIO, AMICO SUO PLURIMUM DILECTO. GRETZ IN STIRIEN.

ERUDITISSIME et praestantissime Vir, Literae tuae e Styria Id. Dec. superioris anni ad me datae circa initia Martij per Tabellarium Helmstadio venientem mihi redditae sunt, quae praeter doctrinam et humanitatem singularem erga me, licet tibi de facie ignotum et longe dissitum, testantur benevolentiam, quo etiam nomine tibi gratias habeo. Librum tuum, quem Prodromum vocas Dissertationum Cosmographicarum, iam ante videram, et quantum aliae occupationes ferebant, perlegeram. Placet is sane non mediocriter, et ingenij tui acumen sagaxque studium non obscure hinc elucent, ut de stylo terso ac rotundo nihil dicam. Ingeniosa certe et succincta est speculatio, Planetarum distantias et circuitus symmetrijs regularium corporum, uti facis, adstringere, et plurima in his satis consentire videntur, non obstante quod proportiones Coperniceae per omnia in minimis quibusque haud ubique adstipulentur, cum eae ab ipsis etiam apparentijs nonnihil deflectant. Quare laudo hanc tuam in excogitandis perquirendisque talibus diligentiam. An vero per omnia ijs acquiescendum sit, non ita facile dixerim. Si veriores Eccentricitatum utrarumque in singulis Planetis commensurationes, quales plurimis annis conquisitas in promptu habeo, adhibeantur, poterint accuratiorem in his trutinam exhibere. Verum mihi in complendis et edendis meis operibus Astronomicis, quae in Dania quodammodo imperfecta reliqueram, occupatissimo non suppetit nunc otium, ea accuratiore lima inter se conferendi. Fiet hoc forte alias. Id autem te scire velim, Eccentricitates ipsas utriusque illius, ut sic nunc loquar, Eccentrici, quas COPERNICUS paulo aliter excusavit, non habere eas adinvicem proportiones, ut una sit alterius tertia pars; veluti ex PTOLEMÆO deduxit. Neuter vero eorum demonstratum reliquit. Sed haec aliam inter se habent rationem et in singulis variant. Neque enim tres acronychiae observationes, utut inter se in Eccentrico (quod praestat) remotae, sufficiunt ad trium superiorum Planetarum Apogaea (quae etiam non paucis gradibus tam ALPHONSI quam COPERNICI placita coelitus eludunt) et Eccentricitates ambas ad amussim extricandas; sed per totum orbis circuitum in plurimis locis haec experiunda veniunt, veluti a nobis quoque factum est, adeo ut etiam tardigradi illius Saturni circuitum expectaverimus. Nam eundem Soli in ♎ oppositum ante annos 29. Augustae Vindelicorum diligenter observavi, quemadmodum et nunc; uti etiam in intermedijs Acronychijs dispositionibus, quotquot habere licuit, in toto Boreali semicirculo nullis omnino praetermissis, quemadmodum etiam in reliquis duobus Planetis, quorum circuitus minori clauditur tempore. Habeo enim in promptu 35 annorum Observationes coelestes, inde ab adolescentia factas, quarum 25 posteriores magna accuratione pollent: quas omnes non vulgaris thesauri loco conservo, et hinc Astronomiae restaurationem molior. Ut vero de Marte aliquid exempli loco addam, cum is maximam ingerat varietatem, atque inter Coelum et Tabulas discrepantiam caeteris evidentiorem, scias tantum abesse, ut eius Eccentricitas sit minor reddita (prout voluit COPERNICUS, quo suae imaginationi circa quietem Solis fidem faceret) ut potius nunc aliquanto maior sit, quam ea, quae a PTOLEMÆO prodita est, licet discrimine non magni momenti, Apogaeo eius quinque gradibus COPERNICI terminos anticipante. Quin et orbis annuus iuxta COPERNICUM aut Epicyclus secundum PTOLEMÆUM non videtur eiusdem semper magnitudinis, quoad ipsum Eccentricum collatione facta; sed alterationem introducit in omnibus tribus superioribus sensibilem, adeo ut angulus differentiae in ♂ ad Gradum unum Minuta 45. excrescat. Quomodo haec cum tua speculatione concilianda sint, tute videris. Veneris Eccentricitas multo minor est, quam statuit vel PTOLEMÆUS vel COPERNICUS, et Apogaeum eius non est in orbe sidereo fixum, uti hic voluit, sed initia ♋ nunc attigit; nec tamen solari (quod in medio sexti gradus haeret) uti volunt Alphonsini, plane unitum. Haec et similia, quorum plura suo tempore habebis, tuae alias industriae excogitationi applicata eam, an undequaque rite constet, probabunt. Ego omnem orbium realitatem, quomodocunque tandem assumantur, coelo eliminandam censeo. Quod me omnes Cometae, inde a nova stella conspecti, qui nullius orbis leges totaliter secuti sunt, quidam vero contrarium quid prae se ferebant, indubitanter edocuerunt (ut de refractionibus frustra multiplicatis ex orbium solidorum copia nihil superaddam). Nec poterit Terra annuo cieri motu, ut de librationibus otiosis (neque enim talis est aequinoctiorum praecessio, ut opinabatur COPERNICUS) taceam: utut diurnus plausibiliter ob minorem gyrum illi attribui queat, modo tam gravi et opaco corpori is competeret, quieti potius, quam motui accommodo. Quare ut tollantur Epicycli, vix alia commodiore ratione Hypotheses ordinari queunt, quam ut circa Solem mobilem sint centra circuituum quinque Planetarum quiescente Terra, et circa hanc revolutis luminaribus et tota octava (uti vocant) Sphaera, eam etiam in meditullio respiciente. Cuius abscessus iuxta COPERNICUM in immensum pene abit, eiusque positionem vel hoc solo absurdo infringit. Nec etiam tua cogitata hîc ullam cum caeteris Symmetriam (prout simul fieri debuit) admittunt. Oportet autem in re bene proportionata nihil dari inproportionatum, alias tota Symmetria confunditur, et suspecta evadit: praesertim in hoc loco, ubi tam magnum atque evidens ingeritur obstaculum. Haec et alia, quae singula commemorare nunc non vacat, me circa tuam inventionem alias solertissimam, optime KEPLERE, dubium reddunt. Interim non possum non conatus tuos, tam eximios et raros magnifacere. Hortorque ut ulterius his animum intendas, desque operam, ut simile quid nostris hypothesibus, quas ubi non incompertas esse liber ille ostendit, applicare possis, cum pene omnia ijs pariter conveniant. Neque ob id opus est, ut orbium statuatur compages solida. Sufficit circuitus et remotiones siderum inter se talem concinnitatem atque harmoniam observare. Nec praetereunda est octava Sphaera, quin in hoc choro proportionabiliter locum aliquem obtineat. Nullum enim est dubium in universo omnia certa harmonia et proportione ad invicem divinitus relata et ordinata esse, ita ut tam numeris quam figuris succincte comprehendantur, veluti a Pythagoreis et Platonicis iamdudum aliquatenus praevisum est. His itaque ulterius ingenij nervos applica, et si omnimodam consonantiam, quae nulla ex parte claudicet, et in qua nihil etiam desideretur, adinveneris, eris mihi magnus Apollo. Quantum ego tuis hac in re arduis conatibus subvenire potero, me neutiquam habebis difficilem, praesertim si me nunc in Germania degentem et e patria huc cum tota familia, ne tantus ille periret Thesaurus Astronomicus, tot annis, laboribus et sumptibus comparatus, profectum aliquando inviseris et mecum coram de eiuscemodi sublimibus rebus iucunde et grate contuleris. Vale. Dabantur Wandesburgi in arce Ranzoviana semisse tantummodo milliaris Hamburgo remota, quam nunc inhabito, et translatis huc e Dania machinis meis Astronomicis totaque suppellectile libraria rebus coelestibus non minus quam antea invigilo, et Divina gratia otio fruor Philosophico.

D. 1. April. stylo veteri Anno 1598.
TYCHO BRAHE.
manu propria.

POSTSCRIPTA. NESCIO quo casu acciderit, ut idem Tabellarius, qui mihi tuas Helmstadio attulit, famosum quoque et nefarium scriptum cuiusdam feri Ursi potius quam veri hominis, inde mihi simul tuis alligatum adportârit. Huius cum impudentes calumnias et infanda mendacia atque convicia, quibus supra modum et citra omnem verecundiam scatet, obiter percurrerem, reperi etiam ibi tuum quoddam Epistolium, quo se ornare et sua flagitia tegere nititur. Quod cum perlegissem, miratus sum te eum tanti facere. Sed errorem excusavi, quod non nisi auditu et scripto isto, quod Fundamentum vocat Astronomicum, hoc faceres. In quo fere nihil habet quod suum est. Omnia potius aliis surrepta et praerepta, prout facere consuevit. Et plagium quod ego ipsi imputo in meis Hypothesibus, non saltem error crassus, quem in delineatione Martij orbis commisit, sed et multa alia, quae suo tempore pandam, evincunt. Estque etiamnum apud me fasciculus quidam eorum, quae quando Nobili cuidam Dano meo affini inserviens anno 84 cum eodem Uraniburgi adesset, clam descripsit. In quibus non dissimilia egit. Ego sane hanc Hypothesium rationem iam longe antea excogitatam, nec eam libro de Cometa Anni 77 inseruissem, nisi tales Plagiarios, quibus haec apud me innotuerat, veritus fuissem. Quare et hoc miror, quod eius voces Hypothesin, cum et in libro tuo et literis hisce eam mihi (nec immerito) attribuas. Interim etiam magna eius praedicas Elogia, utinam non Hyperbolen! In titulo vocas eum Mathematicum nobilissimum (qualis sit Mathematicus, non dixerim, Nobilitas eius, si genus spectes, est ex rusticis Dithmarsis, qui eum etiam fastidiunt). Ais deinde Illustrissimam eius gloriam ipsum tibi notum fecisse, qua Mathematicos huius aevi tantum praecedat unus, quantum Phoebeius orbis minuta sidera. Plura addidisses, si tempus ferret. At quam competenter etiam ulteriora excusas, quod Mathematicorum non sit garrulitas. Nescio an tale quid tunc apud te tacite senseris, genio sic suggerente. Praeterea quod omnes Docti magni ipsum faciant, eum praeceptorem appellas, hypotheses meas illi tribuis. Et tandem concludis, ut sideribus (quorum permodicam habet notitiam) et scientiae, quam sibi hinc inde (si qua est) corradit et suffuratur, tanquam decus Germaniae valeat. Talia de ignoto inconsiderate scripsisse videris: quae ego quidem nullatenus moror. Imo mallem vera esse, vel aliquem eiuscemodi dari, cui tanta in Astronomicis et Mathematicis competerent Elogia. Ego non ultimus essem, qui eius celebraret gloriam. Non tamen supra PTOLEMÆUM aut COPERNICUM, aliosque eximios viros, qualis in Germania MÆSTHLINUS nunc est, efferrem, nisi dignum fore satis perspectum haberem. Sed sint haec qualiacunque velint, id equidem non arridet, quod tua sic in libro isto contra me maledico abutatur Epistola, ut hac quasi se purgare, atque quantus sit, quamque multum ipsi tribuendum (si Dijs placet) probare velit, se ipsum ita nimium extollendo et sibimet placendo atque aliis tacite detrahendo. At quoniam in iuventute ea per ignorantiam scripsisti, nec forte existimâras eum unquam literas illas publicaturum, nedum ut iis in aliorum despectum et contumeliam unquam abusurus foret, idcirco est, cur haec placidius feram. Cuperem nihilominus ut primo quoque tempore apud me declaratum relinqueres, an tibi gratum sit quod sic fecerit, et quid de scripto isto virulento sentias, tum quoque alii Eruditi, ad quorum manus illud pervenit. Neque enim dubito, isthuc perlatum, siquidem Pragae excusum sit, suppresso tamen Typographi nomine, uti fit in famosis libellis. Plura de his non addo. Nec enim digna sunt, ut multis revocentur. Tuum est haec ipsa obiter subiuncta candide interpretari et schedam hanc Vulcano tradere. Iamque iterum et saepius Vale. Datum ut supra in literis. T. B.

To the most renowned and excellent man, Master Johannes Kepler, distinguished mathematician of the Illustrious Estates of Styria in the Gymnasium of Graz, his very dearly beloved friend. Graz in Styria.

Most learned and excellent Sir, your letter, sent to me from Styria on the Ides of December [13 December] of last year, was delivered to me about the beginning of March by a courier coming from Helmstedt; and besides your learning and singular courtesy towards me — though I am unknown to you by face and far distant — it bears witness to your goodwill, on which account too I give you thanks. Your book, which you call the Forerunner of Cosmographical Dissertations [Prodromus Dissertationum Cosmographicarum], I had already seen before, and, so far as my other occupations allowed, had read through. It pleases me, indeed, not moderately, and the keenness of your wit and your sagacious zeal shine forth from it not obscurely — to say nothing of its polished and well-rounded style. It is certainly an ingenious and compact speculation to bind the distances and circuits of the planets to the symmetries of the regular solids, as you do; and very many things in this seem to agree well enough, notwithstanding that the Copernican proportions do not everywhere lend their support in each and every smallest detail, since they themselves depart somewhat even from the appearances. For which reason I praise this diligence of yours in devising and investigating such matters. But whether one ought to rest content in them in every respect, I would not so readily say. If the truer measurements of both eccentricities in each of the planets — such as I have ready to hand, gathered over very many years — be applied, they will be able to display a more accurate balance in these things. But, being most fully occupied in completing and publishing my astronomical works, which I had left in some manner unfinished in Denmark, I have now no leisure to spare for comparing these things with one another by that more accurate file. This will perhaps be done at another time. This, however, I would have you know: that the eccentricities of each of those (so let me now put it) eccentrics — which Copernicus accounted for somewhat otherwise — do not have those proportions to one another such that the one is a third part of the other, as he deduced from Ptolemy. Yet neither of the two left it demonstrated. But these things have another ratio among themselves and vary in each [planet]. For three acronychal observations, however far apart from one another in the eccentric (which is preferable), do not suffice for unravelling exactly the apogees of the three superior planets (which also, by no few degrees, elude in the heavens the doctrines of both Alfonso and Copernicus) and both eccentricities; but throughout the whole circuit of the orb, in very many places, these things must be tried by experience, as has been done by us too, so much so that we waited even for the circuit of that slow-moving Saturn. For I diligently observed that same planet in opposition to the Sun in ♎ Libra 29 years ago at Augsburg, just as now too; as also in the intermediate acronychal dispositions, as many as it was permitted to have, in the whole northern semicircle, with absolutely none passed over — just as in the remaining two planets, whose circuit is closed in a lesser time. For I have ready to hand the celestial observations of 35 years, made ever since my youth, of which the later 25 are strong in great accuracy: all of which I keep in the place of no common treasure, and from these I am labouring at the restoration of astronomy. But, to add something about Mars by way of example, since it brings on the greatest variety, and a discrepancy between the heaven and the tables more evident than the rest — you should know that so far is its eccentricity from being rendered smaller (as Copernicus wished, in order to give credit to his imagining concerning the rest of the Sun) that it is rather now somewhat greater than that which was reported by Ptolemy, though by a difference of no great moment, its apogee anticipating the bounds of Copernicus by five degrees. Indeed, the annual orb according to Copernicus, or the epicycle according to Ptolemy, does not appear to be always of the same magnitude, when a comparison is made with the eccentric itself; but it introduces in all three superior planets an alteration that is perceptible, so much so that the angle of difference in ♂ Mars grows to one degree and 45 minutes. How these things are to be reconciled with your speculation, you yourself will see. The eccentricity of Venus is much smaller than either Ptolemy or Copernicus laid down, and its apogee is not fixed in the starry orb, as he wished, but has now reached the beginning of ♋ Cancer; nor yet is it plainly united to the solar [apogee] (which sticks in the middle of the sixth degree), as the Alfonsines would have it. These and similar things, of which you shall have more in their own time, applied to the devising of your otherwise [admirable] industry, will prove whether it holds good in every respect. I, for my part, judge that the whole reality of the orbs, however in the end they be assumed, is to be eliminated from the heaven. Of which all the comets seen since the new star — which followed totally the laws of no orb, while some indeed bore something contrary — have taught me beyond doubt (to add nothing further about the refractions vainly multiplied out of the abundance of solid orbs). Nor will the Earth be able to be moved by an annual motion — to say nothing of idle librations (for the precession of the equinoxes is not such a thing as Copernicus supposed): although the diurnal [motion] might plausibly be attributed to it on account of its lesser turning, provided it suited so heavy and opaque a body, which is fitter for rest than for motion. Wherefore, in order that the epicycles may be removed, the hypotheses can scarcely be arranged by any more convenient method than that the centres of the circuits of the five planets be about the moveable Sun, the Earth being at rest, and that about this [Earth] the luminaries and the whole eighth (as they call it) sphere be revolved, the latter also regarding it [the Earth] in its very midst. Whose remoteness, according to Copernicus, goes off almost into the immeasurable, and he breaks down his own position by this absurdity alone. Nor, moreover, do your conceptions here admit any symmetry with the rest (as ought to have been the case at the same time). But it is fitting that in a thing well proportioned nothing disproportionate be granted, otherwise the whole symmetry is confounded and turns out suspect: especially in this place, where so great and evident an obstacle is brought in. These and other things, which it is not now free to recount one by one, render me doubtful, most excellent Kepler, concerning your invention, otherwise most skilful. Meanwhile I cannot but make much of your efforts, so distinguished and rare. And I urge you to bend your mind further to these things, and to take pains that you may apply something similar to our hypotheses — which, as that book shows them not to be unknown [to you], since almost everything agrees equally with them. Nor on that account is there need for a solid framework of orbs to be laid down. It suffices to observe such a fitness and harmony of the circuits and distances of the stars among themselves. Nor is the eighth sphere to be passed over without its obtaining some place proportionably in this choir. For there is no doubt that in the universe all things are divinely related and ordered to one another by a certain harmony and proportion, in such a way that they are concisely comprehended as much by numbers as by figures, as was already in some measure foreseen long ago by the Pythagoreans and Platonists. To these things, therefore, apply the sinews of your wit yet further; and if you should discover a consonance of every kind, which limps in no part, and in which nothing whatever is wanting, you shall be to me a great Apollo. As far as I shall be able to assist your arduous efforts in this matter, you will by no means find me difficult — especially if you should one day visit me, now dwelling in Germany, having set out hither from my homeland with my whole household, lest that great Astronomical Treasure, gathered over so many years with such labours and expense, should perish; and if you should confer with me face to face about sublime matters of this kind, pleasantly and gratefully. Farewell. Given at Wandsbek in the Rantzau castle, only half a mile distant from Hamburg, which I now inhabit; and, my astronomical instruments and the whole furniture of my library having been brought hither from Denmark, I keep watch over celestial matters no less than before, and by divine grace enjoy philosophical leisure.

The 1st of April, Old Style, the year 1598.
TYCHO BRAHE.
With his own hand.

[Postscript:] POSTSCRIPT. I know not by what chance it came about that the same courier who brought me your letters from Helmstedt carried hither at the same time, tied up with yours, a notorious and wicked writing of a certain man — a "wild Bear" [Ursus] rather than a true man. As I ran cursorily through his shameless calumnies and unspeakable lies and abuse, with which beyond measure and past all shame it teems, I found there too a certain little letter of yours, with which he tries to adorn himself and to cloak his villainies. When I had read it through, I marvelled that you make so much of him. But I excused the error, [judging] that you did this only on hearsay and on that writing of his which he calls Fundamentum Astronomicum. In it he has hardly anything that is his own; all rather filched and snatched from others, as he is wont to do. And the plagiarism which I impute to him in [the matter of] my Hypotheses — not only the gross error which he committed in the delineation of Mars's orb, but also many other things, which I shall disclose in their own time — prove [it]. And there is still with me a certain bundle of the things which, while he was in the service of a certain Danish nobleman, my kinsman, and was present with him at Uraniborg in the year [15]84, he secretly copied out; in which he acted not unlike [this]. I, in truth, had devised this scheme of the Hypotheses long before, and would have inserted it in the book on the Comet of [15]77, had I not feared such Plagiarists, to whom these things had become known with me. Wherefore I marvel at this too, that you should call it a Hypothesis of his, since both in your book and in this letter you attribute it (and not undeservedly) to me. Meanwhile you also proclaim great Eulogies of him — would they were not Hyperbole! In the title you call him a most noble Mathematician (what kind of Mathematician he is, I would not say; his Nobility, if you look to his lineage, is from the Dithmarsh peasants, who even disdain him). You say next that his most illustrious glory made him known to you, by which he alone so far precedes the Mathematicians of this age as the Phoebean orb [the Sun] the tiny stars. You would have added more, had time allowed. But how fittingly you also excuse the rest, [saying] that garrulity belongs not to Mathematicians! I know not whether you then felt some such thing tacitly within you, your genius so suggesting. Besides, [saying] that all the great Learned make much of him, you call him a teacher, and ascribe my hypotheses to him. And at last you conclude that he may avail, as it were the glory of Germany, for the stars (of which he has very slight knowledge) and for the science which he scrapes together and pilfers for himself here and there (if there be any). Such things you seem to have written inconsiderately about one unknown [to you]: which I, for my part, in no way regard. Nay, I would rather they were true, or that there were someone of such a kind to whom so great Eulogies in Astronomy and Mathematics were due. I should not be the last to celebrate his glory. Yet I would not exalt him above Ptolemy or Copernicus, and the other excellent men — such as Maestlin now is in Germany — unless I held it sufficiently clear that he would be worthy. But be these things whatever they will, this indeed does not please me: that your Letter is so abused in that book of his against me, the slanderer, that by it he wishes, as it were, to purge himself and to prove how great he is, and how much (so please the gods) is to be ascribed to him — by thus too much extolling himself and pleasing himself and tacitly disparaging others. But since you wrote those things in your youth, through ignorance, nor perhaps had supposed that he would ever publish those letters, much less that he would ever abuse them to the contempt and contumely of others, that is why I bear these things more calmly. Nonetheless I should wish that, at the first opportunity, you would leave it declared to me whether it is pleasing to you that he has done so, and what you think of that virulent writing — and likewise the other Learned into whose hands it has come. For I doubt not that it has been carried thither, since it was printed at Prague, the Printer's name being suppressed, however, as is done in libellous tracts. I add no more about these things; for they are not worth being recalled at length. It is for you to interpret candidly these very things subjoined in passing, and to hand this sheet over to Vulcan [the fire]. And now again and often, Farewell. Given as above in the letter. T. B.

Apparatus

  • Sender / recipient. Tycho Brahe (1546–1601), then in self-imposed exile from Denmark; Johannes Kepler (1571–1630), at this date Landschaftsmathematiker (mathematician to the Estates) and teacher at the Protestant Stiftsschule / Gymnasium at Graz in Styria. This is Tycho's celebrated first substantive reply to the young Kepler — his answer to the Mysterium Cosmographicum — and the opening of the correspondence that would bring Kepler to Prague in 1600.
  • Μ. before Iohanni — the abbreviation M. = Magister (academic "Master"), here written with a Greek capital Mu (Μ) in the source; rendered "Master". Reproduced as printed in the Source block.
  • Place. Wandesburgum, arx Ranzoviana = Wandsbek (now in Hamburg), the castle of the Rantzau (Rantzow) family — Tycho's host Heinrich Rantzau's seat — where Tycho resided 1597–98 between leaving Hven and removing to Prague. semisse … milliaris = "half a [German] mile" from Hamburg.
  • Place of address. Gretz in Stirien = Graz in Styria (German address-line on the cover), repeating the Latin Gymnasium Graecense … Styriae.
  • Prodromum … Dissertationum Cosmographicarum — the full title of Kepler's 1596 book, the Mysterium Cosmographicum (Prodromus Dissertationum Cosmographicarum, continens Mysterium Cosmographicum); its thesis nests the planetary orbs within the five Platonic regular solids — Tycho's symmetriae regularium corporum.
  • Dating of the incoming letter. Literae tuae … Id. Dec. superioris anni = Kepler's letter of 13 December [1597] (Ides of December), delivered "about the beginning of March" via a courier from Helmstedt (Helmstadium); Roman-calendar idiom kept and glossed by this chapter's convention
  • Astronomy — technical. acronychiae observationes / Acronychae dispositiones = acronychal observations (observations of a planet at opposition, rising at sunset); Eccentricitates utriusque … Eccentrici = the two eccentricities of the (bisected) eccentric — Tycho denies the Ptolemaic-Copernican simple bisection (one eccentricity = a third of the other / equant-bisection); Apogaea trium superiorum Planetarum = the apogees of the three superior planets (Saturn, Jupiter, Mars); orbis annuus … aut Epicyclus = the Copernican annual orb vs. the Ptolemaic epicycle; octava Sphaera = the eighth sphere (of the fixed stars); aequinoctiorum praecessio = the precession of the equinoxes. Glossary renderings kept consistent.
  • The Mars datum. angulus differentiae in ♂ ad Gradum unum Minuta 45. excrescat — the discrepancy reaches 1° 45′; Apogaeo eius quinque gradibus COPERNICI terminos anticipante — Mars's apogee runs 5° ahead of Copernicus's value. This is the very Mars-anomaly that Tycho here flags and would later set Kepler to work on. Tycho's claim that Mars's eccentricity is greater than Ptolemy's (against Copernicus, who shrank it quo suae imaginationi circa quietem Solis fidem faceret, "to give credit to his imagining concerning the rest of the Sun") is reproduced as his own argued position, not endorsed.
  • The Tychonic system. circa Solem mobilem … centra circuituum quinque Planetarum quiescente Terra — Tycho's geo-heliocentric hypotheses: the five planets circle the moveable Sun, while Sun, Moon and the eighth sphere circle the resting Earth. omnem orbium realitatem … coelo eliminandam — his abolition of the solid orbs, argued from the cometary evidence "since the new star" (the 1572 nova and the comet of 1577 onward).
  • Symbols. ♎ = Libra, ♋ = Cancer, ♂ = Mars; glyphs kept in the Source block and named in English by this chapter's convention
  • eris mihi magnus Apollo — "you shall be to me a great Apollo," a proverbial classical compliment (cf. Virgil, Ecl. 3.104) = "you will be my oracle / supreme authority"; kept as the figure it is.
  • Thesaurus Astronomicus — "the Astronomical Treasure," Tycho's term (cf. non vulgaris thesauri loco conservo) for his 35-year observational archive — the asset he uprooted his household to preserve. The phrase is rendered literally.
  • restitutio / Astronomiae restaurationem — hinc Astronomiae restaurationem molior, "from these I am labouring at the restoration of astronomy," Tycho's astronomia instaurata programme.
  • Hedges preserved. Tycho's epistemic qualifiers are kept un-sharpened: non ita facile dixerim ("I would not so readily say"); forte alias ("perhaps at another time"); ut sic nunc loquar ("so let me now put it"); non magni momenti ("of no great moment"); plausibiliter … modo … competeret (the conditional concession on the diurnal motion). The praise–doubt structure (laudo … An vero … dubium reddunt … non possum non … magnifacere) is mirrored, not flattened to one valence.
  • Calendar. Dateline D. 1. April. stylo veteri Anno 1598 = 1 April 1598, expressly Old Style (Julian) — Tycho marks the style himself, anticipating his Catholic-Imperial correspondent's New Style; the catalogue label (1 April 1598, Wandsbek) agrees, so no header reconciliation is needed. manu propria = "with his own hand."
  • §1 Dreyer collation. The substantial POSTSCRIPTA — Tycho's reaction to the enclosed Ursus Fundamentum Astronomicon, the 1584 Uraniborg-copying plagiarism charge, and the rebuke of Kepler for over-praising Ursus "decus Germaniae" — restored from Dreyer VIII, pp. 46–48 (dropped after "manu propria").
  • Correction. Postscript, l.2-3: 'feri Ursi' (Dreyer, TBOO VIII p.46) restored for the corrupted 'seri Ursi', recovering the 'feri...veri' (wild Bear/true man) pun on Ursus's name; English 'late Bear' corrected to 'wild Bear'. l. following: 'Fundamentum Astronomicum' (Dreyer, TBOO VIII p.47), the real title of Ursus's 1588 tract, restored for the corrupted 'Fundamentum Astronomicon'; English title order corrected to match.

The translation and its checking

Grade Secure · collated against Dreyer's page images, Opera Omnia VIII pp. 44–48; defects the audit noted: text error (3).

Collated against Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia (the edition of record), reading the printed page images. All 6 Dreyer pages read as page images (TBOO VIII pp.44-49) and collated the full letter and its postscript (explicitly flagged in the transcribed original as 'restored from Dreyer... dropped by the working transcription') against the transcribed original and the published English. Checked every proper name (Copernicus, Ptolemy, Alfonso/Alphonsi, Maestlin, Ursus), number (29 years ago at Augsburg, 35 years of observations of which 25 later ones, apogee anticipates Copernicus by 5 degrees, angle of difference 1 degree 45 minutes, service to a Danish nobleman in [15]84, the [15]77 comet book), place (Graz/Gretz in Styria, Wandsbek, Hamburg, Silesia/Styria/Innsbruck as the Emperor's alternate refuges are NOT in this letter - none found misapplied), and the closing formula (1 April 1598 Old Style, half a mile from Hamburg). The main letter body (pp.44-46) is a fully faithful, thorough translation with no fabrication, omission, mistranslation, or hedge-loss. In the postscript, found two low-severity text error where our transcribed original's Latin departs from Dreyer's print (feri->seri; Astronomicum->Astronomicon); the English translation itself faithfully renders whatever the (corrupted) source said, and neither error changes the letter's overall sense (Ursus's plagiarism accusation and self-serving letter remain intact).

2 defects found:
- text error (low). Source Latin reads 'seri' (serus, late) where Dreyer's page image clearly prints 'feri' (ferus, wild); the correct reading makes an antithetical pun 'feri...veri' (wild Bear vs. true man) on Ursus's name, which the corrupted 'seri' loses. The English is a faithful rendering of the corrupted source, so the fault is upstream in our Latin. Dreyer prints: famofum quoque et nefarium fcriptum cuiufdam feri Urfi potius quam veri hominis (TBOO VIII p.46, Postscripta, l.2-3).
- text error (low). Source Latin has the substantive form 'Astronomicon' where Dreyer prints the adjective 'Astronomicum' agreeing with 'Fundamentum'; the real title of Ursus's 1588 tract is 'Fundamentum Astronomicum', not 'Astronomicon'. Translation is faithful to the (corrupted) source, so the fault is upstream in our Latin. Dreyer prints: fcripto ifto, quod Fundamentum vocat Aftronomicum (TBOO VIII p.47, l.2-3).

Repaired. Both defects verified directly against the Dreyer page images (TBOO VIII pp.46-47, within the letter's own extract, read pages 1-4) at the precise lines cited by the audit; the printed long-s/f forms and line breaks match the audit's citations exactly, and the 'feri Ursi...veri hominis' reading is independently supported by grammatical parallelism (genitive adjective-noun pairs) and by the fact that Ursus was still alive in 1598, so 'seri' (late, i.e. deceased) could not be correct. No crux remains in either defect.
