To the Most Illustrious Prince and Lord, Lord Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, Count of Katzenelnbogen, Dietz, Ziegenhain, and Nidda; the most clement Prince and Lord. [He sends] greeting.

When recently I was at the court of our Most Serene King, Most Illustrious Prince, the noble youth Gerhard Rantzau — governor of the castle of Kronborg, which the same renowned King has now for the first time caused to be magnificently built up on the nearby shore of Zealand — delivered to me certain letters sent me by his most noble and most learned father, a man most devoted to astronomical matters, Master Heinrich Rantzau, Royal Vicegerent in Holstein. On opening them I found among them a copy of a letter of Your Highness written to Master Heinrich Rantzau himself, which, received most eagerly, I read over again and again with great pleasure. For it wonderfully refreshed me to perceive from it, not obscurely, that Your Highness has not, even at this riper age, amid such various occupations and cares of governing the commonwealth, laid down from the heart the affection and zeal long since begun toward the deeper investigation of that divine Science of the Stars; but rather, with renewed strength and applied diligence, has furnished certain new instruments, more exact than the earlier ones, by which the very places of the stars may be restored far more scrupulously than before. On which account indeed I most heartily congratulate both Your Highness's heroic virtue and your fame, never to die among grateful posterity, and likewise the increase of astronomical knowledge.

Now in the same letter Your Highness makes mention of a certain small and altogether round comet, observed in the preceding year about 8 October by Your Highness's mathematician Christoph Rothmann in the last decan of Pisces [♓] and in southern latitude, which Your Highness yourself detected on 19 October in 21° of Aries [♈] with a latitude still 4 degrees southerly; and Your Highness asks of Master Rantzau that he signify whether that comet was seen in our regions. But by name Your Highness desires to learn from me whether I too, and by what method especially, observed the same comet in the heavens. And although I had several times resolved in my mind to write to Your Highness — both on account of that lofty, never sufficiently praised, divinely-bestowed industry and untiring assiduity with which you inquire so consummately into matters of the aether (on which account you far surpass all princely men both of the earlier age and of our own), and also because Your Highness, nearly eleven years ago now, embraced me most clemently and benevolently when I was present at Kassel, and most willingly and variously conferred with me about astronomical matters, which are our delight — yet, since I feared lest I should bring some annoyance to Your Highness by my untimely letters (especially since, over the interval of so many years and amid such great affairs of governing the commonwealth, the memory of me might easily have slipped from Your Highness's heart), it came about that I have deferred my long-standing purpose of writing up to this very day. But since Your Highness, by the said letter transmitted to Master Rantzau, has not disdained clemently to greet me, and of your own accord, taking occasion from the recently seen comet, has invited me to write to you, I took up courage more boldly, and, congratulating myself greatly that this opportunity of fulfilling a desire of many years was being offered me so very conveniently, I judged it must by no means be deferred any longer, but that I should reverently send some letter to Your Highness — relying on this clement invitation of Your Highness, which will excuse my boldness.

Concerning the comet, then, the observation of which Your Highness diligently desires of me, I make known to Your Highness that it first of all appeared to me on the 18th day of October of the year just past, as I was contemplating the stars in my usual manner; and that from this very day, up to the middle of November — that is, for as long as it could be seen — whenever convenient clear weather was granted, it was most diligently observed with exquisite instruments liable to no error. But that this comet became known to us a whole ten days later than to Your Highness's mathematician — the cause of that was the continual obscurities of clouds, mixed with very frequent and tempestuous damps, which had so overcast our hemisphere almost from the beginning of October that they entirely shut off the sight of the heaven and the stars throughout all that time, up to the aforesaid day on which the comet was seen. Of those things, however, which from this time, when it first began to be seen by us, throughout its whole duration, whenever the longed-for clear weather shone forth, were variously and diligently detected here in the heavens, I send certain of the more principal ones, written on a separate sheet (lest, by inserting them here, the letter should grow too much), for Your Highness to peruse at leisure. But a fuller and more complete declaration, established by more observations and by sure demonstrations resting on these — not only about this lately-seen comet, but also about three others seen in some of the years just past, and likewise about that new and admirable star that arose near the constellation of Cassiopeia — Your Highness will obtain when our Work On the New Phenomena of the Aethereal World seen in this age comes into the light. For as soon as everything in it has been brought to its colophon and printed here in my own printing-house (the Deity favouring), I shall transmit it to Your Highness to inspect and judge.

But because, in proving all these things more clearly against the standard of Truth as against a touchstone [a Lydian stone], I have resolved to bring forward also the observations and opinions of others, as many as I have been able to have, and to weigh them both among themselves and against the appearances themselves — without any prejudice and without immodest censure of others, only that Truth itself might thereby shine forth the more manifestly — I wish Your Highness humbly and earnestly entreated not to disdain to communicate with me your observations both of that star near Cassiopeia and of the other subsequent comets, which you have made exact. For I do not doubt that, just as Your Highness, in the said new star, entered the inner sanctuaries of truth more rightly than many others (in that, namely, you assert it to have had no perceptible parallax, and therefore to have stood in the supreme aether — while very many of the astronomers who set it among the elementary [sublunary] things erred intolerably), so also in the observations of comets you have attained the appointed goal far more accurately than the rest. But above all I desire to obtain from Your Highness, with what entreaties I can, that you not be loath clemently to share with me also those things which, in the other stars coeval with the World, you have hitherto discovered — especially by those new instruments — both as to distances and as to declinations or meridian altitudes. I in turn shall see to it that like observations both of the same and of other stars, as they have been detected by our instruments in the heavens, are written out and sent back to Your Highness, so that, a comparison being made mutually, the certainty of the instruments and observations may be the better judged. Moreover, you would do me a thing far most acceptable if Your Highness should send me, drawn out, the form and structural plan of your new instruments — especially as regards the sextant. For I greatly long to know whether (as I gather from probable conjecture) these new instruments of Your Highness have much resemblance to certain of mine; and that especially as concerns the subdivisions of the degrees by transversal points, and a peculiar manner of construction of the pinnules by parallel slits, exceedingly suited to nocturnal observations of stars.

For that famous Paul Wittich was here with me five years ago — from whose prescription Your Highness asserts your instruments were renewed — and he explored the construction and use of certain instruments which I partly then had ready, partly resolved to elaborate in future, and the plan of the division and of the pinnules, which in all of these I use in nearly the same way; and he soon betook himself into Germany, alleging certain other matters. And from the sole name of "sextant" one may conjecture that Wittich proposed to Your Highness things not much different from ours. For the instrument of the sextant — of which both the fabric and indeed the name itself had recently been devised by me — I then had ready in use when Wittich was with me. But since experience itself many times admonished me that that fabric of the sextant which I had then devised did not suffice for precisely investigating the intervals of the stars, on account of certain impediments, its use was long ago abolished by us. Wherefore, if that sextant by which Your Highness has the intervals of the stars observed is constructed on the same plan as the one which Wittich saw here, it will scarcely be possible by its means to obtain distances that are fixed and liable to no fallacy. For it is impossible, from a single eye resting at the centre of the instrument, to behold precisely at the same time the differing places of two stars, especially if they are rather remote from one another. For the centre of vision cannot fittingly be united with the centre of the instrument, nor can this difference be excused sufficiently conveniently by any parallaxes; nor does the pupil of the eye, in viewing two stars at greater intervals, remain unmoved, whence it becomes necessary that the centre of vision too vary somewhat. Nay rather — and this is of great moment in this subtle business — since each star is not seen on both sides equally and bisected at the pinnule by the parallel slits, it will by no means be certainly established whether the distance taken is greater or less than the just one.

I therefore afterward devised another way of constructing the sextant, and that a bifurcated one, so that by two observers each star might be seen separately, on both sides through two different pinnules. This plan of construction too I disclosed to Wittich while he was present here; and after his departure I had a sextant of this kind prepared, which was indeed exact enough and sufficed for taking the intervals of the stars without any sensible error. But since in its handling it was somewhat more difficult and laborious, and did not despatch the business quickly enough, this sextant too ceased to be in use with me. And if Your Highness's instrument is constructed in its likeness (for I showed Wittich the idea of this here, and it greatly pleased him), I do not doubt that the distances taken by it are correct, although obtained a little more difficultly and slowly (as I said). Therefore, in order to avoid both all error and also the difficulty in despatching this business dexterously, four years ago I found another plan of constructing the sextant, different from the earlier ones and most expeditious, plainly simple and natural, by which, also by two observers, with the pinnules (which have parallel slits) moved on the circumference of the instrument, the distances of the stars are discerned most expeditiously on both sides, about a cylinder resting at the centre, without any suspicion of error. And I have two such, skilfully wrought and destined for this use, each of which equals four cubits in its span.

But that Your Highness may the more easily comprehend the composition of this sextant, I send both a picture of the building erected by me in recent years on this island of Hven, at no slight cost, in honour of Astronomy, and also a drawing in some fashion representing the sextant (of which I speak), together with a delineation of its support [fulcrum], by whose benefit it is conveniently turned about to every position and inclination of the stars. There is added on the other side a picture of the Equatorial Armillae; for it has pleased me thus to call this instrument, long since devised by me, because it measures the right ascensions and declinations of the stars with respect to the equator. And I have had two such armillae made, of so great a size that they exhibit single minutes distinctly. But even in these very ones — which are far more expeditious than the Ptolemaic armillae and less liable to errors (as experience itself has taught us, since we have Ptolemaic armillae too, of solid metal, of nearly the same size, ready to hand) — two years ago I devised a certain new and much more expeditious structure, which, with but one and a half circles and a round axle, despatches the whole business with marvellous ease and certainty. The machine of this instrument I constructed with the utmost diligence, set on a certain hill outside these houses dedicated to Urania, and surrounded, for convenience of use, by subterranean steps ascending successively on all sides; its diameter contains ten feet. To this I attribute very much, for investigating exactly the right ascensions and declinations of the stars in whatever apparent position of them; by whose aid also I have for the most part investigated the appearances of the comet which I send Your Highness.

But that it is not difficult, from given right ascensions and declinations with respect to the equator, to reduce them into longitudes and latitudes, as far as concerns the ecliptic, Your Highness has sufficiently perceived — whether it be done by the methods of spherical triangles, or by some special table derived from these (such as we have, for each degree of right ascension and of declination, scrupulously computed to 32 degrees on each side of the equator, destined for this business), or also mechanically by a great globe, such as I have ready, six feet in diameter, most aptly covered on all sides with brass and most perfectly round in every part. But it would be too long in this place to recount all my astronomical instruments and their use: which, sixteen in number, exactly elaborated in magnitude of structure, variety of form, and aptness of manifold use, I possess in place of a splendid treasure (for they have been prepared with no slight diligence, labour, and cost); of which most are of solid metal, but some, although of sure and durable wooden material — being lighter, and not, by excessive weight on account of the mass of their size, unfittingly burdening themselves — are composed in manifold fashion by a certain peculiar method, so that they are not even of themselves easily subject to change; nevertheless they are skilfully fortified on the outside with brass laminae, partly that they may turn out suited to the fineness of the division, partly that they may stand the more firmly. And they have a magnitude sufficient for scrupulous distinction, so that some equal six and five cubits, some four and three. For the using of which, and also for reducing into numbers, from triangles, those things which can be demonstrated from observations, I maintain and instruct here with me for the most part eight, sometimes even more, students devoting their effort to the Art of Astronomy; nor do we suffer the convenience of any clear weather, whether by day or by night, to pass in vain, in inquiring into the heavenly phenomena of the Sun, the Moon, the rest of the planets that can be seen, and the fixed stars also.

But concerning the fabric and use of each single instrument, because I have resolved to write a special Book which shall embrace the Mechanical part of Astronomy (on which account also I am now having them all delineated, and their figures engraved on plates of copper or boxwood), I therefore forbear a longer recounting of these things. But when I shall have completed this labour (God granting success), or at least when all the figures shall have been cut, I shall transmit to Your Highness whatever it will be, to be perused and weighed. For then Your Highness will, by a single glance, more easily learn everything than from a long and tedious account of these things. But that these very instruments, which I assiduously use in observing the stars, are very exact, and do not admit even a faintly sensible error — that is proved from this alone, that the place of the same star, detected by several and diversely-fabricated instruments by various methods, falls, for those attending diligently, to exactly the same minute.

But that Your Highness may have some specimen of those things which have hitherto been gained from my observations, I have adjoined an Ephemeris of the Sun's course for this year, derived from my own restitution, and directed to the longitude of 30 degrees, to which Your Highness's dominion of Hesse corresponds. I have noted the seconds, as our Tables in the motion of the Sun (founded geometrically on sure observations) exhibit them — not because that fineness is comprehensible by any instrument in the heaven itself, as Your Highness very well knows, but lest, the seconds being neglected, some ambiguity should arise concerning the minutes. I therefore earnestly ask of Your Highness that you be willing to have this restoration of ours in the numeration of the Sun's course tested by your instruments, and your opinion of it clemently signified to me. But in rightly weighing these things one must presuppose the maximum declination of the ecliptic [the obliquity] to be 23 degrees, 31½ minutes — which we have several times detected with several instruments by a certain peculiar method, not from the maximum altitude of the Sun at either solstice (for this practice, on account of the refraction of the rays, which occurs when the Sun is low near the winter solstice, falls short of the true by more than four minutes in our horizon). That the solar parallax too is everywhere to be added to the observed altitudes, there is no doubt that Your Highness has sufficiently ascertained. The refractions of the solar rays besides — which, when [the Sun is] low, force upon the senses altitudes greater than they really are (and that on account of the diaphanous medium of the Air being a little less transparent than the diaphanous medium of the Aether, as these things are demonstrated in Optics) — are to be avoided, especially if the observed altitudes be less than a third, or at most a half, of the quadrant. For I have, by a certain peculiar industry, found by experience that some perceptible refraction occurs up to nearly this limit. But the closer [the bodies] come to the horizon, the greater refraction they cause, so that the Sun, at its very rising and setting, even in the purest air, appears at least half a degree higher than the truth of the matter allows. So too in the Moon, and the other planets, and the fixed stars, when they are low, their altitudes and declinations and true places are somewhat varied by refraction. Wherefore, at lower altitudes, if the matter is to be handled scrupulously, the observations are not to be trusted too much.

Besides, it makes much difference by what methods the inclination of the equator to the horizon at the place of observation has been detected. For by the meridian altitudes of the Sun — even if they be taken at either solstice, with the parallax also applied — on account of the refraction (of which I have spoken) insinuating itself in the winter-solstitial lowness, this business is by no means despatched with the precision that is fitting. It will therefore be most advisable to seek out studiously the height of the pole from the Pole Star itself, when it is at its maximum and minimum altitude. For since it is of moderate magnitude and sufficiently perspicuous, and describes a small circle about the pole, and on that account does not approach the horizon so much that any refraction alters its true altitude, it is most convenient for despatching this practical operation without any error. If therefore Your Highness should have the maximum or minimum altitude of this Pole Star (I say) adjoined to the rest of your observations, and should make me more certain of it, you will have done a thing much to be wished by me. For thus, applying its distance from the pole (which this year, from our observations, is 2 degrees, 55 minutes, 50 seconds), we shall arrive indubitably at the most exact elevation of the pole and equator of that place in which Your Highness has your observations made, and shall establish a fixed certainty concerning the declinations of the Sun and of the stars, provided the instruments are in good order. Wherefore, if Your Highness shall take into counsel all those things (of which I have already spoken) — namely the parallax of the Sun, the assigned maximum declination, and the elevation of the equator found by the aforesaid method — and shall guard against the complication of the refraction which occurs below the thirtieth degree of altitude, you will assuredly find by experience that the numeration of the Sun's course discovered by us agrees exactly with the celestial appearances.

But how necessary it is, before all else, to have the Sun's motion scrupulously explored, if those things which are sought in the knowledge of the stars are to be restored more surely, Your Highness by no means is ignorant: since without this neither the exact quantity of the year, which is the measure of the motions, nor even the positions of the fixed stars, much less those of the wandering ones (which is evident from these), can be investigated. Inasmuch as the revolution of the Sun stands as the regular norm of all the other celestial revolutions. But what we have hitherto accomplished, with no slight labour and diligence, in the lunar course also (investigated by us in the heavens with no less diligence), and in examining the motion of the rest of the planets, and in rectifying the places of the fixed stars — Your Highness will learn and judge in due time, when everything has been reduced to order and augmented with more observations. For above all I would wish these labours of mine subjected to Your Highness's judgement, because I know Your Highness to be an outstanding artist in this Science, able to judge solidly of these matters.

But I see that, while I converse rather greedily with Your Highness about astronomical affairs, I am overstepping the laws of a letter by excessive prolixity, and detaining Your Highness, occupied in reading these things, longer than is fitting. And so I shall check my pen, and commend Your Highness to God, the Author of celestial as of terrestrial creatures. May He preserve Your Highness, as long as possible, safe and flourishing, both for the protection of his Church — whose care you take, which is now most rare — and of the commonwealth, and for the propagation of the good Arts, and especially of that divine Astronomy. May Your Highness fare well; and if you should deign to answer me anything to these things, entrust it to the bearer of this letter, whose name is Peter Jacobsen [Petrus Jacobi], who was my domestic for some years, and rendered faithful service both in astronomical and in pyronomic [chemical] exercises. And therefore I have judged it surer and more fitting to despatch the business of carrying this letter to Your Highness not through others ignorant of my affairs, but rather through this man, dear and faithful to me, who, where there shall be need, could also relate more than it pleases [me] to comprehend with the pen. To him therefore let Your Highness lend credence in all things, and safely entrust whatever is to be reported back to me. Again may Your Highness fare most happily and most long.

Given at Uraniborg, on the island of Hven in the Danish Sound, on the Kalends of March, in the year 1586. Most devoted to Your Highness, Tycho Brahe.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Wilhelm IV "the Wise," Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel (1532–1592), founder of the Kassel observatory and Tycho's chief astronomical peer-patron; his court mathematician Christoph Rothmann (here named) handled the technical exchange. This is the opening letter of Tycho's printed Epistolae astronomicae (Liber I, Uraniborg 1596) and one of the most important documents of his instrument-making programme: it sets out the Hven establishment, the sextant's evolution, the Wittich episode, and Tycho's solar and refraction theory.
  • Occasion. The exchange was rekindled by a letter Wilhelm IV had written to Heinrich Rantzau (1526–1598), the great Holstein humanist and Danish Statholder (royal vicegerent), a copy of which reached Tycho through Rantzau's son Gerhard Rantzau (1558–1627), then governor (Praefectus) of the newly rebuilt Kronborg castle at Elsinore. Tycho recalls being received by the Landgrave at Kassel "nearly eleven years ago" — i.e. on his 1575 tour.
  • The comet of 1585. Observed by Rothmann from c. 8 October 1585 in the last decan (last 10°) of Pisces (♓), southern latitude; by the Landgrave himself on 19 October in 21° Aries (♈), 4° south latitude. Tycho first saw it 18 October (ten days late, owing to October cloud) and observed it to mid-November. The full treatment is promised in his De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis (the "Work On the New Phenomena of the Aethereal World"), then in press at Uraniborg, which would also treat the 1572 new star in Cassiopeia and three further comets.
  • Paul Wittich. Paul Wittich (Wittichius, c. 1546–1586) of Breslau, the mathematician whose visit to Hven (here "five years ago," i.e. 1580) and subsequent journey to Kassel transmitted Tycho's instrument designs to the Landgrave — the point Tycho is delicately reclaiming here ("from whose prescription Your Highness asserts your instruments were renewed"). The whole sextant discussion is Tycho asserting his own priority for the transversal-point divisions, the parallel-slit pinnules, and the name and fabric of the sextant. (See Thoren, The Lord of Uraniborg, and the Wittich/priority discussion among the works consulted.)
  • The instruments. The letter is the earliest connected description of the Uraniborg instrumentarium later figured in the Astronomiae instauratae mechanica (1598): the three successive sextant designs (single-observer → bifurcated two-observer → the final two-observer cylinder sextant, "four cubits"); the equatorial armillae (armillae aequatoriae — Tycho's own coinage for the instrument measuring right ascension and declination); the simplified armillary of "one and a half circles and a round axle," ten feet in diameter, set on a mound with subterranean steps (the great equatorial armillary in the southern observatory crypt); and the six-foot brass-covered globe. The "sixteen instruments … a precious treasure" and "eight or more students" describe the working establishment c. 1586. The promised "special Book … the Mechanical part of Astronomy" is the Mechanica itself.
  • Technical glossary. Ascensiones Rectas → right ascensions; Declinationes → declinations; Longitudines et Latitudines (ad Eclipticam) → (ecliptic) longitudes and latitudes; Parallaxis → parallax; Refractio → refraction; Armillae aequatoriae → equatorial armillae; Sextans → sextant; Pinnacidia → pinnules (sights); organa/Instrumenta → instruments; restitutio / instauratio → restitution / restoration; Apparentiae → appearances. The obliquity of the ecliptic ("maximum declination of the ecliptic") Tycho gives as 23° 31½′, and the Pole Star's distance from the pole as 2° 55′ 50″ for 1586 — both reproduced as written. Pyronomicis exercitijs = "pyronomic exercises," i.e. chemical/alchemical furnace-work (Paracelsian pyronomia); the bearer Petrus Jacobi = Peter Jacobsen Flemløse, Tycho's chief assistant, served in both astronomy and alchemy.
  • Latin idiom. ad Veritatis normam tanquam Lydium lapidem — "to the standard of Truth as to a Lydian stone [touchstone]," the proverbial assayer's touchstone. calamum inhibebo — "I shall check my pen" (apology for prolixity). Sacris Uraniae register: the building "in honour of Astronomy" is Uraniborg, the "houses dedicated to Urania."
  • Calendar. Dateline Calendis Martij, Anno 1586 = 1 March 1586, Old Style (Lutheran Denmark kept the Julian calendar); rendered with the Roman idiom glossed. Header date agrees with the edition label.
  • Text note. The scattered superscript "A" markers (curasA, depraehenditA, animumA, sibiA, prorogârimA, etc.) are editorial/footnote sigla in the working transcription, not part of the Latin; they are left in the Source block as printed and read through in translation. One word-join in the witness, "Witichiumnon" (= Witichium … non), is likewise a transcription artefact.
