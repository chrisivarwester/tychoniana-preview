The original

TYCHO BRAHE AD IOSEPHUM SCALIGERUM.

S. P.

ILLUSTRIS et Magnifice Vir, Amice inprimis honorande. Redditæ mihi sunt tuæ literæ unâ cum libris mihi dono missis serius quam expectassemus, et ego, quod multis distentus fuerim negotijs atque alias varie praepeditus, nec in promptu esset, qui meas certo referret, tardius etiam quam decuisset, quamque ipse maluissem, rescribo, quod mihi a tua ingenua humanitate condonari cupio. Pro utroque autem libro mihi tam benigne concesso proque tuo non modico labore in excerpendis ijs, quæ apud HIPPARCHUM, et nonnunquam etiam PTOLOMÆUM in hoc negotio de Fixis Sideribus præcipua erant, ijsdemque propria manu in mei gratiam tanta diligentia conscriptis, non possum tibi promeritas satisque dignas agere gratias, nedum referre, neque enim video qua ratione hoc tuum tam eximium in me beneficium compensare valeam, animum tamen gratum, et tui honoris studiosissimum semper me servaturum tibi persuadeas, et si quæ ex mea tenuitate in hisce, quibus utrinque afficimur, studijs proficisci poterint, ea non invite tecum vicissim communicabo. Fateris ipsemet, Vir Excellentissime, multa esse in his, quæ circa Fixas tam ab HIPPARCHO quam PTOLOMÆO consignata sunt, quæ suis numeris rite non constent, nec sibi ipsis consentiant, et sane res ita se habet; ego enim paucissima in ijs reperio, quæ convenienter ad Cœli normam digesta sint, deviatione satis manifesta prorsusque intolerabili plurimis in locis sese prodente, sive id Observationum incuria (quas tamen sic nimis crassas fuisse oportuisset) sive transcriptorum negligentia aut quacunque alia de causa id commissum fuisse existimemus. Si loca Fixarum a me summa diligentia præteritis aliquot annis explorata, pro quibus ipsum Cœlum etiamnum testatur, retro ad tempora PTOLEMAEI et HIPPARCHI, per motum interea ijs competentem, numeraverimus, atque hinc Declinationes, Culminationes, Ortus et Occasus quoscunque ad eadem quibus illi usi sunt Climata deduxerimus, certe rarissime cum eorum annotatis consentire depræhendentur: Et licet nonnunquam concordantia aliqua inveniatur, tamen ea ἐν πλάτει tantum et nimis lato modo sumta est; nullatenus enim admitti poterit stellas irregulariter et diversimode interea motas fuisse, cum omnes uniformem et regularem in uno eodemque Orbe observent progressionem, quod invariata earundem ad invicem distantia et dispositio indubitate ostendit, quæ vel hinc liquet, quod omnes Fixæ, quæ ab HIPPARCHO et PTOLOMÆO in uno lineæ rectæ ductu existere proditæ sunt, etiamnum eundem prorsus tramitem conservent, prout a nobis sedulo disquisitum est. Miror itaque a tam Excellentibus Artificibus non satis accuratam in his præstitam esse diligentiam, cum res sit magni momenti, nec Planetarum motiones certo explorari queant, nisi prius ad amussim præfinita sint Fixarum loca, quod etiam me in ijs, quæ PTOLOMÆUS circa Erraticarum apparentias tradidit, non parum reddit ancipitem quoad exactam illam, quam desidero, rationem. Attamen loca inerrantium stellarum, quæ ex Abaco HIPPARCHI suo Almagesto inseruit, non videntur tantam enormitatem præ se ferre, atque hæc est, quam liber transmissus in plerisque exhibet.

Verum his nunc omissis, quæ limitare, atque quatenus fieri potest in concinniorem rationem redigere, suo forte tempore elaborabimus, aliud est quod nunc potius desiderarem, de quo etiam, si recte memini, prius te rogavi, ut nimirum Æquinoctiorum omnium ab HIPPARCHO observatorum rationes designatas obtineam, ita ut Annis Iulianis retro subactis rite applicentur, et simul quomodo Annus ille respectu bissexti se habuerit, indicetur: Cuperem vero ipsa HIPPARCHI verba, quatenus illa eiuscemodi observationes concernunt, nihil enim horum in libris mihi impertitis invenire licuit; tum quoque si quas alias in his vetustissimas in promptu habes cœlitus deductas animadversiones. Australes Asterismos nobis Europæis hactenus incognitos convenienti diligentia observatos necdum vidi, repræsentarunt quidem circa Polum Austrinum stellas quasdam nobis incompertas Globorum in vestra Batavia confectores; sed nimis ieiuna et parca est ista designatio, crediderim multo plures isthic apparere. Si plenior et accuratior earundem descriptio penes te est, eamque mecum communicare non degravatus fueris, rem admodum acceptam præstiteris. ALBATEGNIUM quoque fidelius quam antea castigatum, si tua opera aliquando obtinuerimus, non parum commodi hisce studijs inde accedet. Utinam Arabicum Exemplum obtinere posses; neque enim dubito quin, cum linguæ illius, uti et plurimarum aliarum usque ad miraculum, apprime gnarus sis, hac in parte plurimum sis huic studio profuturus.

Porro ut rerum mearum statum aliquatenus perspectum habeas, scias me, postquam e loco prioris meæ habitationis discesseram, in Bohemiam ex Cæsareæ Maiestatis clementissima voluntate cogitans, Dresdam Misniæ ubi pervenissem, omniaque in Bohemia ob luem Epidemicam isthic passim grassantem conturbata esse cognovissem, substitisse ibi paulisper, donec mihi ex Aula Cæsarea præscriptum erat, ipsius Maiestatem clementissime cupere, ut alicubi in vicinia me continerem, donec malum illud Epidemicum remitteret. Ideoque huc Wittebergam propter Academiæ celebritatem me aliquantisper recepi. Evocatus autem sum nuper denuo Pragam, ex clementissimo Cæsaris mandato, per literas Amplissimi Domini RUDOLPHI CORADUCIJ Procancellarij Imperialis. Et quamprimum quædam in libris nonnullis, quos Cæsareæ Maiestati humilime offerre decrevi, plane absolvero (quod brevi me effecturum spero) denuo iter Pragense ingredi, atque Cæsareæ Maiestatis benignissimæ voluntati debita subiectione morem gerere in animum induxi. Deus Optimus Maximus successus prosperos et exoptatos largiri dignetur. Ubi vero portum aliquem quietum pro meis studijs, sive isthic sive alibi invenero, plenius te de rebus nostris edocebo, atque de ijs, quæ studia hæc, quibus iucunde utrinque afficimur, concernunt, uberius tecum conferre non intermittam. Interim hæc, ob plurimas occupationes pro præsenti mittendi ad vos literas occasione deproperata, boni consule, inque cœpto erga me favore et amore persiste.

Vale Vir Illustris et Excellentissime, seculi nostri decus.
Dabantur subito Wittebergæ die 17 Aprilis Anni 99, st. vet.
Illustri Magnif. tuæ addictiss.
TYCHO BRAHE, manu propria.

TYCHO BRAHE TO JOSEPH SCALIGER.

Heartiest greeting.

Illustrious and magnificent Man, friend especially to be honoured. Your letter, together with the books sent me as a gift, was delivered to me later than we had expected; and I — both because I have been distracted by many affairs and otherwise variously hindered, and because there was not at hand one who could surely carry mine — write back later too than was fitting, and than I myself should have preferred; which I wish to be pardoned me by your noble humanity. For both books so kindly granted me, and for your no small labour in excerpting what was chief in Hipparchus, and sometimes also in Ptolemy, in this business of the Fixed Stars — and these written out in your own hand, for my sake, with such diligence — I cannot give you the thanks deserved and worthy, much less repay them; nor do I see by what means I might requite this so signal benefit of yours toward me. Yet persuade yourself that I shall always keep a grateful mind, most zealous of your honour; and if anything can proceed from my slenderness in these studies, with which we are both affected, I will not unwillingly share it with you in turn. You yourself confess, most excellent Man, that there are many things in what was recorded about the Fixed [stars] by both Hipparchus and Ptolemy which do not duly tally with their own numbers, nor agree with themselves — and indeed the matter is so; for I find very few in them digested fitly to the norm of the Sky, a deviation manifest enough and altogether intolerable betraying itself in very many places — whether we judge it committed by carelessness of the Observations (which must then have been far too gross), or by the negligence of transcribers, or from any other cause. If we count the positions of the Fixed [stars], explored by me these past years with the utmost diligence (for which the Sky itself still testifies), back to the times of Ptolemy and Hipparchus, by the motion [precession] belonging to them meanwhile, and thence derive declinations, culminations, risings and settings whatsoever for the same Climes they used, they will certainly very rarely be found to agree with their notations. And although some concordance is sometimes found, yet it is taken only broadly [ἐν πλάτει] and in too loose a manner. For it can in no way be admitted that the stars moved irregularly and diversely in the interim, since all keep a uniform and regular progression in one and the same Orb — which their unchanged mutual distance and disposition undoubtedly shows; and this is clear even from this, that all the Fixed [stars] which were reported by Hipparchus and Ptolemy to lie in one straight line still keep the very same track, as has been carefully investigated by us. I wonder, then, that by such Excellent Artificers a diligence not accurate enough was shown in these things — since the matter is of great moment, and the motions of the Planets cannot be surely explored unless the positions of the Fixed [stars] are first precisely predetermined — which also makes me not a little doubtful, in what Ptolemy transmitted about the appearances of the Wandering [stars], as to that exact reckoning which I desire. Yet the positions of the fixed stars which [Ptolemy] inserted into his Almagest from the Abacus [table] of Hipparchus do not seem to bear so great an enormity as this which the book transmitted [by you] exhibits in most places.

But, these things now set aside — which to limit, and so far as can be done reduce to a neater reckoning, we shall perhaps work out in their time — there is another thing I would rather now desire, about which (if I recall rightly) I asked you before: namely that I may obtain the reckonings of all the Equinoxes observed by Hipparchus, set down so that they may be rightly applied with the Julian years reduced backward, and at the same time that it be indicated how that year stood with respect to the bissextile [leap year]. I should wish indeed Hipparchus's own words, so far as they concern such observations — for I could find none of these in the books imparted to me — and also any other most ancient celestial observations of this kind you have at hand. The Southern Asterisms, hitherto unknown to us Europeans, observed with fitting diligence, I have not yet seen: the makers of Globes in your Holland [Batavia] did indeed represent, around the South Pole, certain stars unknown to us; but that designation is too meagre and sparse — I should believe that many more appear there. If a fuller and more accurate description of them is with you, and you would not find it a burden to share it with me, you will do a most welcome thing. Al-Battani [Albategnius] too, more faithfully corrected than before, if we should one day obtain it by your labour, will bring no small advantage to these studies. Would that you could obtain an Arabic copy; for I do not doubt that, since you are excellently skilled in that tongue (as in very many others, to a miracle), you will in this part greatly profit this study.

Further, that you may have my situation somewhat clear: know that I — after I had departed from the place of my former dwelling, intending [to go] into Bohemia by the Imperial Majesty's most clement will — when I had reached Dresden in Meissen, and had learned that everything in Bohemia was thrown into confusion by the epidemic plague raging there far and wide, halted there a while, until it was prescribed me from the Imperial Court that His Majesty most clemently wished me to keep myself somewhere in the neighbourhood until that epidemic evil should abate. And therefore I withdrew hither to Wittenberg for a while, on account of the Academy's renown. But I have lately been summoned again to Prague, by the Emperor's most clement mandate, through a letter of the most ample Lord Rudolf Coraduz, Imperial Vice-Chancellor. And as soon as I shall have quite finished certain things in some books which I have resolved humbly to offer the Imperial Majesty (which I hope to do shortly), I have resolved to set out again on the Prague journey, and to comply with the Imperial Majesty's most kindly will with due submission. May God, Best and Greatest, deign to grant prosperous and longed-for success. And when I find some quiet port for my studies, whether there or elsewhere, I will instruct you more fully of my affairs, and will not cease to confer more amply with you about what concerns these studies, with which we are both pleasantly affected. Meanwhile take in good part these [lines], hurried for the present by the occasion of sending a letter to you, amid very many occupations; and persist in the favour and love toward me you have begun.

Farewell, illustrious and most excellent Man, the ornament of our age.
Given hastily at Wittenberg, the 17th day of April, the year [15]99, Old Style.
Most devoted to your illustrious Magnificence,
Tycho Brahe, in his own hand.

Apparatus

  • Recipient. Joseph Justus Scaliger at Leiden — the chronologist and philologist. This is the earlier of the two Scaliger letters in the literature consulted (cf. the July-1600 precession letter, brahe-t_16000723001 / 16000623001).
  • Significance — Tycho's critique of the ancient star catalogues (a major statement of his fixed-star programme).
  • The Hipparchus/Ptolemy catalogues are corrupt. Tycho agrees with Scaliger that much in Hipparchus's and Ptolemy's fixed-star data "does not tally with its own numbers nor agree with itself," with "intolerable deviation" — from gross observation, transcribers' negligence, or other cause.
  • The precession test. Tycho's method: take his own carefully-determined star positions and reckon them back, by precession, to Ptolemy's and Hipparchus's epochs — they "very rarely agree." The stars cannot have moved irregularly, since their mutual distances are invariant and collinear stars stay collinear — a clean statement of the rigidity of the stellar sphere under uniform precession.
  • Why it matters. "The motions of the Planets cannot be surely explored unless the positions of the Fixed [stars] are first precisely predetermined" — the methodological core of Tycho's whole enterprise (and the ground of his doubt about Ptolemy's planetary data).
  • A textual observation. The star-positions Ptolemy drew from Hipparchus's "Abacus" into the Almagest seem less corrupt than the (more enormous) figures in the book Scaliger sent.
  • Three requests to Scaliger: (1) Hipparchus's own equinox observations, set to reduce against Julian years (with leap-year status) — for chronology/precession; (2) a fuller catalogue of the new Southern asterisms — Tycho has seen only the "meagre" southern stars on the Dutch globe-makers' globes (the Keyser–Houtman southern constellations via the Dutch globes; cf. the globe-maker / Blaeu-at-Uraniborg thread, the letter to Tobias Fischer of 16 May 1600, brahe-t_16000516002) and believes "many more appear there"; (3) a better-corrected al-Battani (Albategnius), ideally an Arabic copy — praising Scaliger's "miraculous" command of Arabic and other tongues.
  • Biographical status report (spring 1599). Tycho's itinerary after leaving Hven/Germany: → Dresden (halted by the Bohemian plague) → Wittenberg ("for the Academy's renown") — now summoned again to Prague by Coraduz's letter, to set out once he finishes the books he means to offer the Emperor (the Mechanica / Progymnasmata presentation set). A precise fix on the pre-Benátky interim.
  • Persons & works named. Joseph Justus Scaliger; Hipparchus, Ptolemy (the Almagest, Hipparchus's Abacus); al-Battani (Albategnius); Rudolf Coraduz (Vice-Chancellor); Rudolf II; the Dutch globe-makers. Places: Wittenberg, Dresden/Meissen, Bohemia/Prague, Holland (Batavia).
  • Calendar. die 17 Aprilis Anni 99, st. vet. → 17 April 1599 [Old Style] (Tycho explicitly marks Old Style at Wittenberg/Saxony); header and label agree.
