The original

SEQVITVR DESCRIPTVM LITERARVM AMPLISSIMI ET GENEROSI VIRI DNI. IACOBI CVRTII, ANTEA PROCANCELLARII IMPERII, AD TYCHONEM BRAHE IN DANIAM SCRIPTARVM ANNO CIϽ IϽ XC.

S. P.

MAGNAS tibi ago gratias, Nobilis æque ac eruditissime vir, quod me non modo literis tuis compellare, sed benevolentiam quoque tuam tam amanter offerre mihi volueris. Ego certe, licet faciem tuam non viderim meliorem tamen tui partem, mentem nempe, ex scriptis tuis, quibus teipsum æternitati jam consecrasti, jam olim et amavi et suspexi, et eius caussa totum te videre, venerari, et amplexari non minus avide concupivi, quam illi olim, qui ex ultimis Hispaniæ finibus ad Livium videndum in urbem profecti sunt. Auxit hoc meum desiderium humanissima hæc tua compellatio, et in tantum auxit, ut, nisi Cæsaris voluntas, et muneris publici, quod gero, ratio prohiberent: ita me DEVS amet, neque itineris intercapedo et molestia, neque suavissimæ conjugis dulcissimorumque pignorum amor et illecebræ me cohiberent, quin arrepto itineris comite communi amico Thaddæo Hageccio ad te in Daniam usque excurrerem. Dicere nequeo, quantopere me delectet cum Hageccio, mente, postqam corpore non possumus, totam Huenam perlustrare, et inprimis exactissima tua observatoria Instrumenta, totum denique Mathematicum illum apparatum vel imaginatione tantum complecti. Quid vero si ipse spectator adessem, teque ipsum omnia non explicantem tantum, sed exercentem videre possem? Sed id quia optare quidem licet, sperare hoc tempore vix (neque enim omnem spem penitus abjeci:) missum jam faciam, et ad Epistolam tuam conversus, paucis ad summa eius capita respondebo. Atque hoc inprimis. Quod judicium meum de scriptis tuis amanter exquiris, quod censuræ meæ, eam quæ tibi cum doctrinis quibusdam viris non tam lis, quam amica est concertatio, subjicis, in eo tantum mihi a te honoris tribui accipio, quanti non facerem, si Reges et Principes de Regnis et Provinciis integris disceptantes, me sibi Judicem constituerent. Ut vero, id quod petis et offers, mihi faciendum sumam, nec eruditio, quæ in me aut nulla est, aut vere exigua, neque modestia mea patitur. Ne tamen ad tam amicam postulationem plane surdus sim, hoc habeto. De scriptis tuis quid in genere sentiam, testabitur Cæsareæ Majestatis privilegium, quod hisce literis adjunctum tibi mitto, cuius ita conscribendi ego Cæsari author, et porro, vera in eo Cæsarem testari, sponsor fui. In specie vero ea quæ de Cometis posterioribus, quotquot ipse observasti, a te tradita solidissimisque rationibus confirmata sunt, talia esse puto, ut de iis ne ambigi quidem amplius ab Eruditis viris queat. Itaque non adducor, ut credam eos qui hac in parte dubios sese adhuc ostendunt, et contra demonstratam veritatem Aristotelis authoritati patrocinari videri volunt, id ita vere sentire. Sed hoc potius eos agere puto, ut, cum omnem moverint lapidem, omniaque undique conquisiverint, quæ Aristoteleam sententiam confirmare, tuam vero labefactare, aut dubiam reddere videantur, ea tibi proponant, tanquam ad Lydium lapidem examinanda, adeoque caussam tibi præbeant, eruditionis tuæ Sole, omnes omnino errorum tenebras non modo, sed vel levissimas nubeculas dissipandi, exactissimamque harum rerum doctrinam posteritati perficiendi. Qui si hac mente tecum certant, næ ego illis quam maxime bene cupio; sin aliud agunt, pertinaciam quidem eorum odio dignam existimo: Id tamen quod pertinaciâ suâ in commune beneficium tibi extorserunt, ipsis valere, vel potius non valere jussis amplector non secus, ac si bona id mente fecissent: de eoque reipublicæ literariæ gratulor.

De prioribus Cometis, quos Regiomontanus et alii observarunt, expecto quæ propediem editurus es, eo usque meam quoque sententiam, an in universum omnes Cometæ ætherei credendi sint, an vero pars ætherei, pars elementares, suspensurus.

Ad ea, quæ novis tuis hypothesibus hactenus objecta sunt, solide mihi videris respondisse, neque ego quicquam in iis absurdum, sed omnia invicem pulcherrime congruentia invenio: an vero hypotheses istæ, id quod in Copernicianis desideratur, præstitutæ sint, ut scilicet exacta Siderum loca ad præterita, præsentia, et futura tempora nobis exhibeant, de eo tum demum judicandum erit, ubi majus illud, quod moliris, Opus, in lucem prodierit. Neque enim dubito, quin in eo opere omnium ætatum quotquot extant exactiores observationes, cum tuis, quas exactissimas esse, certo mihi persuadeo, summâ diligentiâ contuleris, indeque nouam hanc mundi Machinam, divino sane ingenio, extruxeris.

Macte itaque animo, vir nobilissime, et fac ut divinis tuis inventis quam ocyssime fruamur. Etsi enim arduum, et, ut ipse vere scribis, multorum hoc sit opus annorum: spero tamen, imo confido, te maximam jam ejus partem exantlasse, et in recolligendis et ordinandis iis, quæ jam constituisti, tabulisque inde condendis, magis hoc tempore, quam in confirmandis amplius inventis tuis versari. Quâ in re cum discipulorum tuorum operâ magna in parte juvari possis, iterum atque iterum te rogatum volo, ne nos nimium diu suspensos habeas; Vitam tibi ex animo ad Nestoreos usque annos exopto: nôsti tamen quam fallaces sint spes nostræ, et quam immatura plerunque divinis præsertim ingeniis mors obtigerit; Falle itaque moras, et vel non maturo partu, hunc ingenii tui fœtum orbi terrarum êde. Si fata longiorem tibi, quod avidê optamus et speramus, vitam concesserint, poteris et edita recudere, et judicio tandem tuo quoque (nostro enim quin in primâ editione satis facturus sis, nihil dubito) satisfacere. Liberabis interim nos perpetuo metu, ne, dum nimium cunctaris, toto opere frustremur. Ego certe tantam de te, tuisque his hypothesibus spem concepi, ut vel a te, vel a nemine solidam Astronomicæ artis exædificationem expectandam esse pro certo affirmare audeam. Qui enim præter divinam ingenii vim, tot tantisque rebus huc necessariis instructus sit, tot tantosque sumptus, labores et annos contulerit, non est in Europâ (cum Europam dico, totum Orbem dico) qui tibi comparari queat, quisquam. Abest hinc, ita me DEUS amet, omnis adulatio, a quâ et dignitas, quam gero, et gravitas quam cum omnibus tueri laboro, me apud notos atque ignotos, in ignoti præsertim de facie hominis laude, satis superque vindicabunt.

Quæ de exactâ Instrumentorum ad observationes necessariorum constructione et vsu variis in locis, et ad varios a te scripta sunt, summopere me delectârunt. Ego dum hactenus mihi ipsi satisfacere non potui, dum ea quæ non exacta sunt, ne videre quidem, nedum habere patior, omnibus omninô Instrumentis, præter ea, quæ ad voluptatem tantum comparantur, careo. Quadrans Nonnianus multum aliquando me habuit sollicitum: sed quia difficillime, imo fere impossibile est eum conficere, cogitavi ego de aliis mediis, et inveni varia, quorum partem Christophorus Clavius insignis Mathematicus in libello suo de horologiis solaribus per Instrumentum describendis, edidit. Tandem in mentem mihi venit quadrantis cuiusdam constructio, quæ quia doctis quibusdam viris non inelegans visa est, volui eam ad te quoque mittere. Ut tamen ingenue fatear quod res est, quodque verissime affirmas: inventiones hæ omnes minus habent in recessu, quam de se primâ fronte spondent. Gratum tamen mihi erit tuum de hoc ultimo meo invento audire judicium. Ex N. N. Plagiarii tui libello, quem fundamentum Astronomicum inscripsit, unicoque ejus diagrammate, quod Paulo Witichio dedicavit, construxi ego præteritis diebus, cum ob adversam valetudinem publicis negotiis vacare non possem, novam sphæricorum triangulorum doctrinam, in qua per tabulam sinuum, tangentium, et secantium omnes tam rectangulorum quam obliquangulorum casus, sine ulla multiplicatione vel divisione per solam additionem et subtractionem facillime perficiuntur. Eam quoque ad te mitterem, nisi scirem te rem totam, solo eo diagrammate inspecto facile assecuturum. Ex illo enim diagrammate et axiomate a multis jam demonstrato, quod radius sit medius proportionalis inter sinum rectum arcus et secantem complementi, tota ea ratio exstructa est. Quod reliquum est, valere te iterum atque iterum, vir Nobilissime, in multos annos precor, et omnia, quæ a me in te proficisci officia amoris et benevolentiæ possunt, tibi peramanter offero, enixe rogans, quod ultro facere cæpisti, me te ex animo amantem amare pergas.

Datæ Pragæ 28. Junii. Anno 90.
Jacobus Curtius a Senfftenaw.

English translation machine translation, Secure

THERE FOLLOWS A COPY OF A LETTER OF THE MOST EMINENT AND NOBLE GENTLEMAN MASTER JACOB CURTIUS, FORMERLY VICE-CHANCELLOR OF THE EMPIRE, WRITTEN TO TYCHO BRAHE IN DENMARK IN THE YEAR 1590.

Heartiest greeting.

I render you great thanks, sir as noble as you are most learned, that you have been willing not only to address me with a letter of yours, but also to offer me your goodwill so affectionately. For my part, certainly — though I have not seen your face — yet your better part, namely your mind, I have already long since both loved and looked up to, from your writings, by which you have now consecrated yourself to eternity; and for its sake I have longed to see, revere, and embrace the whole of you no less eagerly than did those men of old who set out from the farthest bounds of Spain to come to the city to see Livy. This longing of mine your most courteous address has increased, and increased to such a degree that, were it not that the Emperor's will and the duty of the public office which I hold forbade it — so help me God — neither the distance and trouble of the journey, nor the love and allurements of a most sweet wife and dearest children, would hold me back from snatching up our common friend Thaddaeus Hagecius as my travelling companion and running off all the way to you in Denmark. I cannot say how much it delights me to range over the whole of Hven with Hagecius — in mind, since in body we cannot — and above all to take in, if only in imagination, your most exact observing instruments, and indeed that whole mathematical apparatus. But what if I were present as a spectator myself, and could see you not merely expounding everything, but performing it? Yet because this may indeed be wished for, but at this time scarcely hoped for (for I have not utterly cast away all hope) — I will now let it go, and, turning to your letter, will answer briefly to its chief heads. And this first of all. That you affectionately seek out my judgement on your writings, that you submit to my censure that contest of yours with certain learned men — which is not so much a quarrel as a friendly dispute — in this I take so much honour to be granted me by you as I would not reckon it, were kings and princes, disputing over whole kingdoms and provinces, to appoint me their judge. But that I should take upon myself to do what you ask and offer, neither my learning — which in me is either none, or truly very slight — nor my modesty permits. Yet, lest I be wholly deaf to so friendly a request, take this. What I think of your writings in general, the privilege of His Imperial Majesty will testify, which I send you enclosed with this letter — of whose drawing-up in this form I was the author with the Emperor, and further was the warrant that the Emperor therein attests what is true. But in particular, those things which, concerning the later comets — as many as you yourself observed — have been set forth by you and confirmed by the most solid reasonings, I judge to be such that they can no longer even be called into doubt by learned men. And so I am not brought to believe that those who in this matter still show themselves doubtful, and who wish to seem to be defending the authority of Aristotle against the demonstrated truth, truly think so. Rather I think they are doing this: that, having moved every stone and gathered together from every side all that might seem to confirm the Aristotelian opinion but to undermine yours or render it doubtful, they put these things before you, as if to be tested against the Lydian stone [touchstone], and so afford you occasion — by the sun of your erudition — to dispel not only all the darkness of errors, but even the very lightest little clouds, and to perfect for posterity the most exact doctrine of these matters. If they contend with you in this spirit, then truly I wish them very well indeed; but if they are about something else, I judge their obstinacy worthy of hatred — yet that which by their obstinacy they have wrung from you to the common good, I embrace, with them bidden to fare well, or rather not to fare well, no otherwise than if they had done it with good intent; and for it I congratulate the republic of letters.

Concerning the earlier comets, which Regiomontanus and others observed, I await what you are shortly to publish, and until then I shall hold in suspense my own opinion too — whether all comets are in general to be believed ethereal, or rather some ethereal, some elemental.

To the objections that have hitherto been raised against your new hypotheses, you seem to me to have replied solidly, and I find nothing absurd in them, but everything most beautifully agreeing one with another; but whether those hypotheses are furnished with that which is wanting in the Copernican ones — namely, so as to set forth for us the exact places of the stars for past, present, and future times — of that judgement will only then be possible, when that greater Work which you are labouring at comes into the light. For I do not doubt that in that work you will have collated, with the utmost diligence, the more exact observations of every age, as many as exist, with your own (which I am certainly persuaded are the most exact), and from them will have built up this new Machine of the world, by a truly divine genius.

Be of good courage, then, most noble sir, and bring it about that we may enjoy your divine discoveries as quickly as may be. For although this is an arduous task, and, as you yourself truly write, the work of many years, yet I hope — nay, I am confident — that you have already drained off the greatest part of it, and that at this time you are engaged more in re-collecting and ordering the things you have already established, and in compiling tables therefrom, than in any further confirming of your discoveries. Since in this matter you can in great part be helped by the labour of your pupils, I would have you asked again and again not to keep us too long in suspense. With all my heart I wish you life right up to the years of Nestor; yet you know how deceptive our hopes are, and how untimely a death has for the most part befallen divine geniuses especially. Cheat delay, then, and even by a birth not brought to term, bring forth this offspring of your genius to the whole world. If the fates grant you the longer life which we eagerly wish and hope, you will be able both to reprint what has been published, and at length to satisfy your own judgement too (for that you will satisfy ours in the first edition, I in no way doubt). Meanwhile you will free us from a perpetual fear — that, while you delay too long, we be cheated of the whole work. I, for my part, have certainly conceived such hope of you and these hypotheses of yours, that I would dare to affirm for certain that a solid building-up of the astronomical art is to be expected either from you, or from no one. For one who, besides a divine force of genius, is furnished with so many and so great resources necessary to this, and has devoted so many and so great expenses, labours, and years — there is no one in Europe (and when I say Europe, I say the whole World) who could be compared to you. Far from this — so help me God — is all flattery; from which both the dignity I bear and the gravity which I strive to maintain before all men will, among those who know me and those who do not, and especially in the praise of a man unknown by face, vindicate me well and abundantly.

The things you have written in various places, and to various men, concerning the exact construction and use of the instruments needed for observations, have delighted me in the highest degree. As for me, since hitherto I have been unable to satisfy myself, and since I cannot bear even to see, much less to possess, things that are not exact, I am wholly without instruments — apart from those that are procured only for pleasure. The Nonian quadrant once kept me much in anxiety; but because it is most difficult, nay almost impossible, to construct, I bethought myself of other means, and found various ones, part of which Christoph Clavius, the distinguished mathematician, has published in his little book on describing solar dials by means of an instrument. At last there came into my mind the construction of a certain quadrant, which, because it seemed not inelegant to certain learned men, I wished to send to you as well. Yet, to confess frankly how the matter stands, and as you most truly affirm: all these inventions have less in the recess than they promise of themselves at first appearance. It will nonetheless be pleasing to me to hear your judgement of this latest invention of mine. From the little book of your plagiarist, N. N. — which he entitled Astronomical Foundation — and from its single diagram, which he dedicated to Paul Wittich, I in past days (since on account of ill health I could not attend to public business) constructed a new doctrine of spherical triangles, in which, by means of a table of sines, tangents, and secants, all cases, of right-angled as well as oblique-angled [triangles], are most easily worked out without any multiplication or division, by addition and subtraction alone. This too I would send you, did I not know that you would readily attain the whole matter from inspecting that diagram alone. For from that diagram, and from the axiom — already demonstrated by many — that the radius is the mean proportional between the right sine of an arc and the secant of its complement, that whole method is built up. For the rest, I pray that you fare well again and again, most noble sir, for many years, and I most affectionately offer you all the services of love and goodwill that can proceed from me toward you, earnestly asking that — as you have of your own accord begun to do — you may continue to love me, who love you from the heart.

Given at Prague, 28 June, in the year [15]90.
Jacob Curtius of Senftenau.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Iacobus Curtius a Senfftenaw / Jacobus Curtius = Jacob Kurz von Senftenau (Jakob Kurz zu Senftenau, c. 1554–1594), Imperial Aulic Councillor and (here styled antea Procancellarii Imperii, "formerly Vice-Chancellor of the Empire") Imperial Vice-Chancellor under Rudolf II in Prague — a councillor of "perspicacious genius and keen judgement, not only in political but in mathematical matters" (so Tycho describes him, Opera Omnia VI, p. 287). He was the channel through whom Tycho obtained the imperial printing privilege for his works, and an early Prague advocate whose esteem prefigured Tycho's later move to Bohemia. Tycho printed this letter (with two of Kurz's others) in the Mechanica as testimony to his standing among the learned.
  • Edition / document type. Printed in Tycho's Astronomiae instauratae mechanica (Wandsbek, 1598; = Dreyer, Opera Omnia V/VI), not a chancery register or autograph; the edition records no surviving MS witness. The Latin rubric SEQVITVR DESCRIPTVM LITERARVM … ("There follows a copy of a letter …") is Tycho's editorial heading, reproduced verbatim. CIϽ IϽ XC is the year-numeral 1590 (CIƆ = M = 1000, IƆ = D = 500, XC = 90).
  • ad te in Daniam … excurrerem — written while Tycho was still on Hven (Huena, Hven); the wish to "run off to Denmark" with Hagecius is counterfactual, the duty of office forbidding it.
  • Thaddaeus Hageccius = Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku (Thaddaeus Hagecius, 1525–1600), the Bohemian court physician-astronomer in Prague and Tycho's long-standing correspondent on the 1572 nova and on comets; here named the would-be travelling companion.
  • ad Livium videndum … ex ultimis Hispaniae finibus — the topos (from Pliny, Ep. 2.3 / Jerome) of a Spaniard travelling from the ends of the earth to Rome merely to see the historian Livy; Kurz applies it to his desire to see Tycho. Kept as the classical allusion it is.
  • Caesaris … privilegium / Caesareae Majestatis privilegium — the imperial printing privilege (of Rudolf II) for Tycho's books, which Kurz drafted and stands surety for; he encloses it with this letter. Caesar = the (Holy Roman) Emperor.
  • Comets. Cometis posterioribus … prioribus Cometis, quos Regiomontanus et alii observarunt — "the later comets" (Tycho's own observed comets, chiefly that of 1577 and after, on which Tycho argued the supralunary, aetherei, nature of comets against Aristotle) vs. "the earlier comets" observed by Regiomontanus (Johannes Müller of Königsberg, 1436–1476) and others, on which Tycho was still to publish. The open question Kurz suspends — an … omnes Cometae aetherei … an … pars aetherei, pars elementares ("whether all comets are ethereal, or some ethereal, some elemental") — is preserved as his explicit hedge, not resolved.
  • ad Lydium lapidem examinanda — "to be tested against the Lydian stone," i.e. the touchstone; the assayer's-stone metaphor for putting Tycho's results to the test. Kept with gloss.
  • novis tuis hypothesibus … nouam hanc mundi Machinam — Tycho's "new hypotheses" / "new Machine of the world" = the geo-heliocentric (Tychonic) world-system; id quod in Copernicianis desideratur ("that which is wanting in the Copernican [hypotheses]") flags Kurz's reservation about predictive exactness, set against majus illud … Opus — the great work (the projected restoration of astronomy, the Theatrum astronomiae / planetary tables) still to appear.
  • ad Nestoreos … annos — "to the years of Nestor," i.e. an extreme old age (the Homeric Nestor); a learned wish for long life.
  • non maturo partu … foetum … êde — the childbirth metaphor for premature publication ("even by a birth not brought to term, bring forth this offspring of your genius"); Kurz urges Tycho not to delay. Kept as metaphor.
  • Instruments. Quadrans Nonnianus = the "Nonian" quadrant, the nonius (the diagonal/subdivision device of Pedro Nunes, Petrus Nonius) for reading fractional arc — notoriously hard to make ("almost impossible to construct"). Christophorus Clavius … libello … de horologiis solaribus per Instrumentum describendis = Christoph Clavius (1538–1612), the Jesuit mathematician, and his treatise on tracing sundials by instrument. quadrantis cuiusdam constructio — a quadrant of Kurz's own devising, offered to Tycho with the self-deprecating caveat inventiones hae omnes minus habent in recessu, quam … primâ fronte spondent ("all these inventions have less in the recess than they promise at first sight"). Per glossary, Instrumenta / organa → "instruments," quadrans → "quadrant."
  • N. N. Plagiarii tui … fundamentum Astronomicum … Paulo Witichio dedicavit — "the little book of your plagiarist, N. N." = the Fundamentum astronomicum (Strasbourg, 1588) of Nicolaus Reimers Ursus (Nicolaus Raimarus Ursus / "Bär"), whom Tycho accused of stealing the Tychonic system; Kurz pointedly leaves him anonymous as N. N. ("so-and-so"). The work was dedicated to Paul Wittich (Paulus Witichius, c. 1546–1586), the Silesian mathematician whose prosthaphaeretic methods stand behind the diagram in question. The crux is left as Kurz wrote it: N. N. is preserved, not expanded into a name within the translation.
  • Prosthaphaeresis / spherical trigonometry. Kurz's "new doctrine of spherical triangles" solving all cases per solam additionem et subtractionem ("by addition and subtraction alone," without multiplication or division) is the prosthaphaeretic computing method (per glossary, prosthaphaeresis kept — though Kurz here uses the descriptive phrase, not the term). The stated axioma — radius sit medius proportionalis inter sinum rectum arcus et secantem complementi ("the radius is the mean proportional between the right sine of an arc and the secant of its complement") — is rendered literally; sinus rectus = "right sine," secans complementi = "secant of the complement" (i.e. cosecant in modern terms), kept in period form.
  • Calendar. Dateline Datae Pragae 28. Junii. Anno 90 = "Given at Prague, 28 June [15]90." The edition label marks this n.s. (New Style): as an Imperial councillor in Catholic Prague, Kurz wrote on the Gregorian calendar, 10 days ahead of Tycho's Danish Old Style. Header date follows the dateline as written (28 June); the [N.S.] tag records the style by this chapter's convention, with no silent conversion. No in-letter date discrepancy.
  • Formulae. S. P. = salutem plurimam ("heartiest greeting,"); ita me DEVS amet ("so help me God," twice) kept as the asseveration it is; valere te … precor and the closing me … amantem amare pergas rendered in the affectionate epistolary register.
