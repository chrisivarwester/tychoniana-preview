The original

CLARISSIMO D. D. HENRICO BRUCÆO.
S. P.

EX literis tuis nuper ad me datis, charissime D. D. BRUCÆE, intellexi te recte valere, id quod pergratum cognitu fuit. Quod vero studia Mathematica paulatim negligas, non probo. Nec enim tanta tibi est aetas, ut ea iucundissimam horum tractationem avertere possit, sed quaestuosior est vestra Medicina iuxta vetus dictum: Dat GALENUS opes etc. Et plerumque mortales quo plus senescunt, eo redduntur avariores, ac terrestribus plus proni inhiant, coelestibus posthabitis. Quod tamen nonnihil ad neglecta haec studia redire, et PROCLI Hypotheses explicare decreveris, laudo plurimum, velimque mihi exemplar Hypothesium illarum PROCLI mittas. Nondum enim Latinum habere potui, quamvis satis mihi constet, eas, siquidem Ptolemaicis nituntur fundamentis, coelo minime esse ad amussim consonas. Nos enim aliquot annorum praecedentium exquisitissimis in motu Solis observationibus deprehendimus, nec Ptolemaicam, nec Copernianam Hypothesin ipsius apparentijs satisfacere, longeque aliam esse Apogei et Eccentricitatis rationem, quam uterque horum artificum praesupposuit: ut brevi, Deo volente, ex quamplurimis ijsdemque minime fallacibus observationibus Astronomiae cultoribus palam reddemus. Motus enim Solis adeo exacte nobis constat, ut singulis diebus cum serenitas observationem ratam largitur, nostrae calculationi ex proprijs Tabulis in ipso scrupulo ad amussim correspondeat, differenti tam Alphonsina quam Coperniana ratione saepenumero quamplurimum, adeo ut hoc anno, Sole aequinoctium vernum ingrediente, defecerit in ipsius motu COPERNICI ratio semisse partis, abundaverit vero Alphonsinorum ratio partis quadrante. Paulo enim propiores nunc sunt Alphonsini vero, quam ipse COPERNICUS, quantum ad Solis motum attinet, quamvis ambo intolerabiliter devient. In motu vero Lunae verior est exactiorque Hypothesis COPERNICI. Idque tum crebrae observationes Lunaris cursus circa 90 gradum ab ortu, tum parallaxes ipsius diligentius a nobis examinatae nos saepenumero docuerunt; utut et hae non parum devient. Desideratur enim aliquid in medijs motibus. Et proportio semidiametrorum Epicycli utriusque ad semidiametrum Concentrici paulo aliter limitanda venit, et forte alia adhuc minus perspecta inaequalitas. COPERNICUS enim rudiori Minerva suas observationes perfecit destitutus magnis, exquisiteque e solido metallo elaboratis instrumentis, qualia nos quamplurima, tum varietate structurae, tum magnitudinis et elaborationis affabre et perfecte, ingenti sumtu, industriaque assidua parata habemus, adeo ut neminem etiam priscorum Mathematicorum, utut regijs sumtibus adiutorum (quatenus ex scriptis eorum colligere est) unquam ad coelestes motus perscrutandos tanta magnitudine diligentiaque elaborata organa in usu habuisse, si haec aspiceres, facile nobis concederes. Neoterici vero nec nostra nec superiori aetate necessariam in his elaborandis constructionis formam diligentiamque adhibuerunt, ut ob id mirum non sit, paulatim collabascere Astrorum scientiam, adeo ut ipsius motus nondum exacte constet, nec anni quantitas rata sit, ut nihil nunc dicam de ceteris. Verum in his omnibus alio tempore, Deo volente, tibi alijsque Eruditis nostras observationes et his innixas demonstrationes communicabo, instrumentorumque, quibus in motu coelestium corporum ad amussim perscrutando utor, structuram exactamque elaborationem indicabo, ut constare possit, observationes a nobis habitas, quibus fundantur demonstrationes, exactas esse, nullique fallaciae obnoxias. Nec enim unico, sed pluribus instrumentis, ijsdemque diversimode fabrefactis eandem veritatis metam attingere solemus, modo observationi fides rata habebitur. Si tibi commodum esset hac aestate nos invisere, forte hîc videres, quae frustra remotius quaereres, nec te poeniteret eius itineris, pergratumque mihi advenires hospes, omni officiorum genere excipiendus; tunc coram de quamplurimis colloqui conferreque possemus, afpiceresque ea praesens, quae calamus chartaque non capiunt. Petis, ut Instrumentum aliquod Hypothesium Copernicearum tibi mittam, quale olim apud SCHRECKENFUXIUM vidi, id quod lubens facerem, si tale paratum haberem, neque enim unquam animum adieci ad eiusmodi organa conficienda, quae saltem inventa aliorum explicarent, cum ex nuda delineatione ea facile percipi possint, et adhuc sub iudice lis est, an res ita se habeat, nec ne. Quapropter omnem potius operam impendi in extruendis organis, quibus motus coelestes exacte constarent, ut antecessorum inventa per haec in trutinam vocari possint et ad veritatis metas examinari. Quorsum enim opus erat in nondum satis perspecta re illustranda aliquid laboris et temporis impendere, cum praesertim haec alias leviori negotio facile intelligi possit? Vidi equidem apud SCHRECKENFUXIUM parvulam machinam, qua se Hypotheses COPERNICI effinxisse existimabat. Sed dum rem postea penitius perpenderem, deprehendi, eam structuram minime menti COPERNICI sufficere. Putabat enim is eadem machina, prout inveheretur diversimode, tam Ptolemaicas quam Copernianas Hypotheses exprimi posse, id quod plane est impossibile. Nec facile dabitur, omnes motus Copernianae inventionis unica machina mechanice exprimere, quae sibi ipsi omnibus in partibus correspondeat, praesertim ob incomparabilem Octavae Sphaerae ad orbem Terrae distantiam, utquamvis longe cincinnior sit, paucioribusque indigeat circulis COPERNICI speculatio circa mundi symmetriam quam Ptolemaica. Et per triplicem illum motum unius Terrae plurima in coelo apparentia concinne salvari possunt, quibus plurimi alias excogitati orbes, circulique vix sufficerent, dummodo res se re vera ita haberet, et tales ipsi Terrae congruerent revolutiones, quod nos in duobus motibus, quos huic attribuit COPERNICUS, diversimode se habere, exquisitis observationibus deprehendimus. Motum enim annuum in circulo magno circa Solem Terrae nequaquam adesse, expertus sum in fine anni elapsi 82 et principio 83 ex parallaxibus Martis tum temporis acronychij, et ob id Terrae in minori distantia vicini, quam Sol a Terra removetur, idque iuxta COPERNICI placita ad 1/3 quasi partem, unde maiores parallaxes, quam Sol ipse inducere debuisset, cum tamen longe minores fuisse, creberrimis exquisitissimisque et sibi invicem correspondentibus observationibus eas deprehendimus; ut ob id tota Martis sphaera ulterius removeatur a nobis, quam ipse Sol. Quapropter tollitur illa Hypothesis in totum, qua COPERNICUS Solis motum et Epicyclos omnes reliquorum Planetarum ex Terrae annuo circuitu ingeniose quidem et satis concinne ex ARISTARCHI Samij fundamentis in lucem productis excusare nititur. Alterum vero declinationis motum cum suis librationibus, per quem aequinoctiorum inaequalem anticipationem et obliquitatis Eclipticae mutationem excusat Copernicus, neque ipsi Terrae adesse, diversa, longeque celerior, aequinoctiorum mutatio, quam tantillo etiam tempore admittit COPERNICI ratio, nos certiores reddet, et anni quantitas a REGIOMONTANI observationibus huc usque Copernianae rationi nequaquam consentiens. Ut taceam obliquationem Signiferi etiam nunc esse trinis scrupulis maiorem quam COPERNICUS et antecessores nostri superiori aetate annotârunt. Causam erroris ex Opticis fundamentis alibi declarabo. Et de his omnibus alijsque quamplurimis ad artem ipsam restituendam, motusque coelestes ad amussim in numeros revocandos pleniorem commemorationem, Deo adiuvante, instituam. An vero tertius ille motus, quo quotidiana revolutio salvatur, Terrae et vicinis elementis insit, non facile quis dixerit: Cum eadem ratione in hac atque in primo mobili apparentiae tantae concitationis excusari possint, et minori quidem circuitu, ideoque commodiore cornpendio omnium sphaerarum secundi mobilis repentina ab ortu in occasum restitutio salvari, prout etiam Pythagoreis et Platonicis placuisse video. Verisimile tamen est, Terrae, tamquam gravissimo et densissimo opacoque corpori nequaquam hunc celerrimum inesse motum, sed potius ipsi coelo, cuius forma et materiae subtilitas constantiaque motui perpetuo et quamvis celerrimo aptior est. Frustra itaque expetis, charissime BRUCÆE, eorum in organo ob oculos planiorem repraesentationem, quorum in ipso coelo realis non existit forma. Sed tantum ingeniose ad salvandas aliqua ratione motuum apparentias a COPERNICO excogitata sunt. Si tamen Studiosos ipsius inventis imbuere voles, id per delineationem planam, quantum quidem opus est, facile perfeceris.

Sed est aliud, quod mea plurimum interest, de quo tecum agam. Conscripsi iamdudum quaedam de trium Cometarum intra proximum septennium visorum, et de Novae Stellae Phaenomenis, quibus motus omnes distantiasque et cetera, quae in considerationem veniunt, ex certis observationibus demonstro, et plurima alia tum ad hanc contemplationem tum ad totam artem facientia in medium profero, eaque tandem tum amicorum hîc in patria, tum plurimorum in Germania Angliaque solicitationibus persuasus in lucem edere constitui. Cum autem ob luem Epidemicam, multo iam tempore grassantem, non tutum fuerit, Hafniae apud Typographos nostrates quippiam eiusmodi expedire et nunc alijs quibusdam occupentur impressionibus, ut mature editionem hanc per eos absolvere non liceat, decrevi itaque ipsemet Typographiam quandam, quatenus proprijs meis lucubrationibus excudendis sufficiat, hîc Uraniburgi constituere, ut tam haec, quam alia, quae Deo volente in posterum absolvere conabor, Astronomiaeque cultoribus communicare, hîc penes meipsum Typis nostris mandari possint, eoque pacto figurae demonstrationum numerique et characteres, quibus, uti scis, in hac materia opus est, correctas certiusque adornari, et omnia a meipso ut emendate prodeant cognosci; siquidem pauci reperiantur, qui in his studijs exercitentur, et errores incidentes inter imprimendum rite discernere emendareque possint. Conduxi itaque hunc Typographicum servum, IOACHIMUM nomine, ut mihi hoc in negotio exsequendo inserviat, ablegavique illum nunc Witebergam, ut illinc mihi praelos, omniaque necessaria, quae hîc haberi nequeunt, comparet, cumque ipsi soli, nec mihi satis noto non adeo tutum sit pecuniam concredere, ad Scribam Arcis Witebergensis, cui nomen est VALENTINUS (cuius vitricus filias Regis nostri instituit, quique olim Witebergae familiaris mihi est) 50 Ioachimicos transscripsi, ut is illic exponat pro Typis alijsque ad opus pertinentibus, prout hic noster necessarium esse significaverit. Mitto itaque, charissime BRUCÆE, tibi 50 illos Ioachimicos, quos velim aurigae Witebergensi, quam primum ad vos venerit, tradas, ut is dicto Arcis Scribae VALENTINO eos unâ cum literis meo nomine perferat. Debet autem hic meus Typographus unâ Witebergam ire, vel potius praecedere, si auriga illic non adfuerit, illicque tam diu commorari, donec omnia necessaria ad Typographiam instituendam parata, huc per aurigam eundem abducere possit; si vero quid interea ad te literarum dederit, exigente forte id ipsum rei exsequendae conditione, rogo, ut confestim Hafniam ad Doctorem IOHANNEM FRANCISCUM transmittas, ut inde absque omni mora eas habere possim. Addidi etiam praeter illos 50 Witebergam mittendos, duos Thaleros, partim ut aurigae pro sua opera debitam mercedem hinc numerare possis, partim ut pro superfluo libellos quosdam Mathematicos mihi compares, quales nosti me expetere, ut Astronomica MÆSTLINI, si quae postea ad vos pervenere. Desidero etiam scriptum illud Germanicum MÆSTLINI, quod significasti ipsum edidisse contra restitutionem Calendarij Gregorianam. Cupio enim illud videre, si forte ipsius rationes in anni metis constituendis inde perspicere licuerit. Non usque adeo mihi displicet illa temporis reformatio a GREGORIO Pontifice noviter instituta: quamvis plurimi plus odio Pontificum quam veritatis amore huic contradicunt. Qua in re affectibus veritatis lumen offuscantibus nimium indulgent. Certum est scrupula illa, quae ad 6 horarum completionem deficiunt, super integros dies anni longo jam tempore Solstitia aequinoctiaque per plurimos dies anticipasse, ut non coincidant dies festi amplius cum constitutionibus primitivae Ecclesiae. Quam si rem penitius perspiciamus in Christiana Republica, nulla revera fit dierum differentia. Neque rerum externarum usus necessarius, ubi divinitus non mandantur. Vult enim vulgus contineri in officio, ordine et distributione temporum atque ceremonijs. Praestabat itaque vero propius in his accedere et cum veterum constitutionibus potius concordare, quam longius inde deviare, quamvis hac in ipsa restitutione Gregoriana non habeatur ratio exactae annuae quantitatis. Nam et haec vulgaribus Astronomicis adhuc penitus incognita est. Rogo itaque ut omnium eorum scripta in Germania, qui aliquid hoc in negotio publicârunt, sive pro sive contra, mihi transmittas, ut quomodo invicem et cum veritate consentiant, cognoscere possim. Libellum etiam Sphaericum MÆSTLINI mihi mittas, et SCALIGERUM de emendatione temporum, Gratiopolitanum etiam de Triangulis, operaque Abbatis Messanensis Siculi. Nulla enim eorum habeo. Et oro, ut mihi quam primum haec et quaecunque alia, quibus scis me delectari, illic adipisci poteris, quanticunque constent, compares, Hafniamque ad D. IOHANNEM FRANCISCUM mittas; quicquid exposueris, statim remittam, tibique quacunque in re usus postulaverit, vicissim gratificari non intermittam. Sed video chartam amplius vix sufficere, meque, dum tecum avidius loquor, epistolae metas currenti calamo transscendere. Quare nunc illi frenum inijciam: teque Deo Opt. Max. commendo. Vale et saluta meo nomine clarissimum virum D. DAVIDEM CHYTREUM et D. LEVINUM BATTUM Doctoremque PETRUM MENIUM, ceterosque amicos. De toto negotio mihi rescribe.

T.
TYCHO BRAHE.

To the most distinguished Master Heinrich Brucaeus. Heartiest greeting.

From your letter lately sent to me, dearest Master Brucaeus, I learned that you are in good health, which was most welcome to know. But that you are gradually neglecting the mathematical studies, I do not approve. For your age is not so great that it might turn you from the most delightful pursuit of these things; rather your Medicine is more profitable, according to the old saying: "Galen gives wealth," and so on. And for the most part mortals, the older they grow, the more grasping they become, and the more eagerly they gape after earthly things, with the heavenly set aside. Yet that you have resolved to return somewhat to these neglected studies, and to expound the Hypotheses of Proclus, I praise most highly, and I would wish you to send me a copy of those Hypotheses of Proclus. For I have not yet been able to obtain a Latin one — although I am sufficiently sure that they, inasmuch as they rest upon Ptolemaic foundations, are by no means exactly consonant with the heaven. For we, by the most exquisite observations of the Sun's motion over several preceding years, have found that neither the Ptolemaic nor the Copernican hypothesis satisfies its appearances, and that the reckoning of the apogee and of the eccentricity is far other than either of these craftsmen presupposed: as shortly, God willing, we shall make plain to the cultivators of astronomy from very many and by no means deceptive observations. For the motion of the Sun is so exactly established for us that on every single day, when clear weather grants a sound observation, it corresponds to our calculation from our own Tables to the very arc-second, exactly — often differing very greatly from both the Alphonsine and the Copernican reckoning, so much so that this year, as the Sun entered the vernal equinox, Copernicus's reckoning fell short in its motion by half a degree, while the Alphonsine reckoning ran over by a quarter of a degree. For the Alphonsines are now a little nearer the truth than Copernicus himself, so far as the Sun's motion is concerned, although both deviate intolerably. But in the motion of the Moon the hypothesis of Copernicus is truer and more exact. And this both the frequent observations of the Moon's course around the 90th degree from rising, and its parallaxes more diligently examined by us, have many times taught us — even though these too deviate not a little. For something is wanting in the mean motions. And the proportion of the radii of each epicycle to the radius of the concentric must be set a little otherwise, and perhaps there is yet another, less perceived, inequality. For Copernicus completed his observations with too rough a craft [rudiori Minerva], being without great instruments wrought exquisitely out of solid metal, of which we have very many ready — both in the variety of their construction and in their magnitude and workmanship, skilfully and perfectly [made], at enormous cost and with unremitting industry — so much so that, if you were to look upon these, you would readily grant us that not even any of the ancient mathematicians, though aided by royal expense (so far as one can gather from their writings), ever had in use instruments wrought to such magnitude and accuracy for scrutinising the celestial motions. But the moderns, neither in our own age nor in the one before, applied the form and the diligence of construction needful for working these things out, so that it is no wonder the science of the stars has gradually crumbled, to such a degree that not even its [the Sun's] motion is yet exactly established, nor is the quantity of the year sure — to say nothing now of the rest. But concerning all these matters, at another time, God willing, I will communicate to you and to other learned men our observations and the demonstrations resting upon them, and I will set forth the structure and exact workmanship of the instruments which I use in scrutinising the motion of the celestial bodies to the very mark, so that it may be established that the observations made by us, on which the demonstrations are founded, are exact and liable to no deception. For it is our custom to reach the same goal of truth not with a single instrument but with several, and those fashioned in diverse ways, provided sound trust be placed in the observation. If it were convenient for you to visit us this summer, perhaps you would see here what you would seek in vain further afield, nor would you regret that journey, and you would come to me as a most welcome guest, to be received with every kind of courtesy; then we might converse and confer face to face about very many matters, and you would behold in person those things which pen and paper do not contain. You ask that I send you some instrument of the Copernican hypotheses, such as I once saw at Schreckenfuchs's, which I would gladly do if I had such a one ready — for I have never set my mind upon constructing instruments of that kind, which would only display the inventions of others, since they can easily be grasped from a bare drawing, and the case is as yet before the judge [sub iudice lis est] whether the matter stands so or not. Wherefore I have rather spent all my effort on building instruments by which the celestial motions might be exactly established, so that through these the inventions of our predecessors may be called to the balance and tested against the marks of truth. For to what purpose was it to spend any labour and time in illustrating a matter not yet sufficiently understood, especially when this can otherwise easily be grasped with lighter trouble? I did indeed see at Schreckenfuchs's a little machine, by which he supposed he had figured forth the hypotheses of Copernicus. But when afterwards I weighed the matter more deeply, I found that this construction is by no means adequate to the mind of Copernicus. For he supposed that by the same machine, according as it was driven in diverse ways, both the Ptolemaic and the Copernican hypotheses could be expressed — which is plainly impossible. Nor will it readily be granted that all the motions of the Copernican invention can be mechanically expressed by a single machine that corresponds to itself in all its parts, especially because of the incomparable distance of the Eighth Sphere from the orb of the Earth; even though, as regards the symmetry of the world, the Copernican speculation is far more elegant and needs fewer circles than the Ptolemaic. And through that threefold motion of the single Earth very many of the appearances in the heaven can be neatly saved, for which the many orbs and circles otherwise devised would scarcely suffice — provided the matter truly stood so, and such revolutions agreed with the Earth itself: which we, by exquisite observations, have found to stand otherwise in the two [of the] motions that Copernicus attributed to it. For that the annual motion in the great circle about the Sun is by no means present in the Earth, I have proved at the end of the past year [15]82 and the beginning of [15]83 from the parallaxes of Mars, at that time acronychal [rising at sunset], and on that account nearer the Earth, at a lesser distance than the Sun is removed from the Earth — and indeed, according to the tenets of Copernicus, nearer by about a third part — whence [there should have arisen] greater parallaxes than the Sun itself ought to have induced; whereas in fact we found them [to be] far smaller, by the most frequent and exquisite observations corresponding mutually to one another; so that on that account the whole sphere of Mars is removed further from us than the Sun itself. Wherefore that whole hypothesis is overthrown by which Copernicus strives — ingeniously indeed and neatly enough — to account for the Sun's motion and all the epicycles of the remaining planets out of the Earth's annual circuit, the foundations of Aristarchus of Samos being brought back into the light. And as for the other motion, of declination with its librations, by which Copernicus accounts for the unequal anticipation of the equinoxes and the change in the obliquity of the Ecliptic — that this too is not present in the Earth, a different and far swifter change of the equinoxes, than the reckoning of Copernicus admits even in so little a time, will assure us; and the quantity of the year, [as derived] from the observations of Regiomontanus, in no way agreeing thus far with the Copernican reckoning. To say nothing of the fact that the obliquity of the Zodiac [Signifer] is even now three arc-minutes greater than Copernicus and our predecessors in the former age noted. The cause of the error I will explain elsewhere from optical foundations. And concerning all these matters, and very many others bearing upon the restoration of the art itself and upon recalling the celestial motions exactly into numbers, I will, with God's help, set forth a fuller account. But whether that third motion, by which the daily revolution is saved, is present in the Earth and the neighbouring elements, no one would readily say: since by the same reckoning the appearances of so great a rushing can be accounted for in this [hypothesis] as in the primum mobile, and indeed with a lesser circuit, and therefore with a more convenient economy the sudden restoration of all the spheres of the second movable [heaven] from east to west may be saved — as I see was the opinion of the Pythagoreans and Platonists too. Yet it is likely that this swiftest motion is by no means present in the Earth, as a most heavy and most dense and opaque body, but rather in the heaven itself, whose form and the subtlety and constancy of whose matter is fitter for a perpetual and ever so swift a motion. In vain, therefore, do you ask, dearest Brucaeus, for a plainer representation before the eyes, in an instrument, of those things of which there exists in the heaven itself no real form. But they were only ingeniously devised by Copernicus to save by some reckoning the appearances of the motions. If, however, you wish to ground students in his inventions, you will easily accomplish this — so far as need be — by a flat drawing.

But there is another matter, in which I am most concerned, that I shall take up with you. I composed, some while ago, certain things about the phenomena of the three comets seen within the last seven years, and about the New Star, by which I demonstrate, from sure observations, all the motions and distances and the rest of the matters that come into consideration; and I bring forward very many other things bearing both on this contemplation and on the whole art; and at length, persuaded by the solicitations both of friends here in my homeland and of very many in Germany and England, I have resolved to publish them. But since, on account of the epidemic pestilence now long raging, it has not been safe to dispatch anything of the kind at Copenhagen among our local printers — and they are now occupied with certain other printings, so that it is not possible to complete this edition through them in good time — I have therefore resolved to set up myself, here at Uraniborg, a certain printing-house, so far as suffices for printing my own lucubrations, so that both these and other things which, God willing, I shall hereafter strive to complete and to communicate to the cultivators of astronomy, may be committed to type here with myself, by our own press; and in that way the figures of the demonstrations and the numbers and the characters, of which, as you know, there is need in this subject-matter, may be set out corrected and more surely, and that all things may be known to come forth from me correctly — since few are to be found who are practised in these studies and can duly discern and amend the errors that occur in the course of printing. I have therefore hired this typographic servant, Joachim by name, to serve me in carrying out this business, and I have now sent him off to Wittenberg, to procure for me thence the presses and all the necessaries that cannot be had here; and since it is not very safe to entrust money to him alone, who is not sufficiently known to me, I have transferred 50 joachimsthalers to the secretary of the Castle of Wittenberg, whose name is Valentinus (whose stepfather instructs the daughters of our King, and who was formerly a familiar of mine at Wittenberg), that he may lay them out there for the type and the other things pertaining to the work, as this man of ours shall have signified to be necessary. I therefore send to you, dearest Brucaeus, those 50 joachimsthalers, which I would wish you to hand over to the Wittenberg carrier as soon as he comes to you, that he may carry them, together with a letter in my name, to the said Castle-secretary Valentinus. Now this printer of mine ought to go to Wittenberg together [with the carrier], or rather to go ahead, if the carrier should not be there, and to tarry there so long until, all the necessaries for setting up the printing-house being ready, he may bring them hither by the same carrier; but if meanwhile he should send any letter to you — the very condition of carrying out the business perhaps requiring it — I ask that you forward it at once to Copenhagen, to Doctor Johannes Franciscus, so that I may have it thence without any delay. I have added too, besides those 50 to be sent to Wittenberg, two thalers, partly that you may pay out from here to the carrier the wage due for his labour, partly that with the surplus you may procure for me certain mathematical booklets, such as you know I seek — like the astronomical [works] of Maestlin, if any have lately reached you. I also want that German writing of Maestlin which you indicated he had published against the Gregorian restoration of the Calendar. For I desire to see it, in case I might perceive from it his reckonings in fixing the marks of the year. That reform of time newly instituted by Pope Gregory does not displease me so very much — although very many contradict it more out of hatred of the Popes than out of love of truth. In which matter they indulge too much in passions that darken the light of truth. It is certain that those scruples [fractions] which fall short of the completion of the 6 hours, over the whole days of the year, have for a long time now made the solstices and equinoxes fall earlier by very many days, so that the feast-days no longer coincide with the constitutions of the primitive Church. But if we look into this matter more deeply, in the Christian Commonwealth no real difference of days arises. Nor is the observance of external things necessary, where they are not divinely commanded. For the common crowd wishes to be kept to duty, order, and the distribution of times, and to ceremonies. It was therefore preferable in these matters to come nearer to the truth and rather to concord with the constitutions of the ancients than to deviate further from them — although in this very Gregorian restoration no account is taken of the exact quantity of the year. For this too is as yet utterly unknown to the common run of astronomers. I therefore ask that you forward to me the writings of all those in Germany who have published anything in this business, whether for or against, so that I may learn how they agree with one another and with the truth. Send me also the Spherical booklet of Maestlin, and Scaliger On the Emendation of Times, the Grenoble man [Gratiopolitanus] too On Triangles, and the works of the Sicilian Abbot of Messina. For I have none of them. And I beg that, as soon as you can obtain them there, you procure for me these and whatever else you know delights me, at whatever they cost, and send them to Copenhagen to Master Johannes Franciscus; whatever you lay out, I will at once repay, nor will I fail to gratify you in return in whatever matter your need shall require. But I see that the paper now scarcely suffices any longer, and that I, while I talk with you too eagerly, am overstepping the bounds of a letter with a running pen. Wherefore I will now put a bridle on it: and I commend you to God, Best and Greatest. Farewell, and greet in my name the most distinguished man Master David Chytraeus and Master Levinus Battus and Doctor Petrus Menius, and the rest of my friends. Write back to me about the whole business.

T.
Tycho Brahe.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Henricus Brucaeus = Heinrich Brucaeus (Hendrik van den Broeck, 1530/31–1593), professor of medicine and mathematics at the University of Rostock, where Tycho had studied; a senior scholarly friend. This chapter's person register lists him among Tycho's German-academic correspondents (Rostock).
  • Date / place. The source carries no dateline and no place — it ends simply "T. TYCHO BRAHE." The edition label is s.l. (sine loco, "without place"), and the identifier encodes only the year, 1584 (the day-component is 0000). The header therefore asserts only "[1584]" with the bracketed note that the source supplies no day-date, rather than manufacturing one. The standard scholarly dating is spring 1584 (Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII, p. 80; cf. Mosley, Bearing the Heavens; Thoren, Lord of Uraniborg, who place it in spring 1584). That secondary date is not in the source text and so is kept out of the header, recorded here instead. No O.S./N.S. tag is given because the source supplies no day to qualify; Tycho in Denmark wrote Old Style.
  • Significance. This is the much-cited letter in which Tycho first reports to a colleague that the Mars parallax measured at the 1582/83 opposition was far smaller than the Copernican annual orbit requires, which he takes to overthrow the Earth's annual motion. The passage is the documentary anchor for the genesis of the Tychonic system; the translation preserves the empirical, still-tentative register (he is reporting a result and its inference, not yet announcing a finished world-system).
  • Salutation. S. P. = Salutem plurimam (dicit), "(sends) heartiest greeting". Clarissimo D. D. Henrico Brucaeo: D. D. = Domino Doctori, "to Master Doctor"; rendered "to the most distinguished Master Heinrich Brucaeus."
  • Astronomy — technical terms: parallaxis → parallax; apogaeum/eccentricitas → apogee/eccentricity; motus → motion; hypothesis → hypothesis (kept); epicyclus → epicycle; concentricus → concentric; medii motus → mean motions; octava sphaera → Eighth Sphere (the fixed stars); primum mobile / secundum mobile → kept Latin (the prime mover / second movable heaven of the scholastic cosmos); obliquitas Eclipticae / obliquatio Signiferi → obliquity of the Ecliptic / of the Zodiac (Signifer, "the sign-bearer," = the zodiac); aequinoctiorum anticipatio → anticipation (precession) of the equinoxes; anni quantitas → the quantity (length) of the year; acronychius → acronychal (rising at sunset, i.e. at opposition) — glossed in brackets.
  • Measurement idiom. in ipso scrupulo ad amussim — "to the very arc-second, exactly"; scrupulum here = arc-second / minute fraction (the period term for a sexagesimal fraction of a degree); ad amussim ("to the carpenter's line") = "to the very mark / exactly," a recurring Tychonic tag for instrumental precision, kept consistent. semisse partis = half a degree (pars = degree); partis quadrante = a quarter of a degree; trinis scrupulis = three arc-minutes. scrupula illa, quae ad 6 horarum completionem deficiunt (in the calendar passage) = the fractional residue short of the full 6 hours (the ¼-day surplus of the tropical year over 365 days) — "scruples/fractions," kept as the period term.
  • rudiori Minerva — proverbial ("with rougher Minerva," i.e. with cruder skill/equipment); kept and glossed "with too rough a craft."
  • sub iudice lis est — Horace, Ars Poetica 78: "the case is [still] before the judge," i.e. the matter is unsettled; retained as the embedded tag with a gloss.
  • Copernican motions. Tycho counts the three Copernican terrestrial motions: (1) the annual motion in the circulus magnus about the Sun; (2) the motion of declination with its librations (Copernicus's mechanism for variable precession and changing obliquity); (3) the daily rotation. The translation keeps his careful gradation: he holds (1) disproved by the Mars parallax, (2) contradicted by the equinox/year evidence, and (3) merely undecided ("no one would readily say") — leaning to a moving heaven on physical grounds. No hedge is sharpened: non facile quis dixerit, verisimile tamen est are rendered "no one would readily say," "yet it is likely."
  • Aristarchus of Samos — Aristarchi Samii: the ancient heliocentrist whose "foundations" Tycho says Copernicus revived.
  • Persons named in the business section.
  • Proclus — the Neoplatonist; his Hypotyposis astronomicarum positionum ("Hypotheses [of the astronomers]"), which Brucaeus meant to expound; Tycho wants a Latin copy.
  • Schreckenfuxius = Erasmus Oswald Schreckenfuchs (1511–1579), mathematician (Freiburg/Tübingen); Tycho recalls a planetary model-machine he saw at his house.
  • Maestlinus = Michael Maestlin (1550–1631), the Tübingen astronomer (later Kepler's teacher); Tycho asks for his astronomical works, his Sphaera booklet, and his German tract against the Gregorian calendar.
  • Scaliger de emendatione temporum = Joseph Justus Scaliger, De emendatione temporum (1583).
  • Gratiopolitanus de Triangulis = "the Grenoble man on Triangles" — most likely Bartholomaeus Pitiscus is anachronistic here (1595); the period reference is to a trigonometric work by a writer styled Gratiopolitanus (of Grenoble, Gratianopolis). Identity not certain from the text; flagged rather than asserted.
  • Abbas Messanensis Siculus = "the Sicilian Abbot of Messina" — Tycho wants his works; the precise figure is not specified in the letter and is left unidentified rather than guessed.
  • Ioachimus — the hired printer ("typographic servant") sent to Wittenberg to procure the press; the Uraniborg printing-office Tycho is here founding (1584) would produce De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis and the Epistolae.
  • Valentinus — the secretary (scriba) of Wittenberg Castle, a former Wittenberg acquaintance, whose stepfather tutored the Danish king's daughters; entrusted with the 50 joachimsthalers.
  • Iohannes Franciscus — Johannes Franciscus (Hans Frandsen / Johannes Franciscus Ripensis), Tycho's Copenhagen forwarding contact.
  • David Chytraeus (1530–1600), Rostock theologian-historian; Levinus Battus (Levinus Battus, Rostock physician); Petrus Menius — Rostock friends to be greeted.
  • Coinage. Ioachimici → joachimsthalers (the Joachimsthal silver coin, the "thaler"); Thaleri → thalers (kept distinct as the source distinguishes the 50 Ioachimici from the additional 2 Thaleri).
  • Calendar passage. Tycho's measured approval of the Gregorian calendar reform (1582) is notable for a Lutheran: he separates the astronomical correctness of dropping the accumulated surplus from confessional opposition ("more out of hatred of the Popes than love of truth"), while noting the reform still does not embody the exact length of the year. No date is converted; the discussion is doctrinal-astronomical, not a dateline.
  • Negation check. The pivotal scientific clauses turn on negations, rendered count-for-count without addition or removal: Motum … annuum … Terrae nequaquam adesse ("the annual motion is by no means present in the Earth"); cum tamen longe minores fuisse — the observed parallaxes were far smaller, not greater, than required; neque ipsi Terrae adesse (the declination-motion likewise not present in the Earth); non facile quis dixerit (of the daily motion, "no one would readily say" — left open, not denied). These are the clauses whose sense the whole letter rests on, and the polarity is preserved exactly.
  • Orthography. As-printed early-modern forms retained in the Source block: BRUCAeO/BRUCAeE (ae-ligature), ijs/alijs/proprijs (ij for ii), afpiceres and cornpendio (printer's f-for-s and rn-for-m misreadings in the source transcription) — these last two are left as printed in the Source block; read aspiceres ("you would behold") and compendio ("economy") respectively, which the translation follows.
  • Correction. Five word-level corrections collated against Dreyer, TBOO VII pp.78-80: struehirae emended to structurae (p.78); the inserted first name Henrico removed from two body addresses to Brucaeus where Dreyer prints the surname alone (p.78, p.80/81), with the corresponding Heinrich removed from the English; exacto corrected to exacte (p.79); and the inserted pronoun ea removed before videres (p.79), with the English clause those things which collapsed to what accordingly.
  • Correction. Restored Dreyer's reading in the sentence sending the joachimsthalers to Brucaeus a second time: removed the duplicated first name 'Henrico'/'Heinrich' (not present in Dreyer's mid-letter apostrophe, which repeats only 'BRUCAEE') and restored the omitted sum '50' before 'illos Ioachimicos' / 'those... joachimsthalers' (Dreyer, TBOO VII, p. 81).
