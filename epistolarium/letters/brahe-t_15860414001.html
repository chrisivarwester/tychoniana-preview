English translation machine translation, Probable

To the steadfast, Our trusty and well-beloved Tycho Brahe, Lord of Knudstrup. — Wilhelm, by the grace of God Landgrave of Hesse, Count of Katzenelnbogen, Dietz, Ziegenhain, and Nidda, etc.

Our gracious greeting beforehand, Our trusty and well-beloved. We have received your letter, dated the 1st of March, and read it, and understood from it, among other things, that the King's Majesty of Denmark's governor [Statthalter] in Holstein, Heinrich Rantzau, forwarded to you our letter — the one we had addressed to him concerning the comet last seen [des letzt erschienen Cometens]. That you have now, in response, so willingly sent us your observations of that same comet — for that we graciously give you our thanks; and we will not conceal from you that, as soon as we had seen your observations, we summoned our mathematician, and compared his observations of the same comet [Obseruationes eiusdem Cometae] with yours, and found that, after the due equation of times and longitudes [aequation temporis et Longitudinum], the two sets of observations throughout scarcely differ from one another by more than one minute — which is truly a great thing, and a sign that both our and also your instruments are exact and good, and that the observers, too, were diligent and had good eyesight. Our instruments, namely the quadrant and sextant that our mathematician uses, are not so large as yours, but are only four feet [in size] [quadrupedalia]; and it seems to us, too, that were they larger, they would be the less serviceable, and on account of their bulk [propter molem] the harder to handle. Yet we should be glad to see and examine your instruments too, especially the globe; and we wish you, too, that you had your dwelling in a place less exposed to the vapours of the south [in loco Meridionalis vaporibus minus obnoxio], so that you might observe these things the better. Now as for our sending you our observations of the new star toward Cassiopeia [Obseruationes nouae Stellae ad Cassipeiam], and likewise of the other comets and of the Sun [Solis] — the case is that at the time, when those observations were made, our instruments had not yet been so carefully calibrated as they are now set in order [angerichtet] through the indications of Wittich [WITTICHII] and the diligence and industry of our clockmaker Master Just Bürgi, who is, as it were, another Archimedes in his ingenuity [qui quasi indagine alter Archimedes ist]. Be that as it may, we have granted your messenger sent to us [leave] to copy those out and to bring them to you. But as concerns the aforesaid new star [praedictam nouam Stellam], we have been able to detect in it no parallax at all [gar keine Parallaxin], and also no motion at all, neither in longitude nor in latitude, but only along a straight line, in altitude or in depth [gar keinen motum weder in Longitudinem noch Latitudinem, sed tantum in recta Linea in Altitudinem aut profunditatem]; whereas in the great comet that appeared in the year [15]77, we had at that time no court mathematician [aulicum Mathematicum], and therefore we could not properly observe its parallax. But as you may see from our present mathematician's observation, he too will not have it that the comet last now appeared had any parallax whatever. Therefore [Ergo] the philosophical foundation [fundamentum Philosophicum] is void — the one that the comets ought to be generated in the highest region of the air, below the circles of the Moon [in summa Regione Aëris infra circulos Lunae]. Likewise, too, we find, not only from our former but also from our present mathematician's most diligent observations, that in truth the fixed stars [Stellae fixae] stand much in another position than they are noted in the Tables [Tabulis]; for many of them we have found to be by several degrees otherwise, both in longitude and in latitude [tam in Longitudine, quam in Latitudine]; added to which the latitude — which nevertheless, by the consensus of all astronomers, ought to be invariable [accedit quod Latitudo, quae tamen consensu omnium Astronomorum debebat esse inuariabilis] — has shifted in many, indeed in almost all of them very sensibly [valde sensibiliter], nay by nearly half a degree and more. Now whether this has come about through a fault of the printers [vitio Typographorum], or whether the fixed stars have some proper motion of their own [in se proprium aliquem motum], we leave to you to judge — as the foremost mathematician of this age, who himself also takes the matter in hand and understands it, and is not a mathematician under the roof [a closet astronomer], as most are. As concerns our instruments, we let your messenger see them all, who will be able to report to you about them; for the sextant we truly do not let be despised, inasmuch as several times we ordered our mathematician to sight two stars through it — whereupon, after we ourselves had taken the distance of the two [distantiam vtriusque], shifted the instrument, and let him sight anew, he sighted them so sharply that within the first, second, and third observation [intra primam, secundam et tertiam Obseruationem] not a quarter of one minute of difference was to be felt. Therefore, when there is an error, one must, in our opinion, not seek it so subtly in the centre of vision [in centro visus], but much rather in the craftsman [beym artifice]. Further, we have very gladly heard your judgement concerning the inquiry into the true altitude of the pole or of the equator [iudicium de inquirenda vera Altitudine Poli vel Aequatoris]; for we too have found that if one will seek these things from the greatest altitude of the Sun in either tropic [ex Altitudine Solis maxima in vtroque tropico], it does not at all agree with the observation of the circumpolar stars. On that account we have, through our mathematician, had several stars that do not set [quae non occidunt] observed in the meridian on this side of and beyond the pole [in Meridiano cis et vltra Polum], as you may see herewith; from which we have taken our polar elevation of this place, and the things that pertain thereto [Eleuationem Polarem huius loci et quae his accidunt]. Likewise, too, for some years past we have found a very sensible refraction [Refractionem valde sensibilem], both in the Sun and in the stars near the horizon [in Sole et Stellis circa Horizontem]; and once, too, we found setting Venus [Venerem occidentem] (which we observed most diligently) to remain stationary [stationariam] for very nearly a quarter of an hour, so much so that at that time nothing was moved by the sweep of the primum mobile, and it still appeared elevated by two degrees and more above the horizon, when in reality it was just as far below the horizon, and then it vanished as if in an instant [adeo vt eo tempore nihil raptu primi mobilis moueretur, et adhuc appareret Eleuata duobus et amplius gradibus supra Horizontem, cum totidem reuera esset sub Horizonte, deinde autem quasi ad momentum euanuit]. All of which would have seemed miraculous, had not Witelo, in his Optics, supplied the explanation of them [Quae omnia videbantur miraculosa nisi Vitellio in suis Opticis harum rationem praebuisset]. We also send you herewith — yet in good confidence, in which you are also to keep it and not yet for the present divulge it [noch zur zeit nicht spargiren], but only compare it with your own observations [nur mit Ewren Obseruationibus conferiren wöllet] — the observations of several notable stars, as our mathematician observed them both in the meridian [in Meridiano] and also by the sextant [per Sextantem]. If you now have a mind to compute, you may readily find from them the true places, both of longitude and of latitude [loca vera tam Longitudinis, quam Latitudinis]; in our globe they match well. But our mathematician is yet to reform all these by calculation [per calculum reformiren], for the instruments — be they made as carefully as they will — are not to be trusted to a hair's exactness [adamussim]. All of which we would not, in turn, withhold from you in reply to your letter; with the gracious intention that you should, in turn, send us your observations, and likewise the true places of our observations [loca vera nostrarum Obseruationum], as you find ours against yours by calculation [per calculum], so that we may collate it with our own. The rest you will gather from the enclosed letter of [our] mathematician [Caetera ex adiuncta Mathematici Epistola intelliges]. And we are at all times well disposed toward you with gracious goodwill.

Given in our fortress [of] Kassel, the 14th of April, in the year [15]86.

Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse.
Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse.

[Postscript:] Postscript. Although We say in the letter that We had handed Our messenger sent to us the observations to copy, yet since they were rather laborious to discern, We have therefore had the said observations — both of the comets and of the Sun, so many of them as We still have to hand — copied out by another, and We herewith send them over to you. But as concerns the observation of the fixed stars, We have noted into it the places of longitude and latitude, as Our globe gives them. But Our mathematician is yet to compute them all, when he obtains besides the rest, which he has hitherto been unable to observe on account of the vaporous sky; We believe, however, that there will be no great difference between the two sets of observation. The eye [of the Bull, i.e. Aldebaran], the right shoulder of Orion [Betelgeuse], the Lesser and Greater Dog [Procyon and Sirius] — these We have had observed not only by their distance from one another and their meridian latitude, but also by Our minute- and second-clocks, which give quite exact hours and from meridian to meridian often do not vary by even one minute; their time of culmination in the meridian We have observed quite [accurately] several times over, and from it taken their longitude, or rather their mensuration of the heaven, so that in Our opinion We are sure of them. Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse.

Apparatus

  • Sender. Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel (1532–1592), "the Wise," the founder of the Kassel court observatory and Tycho's chief astronomical correspondent among the German princes. The full titulature (Katzenelnbogen, Dietz, Ziegenhain, Nidda) is the standard Hessian chancery style; preserved by this chapter's convention
  • Recipient address. Dem Vesten … Vester Lieber Besonder — the princely chancery address-formula; vest ("steadfast, firm") is the honorific for an untitled nobleman, and Vester Lieber Besonder is rendered "Our trusty and well-beloved" by this chapter's convention Herrn zu Knudstrup — Tycho's hereditary seat at Knudstrup in Scania.
  • The "A" markers in the source. The base text (the 1596 Epistolae astronomicae print, as transmitted in the working transcription) carries scattered capital A glyphs at certain word- and line-junctions (e.g. KNUDSTRUP.A, StadthalterA, abgefertigtenA). These are structural/transcription artefacts (line- or column-break markers), not part of the German text; reproduced verbatim in the Source block by this chapter's convention(2), and silently ignored in the translation.
  • Persons named.
  • Henrich Rantzow = Heinrich Rantzau (1526–1598), the royal Statthalter (governor) of the Danish king in Holstein, humanist and astrological patron, who acted as the intermediary relaying Wilhelm's letter to Tycho. Kön. W. zu Denmarck = "the King's Majesty of Denmark" (Frederik II).
  • WITTICHII = Paul Wittich (c. 1546–1586), the Silesian mathematician who had worked at Uraniborg in 1580 and then at Kassel (1584), carrying Tycho's instrument-design principles to Bürgi; durch angeben des Wittichii = "through the indications/instructions of Wittich."
  • M. Just Burrjj = Jost Bürgi (1552–1632), Wilhelm's clockmaker and instrument-maker (also "Joost/Jobst Bürgi"); Vhrmacher = "clockmaker." qui quasi indagine alter Archimedes — indagine (ablative of indago, "investigation, ingenuity") gives "another Archimedes, as it were, in his ingenuity," Wilhelm's famous compliment.
  • vnser Mathematicus / aulicus Mathematicus — the court mathematician, i.e. Christoph Rothmann (named only by office here, not by name); the adiuncta Mathematici Epistola ("enclosed letter of the mathematician") is Rothmann's accompanying letter.
  • Vitellio = Witelo (fl. 1270s), author of the Perspectiva (here called Optica), the standard medieval optical authority; Wilhelm invokes him for the theory of atmospheric refraction that explains the anomalous setting of Venus.
  • Latin code-switching. The letter constantly switches into Latin for technical nouns and whole clauses; retained italics on first use by this chapter's convention The longest fully-Latin stretch (the setting-Venus / primum mobile / Witelo passage) is translated in full and the Latin flagged inline.
  • Technical terms (editorial glossary). Obseruationes → observations; Quadrant / Sextant → quadrant / sextant; Globus → globe; Instrumenta → instruments; Parallaxin (acc. of parallaxis) → parallax; motum … in Longitudinem / Latitudinem → motion in longitude / latitude; Stellae fixae → fixed stars; Refractionem → refraction; Eleuatio Polaris → polar elevation (altitude of the pole); aequatio temporis → equation of time(s); loca vera → true places; primum mobile → kept (the outermost sphere driving the daily rotation). adamussim = "to the line, exactly to a hair's breadth" — rendered "to a hair's exactness."
  • quadrupedalia — "four-foot [instruments]," i.e. of about four feet in dimension; Wilhelm contrasts the modest size of the Kassel quadrant and sextant with Tycho's larger instruments.
  • Mathematicus vnderm Dach — literally "a mathematician under the roof," i.e. a purely theoretical/closet astronomer who does not observe; the bracketed gloss "[a closet astronomer]" is editorial.
  • Symbols / glyphs. None present (no planet or zodiac glyphs); celestial bodies are named in words (Solis, Venerem, Cassipeiam).
  • Scientific substance (for the apparatus record, not interpolated into the translation). This is the celebrated letter in which Wilhelm reports two of the Kassel programme's headline findings: (1) the comet of 1585 (and the 1572 nova ad Cassiopeiam) showed no parallax, refuting the Aristotelian fundamentum philosophicum that comets are sublunary atmospheric exhalations; and (2) the fixed-star latitudes have shifted by up to half a degree from the received Tables — Wilhelm leaves open whether this is vitio Typographorum (printers' error) or a real proper motion, and refers the question to Tycho. (Mosley, Bearing the Heavens, identifies this as the origin of the fixed-star-latitude question in the printed correspondence.) These points are rendered faithfully from the text; the contextual gloss is not added to the body.
  • Negation check. The clauses carrying legal/scientific force are preserved one-for-one: wöllen euch nicht verhalten ("we will not conceal"); gar keine Parallaxin … gar keinen motum (no parallax … no motion at all); wil der auch nicht, das … Parallaxin gehabt habe ("he too will not have it that [the comet] had any parallax"); lassen wir warlich nicht verachten ("we truly do not let [the sextant] be despised"); so subtil nicht suchen ("not seek it so subtly"); gar nicht … eintreffen ("does not at all agree"); noch zur zeit nicht spargiren ("not yet … divulge"); adamussim nicht zu trawen ("not to be trusted to a hair's exactness"); nicht wolten verhalten ("we would not … withhold"). No negation added or dropped.
  • Calendar / place. Datum … Cassel am 14 Aprilis, Anno 86 = "Given at Kassel, 14 April [15]86." Wilhelm and the Kassel court used the Julian calendar (Old Style), as did Tycho; so 14 April 1586 is O.S. The incoming letter it answers is dated den 1 Martij (1 March), likewise O.S. The edition label records only the place ("Kassel"); the header date is taken from the dateline by this chapter's convention(1). The double subscription line (WILHELM LANDTGRAFF ZU HESSEN twice) is as transmitted; reproduced.
  • §1 Dreyer collation. German closing postscript, restored from Dreyer VI, pp. 50–51 (dropped by the working transcription); star-names and one adverb disambiguated against Dreyer's facing Latin (VI, p. 53).
