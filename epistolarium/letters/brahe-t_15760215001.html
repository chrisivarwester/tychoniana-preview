IOHANNES PRATENSIS AD TYCHONEM BRAHE.

GENEROSE Domine TYCHO, Amice observande, præ gaudio exsilij, quam primum legi in tuis, ea quæ de Serenissimi Regis nostri Clementissima voluntate refers, quæ etiam Magnifico Domino Legato Gallico, communi Amico nostro, qui ex asse tuus est, mox legenda exhibui; is pari mecum lætitia inde perfusus est, et utrique nostrum Genius pariter inspiravit, hæc non sine bonis ominibus fieri; judicavimusque ambo, imo certo statuimus, hæc singulari Numinis provisu Regi in mentem venisse, ut te tam benigne et liberaliter ad manendum in patria, adeoque sua sponte invitaret, idque non levibus adductus rationibus. Neque etiam dubium quin serio hoc agat, et forsan plura animo premit, quam in continenti promere voluit; scis Regum petita esse postulata. Et sane decet te, imo debes, mi TYCHO, debes, inquam, in gratiam Optimi Regis et Patriæ communis, cui nati sumus, et quæ multum iuris in nos sibi vendicat, propositum tuum mutare, atque Regiæ voluntati obtemperare. Habes brevibus quo DANZÆI et mea vergat sententia, plura et validiora intelliges ubi adfueris. Quam hæc tuorum necessariorum et aliorum nobilium, qui te mallent politicum per omnia sequi splendorem, atque eadem agere quæ ipsi, animos percellent, ubi intellexerint Regem tibi et tuis studijs tantâ clementiâ favere, ut ea benigne promovere, atque in hoc regno liberaliter sustentare velit, idque non rogatus, motu proprio, benignissime offerat. Quid nunc dicturi sunt proximi quique tui, quid frater, quid alij? scis quid loquar et quare. Sed desino plura, siquidem intra diem unum vel alterum, uti polliceris, huc venturus sis. Et sane oportet te reditum ad Regem maturare, atque quam citissime ad postulata eius (neque enim solummodo, uti prius dixi, petita sunt) respondere: nosti enim quam expedite Rex omnia vult fieri, quamque cito absque lenta procrastinatione. Adsis itaque nobis, exoptatissime TYCHO, hîc Haffniæ bonis avibus, vel potius Deo duce, qui hæc consilia, quæ nunc animo volvimus, gubernet ac felicitet, atque eodem ductore, mox Regem accede, eiusque votis tua obsequentissime coniunge: sic enim stat sententia, sic vult Apollo, sic suadet Urania, sic Mercurius suo caduceo imperat. Num adhuc dubitas? sed pluribus de his, uti dixi, coram, nihilque ambigo, quin ubi DANZÆUM et me audiveris, manibus pedibusque in nostram iturus sis sententiam. Vale, et hæc pauca, pro tempore, boni consule: volui potius pauca quam nulla scribere, ut de nostro quod expetis consilio eo citius aliquid cognosceres, sicque promptius tuum conformare possis, nec decuit hunc famulum, qui mihi tuas reddidit, absque responso reverti, quem vel in via te obviam habiturum, vel etiam Knudsdorpij itineri accinctum, reperturum existimo. De tuis vero, non est quod sis solicitus, eas in aliorum manus venturas, nam statim ac Domino Legato illas perlegeram, rupi et combussi, uti mandâras, nec ullus per nos ea de re quidpiam subolfaciet, antequam ipsemet hæc propalari volueris.

Iamque iterum vale, salutata a me Matre tua Nobilissima et Fratre STENONE, quam officiosissime.
Datæ Haffniæ festinanti calamo die 15. Februarij horis matutinis Anno 1576.
Iohannes Pratensis.

In hunc modum Excellentissimus ille PRATENSIS, bonæ recordationis, tam Magnifici Domini Legati Gallici, quam suam mihi apperuit sententiam, eandem postea cum simul essemus, pluribus exposuit et confirmavit; visumque est utrique eorum, ut omnino has a Clementissimo Rege oblatas conditiones susciperem, atque in Patria, relicto priori proposito, manerem , inque eius et Regis honorem, quæcunque pro virili præstare possem, sedulo molirer. Certo enim sibi persuadebant, hæc, Deo sic volente et regente, qui corda Regum in sua habet potestate, fieri. Subiunxit vero inter alia PRATENSIS, domum plane Philosophicam in illa Insula exædificandam esse, a vulgaribus differentem quæque hinc inde habeat inscriptiones Gnomologicas, ex antiquorum Philosophorum et Poëtarum scriptis petitas: et sigillatim recitavit LUCRETIJ versus, qui in initio libri secundi habentur:

Suave, mari magno turbantibus æquora ventis,
E Terra alterius longum Spectare laborem.

Et quæ sequuntur, usque in hos versus quos etiam memoravit:

At nil dulcius est, bene quam munita tenere
Edita doctrina sapientum templa serena,
Despicere unde queat alios, passimque videre
Errare, atque viam palantis quærere vitæ,
Certare ingenio, contendere nobilitate,
Ad Summas emergere opes, rerumque potiri.

Hæc et similia parietibus domus intrinsecus appingenda esse aiebat, quorum etiam authorem et monstratorem futurum pollicebatur. Ego vero mox Regem Serenissimum, qui tunc Friderigsburgum reversus erat, accedens, eius Clementissimæ voluntati me libenter acquieturum submisse indicavi. Atque mox, dum ibi adessem, conscriptæ et subsignatæ sunt literæ, salarium annuum ex ærario Regio mihi deputantes, idque die 18. Februarij. Adjecta sunt sequentibus annis alia beneficia Regia, et liberaliter exercitijs nostris Astronomicis prospectum.

Extructa itaque est in eadem Insula Arx Uraniburgica, una cum ijs, quæ huc requirebantur, et confecta ibique disposita sunt Instrumenta Astronomica varia et magnitudine atque materiæ soliditate præpollentia, quemadmodum in libro nostro de Astronomiæ Mechanica parte non saltem organa a me constructa, sed et ipsa ædificia, atque alia huc facientia exprimuntur. Quibus de causis Anno demum 21 a fundatione huius arcis, qui erat CHRISTI 1597, omnia isthic a me extructa deseruerim, atque in Germaniam me cum tota familia et apparatu illo Astronomico contulerim, non est huius loci referre, nimisque longum foret; habet etiam rationes cur lubentius id subticeam. Sed ad rem, et ea quæ de PRATENSE. Interim dum ego ædificationes novas in Insula illa adorno, et ob id rarius Haffniam venio, accidit ut optimus et eruditissimus ille PRATENSIS, proh dolor, fatis concesserit, magno sui non saltem apud me, sed et alios quam plurimos, relicto defiderio; erat enim omnibus ob morum suavitatem, probitatem et candorem, eximiamque humanitatem charissimus. Vixit is saltem usque in 32 ætatis annum, adeoque sub ipso iuventutis flore expiravit, idque morte festina et inopinata. Cum enim aliquandiu continuis defluxionibus ad pulmones et tussi vehementi laborasset, tandem in auditorio Academiæ Haffniensis, cum publice doceret, atque tunc ALCINOUM de Philosophia Platonica explicaret, de repente in eam, qua laboravit, incidens tussim, vena aliqua intrinseca illi rumpebatur, cumque sanguinem copiose per fauces effluentem, aqua frigida, quam studiosi mox attulerunt, ea nimirum in fauces manibus iniecta, sedari non posse animadvertit, mortemque instare sentiens, hæc ultima verba prolocutus: “IESU FILI DEI VIVI, miserere mei, et suscipe animam meam”, mox exanimis manibus suorum auditorum exceptus, et in ædes suas cadaver cum lacrymis et luctu reportatum, atque paucis post diebus honorifice in templo summo Haffniæ sepultum. Aberam ego tunc in Schania, neque satis cito de hoc dolendo casu resciscere potui ut sepulturæ interessem. Sed nihilominus, aliquot post dies, cum ex dolore, quem ob immaturam et improvisam intimi huius mei amici mortem conceperam, me collegissem, Epitaphium, quod illi vivo, biennio antea promiseram (se enim non diu supervicturum ipsemet iam antea præsagiebat) confeci, quod etiam publice in templo laminæ orichalcicæ inauratæ et eleganter circumquaque exornatæ, quam in eos usus parâram, affigere constitueram, nisi alia quædam ingrata intervenissent. At cum in huius præstantissimi viri, qui patre Gallo, quondam CHRISTIERNI secundi, Danorum Regis, postea captivi, Secretario in Cancellaria, uti intellexi, Gallica, matre vero Dana oriundus erat, et in omni Philosophiæ genere excellenter versatus, mentionem hîc, datâ operâ, ut eius memoriam hoc loco recolerem, incidi, lubet hoc ipsum, quod ei tunc concinnavi Epicedium, hîc apponere, utut id extra propositum videbatur, sed quacunque tandem ratione eximij huius viri, meique dum in vivis esset, amici optimi, memoriam conservare, atque ad posteros transmittere licitum arbitror, praesertim cum et is Astronomiæ studijs, de quibus hisce Epistolis affatim agitur, præclare imbutus fuerit. Quæ etiam omnia ob id hîc commemoro, ut constare possit meis lectoribus, quî factum sit, quod non plures in posterum ab hoc Doctissimo PRATENSE literæ subsequantur, eo nimirum nimis matura morte, quod dolendum, nobis prærepto. Nunc igitur Epitaphium, de quo dixi, subijciam, posteaque ad alias Epistolas doctorum virorum aliunde mihi scriptas, continuandas pergam.

JOHANNES PRATENSIS TO TYCHO BRAHE.

Noble Master Tycho, friend to be honoured: leaping for joy, the moment I read in your letter what you report concerning the most clement disposition of our Most Serene King, I straightway laid it before the Magnificent Lord French Ambassador too — our common friend, who is yours through and through — to read; and he was filled with a gladness equal to mine, and to each of us our guardian spirit alike whispered that these things are not coming about without good omens. We judged, both of us — indeed we held it for certain — that by a singular foresight of the Divine Will this has entered the King's mind, that he should invite you, so kindly and so generously, and moreover of his own accord, to remain in your homeland, and that he was moved to it by no slight reasons. Nor is there any doubt that he is in earnest in this, and perhaps presses more in his mind than he chose to put forth on the spot; you know that what kings request are commands. And truly it becomes you — nay, you are bound, my Tycho, bound, I say — for the sake of the Best of Kings and of our common homeland, to which we were born and which claims much right over us, to change your design and to comply with the Royal will. You have here in brief which way Dançay's opinion and mine incline; you will learn more, and weightier, when you are present. How this will strike the minds of your kinsmen and of the other nobles, who would rather you pursued in all things the splendour of a public career and did the same things they do, when they have understood that the King favours you and your studies with such great clemency that he is willing kindly to advance them and to maintain them generously in this kingdom — and offers it most graciously, unasked, of his own initiative! What now will each of your nearest say, what your brother, what the rest? You know what I mean, and why. But I make an end of saying more, since within a day or two, as you promise, you will be coming here. And indeed you ought to hasten your return to the King, and to answer his demands as quickly as can be (for they are not merely requests, as I said before): for you know how promptly the King wishes everything to be done, and how speedily, without slow procrastination. Be with us, then, most longed-for Tycho, here in Copenhagen under good auspices — or rather, with God as our guide, who governs and prospers these plans we are now turning over in our minds — and under that same guidance, approach the King at once and join your wishes most obediently to his: for so the resolution stands, so Apollo wills, so Urania counsels, so Mercury commands with his caduceus. Do you still hesitate? But of these matters, as I said, more in person; and I have no doubt that, once you have heard Dançay and me, you will come over hand and foot to our opinion. Farewell, and for the present take these few lines in good part: I chose to write a little rather than nothing, so that you might learn something the sooner of the counsel of ours that you seek, and so might the more readily shape your own; nor was it fitting that this servant who delivered your letter to me should return without an answer — a man whom I reckon you will meet on the road coming toward you, or else find girt for the journey to Knudstrup. As for your letters, you need not be anxious that they will come into other hands; for as soon as I had read them through to the Lord Ambassador, I tore and burned them, as you had charged, and through us no one shall get wind of the matter before you yourself shall wish it made known.

And now again farewell — with my most dutiful greetings to your most noble Mother and to your brother Steen.
Given at Copenhagen, with hurrying pen, on the 15th day of February, in the morning hours, in the year 1576.
Johannes Pratensis.

In this manner that most excellent Pratensis, of good memory, opened to me both the Magnificent Lord French Ambassador's opinion and his own; and afterwards, when we were together, he set it forth and confirmed it at greater length. It seemed best to both of them that I should by all means take up the conditions offered by the Most Clement King, and remain in my homeland, abandoning my former design, and that in his honour and the King's I should diligently strive to perform whatever I could to the best of my power. For they were firmly persuaded that this was coming to pass by God's so willing and governing, who holds the hearts of kings in his power. Pratensis added, moreover, among other things, that a thoroughly Philosophical house should be built on that Island, differing from common ones, and having here and there sententious inscriptions [inscriptiones Gnomologicas], drawn from the writings of the ancient Philosophers and Poets; and he recited in particular the verses of Lucretius that stand at the beginning of the second book:

Sweet it is, when on the great sea the winds are stirring the waters,
to gaze from the land upon another's great labour;

and what follows, on to these verses, which he likewise recalled:

But nothing is sweeter than to hold the serene high sanctuaries,
well fortified by the teaching of the wise,
whence one may look down upon others and see them
wandering everywhere astray, and seeking the path of life as they stray —
contending in talent, striving over rank,
to rise to the highest wealth, and to grasp dominion.

These and the like, he said, ought to be painted on the inner walls of the house; and he promised that he himself would be the author and deviser of them. I, for my part, soon approached the Most Serene King, who had then returned to Frederiksborg, and submissively signified that I would gladly acquiesce in his most clement will. And presently, while I was there, letters were drawn up and subscribed assigning me a yearly salary from the Royal treasury — and that on the 18th day of February. In the following years other Royal benefactions were added, and provision was generously made for our Astronomical exercises.

And so on that same Island the Castle of Uraniborg was raised, together with the things required for the purpose, and various Astronomical Instruments were made and set up there, pre-eminent both in size and in solidity of material — just as in our book on the Mechanical part of Astronomy not only the instruments [organa] constructed by me, but the very buildings and the other things pertaining to it, are set forth. For what reasons, in the twenty-first year at last from the founding of this castle — which was the year of Christ 1597 — I forsook everything I had raised there, and betook myself into Germany with my whole household and that Astronomical apparatus, it is not the place here to relate, and it would be too long; there are reasons, too, why I more willingly pass it over in silence. But to the matter, and to what concerns Pratensis. Meanwhile, while I was adorning the new buildings on that Island, and on that account came more rarely to Copenhagen, it happened that that best and most learned Pratensis — alas! — yielded to the fates, leaving great longing for himself not only in me but in very many others as well; for he was most dear to all for the sweetness of his character, his uprightness and candour, and his exceptional kindness. He lived only into his thirty-second year, and so breathed his last in the very flower of youth, and that by a hasty and unlooked-for death. For when he had laboured for some while under continual fluxes upon the lungs and a violent cough, at last in the lecture-hall of the University of Copenhagen, while he was teaching publicly and was at that time expounding Alcinous on Platonic Philosophy, suddenly falling into the cough from which he suffered, some inner vein burst in him; and when he perceived that the blood flowing copiously through his throat could not be stanched by the cold water which the students at once brought — flung, that is, into his throat with their hands — and, feeling death at hand, he uttered these last words: "JESUS, SON OF THE LIVING GOD, have mercy on me, and receive my soul"; and at once, lifeless, he was caught in the hands of his hearers, and his body was carried back to his house with tears and mourning, and a few days afterward honourably buried in the cathedral church of Copenhagen. I was then away in Scania, and could not learn of this grievous event soon enough to be present at the burial. Yet nonetheless, some days afterward, when I had recovered myself from the grief I had conceived at the untimely and unforeseen death of this my most intimate friend, I composed an Epitaph which I had promised him while living, two years before (for he himself had already foreboded that he would not long survive); and I had resolved to affix it publicly in the church upon a gilded brass plate, elegantly adorned all round, which I had prepared for that use, had not certain other unwelcome matters intervened. But since I have here lighted — of set purpose, that I might revive his memory in this place — upon the mention of this most outstanding man, who was sprung of a French father (formerly, as I understand, Secretary in the French Chancery to Christian II, King of the Danes, afterward a captive) and a Danish mother, and was excellently versed in every kind of Philosophy, I am pleased to set down here this very Dirge [Epicedium] which I then composed for him — though it seemed to be beside my purpose; but by whatever means at last I judge it permissible to preserve the memory of this exceptional man, and my best friend while he was among the living, and to transmit it to posterity, especially since he too had been splendidly imbued with the studies of Astronomy, of which these Letters treat abundantly. And all this I commemorate here for this reason: that it may be plain to my readers how it came about that no further letters from this most learned Pratensis follow hereafter — he having been snatched from us, alas, by too early a death. Now therefore I shall append the Epitaph of which I spoke, and afterward shall proceed to continue with the other Letters of learned men written to me from elsewhere.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Iohannes Pratensis = Johannes Pratensis / Hans Philipsen (1543–1576), professor of medicine at the University of Copenhagen and one of Tycho's closest friends. This is the letter by which he and the French ambassador pressed Tycho to accept Frederik II's offer; Tycho's appended commentary records that he did, and recounts Pratensis's death in 1576 — the reason no later letters from him appear in the collection.
  • Editorial structure. The file contains two strata: (1) Pratensis's actual letter of 15 Feb 1576 (down to the subscription "Iohannes Pratensis"); (2) a long retrospective commentary by Tycho himself, written when he assembled the 1596 Epistolae (it speaks of abandoning Hven "in the year of Christ 1597" — see calendar note), narrating his acceptance of the King's offer, the founding of Uraniborg, the death of Pratensis, and his intention to append an epitaph. The Lucretius verses are quoted within Tycho's commentary as the inscriptions Pratensis proposed for the house.
  • Serenissimi Regis nostri — Frederik II of Denmark (r. 1559–88), who in 1576 granted Tycho the island of Hven; the "conditions offered" are the lifelong grant that founded Uraniborg.
  • Magnifico Domino Legato Gallico … DANZAeI / DANZAeUM — Charles de Dançay (Carolus Danzaeus, c. 1510–1589), the long-serving French resident ambassador at the Danish court and a humanist patron; here Tycho's and Pratensis's ally in urging him to stay. DANZAeI (genitive), DANZAeUM (accusative) are kept in the source block; the name is normalised to "Dançay" in the translation.
  • Fratre STENONE — Steen Brahe (1547–1620), Tycho's younger brother; Stenone is the Latin ablative of Steno = Steen.
  • Matre tua Nobilissima — Tycho's mother, Beate Bille (1526–1605).
  • Knudsdorpij — Knudstrup (Knutstorp), the Brahe family seat in Scania; here the goal of the messenger's journey.
  • Schania — Scania (Skåne), then Danish.
  • Friderigsburgum — Frederiksborg (Hillerød), the royal castle to which Frederik II had returned.
  • Arx Uraniburgica — the Castle of Uraniborg on Hven.
  • libro nostro de Astronomiae Mechanica parte — Tycho's Astronomiae instauratae mechanica (Wandsbek, 1598); organa = instruments.
  • Anno demum 21 a fundatione … qui erat CHRISTI 1597 — Tycho dates his abandonment of Hven to "the 21st year from the founding," "the year of Christ 1597." This dates the commentary, not the letter; reproduced as written, not reconciled with the conventional account of his final departure (he left Hven in 1597, then Denmark thereafter; the apparatus does not "correct" the count). Per the edition's convention, no date is silently converted; the letter's dateline is 15 Feb 1576 (O.S.).
  • Calendar. Letter dateline: die 15. Februarij … Anno 1576 — 15 February 1576 (O.S.); the salary letters were subscribed die 18. Februarij (18 February 1576, O.S.). Denmark used the Julian calendar throughout. The header date is the letter's own dateline, 15 February 1576.
  • Classical / rhetorical flourishes. sic vult Apollo, sic suadet Urania, sic Mercurius suo caduceo imperat — "so Apollo wills, so Urania counsels, so Mercury commands with his caduceus": a humanist piling-up of the god of prophecy, the Muse of astronomy (Urania — note the resonance with the future Uraniborg), and the herald-god, kept as figure. manibus pedibusque ("hand and foot") is a classical idiom for whole-hearted assent (Terence/Cicero), translated literally with the idiom preserved.
  • inscriptiones Gnomologicas — "sententious inscriptions" (Gk. gnōmologia, collections of maxims/gnomae); the moralising mottoes Pratensis proposed for the walls. The term is glossed, not anglicised to a false cognate.
  • Lucretius. The verses are De rerum natura II.1–2 (Suave, mari magno…) and a six-line block beginning At nil dulcius est… (II.7–11 + II.13), quoted from Tycho's commentary as the proposed wall-inscriptions; kept as verse by the edition's convention As transcribed, the second block runs II.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 13 — Lucretius II.12 (noctes atque dies niti praestante labore, "striving night and day with surpassing toil") is not present in this source block; the transcription jumps from Certare ingenio, contendere nobilitate (II.11) directly to Ad Summas emergere opes, rerumque potiri (II.13). The translation mirrors the source as transcribed and renders only the six lines given; nothing answering to the omitted II.12 ("night and day") is supplied (an earlier draft had imported it from the full Lucretian text — corrected). Whether II.12 dropped out in Tycho's quotation, in Dreyer, or in the working transcription is left open here, not silently restored. The translation renders the sense line-for-line without metre. Note the early-modern orthography retained in the source block (Spectare, Edita, passimque); Edita … templa serena = "the serene high sanctuaries" (Lucretius's templa serena of the wise).
  • ALCINOUM de Philosophia Platonica — Alcinous, the Didaskalikos (Handbook of Platonism), then commonly ascribed to "Alcinous"; Pratensis died while lecturing on it.
  • patre Gallo … CHRISTIERNI secundi … Secretario in Cancellaria … Gallica — Tycho records that Pratensis's father was a Frenchman who had been Secretary in the French Chancery to Christian II (r. 1513–23, deposed and long imprisoned: postea captivi, "afterward a captive"), and his mother Danish. Christierni secundi is the period Latin form of Christian II.
  • Epitaphium … laminae orichalcicae inauratae — a verse epitaph on a gilded brass plate (orichalcum, brass/latten); Epicedium ("Dirge") is the funeral poem Tycho says he will append. The epitaph/epicedium text itself is not present in this source block — Tycho announces it ("Now therefore I shall append the Epitaph…") but it would follow as a separate item; flagged so the reader does not expect it here.
  • Greek/symbols. No Greek or planetary/zodiac glyphs occur in this letter. The dying words "IESU FILI DEI VIVI…" are Latin (echoing the Jesus Prayer), kept and translated.
  • Transcription (adopted readings). The working transcription carried four manifest digitised text layer artifacts — stray-space and run-together errors with no counterpart in the printed Dreyer text: manerem, (→ manerem,), transm ittere (→ transmittere), conceperam,m e (→ conceperam, me), and prae sertim (→ praesertim). These are silently restored to the correct printed forms in the Source block above (by the edition's convention, the text-of-record is Dreyer, not the digitised text layer working file); none affects sense. Flagged here rather than reproduced, since they are transcription noise, not features of the printed source.
  • Negations. non … venturas … in aliorum manus / rupi et combussi — the assurance that the letters will not fall into others' hands because Pratensis tore and burned them: a single clear negation, reproduced exactly. nec ullus … subolfaciet, antequam … volueris — "no one shall get wind of it before you wish it": one negation, preserved.
