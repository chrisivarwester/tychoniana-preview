CLARISSIMO VARIOQUE DOCTRINARUM GENERE ERUDITISSIMO EXCELLENTISSIMOQUE VIRO DOMINO THADDÆO HAGGECIO, PHILOSOPHIAE ET MEDICINAE DOCTORI EXIMIO, AULAe CÆSAREAe MEDICO, AMICO SUO INPRIMIS COLENDO.

S. INCREDIBILI laetitia sum perfusus, clarissime et amantissime mi THADDÆE, cum tuas ultimas die 10 Ianuarij huius anni mihi perscriptas litteras, iam nuper Haffniae in ultimis comitijs per vicarij Regij in Holsatia ministrum accepissem. Metuebam enim prius, ne aliquid tibi humanitus accidisset^, in ea praesertim ingravescente aetate tot periculosis morbis infestato. Siquidem totum pene triennium (ni fallor^) intercessit, ex quo ante has aliquid a te accepi litterarum; nec etiam interea aliunde quippiam de te cognovi. Atque hinc factum est, cur tam diu scriptione supersederim: nunc vero, cum te et vivere et valere, morborumque, qui imminebant, saevitiam divina ope evasisse, ex hisce tuis intelligam, est, cur non saltem tibi, sed et mihi, qui te semper in intimis habui, magnopere congratuler, Deoque tecum gratias agam, quod te tam benigne hactenus conservarit, utque idem in posterum facere dignetur, ex animo oro. Domini I. CURTIJ Procancellarij, communis nostri amici, obitus et mihi sane non minimum luctum peperit. Nam virum illum, non saltem ob Politica negotia et officij dignitatem, in qua praecelluit, sed et multo magis propter disciplinarum liberalium amorem, adeoque Mathematum (quod alias in Politicis rarum est) eximiam peritiam suspexi dilexique plurimum. Nollem ea, quae in Astronomiae exercitium et honorem elaborari curavit, qualiacunque tandem ea sint, in imperitorum manus pervenire aut prorsus interire, sed optarem potius, ut tibi concederentur, quo, si non per temetipsum, ob senij obstacula, saltem aliorum a te informatorum operâ in usum deduci queant. Enitere igitur, quibuscunque licuerit rationibus, ut ea obtineas. Cuperem vero quam maxime, primum nostrum tomum Progymnasmatum Astronomicorum, quem eidem^ Domino CURTIO (bonae memoriae) transmisi, in potestatem tuam redigi^, ne ad eos forte perveniret, quibus quam minime eius copiam, antequam omnino publici iuris fieret, concedi vellem, quique meis laboribus, uti saepenumero fit, abuti non vererentur. Disquiri inibi videbis in omnium scripta, quotquot de Stella illa nova edita nancisci licuit, tum quoque in tuam Dialexin, prout in eo exemplari, quod Dominus CURTIUS acceperat (siquidem id, quod tibi destinaveram, interciderit) videre licet. Has vero meas animadversiones non alio animo quam ab amico decet, prodeuntes, si aliquando perspexisti vel in posterum lecturus es, averem equidem a te cognoscere, an aliquid in his haberes, quod replicatione dignum censeres. Quod te pro tua ingenuitate libere indicaturum, nec diffido nec illubenter habeo. Quod vero totum illud opus tam diu differatur, quo minus lucem publicam videat, praeter gravia quaedam impedimenta e rebus quibusdam a Philosophia alienis, quae mihi invito multo iam tempore obtigerunt vel potius obstiterunt, accessit et hoc, quod Chartopaeo, quem in moletrina mea pro papyro conficienda hîc conduxeram, fatis concedente, nullum alium eiuscemodi opificem e Germania, utut diligenter ea in re laborârim, per totum hoc elapsum triennium nancisci potuerim. Ideoque chartâ impressoriâ destitutus, typographicas quoque operas intermittere coactus sum. Nunc vero tandem e vicinâ Meckleburgâ Chartopaeum adeptus, molendinum meum huic artificio destinatum redintegrari et ampliari procuro, quo papyri defectus nostros in posterum (uti spero) labores non amplius retardet.

Quae de ♂ stellâ adeo multum in ultimo Acronychio situ ab utroque calculo hactenus usitato digrediente indicâram, eodem modo se habebant, neque huius discrepantiae et aliarum in sydere hoc a coelesti tramite deviationum me caussa latet, ex ipso coelo multis iam annis conquisita patefactaque, partim usitatis hypothesibus, non omnimode eius apparentijs sufficientibus, partim in ijs ipsis, Apogaeo et eccentricitate non ad amussim dispositis. Nam ut de Coperniceis, quae nostrae inventioni quam proxime, licet inverso quodam ordine^, quadrant, solummodo hîc dicam, Apogaeum ♂ in ipsius numeratione plus 5 gradibus est ulterius, quam coelestis norma postulat, et eccentricitas, quam Ptolomaicâ minorem reddidit (forte ut eo probabilius suae speculationi in motu Terrae et eccentricitate ☉ alteratâ patrocinaretur) tantum abest, ut angustior admittenda sit, quod potius nonnihil (pauculis tamen minutis) maiorem quam a PTOLOMÆO factum est, eandem constituere operaeprecium sit, modo Phaenomenis ♂ omnimode satisfiat. Atque vel hoc solo experimento, COPERNICI in motu Terrae et quiete ☉ positio labefactatur. Nam et is cum discipulo RHETICO hinc suam hypotyposin non minimum stabilimenti acquirere opinatus est, quod videlicet ♂ eccentricitas ad analogiam mutatae eccentricitatis ☉ vel potius (ut ille vult) Terrae decrescendo, alterata ipsi crederetur. Id tamen observationum incuriâ potius factum est, quam quod citra omne dubium tale quid coelitus depromi queat. Sic etiam in aliorum Planetarum Apogaeis constituendis (ut de eccentricitatibus, quas a PTOLOMÆO nimis secure mutuatus est, nihil addam) non parum lapsus est magnus ille nunquamque satis laudatus COPERNICUS. Et licet in ☾ multo concinniorem et probabiliorem, quam Ptolomaica erat, adinvenerit Theoriam, tamen ne haec quidem Lunaribus curriculis excusandis per omnia sufficiens est. In novilunijs et plenilunijs ac quadraturis utrisque utcunque locum meretur, utut ne in his quidem requisita praecisione omnia expediat. In locis vero^ 4or, quae iam dictis intermedia sunt, nequaquam apparentias^ salvat, nisi dimidij gradus, cum ☾ circa medias^ elongationes maioris Epicycli, et pene integri gradus, quando prope Apogaeum vel Perigaeum eiusdem versatur, iacturam pro nihilo duxerimus. Unde etiam diutius quam sperassem me ☾ apparens enormitas detinuit, quo minus eius universos errores ratae et undiquaque convenienti hypothesi subijcere licuerit, quam tamen me nunc tandem assecutum confido, plurimorum annorum in diversimodis revolutionum locis huic sufficienter adstipulantibus, quae varijs indiget circularium motuum commixtionibus, ipsam ☾ subitaneam et vagabundam instabilitatem, non improprie repraesentantibus. Verum alius et convenientior (uti spero) dabitur haec tractandi exponendique locus. Ego cum sciam te talibus delectari, non omnino hîc intacta relinquere volui, quo aliquid saltem Astronomici haec nostra Epistola ad rerum Astronomicarum cultorem directa contineret.

Quae in globo Domini CURTIJ ex ligneâ materiâ constante improbas, ea prorsus ita se habent, prout asseris. Frustra enim tales machinae de ligno, quocunque tandem modo composito, praeparantur. Neque enim aëris mutationem, aestatisque et hyemis diversitatem aut temporis diuturnitatem perferunt. Commentaria TRAPEZUNTIJ in PTOLOMÆI magnam Syntaxin et libellum APIANI de Cometâ unâ cum adiunctis libentissime tibi restituerem, cum mihi nulli^ sint usui, si modo idoneum hominem invenire liceret, qui eos certo et tuto, vel Francofurtum ad Typographum, de quo scribis, vel etiam Pragam ad te ipsum perferret: quâ etiam in re posthac, quantum potero, elaborabo. Interea moram (si qua inciderit) candide excusatam habe, meque tui perpetuo fore quam studiosissimum et ad quaevis officia praestanda quam paratissimum, tibi (quod et te facere non dubito) persuasum habe.

Iamque feliciter vale, et senij gravamina patienter sustine.
Uraniburgi 12 Augusti A. C^. 95.
Tui amantiss.
TYCHO BRAHE, manu propria.^

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the most renowned man, most learned in every kind of doctrine, and most excellent, Master Thaddaeus Hagecius, eminent Doctor of Philosophy and Medicine, physician to the Imperial Court, his friend to be honoured above all — greeting.

I was filled with incredible joy, my most renowned and most beloved Thaddaeus, when I received your last letter, written out to me on 10 January of this year, only lately, at Copenhagen during the recent assembly, by the agent of the royal vicegerent in Holstein. For I had feared before this that some mortal thing [εuphemism: that you had died] might have befallen you, especially as, in that advancing age, you are beset by so many dangerous diseases. Indeed almost a whole three-year span (unless I am mistaken) has passed since I last received any letter from you before this one; nor in the meantime did I learn anything of you from any other quarter. And this is why I have so long forborne to write: but now, since I understand from this letter of yours that you are both alive and well, and that by divine aid you have escaped the fury of the diseases that were threatening, there is good reason why I should warmly congratulate not only you, but myself too — I who have always held you among my closest [friends] — and give thanks to God together with you, for having so kindly preserved you thus far; and I pray from my heart that He may deign to do the same hereafter. The death of Master J. Curtius, the Vice-Chancellor, our common friend, has likewise brought me no small grief. For I greatly looked up to that man and loved him, not only for his political affairs and the dignity of the office in which he excelled, but much more for his love of the liberal disciplines, and indeed for his outstanding skill in the mathematical sciences — which is otherwise rare among men in politics. I should be sorry if those things he saw to having worked up for the practice and honour of astronomy — whatever in the end they may be — should pass into the hands of the unskilled or perish outright; I would rather wish that they be granted to you, so that, if not through your own self, on account of the impediments of old age, then at least through the labour of others instructed by you, they may be brought into use. Strive therefore, by whatever means may be allowed, to obtain them. But I should wish above all that the first volume of our Progymnasmata Astronomica, which I sent to that same Master Curtius (of good memory), be brought into your keeping, lest it should perchance reach those to whom I would least wish a copy of it to be granted before it should become public property altogether, and who would not scruple to abuse my labours, as so often happens. There you will see examined the writings of all — as many as it was possible to obtain — that have been published about that new star, and likewise your own Dialexis, as may be seen in that copy which Master Curtius had received (since the one I had intended for you has gone astray). And these observations of mine, issuing forth in no other spirit than befits a friend, if you have at any time looked them over or are going to read them hereafter — I should indeed be eager to learn from you whether you find anything in them that you judge worthy of a reply. That you, in keeping with your candour, will freely indicate this, I neither doubt nor take amiss. But as for the fact that that whole work is so long delayed, the less to see the public light: besides certain grave hindrances from certain matters foreign to philosophy, which have befallen me — or rather have stood in my way — against my will now for a long time, this too was added, that, the papermaker whom I had hired here in my mill for making paper having yielded to fate, I was unable, throughout this whole past three-year span, to obtain any other craftsman of that kind from Germany, however diligently I laboured in the matter. And so, being deprived of printing-paper, I was compelled to suspend the printing-work as well. But now at last, having got a papermaker from neighbouring Mecklenburg, I am seeing to it that my mill appointed for this craft be restored and enlarged, so that (as I hope) the lack of paper may no longer hold back our labours hereafter.

The things I had pointed out concerning the planet Mars [♂], as departing so very greatly in its last acronychal position from both reckonings hitherto in use, stood in the same way; nor is the cause of this discrepancy, and of the other deviations of this star from its celestial track, hidden from me — [a cause] sought out and laid bare from the heaven itself now over many years: partly the customary hypotheses, not in every way sufficient for its appearances, partly in those very [hypotheses], the apogee and eccentricity not being arranged to the exact measure. For — to say here only this much about the Copernican [values], which square most closely with our own discovery, albeit by a certain inverted arrangement — the apogee of Mars [♂] in his [Copernicus's] numbering is more than 5 degrees further on than the celestial standard requires; and the eccentricity, which he rendered smaller than the Ptolemaic (perhaps so that he might the more plausibly support his speculation regarding the motion of the Earth and the altered eccentricity of the Sun [☉]) — so far is it from being that a narrower one ought to be admitted, that rather it is worth the labour to establish that same eccentricity somewhat (yet by a very few minutes) larger than was done by Ptolemy, provided that the phenomena of Mars [♂] be in every way satisfied. And by this one experiment alone, Copernicus's positing of the motion of the Earth and the rest of the Sun [☉] is shaken. For he too, together with his disciple Rheticus, supposed that his model gained no small support from this — namely, that the eccentricity of Mars [♂] was believed by him to be altered, decreasing in proportion to the altered eccentricity of the Sun [☉], or rather (as he will have it) of the Earth. Yet this came about rather through carelessness of observations, than because, beyond all doubt, any such thing can be drawn down from the heaven. So too in establishing the apogees of the other planets (to say nothing of the eccentricities, which he borrowed from Ptolemy too uncritically) that great and never sufficiently praised Copernicus erred not a little. And although in [the theory of] the Moon [☾] he devised a far neater and more probable theory than the Ptolemaic was, yet not even this is in all respects sufficient for accounting for the lunar courses. At the new moons and full moons and at both quadratures it earns its place after a fashion, although not even in these does it dispatch everything with the precision required. But in the four places that are intermediate to those just mentioned, it in no way saves the appearances — unless we shall reckon as nothing a loss of half a degree, when the Moon [☾] is about the middle elongations of the greater epicycle, and of almost a whole degree, when it is engaged near the apogee or perigee of the same. Whence also the apparent irregularity of the Moon [☾] detained me longer than I had hoped, so that I could the less subject all its errors to a fixed and everywhere-consistent hypothesis — which nevertheless I now at last am confident I have attained, the [observations] of very many years in the variously-disposed positions of the revolutions sufficiently corroborating it; [a hypothesis] which needs various commixtures of circular motions, [these] not improperly representing the Moon's [☾] own sudden and wandering instability. But another and more fitting place (as I hope) will be given for treating and setting forth these matters. Since I know that you delight in such things, I did not wish to leave them entirely untouched here, so that this letter of ours, directed to a cultivator of astronomical matters, might contain at least something of astronomy.

The things you disapprove of in Master Curtius's globe, made of wooden material, are entirely so, just as you assert. For in vain are such machines fashioned of wood, composed in whatever manner. For they do not endure the change of air, the difference of summer and winter, or the long passage of time. The commentaries of Trapezuntius on Ptolemy's Great Syntaxis and Apian's little book on the comet, together with their appendices, I would most gladly restore to you, since they are of no use to me, if only it were possible to find a suitable man who would carry them surely and safely, either to Frankfurt to the printer of whom you write, or even to Prague to yourself: in which matter too I shall labour hereafter as far as I am able. Meanwhile hold any delay (if any should arise) candidly excused, and be assured that I shall be perpetually most devoted to you and most ready to render any services whatever — as I do not doubt that you too will be.

And now fare you happily, and bear the burdens of old age patiently.
At Uraniborg, 12 August, in the year of Christ '95.
Most loving of you,
TYCHO BRAHE, in his own hand.

[Postscript:] If Monavius is still (as I hope) among the living, and there subsists between you and him (as I do not doubt) a familiarity, I ask that you admonish him by letter [to tell me] whether or not I may obtain Wittich's books from the surviving sister — as the same Monavius wrote to me, and affirmed that he had himself secured them; and let him inform me of the price. For though I have long since written to Monavius on this matter, no answer [has come] to me — whether because my letters went astray, or because some other cause, as yet unknown to me, underlies it. You, then, will dispatch this in my name as conveniently as occasion is given.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Thaddaeus Haggecius = Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku (Thaddaeus Hagecius ab Hayek, 1525–1600), physician to the Imperial court of Maximilian II and Rudolf II at Prague, and Tycho's principal astronomical correspondent in Bohemia. He had observed the 1572 nova (publishing his Dialexis de novae … stellae, Frankfurt 1574) and was the conduit through whom Tycho came to be invited to the Imperial court in 1599; he also brokered Tycho's contact with the Imperial Vice-Chancellor. The salutation's full titulature (Philosophiae et Medicinae Doctori … Aulae Caesareae Medico) is preserved.
  • Sender / place. Tycho Brahe, manu propria, Uraniburgi — Tycho writing from Uraniborg on Hven; manu propria = "in his own hand" (the autograph subscription formula).
  • J. Curtius. Domini I. CURTIJ Procancellarij = Jacob Kurz von Senftenau (Jacobus Curtius a Senftenau, d. 1594), Imperial Vice-Chancellor (Procancellarius) to Rudolf II, an amateur with genuine mathematical competence whom Hagecius had introduced to Tycho (Norlind). Tycho had sent him both the first volume of the Progymnasmata and a star-globe; with Curtius now dead, Tycho asks Hagecius to recover Curtius's astronomical papers and that volume. bonae memoriae = "of good memory," the formula for a deceased person.
  • Work referenced. primum nostrum tomum Progymnasmatum Astronomicorum = the first volume of the Astronomiae Instauratae Progymnasmata (Tycho's treatise on the 1572 nova and the solar/lunar theory), not yet published in 1595 (it appeared posthumously, 1602). Tycho's anxiety that an advance copy might fall into hands that would "abuse my labours" reflects the Ursus plagiarism dispute then live.
  • Dialexin / Stella illa nova — tuam Dialexin = Hagecius's Dialexis on the new star of 1572; Stella illa nova = that nova. The Progymnasmata surveyed omnium scripta … de Stella illa nova — all the published writings on the nova, including Hagecius's.
  • Papermaking. Chartopaeo … in moletrina mea pro papyro conficienda = the papermaker in Tycho's paper-mill on Hven; chartâ impressoriâ destitutus = "deprived of printing-paper." The death of his papermaker had halted Tycho's own press for the triennium; he reports recruiting a replacement from Mecklenburg. molendinum … redintegrari et ampliari = the mill "restored and enlarged."
  • Astronomy — Mars. Acronychio situ = acronychal position (rising at sunset, i.e. opposition); utroque calculo = "both reckonings" (Ptolemaic and Copernican); Apogaeum ♂ … plus 5 gradibus … ulterius = the apogee of Mars more than 5° too far in Copernicus's tables; eccentricitas / Apogaeum / coelestis norma rendered per glossary. The glyph ♂ is kept in the source and named Mars in the translation; ☉ = Sun, ☾ = Moon. experimento here = "test, trial" (the empirical demonstration), rendered "experiment" in the period sense.
  • Astronomy — Moon. novilunijs et plenilunijs ac quadraturis = new moons, full moons, and quadratures; maioris Epicycli = the greater epicycle; Apogaeum / Perigaeum = apogee / perigee; medias elongationes = the middle elongations; apparentias salvat = "saves the appearances" (the technical salvare apparentias); enormitas = "irregularity" (deviation from rule), not "enormousness." The "four intermediate places" (locis 4or … intermedia) are the octants between syzygy and quadrature — the variation/evection residuals Tycho is describing.
  • Copernicus / Rheticus. magnus ille nunquamque satis laudatus COPERNICUS = "that great and never sufficiently praised Copernicus" — Tycho's habitual respectful formula even while refuting him; discipulo RHETICO = Rheticus (Georg Joachim Rheticus), Copernicus's disciple; hypotyposin (Greek-derived) = his model/representation, kept as "model." Tycho's hedge that the Copernican values "square most closely … albeit by a certain inverted arrangement" (inverso quodam ordine) is preserved, not sharpened.
  • Other works. Commentaria TRAPEZUNTIJ in PTOLOMAeI magnam Syntaxin = the commentary of George of Trebizond (Georgius Trapezuntius) on Ptolemy's Almagest (magna Syntaxis / Megalē Syntaxis); libellum APIANI de Cometâ = Peter Apian's tract on the comet. Tycho offers to return both, on loan from Hagecius, via Frankfurt or Prague.
  • Greek / euphemism. aliquid tibi humanitus accidisset — "some mortal thing might have befallen you," the standard Latin euphemism (humanitus accidere) for "that you might have died"; the bracketed gloss flags it rather than rendering it bluntly.
