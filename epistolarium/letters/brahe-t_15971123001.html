The original

NOBILISS. ET EXCELLENTISS. DOMINO TYCHONI BRAHE, DOMINO IN KNUDSTRUP ET EQUITI DANO, DOMINO ET AMICO MEO SUMMA OBSERVANTIA COLENDO.

NOBILISSIME et Excellentissime Domine TYCHO, cum post abitum tabellarij summopere tunc festinantis observationes in ♃ ad 20 Nov. diem habitas calculo subijcere vellem, inveni me errasse in assignatione distantiae ♃ ab Aldeb. quae revera tunc erat 8° 51', non vero 9°51', quod ex festinatione factum esse scias. Distantia vero ♃ et Hu. Orio. vera fuit 19°8', ut post diligenti consideratione sextantis inveni. Verum sextans quo utor (ut et in literis indicavi) mihi nequaquam satisfacit, maxime propter vulgarem divisionem, eamque minus ratam. Sic ergo se habent.

20 Nov. H. 12 P. M.

Dist. ♃ et Aldeborae 8° 51'
Dist. ♃ et Hu. dext. Orion. 19 8
Altit. Merid. ♃ 57 54½ vel 55'
Altit. Aldeb. 52 0
Altit. Aequat. 36 22 Ergo se recte positio instr. quadr. tunc habuit.
Decl. ♃ 21 33 fere B.
Asc. R. 70 13

Asc. hanc ex asc. R. Aldeb. et ex asc. R. Hu. dex. Orion. inquisivi viceversa. Asc. R. Hu. Orio. ex loco longit. et latit. iuxta tuum Canonem constitui videlicet hoc tempore 83 Gr. 23' fere.

Longitudo ♃ 11 39 ♊
Latitudo ♃ 0 42½ Merid.

Fuit ergo medio motu ☉ iuxta tuam restitutionem oppositus die 22 Nov. Hor. 2½ circiter P. M. iuxta verum motum ☉ opponebatur die 23 Nov. Hor. 9 A. M.

Si Ephemerides STADIJ se recte habent, calculus parum deviat. Sed tuum de his iudicium peto, et quae calculi utriusque sit differentia.

♂ quoque hisce diebus aliquoties diligenter considerabo, quod tamen ut in posterum subtilius et expeditius fiat, T. Ex. observationum mearum in literis propositarum dilucida resolutione haud dubie efficiet. Et hanc designationem Tabellario in vicina urbe adhuc commoranti transmisi, ut literis meis eam insereret. Vale.

De altitudine ☾ capienda dubium.

Cum ☾ limbus uno quasi minuto extans per rimulam capitur, observatio certa esse non potest; nam centrum ☾ haberi nequit. Nam circa unum latus pinnacidij per unam rimulam conspicitur limbi extremitas, per alteram rimam conspicitur maior portio ☾. Relinquitur igitur, incertam esse altitudinem, cum nec medium Lunae (ut in stellis) habeatur, nec etiam revera sciri possit, an portio unius vel 2 minut. vel plus minusve de corpore Lunari conspiciatur, et proinde ignoretur, an semidiameter ☾ uno vel duobus minutis minor sit addenda vel subtrahenda. Hoc dubium in accipienda ☾ altitudine certa me valde torquet. Cupio igitur certi quid in hoc negotio ex T. E. cognoscere.

Vale.
23 Nov. 97.
T. Exc.
observ.
D. FABRICIUS.

N. B. Quomodo etiam minimarum stellarum centrum per rimulas
sext. vel quadr. haberi possit, valde miror (cum illarum diameter vix 1
minut.). Aut enim rimulas valde amplas esse oportet, et sic incertus per
rimulam talem laxam vel amplam prospectus esse videtur, aut si rimulæ
mediocres, non videbitur. Quomodo igitur agendum sit, magnopere scire cupio,
et quæ rimularum pro minimis stellis laxitas vel amplitudo maxima esse
debeat in instru. 4 pedum, cognoscere valde desidero.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the most noble and most excellent lord Tycho Brahe, lord of Knudstrup and Danish knight, my lord and friend to be honoured with the deepest respect.

Most noble and most excellent lord Tycho, when, after the departure of the courier — who was then in the greatest haste — I wished to subject to calculation the observations made on Jupiter [♃] for the day of 20 November, I found that I had erred in the assignment of the distance of Jupiter [♃] from Aldebaran, which in truth was then 8° 51', and not 9° 51' — which, you should know, came about through the haste. But the true distance of Jupiter [♃] and the shoulder of Orion [Hu. Orio.] was 19° 8', as I afterwards found upon careful examination of the sextant. The sextant I use, however (as I also indicated in my letter), by no means satisfies me, chiefly on account of its common [scale] division, and that one less exact. So, then, they stand as follows.

20 November, 12 hours after noon [12 P.M.].

Distance of Jupiter [♃] and Aldebaran 8° 51'
Distance of Jupiter [♃] and the right shoulder of Orion 19 8
Meridian altitude of Jupiter [♃] 57 54½ or 55'
Altitude of Aldebaran 52 0
Altitude of the equator 36 22 Therefore the setting of the quadrant instrument was then correct.
Declination of Jupiter [♃] 21 33 nearly, North.
Right ascension 70 13

This right ascension I sought out from the right ascension of Aldebaran and from the right ascension of the right shoulder of Orion, [and] conversely. The right ascension of the shoulder of Orion I established from its place in longitude and latitude according to your Canon, namely at this time 83 degrees 23' nearly.

Longitude of Jupiter [♃] 11 39 Gemini [♊]
Latitude of Jupiter [♃] 0 42½ South [Merid.]

It [Jupiter] was therefore, by the mean motion of the Sun [☉] according to your restoration, in opposition on the day of 22 November, at about hour 2½ after noon [P.M.]; by the true motion of the Sun [☉] it was in opposition on the day of 23 November, at hour 9 before noon [A.M.].

If Stadius's Ephemerides are correct, the calculation deviates but little. But I ask your judgement on these matters, and what the difference of the two calculations may be.

Mars [♂], too, I shall examine carefully several times in these days; yet that this may be done more subtly and more expeditiously hereafter, Your Excellency will doubtless bring about by a clear resolution of my observations set out in my letter. And I have sent this notation to the courier, who is still staying in the neighbouring town, so that he might insert it among my letters. Farewell.

A doubt concerning the taking of the Moon's [☾] altitude.

When the limb of the Moon [☾], standing out by as it were one minute, is captured through the little slit, the observation cannot be certain; for the centre of the Moon [☾] cannot be obtained. For around one side of the sight [pinnule], through one little slit the edge of the limb is seen, [and] through the other slit a greater portion of the Moon [☾] is seen. It remains, therefore, that the altitude is uncertain, since neither is the middle of the Moon obtained (as with the stars), nor indeed can it truly be known whether a portion of one or 2 minutes, or more or less, of the lunar body is seen, and accordingly it is not known whether the semidiameter of the Moon [☾], smaller by one or two minutes, is to be added or subtracted. This doubt in taking a certain altitude of the Moon [☾] greatly torments me. I desire, therefore, to learn something certain in this matter from Your Excellency.

Farewell.
23 November [15]97.
Your Excellency's
observer,
D. Fabricius.

N.B. How, too, the centre of even the smallest stars can be obtained through the slits of a sextant or quadrant, I greatly wonder (since their diameter is scarcely 1 minute). For either the slits must be very wide — and then the sighting through such a loose or wide slit seems uncertain — or, if the slits are of middling [width], nothing will be seen [at all]. How, therefore, one is to proceed, I very much desire to know; and what the greatest looseness or width of the slits ought to be for the smallest stars, in an instrument of 4 feet, I very much desire to learn.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. D. Fabricius = David Fabricius (1564–1617), Frisian Lutheran pastor (then at Resterhave / Ostfriesland) and observational astronomer; discoverer of the variable star Mira Ceti (1596). He had opened a correspondence with Tycho and would visit him at Wandsbek in 1598; here he is in the deferential posture of a provincial observer submitting raw data to Tycho for adjudication. The subscription observ[ator] ("observer") marks the role he claims toward Tycho. This letter survives as an autograph inserted between leaves 153 and 154 of GKS 316 fol. (the Thesaurus Observationum), the leaf-block (150–157) of Fabricius's own observations Tycho appended to his 1597 observations.
  • Recipient titulature. Dominus in Knudstrup et Eques Danus = "lord of Knudstrup and Danish knight" — Tycho's ancestral seat in Scania and his noble rank; titulature kept in full by the edition's convention
  • Astronomical objects / abbreviations.
  • Aldeb. / Aldeborae = Aldebaran (α Tauri).
  • Hu. Orio. / Hu. dext. Orion. = Humerus dexter Orionis, the right shoulder of Orion (the bright star at Orion's right shoulder, conventionally α Orionis / Betelgeuse). Expanded in the translation; the manuscript's contracted form is kept in the Source block.
  • The planet/luminary glyphs are named in English by the edition's convention with the glyph retained in the Source block: ♃ = Jupiter, ☉ = Sun, ☾ = Moon, ♂ = Mars, ♊ = Gemini.
  • Technical terms (editorial glossary,–5.2). distantia → distance (angular separation); sextans → sextant (Tycho's sextans astronomicus); altitudo meridiana → meridian altitude; altitudo aequatoris ("altitude of the equator") → the altitude of the celestial equator, i.e. the co-latitude check by which Fabricius confirms the quadrant's setting; declinatio → declination; ascensio recta (Asc. R.) → right ascension; longitudo / latitudo → (celestial) longitude / latitude; semidiameter → semidiameter; pinnacidium → sight (pinnule); quadrans → quadrant. The instrument-noun instr. quadr. is expanded as "the quadrant instrument."
  • vulgaris divisio — "the common [scale] division": Fabricius complains his sextant has only an ordinary (undivided-into-transversals) graduation, "and that one less exact" (eamque minus ratam). The bracketed "[scale]" marks an editorial insertion clarifying divisio.
  • tuum Canonem — "your Canon": Tycho's canon/table of fixed-star places (his star catalogue), against which Fabricius reduces the shoulder of Orion to 83°23' right ascension. Rendered "Canon," not modernised.
  • iuxta tuam restitutionem — "according to your restoration": Tycho's restored solar theory / tables (restitutio by the edition's convention, Tycho's programme of astronomia instaurata). The mean-motion opposition (22 Nov.) and true-motion opposition (23 Nov.) of Jupiter to the Sun are reckoned from it.
  • Ephemerides Stadij — the Ephemerides of Johannes Stadius (1527–1579), the widely used Copernican-based ephemerides; Stadij is the genitive of Stadius. Name kept.
  • Symbols / numerals. B. after the declination value (21 33 fere B.) is read as Borealis / Boreal = North (the declination is north); rendered "North." This reading is consistent with Merid. beside the latitude (see next). Flagged: B. is an abbreviation; "North" is the supported expansion in context (a +21° declination in Gemini), but the single letter is not spelled out in the source.
  • Merid. (beside Latitudo ♃ 0 42½) — abbreviation, here taken as Meridiana = northern (boreal) ecliptic latitude, i.e. the latitude lies to the north; rendered "[North]" in brackets. Crux flagged: Merid. could in principle abbreviate Meridionalis (southern); the bracketed "North" follows the boreal sense paired with the boreal declination, but the abbreviation alone does not decide the hemisphere — recorded, not silently resolved. (See Confidence.)
  • Calendar. The dateline 23 Nov. 97 (O.S.; Denmark and the Lutheran German lands kept the Julian calendar) agrees with the edition label 1597. 23. november; no discrepancy. The internal dates (20 Nov. observation; 22/23 Nov. computed oppositions) are reproduced exactly as written and are not converted. The time-notation P. M. / A. M. is the period's "post/ante meridiem" reckoning from noon (so H. 12 P. M. = 12 hours after noon = midnight ending 20 Nov.); rendered "after noon / before noon" with the Latin retained in brackets, not silently modernised to clock time.
  • Negations. The Moon-altitude paragraph turns on a chain of negated clauses (non potest … nequit … nec … nec … ignoretur); all four negations are reproduced one-for-one, and the disjunction addenda vel subtrahenda ("to be added or subtracted") is kept as a genuine either/or — Fabricius's point is precisely that he cannot tell which.
  • Epistolary formulae. Vale → "Farewell" (×3); subscription T. Exc. observ. D. Fabricius → "Your Excellency's observer, D. Fabricius."
  • What this is. A further note, headed "N.B.", added after Fabricius's signature and dateline (23 November 1597) — a true postscript in substance if not in name, continuing the instrument-sighting question already under way in the published tail (the difficulty of centring the Moon's disc in a pinhole sight, "per rimulam").
  • The correspondent. David Fabricius (1564–1617), the East Frisian pastor-astronomer later famous for discovering the variability of Mira Ceti; an independent observer whom Kepler rated the finest observational astronomer in Europe after Tycho's death, and who briefly joined Tycho's household staff at Wandsbek in 1598 and again at Prague in 1601 [Christianson, On Tycho's Island (Cambridge, 2000)].
  • Where this letter physically sits. This exact letter of 23 November 1597 is not merely epistolary — it survives bound into Tycho's own manuscript observation volume, inserted between folios 153 and 154, immediately after a note in Tycho's hand that his 1597 observations were "somewhat poor" and needed supplementing with Fabricius's own (folios 150–157, all in Fabricius's hand).
  • Technical vocabulary. "Rimula" (little slit/crack) is the standard term for the narrow aperture-sight fitted to Tycho's sextants and quadrants; Mosley's index glosses the type as "slit-sights" (pp. 55–56, 105–106) [Mosley, Bearing the Heavens (Cambridge, 2007)] — confirming the rendering used here.
  • Crux. The passage poses a genuine, unresolved technical dilemma in Fabricius's own words (wide slit = imprecise sighting; narrow slit = invisible star) rather than a corrupt reading; nothing in the Latin itself is in doubt, but the abbreviations "sext.", "quadr.", and "instru. 4 pedum" are silently expanded in translation only, not in the source.
  • Correction. 'Latitude of Jupiter 0°42½′ North [Merid.]' corrected to 'South [Merid.]' — 'Merid.' abbreviates Meridionalis (South), as confirmed by Dreyer TBOO VIII p.13, where the same letter uses 'B.' (Borealis) for North two lines above in Jupiter's declination.

1 defect found:
- mistranslation (high). "Merid." abbreviates Meridionalis (South), not North; the same letter uses "B." (Borealis) for North two lines earlier when giving Jupiter's declination, so "Merid." here is the parallel abbreviation for the opposite direction. The English silently glosses it as "North", flipping Jupiter's ecliptic latitude to the wrong hemisphere in a letter whose entire content is precise numerical astronomical data. At: “Latitude of Jupiter [♃] 0 42½ North [Merid.]”.

Repaired. Collated against the Dreyer page images for this letter (TBOO VIII pp.13-15, all 3 pages read). P.13 clearly shows 'Decl. 4 21 33 fere B.' (Declination, North) followed a few lines later by 'Latitudo 4 0 42½ Merid.' (Latitude, South) — the letter's own internal contrast between 'B.' and 'Merid.' confirms the audit's identification. No other cruces found on the three pages.
