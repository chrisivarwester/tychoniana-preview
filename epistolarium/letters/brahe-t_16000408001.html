The original

TYCHO BRAHE AD IOHANNEM IESSENIUM A IESSEN.

S. P.

CLARISSIME vir, amice plurimum mihi semper dilecte, quae tuae fidei concredidi, Generoso D. HOFFMANNO seorsim monstranda, cupio mihi nunc remitti; litteras tamen illas, quas TEGNAGLIUS ultimo ad me dedit, quae de Magnifici D. CORRADUCIJ resolutione faciunt, vellem bene tuo sigillo munitas ac tuis inclusas unâ cum meis ad DANIELEM mitti, siquidem in ijs faciat mentionem eorum, quae D. CORRADUCIUS apud Caesar. Mai. meo nomine impetravit, ut sciat DANIEL, eo rectius se facere, si apud D. CORRADUCIUM in meis rebus sollicitet, non minus quam apud D. BARVICIUM, quem quodammodo lentum in ijs promovendis ............. Dubitat enim ipse DANIEL in ultimis suis ad me datis, an hoc facere debeat nec ne. Ego vero rescripsi, mihi id non inconsultum videri, caute tamen omnia agenda, ne D. BARVICIUM per imprudentiam offendat, nam amborum retinere benevolentiam, quoad fieri poterit, satius duco. Coeterum cum tu hîc spectator et auditor fueris eorum, quae a KEPLERO dicta factaque sunt, sub ipsum discessus momentum, et simul quomodo postea, iuxta currum mihi valedicturus, poenitentia quadam eorum duci prae se ferret, atque ea omnia, quod affectus suos regere non posset, excusaret, egoque a condonatione non alienus essem, tibique in aurem susurraverim, ut efficeres illum Pragae literis ad me datis id ipsum testari, quod male ipsum habeat, se sic exorbitasse cupereque mecum in gratiam redire, quod et te facturum aiebas, et sponte antea mihi seorsim dixeras, te in itinere ipsum serio admoniturum, imo reprehensurum, quod se tam impotenter et biliose gesserit, contra quam decet virum doctum et honestum: iccirco mitto tibi hîc inclusas ipsius literas manu eius propria exaratas, quarum effrenem petulantiam et nimis arrogantem dicacitatem nec per meum vinum nec per meum contemtum, nec quicquid eiuscemodi aliud quod praetendit, excusare poterit, nisi et tunc furiosus fuit (uti forte huius morbi semina, etiam cum sibi maxime constare videtur, clam fovet) cum ista composito animo scriberet, et ipsemet calamo, me ignorante (qui ipsi iam ignoscere coeperam, siquidem resipiscere voluisset) praeest. Miraberis, non dubito, hominis in malitia citra omne meum meritum pertinaciam, nec forte habebis aliud, quid dicas, quam me mea humanitate, uti etiam tacite exprobrâsti, effecisse, ut iste hominem exueret, atque in rabidum canem, quem suo stomacho se aemulari dicere solitus est (utinam etiam cerebro et corde) transformaretur. Inspice igitur et despice, quod et ego faciam. Quis enim scienter et volenter furentem facile curaret? Si ipsum in via admonuisti, et ut rationes inveniret, mecum denuo in amicitiam redeundi, adhortatus es, uti proculdubio fecisti, vide quaeso, quales fructus ediderit haec tua sobria, candida et amica admonitio et adhortatio. Ego, etsi facilimum mihi sit, omnes ipsius oblatratus vera redargutione compescere, nolo tamen cum tam procaci lingua praedito, nec modestiae aut virtutis curam habente, contendere, memor illius Catonici:

Contra verbosum noli contendere verbis.

Constitui igitur in posterum nihil cum eo habere commercij, sive per literas sive oretenus, et optarem me nunquam habuisse, tunc minus mea humanitate abuti posset. Iussi quidem, statim ac is abierat, MATHIAM studiosum nonnulla ex chartis illis, quas ad me recepi, describere, ut ea promptius legere, cum ipso, quando unâ cum DANIELE redierit, conferre possem, atque mea dubia illi subgerere, et quomodo peragendum esset, expiscari, atque tum illi a se conscripta, uti sancte promisi, restituere. Verum cum ex his malitiosis literis plus satis percipiam, eum non reversurum, uti etiam non cupio, ipsemet in culpa est, quod pollicitis stare nequeam. Et super hoc, quod in Epistola iam missa iecit fundamentum, vix utar eo tamquam archi[tec]to, cui aliquid ex meis observationibus et laboribus superstruatur. Ille ipse me iam magis cautum, quam antea fui, reddidit; genio, uti spero, bono mei curam habente, et omnia sic in meum commodum digerente. Nolim tamen, ut cuipiam has Keplerianas literas ostendas, et ne quidem D. HOFFMANNO, aut cum ipso KEPLERO hac de re expostules, nisi forte sua quadam importunitate tibi occasionem dederit, sed actutum cum hoc eodem nuntio, bene conclusas et iterum obsignatas, mihi remittas. Si tamen subolfeceris ipsum apud D. HOFFMANNUM vel alios, ut suam probaret innocentiam, me calumniatum esse, aut quippiam veritati minus consonum effutijsse, fac quod bonum et integrum virum decet, quod et non gravatim effecturum mihi polliceor (prout et ego te saepius contra aliorum sugillationes tutatus sum) ut meam a te defendi causam, atque id, quod veritas postulat, asseverari resciscam. Interim etiam an apud URSUM istum aliquid in mei contumeliam tacite moliatur (quomodo enim illi amplius fidam?) per tertiam vel quartam manum inquire, quamvis nihil morer, quicquid dixerit, modo vera dixerit, et cum Deo V. simul fuerimus, tibi aperiam, quidnam id sit, de quo ita minari videtur, et plura, quae ad dilutionem illorum, quorum foecundus et facundus est consarcinator, faciunt, licet ipse plaeraque nôsti, suggeram.

Vale.
Dabantur ex arce Benateka die 8 Aprilis Anno 1600.

English translation machine translation, Secure

TYCHO BRAHE TO JOHANNES JESSENIUS A JESSEN.

Heartiest greeting.

Most renowned man, friend always most dear to me: what I entrusted to your good faith, to be shown separately to the noble Lord Hoffmann, I wish now to be sent back to me. But those letters which Tengnagel last sent me, which bear on the resolution of the magnificent Lord Coraduz, I should wish — well secured with your seal and enclosed in yours — to be sent together with mine to Daniel, since in them he makes mention of the things which Lord Coraduz obtained from the Imperial Majesty in my name, so that Daniel may know he does the more rightly if he solicits my affairs with Lord Coraduz, no less than with Lord Barvitius — whom [I find] somewhat slow in promoting them …………… [text breaks off — a lacuna in the copy]. For Daniel himself, in his last letter to me, doubts whether he ought to do this or not. But I wrote back that it does not seem to me ill-advised — yet that all is to be done cautiously, lest through imprudence he offend Lord Barvitius; for I judge it better to keep the goodwill of both, so far as may be.

For the rest: since you were here a spectator and hearer of what was said and done by Kepler at the very moment of [his] departure — and likewise how afterward, about to bid me farewell beside the wagon, he showed signs of a certain repentance of them, and excused it all on the ground that he could not govern his passions, and I was not averse to pardon — and since I whispered in your ear that you should get him, by a letter sent me from Prague, to attest that very thing: that it grieved him to have so overstepped, and that he wished to return into favour with me (which you said you would do, and had earlier of your own accord told me separately, that you would seriously admonish him on the journey, nay reprehend him, for having borne himself so impotently and biliously, contrary to what befits a learned and honourable man): for that reason I send you here enclosed his own letter, written in his own hand — whose unbridled petulance and over-arrogant scurrility he will not be able to excuse by my wine, nor by my contempt, nor by any other such thing he pretends, unless he was then too in a frenzy (as perhaps he secretly nurses the seeds of this disease even when he seems most composed of himself) when he wrote those things with composed mind, and himself wields the pen — without my knowledge (I who had already begun to pardon him, had he but wished to come to his senses). You will wonder, I do not doubt, at the man's pertinacity in malice, beyond all my deserving; and perhaps you will have nothing else to say than that I, by my humanity — as you even tacitly reproached me — brought it about that this man put off the man, and was transformed into a rabid dog, which he was wont to say he rivalled with his stomach (would that [he did so] also with brain and heart). Inspect it, then, and despise it, as I shall too. For who would readily cure one knowingly and willingly raving? If you admonished him on the way, and exhorted him to find reasons for returning anew into friendship with me — as doubtless you did — see, I pray, what fruits this sober, candid, and friendly admonition and exhortation of yours has borne. I, though it would be most easy for me to curb all his barkings by a true refutation, am yet unwilling to contend with one endowed with so insolent a tongue, and having no care for modesty or virtue — mindful of that saying of Cato:

"Do not strive with words against a wordy man."

I have resolved, therefore, to have henceforth no commerce with him, whether by letter or by word of mouth; and I could wish I never had — then the less could he abuse my humanity. I did indeed order, as soon as he had gone, the student Mathias to copy out some things from those papers which I took back to myself, that I might read them more readily, and confer about them with him [Kepler] when he should return together with Daniel, and put my doubts to him, and fish out how it ought to be carried through, and then restore to him what he had written, as I solemnly promised. But since from this malicious letter I perceive more than enough that he will not return (as I also do not wish), he himself is at fault that I cannot stand by my promises. And upon this — on which he laid a foundation in the letter now sent — I shall hardly use him as an architect, upon whom anything of my observations and labours should be built. He himself has now made me more cautious than I was before; my good genius, I hope, taking care of me, and disposing all things thus to my advantage. I should not wish, however, that you show this Keplerian letter to anyone — not even to Lord Hoffmann — nor that you expostulate with Kepler himself about this, unless perhaps by some importunity he gives you occasion; but that you send it back to me at once, by this same messenger, well closed and sealed again. If, however, you scent out that he, to Lord Hoffmann or others — to prove his own innocence — has calumniated me, or blurted out anything less than consonant with the truth, do what becomes a good and upright man (which I promise myself you will do not grudgingly — even as I have often protected you against the carpings of others), so that I may learn my cause is defended by you, and that what truth demands is affirmed. Meanwhile inquire also, through a third or fourth hand, whether he secretly contrives anything against my honour with that Ursus (for how should I trust him further?) — although I care nothing for whatever he may say, provided he say the truth; and when, God willing, we are together, I will open to you what that thing is about which he seems so to threaten, and will suggest more things conducing to the dissolving of those of which [Ursus] is a fertile and fluent botcher — though you yourself know most of them.

Farewell.
Given from the castle of Benátky, the 8th day of April, the year 1600.

Apparatus

  • Recipient. Johannes Jessenius / Jan Jessenský (1566–1621), the anatomist and Tycho's friend, who had mediated between Tycho and Kepler and was present at the Benátky quarrel. (He is the "Planet" of brahe-t_16000327002; later executed in the 1621 Prague Old-Town-Square execution.) The witness is a Vienna copy.
  • Significance — the great Tycho–Kepler rupture of April 1600 (a primary document). Kepler had come to Benátky early in 1600; a violent quarrel erupted over his working conditions, his access to the observations, and his keep. This letter, written days after, is Tycho's raw account to the mediator:
  • The quarrel and the wagon-side repentance. Jessenius "was a spectator and hearer of what Kepler said and did at the very moment of departure," and saw how Kepler, bidding farewell beside the wagon, showed "a certain repentance," excusing himself as unable to "govern his passions." Tycho "was not averse to pardon" and had Jessenius coax a written apology from Prague.
  • The "malicious letter." Instead, Kepler sent an angry letter "in his own hand," whose "unbridled petulance" Tycho refuses to excuse "by my wine, nor my contempt" — the very pretexts Kepler had alleged (Tycho's table-wine; Tycho's slighting of him). Tycho, wounded, half-suggests Kepler is subject to fits of "frenzy" (furor).
  • The famous metaphor. Tycho throws back Kepler's own self-description: that Kepler "put off the man and was transformed into a rabid dog, which he was wont to say he rivalled with his stomach — would that [also] with brain and heart." (Kepler's own self-portrait as choleric/dog-like, turned against him.)
  • The resolution to break off — citing the Disticha Catonis: "Contra verbosum noli contendere verbis" ("Do not strive with words against a wordy man") — and the pointed line "I shall hardly use him as an architect, on whom anything of my observations and labours should be built." (The bitter irony: within weeks they reconciled; Kepler returned, inherited the observations, and built the Astronomia nova — and the Rudolphine Tables — precisely on them.)
  • The Ursus fear (the sore point). Tycho asks Jessenius to inquire "through a third or fourth hand" whether Kepler secretly colludes with Ursus against Tycho's honour — a live anxiety, since Kepler had earlier written a flattering letter to Ursus (Nicolaus Reimers Bär), who printed it in his defamatory De astronomicis hypothesibus. "[Ursus] is a fertile and fluent botcher" (consarcinator). (Cf. the Ursus thread, brahe-t_16000401001, brahe-t_16000328001.)
  • The court-business frame (opening). Tycho's affairs at the imperial court: papers to be returned (shown privately to Hoffmann); Tengnagel's letters on Coraduz's "resolution" (what Coraduz obtained from the Emperor in Tycho's name) to be forwarded to Daniel, who should solicit Tycho's business with both Coraduz and Barvitius (the latter "somewhat slow"), keeping both men's goodwill. A lacuna (…………) interrupts the Barvitius clause in the copy.
  • Persons named. Johannes Jessenius; Johannes Kepler (the quarrel); Johann Friedrich Hoffmann von Grünbüchel (imperial councillor, Kepler's Prague host); Franz Tengnagel (Tegnaglius); Rudolf von Coraduz (Vice-Chancellor); Johann Anton Barvitius (imperial secretary); Daniel (an unidentified court-agent/solicitor of Tycho's at Prague who travels with Kepler — left unidentified, flagged); the student Mathias (a studiosus/assistant, plausibly Matthias Seiffart — not confirmed, flagged); Nicolaus Reimers Bär (Ursus); Cato (the Disticha Catonis). Place: Benátky.
  • Cross-references. A keystone for the Tycho–Kepler convergence thread and the Ursus dispute thread; route alongside the reconciliation that followed (Kepler's return and succession).
  • Calendar. die 8 Aprilis Anno 1600 at Benátky (imperial reckoning) → 8 April 1600 [N.S.]; header and label agree.
  • Correction. Restored singular translation of 'has Keplerianas literas' ('this Keplerian letter') for consistency with the two earlier renderings of the same epistolary-plural noun in this letter, both referring to the single enclosed Kepler letter (Dreyer, TBOO VIII p.299).

1 defect found:
- mistranslation (low). Latin 'literas' here is the same epistolary-plural noun used twice earlier in this same letter for the single enclosed Kepler letter ('ipsius literas manu eius propria exaratas' -> rendered 'his own letter'; 'ex his malitiosis literis' -> rendered 'this malicious letter'). The translation renders the first two occurrences as singular but this third occurrence of the same recurring noun, referring to the same one document, as plural 'letters' — an internal inconsistency that could mislead a reader into thinking multiple Kepler letters are in play. At: “I should not wish, however, that you show these Keplerian letters to anyone”.

Repaired. Verified the clause directly against the Dreyer page image (TBOO VIII p.299): the Latin 'Nolim tamen, ut cuipiam has Keplerianas literas oftendas' matches our source text exactly, so this is a translation-only defect, not a text corruption. Fixed the English for internal consistency with the letter's two earlier singular renderings of the same noun. No remaining crux.
