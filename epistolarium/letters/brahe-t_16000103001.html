The original

ILLUSTRISSIMO ET CLARISSIMO VIRO DOMINO VINCENTIO PINELLIO PATRICIO PATAVINO DOMINO ET AMICO SUO OBSERVANDISSIMO.
ILLUSTRIS ET MAGNIFICE VIR.
S.

CUM superiore Anno Italiam peragrasset Nobilis et Eruditus adolescens FRANCISCUS TENGNAGLIUS, qui aliquamdiu antea tam in Daniâ quam Germaniâ meus domesticus fuit, et Dresdâ a me in Italiam profectus inter alia, ut Patavium transiens te officiose meo nomine salutaret, atque de statu mearum rerum edoceret, in mandatis habuit. Reversus autem nuper ad me, cum ab ipso percontarer, an te allocutus esset, respondit se, quam primum Patavium permeasset, saltem biduum ibi mansisse: interim te conveniendi nullam datam fuisse commoditatem. Reiecit itaque illud in reditum suum, donec ulteriore Italiâ perlustrata in Germaniam ipsi revertendum foret. At cum rursus Urbem vestram accederet, et quod antea omissum erat, præstare satageret, te ibi non invenit; sed in villam tuam animi caussâ secessisse Mathematicus vester GALILÆUS DE GALILÆIS referebat. Re itaque hac præter utriusque nostrûm expectationem infectâ is ad me (uti dictum) redijt. Ex quo vero Excellentissimo Domino MAGINO ad suas, quas idem mihi attulit, respondendum nunc foret literas, te quoque non prætereundum censui, ut quod antea per præsentem TENGNAGLIUM nostrum fieri non poterat, nunc literis (siquidem aliter non licet) compensetur.

Existimo te ab alijs iam antea utcunque percepisse, quomodo Patrium solum verterim, atque in Germaniam ultro concesserim, derelicto Uraniburgo nostro Danico totoque eius apparatu Astronomico mecum ad exteros translato; siquidem quorundam invidiâ et malevolentiâ factum sit, ut omnes reditus ad sustentationem Divinæ illius scientiæ Astronomicæ a nunquam satis laudatæ memoriæ Rege FRIDERICO deputati, statim ac novitius hîc Rex coronatus atque rerum administrationi adhibitus esset, subtracti fuerint, et simul a Cancellario pariter novitio, quem per literas diligenter admonueram, ut hoc sublime studium iuxta intentionem antecedentis Regis et Senatusconsulti, Patriæ saltem honoris caussâ isthic conservari et perpetuari posse, a Rege hocce obtineret, et simul discrete præmonui de ijs, quæ subsequerentur, si id non fieret, mihi rescriptum sit, Regi non esse integrum quicquam in Instrumentorum meorum huic exercitio inservientium sustentationem de suo conferre. Secessi itaque primum Haffniam, quæ Regni metropolis est, trium vel 4 horarum saltem itinere maritimo ab Insulâ illâ Uraniburgicâ distantem. Ubi cum in ædibus amplis iuxta vallum a me ipso exædificatis studia mea proprijs sumptibus continuare in animum induxissem, eâque de caussâ nonnulla ex Instrumentis meis ibi commode disposuissem, a Curiæ Magistro nomine Iunioris Regis, licet tunc in Germaniâ absentis, id efficere prohibitus sum. Quare cum has præclaras Artes isthic adeo neglectas, imo exosas animadverterem, ut neque publicis neque privatis impensis ijs invigilandi otium atque commoditas concederetur, malui universam Patriam plurimosque in eâ sanguine iunctos et Amicos inibi omnes derelinquere, quam laborum Uraniæ nostræ frustrationem atque interitum spectare. Substiti tamen in transmarino Ducatu Megapolitano per Anni quadrantem et postea in vicinâ Cimbris Holsatiâ integrum Annum, ut nostratibus, Patriæ adhuc studens, rem rectius deliberandi satis relinquerem temporis. Quod cum incassum fieri viderem, et interea ab Augustissimo et Potentissimo Imperatore RUDOLPHO Secundo, Domino nostro Clementissimo, inde in Bohemiam evocarer, rebus meis, quam primum fieri potuit, in Germaniâ compositis et translatis Magdæburgum ad Albim situm Instrumentis unâ cum reliquâ supellectile domesticâ Dresdam veni, ubi, postquam de Epidemicis morbis in Bohemiâ populariter grassantibus, quodque Cæsar ob ea pericula Pragâ relictâ in Arcem quandam seorsim se recepisset, intellexissem, et simul a Magco Domino CORRADUCIO, Imperij Procancellario, per literas de Cæs. Matis Clementissimâ voluntate admonerer, ut nimirum paulisper in viciniâ me continerem, donec malum illud in Bohemiâ remitteret, atque Aula Cæsa recolligeretur, expectavi aliquandiu in propinquâ Saxoniâ, quoad iterum ex mandato Cæsaris a prædicto Domino CORRADUCIO Pragam per literas vocarer, quo superiore æstate cum totâ familiâ adveniens, a Cæsa Mate mox benignissime receptus sum, atque annuo salario satis liberali, quo Astronomiæ studio et rei domesticæ consuleretur, provisum, concessaque Arx quædam Cæsareana Benachia, quam ob loci eximiam amœnitatem et fluvium Isaram circumcurrentem indigenæ Venetias Bohemorum nuncupant, quæ quinque saltem milliaribus Germanicis sive sex horarum itinere Pragâ metropoli removetur. Hanc tribus alijs pariter oblatis, quod locus sit tranquillus et iocundus, ac simul admodum êditus, adeoque Observationibus cœlestibus commodior, prætuli: ubi etiam convenientia pro meis Instrumentis ordine et sigillatim disponendis ædificia mandato et sumptu Cæsaris confestim structa sunt, in quæ in Bohemiam mecum allata Organa, prout usus requirit, collocata sunt. Reliqua ex Germaniâ, quam primum hyems desierit, huc pariter advecta, ibidem usui adaptabuntur, ut sic, Cœlestium favente Opifice, Uraniæ nostræ, quam Patria repudiavit, fundamina hîc reponantur, atque in Cæsæ Matis totiusque Imperij honorem Astronomiæ redintegratio continuetur, atque quam primum fieri possit, compleatur. Quod post Divini Numinis providentiam huic optimo Imperatori omnium liberalium Artium, inprimisque Mathematicarum Mæcenati benignissimo acceptum est ferendum. Namque is Clementi manu nobis hæc otia fecit.

Hæc te, Clarissime Domine PINELLE, scire volui, si forte antea non satis innotuerunt, ut rerum nostrarum vicissitudo et in Bohemiâ a Cæsare benignissime restitutum hospitium te non lateret. Libros autem, quos Cæsæ Mati dedicatos, cum primum adveni, humillime obtuli, quoad duos priores, te vidisse autumo. Nam et Sermo Principi atque Senatui Veneto a prænominato Iuvene meâ caussâ reverenter exhibiti sunt, qui etiam eum Equitis aurati honore decorârunt. Si vero nondum eos tibi inspicere contigit, ab Illustri et Clarissimo viro IOHANNE FRANCISCO SAGGREDO id facile (uti opinor) impetrabis. Tertius erat de restitutis motibus Solis et Lunæ eorundemque cum consuetis calculis comparatione, quem alias (uti spero) videbis.

Porro ut per occasionem, quæ nunc ob Aulæ Cæsareæ propinquitatem promptior quam antea occurret, me Eruditis tuis literis invisere, atque quod hisce meis incomptis prævenerint, candide accipere non degraveris, quæso. Vale, salutato a me peramanter ob conformia studia Præstantissimo isthic Mathematum Professore GALILÆO DE GALILÆIS; qui si mihi (uti constituerat) per dictam occasionem scripsisset, me in respondendo non invenisset difficilem. Poterit tamen id alio fortassis tempore præstari. Iamque iterum vale, vir illustris et celeberrime, atque Annum hunc Iubilæum pluresque alios subsequentes quam felicissime transige.

Dabantur ex Arce Cæsareanâ Benaticâ 3 die Ianuarij Anno 1600.

To the most illustrious and renowned man, Lord Vincenzo Pinelli, patrician of Padua, his most esteemed Lord and friend.
Illustrious and magnificent Man.
Greeting.

When last year the noble and learned youth Franz Tengnagel — who for some time before had been a member of my household, both in Denmark and in Germany — travelled through Italy, having set out from Dresden to Italy by me, he had it among his charges that, passing through Padua, he should dutifully greet you in my name, and inform you of the state of my affairs. But lately returned to me, when I asked him whether he had spoken with you, he answered that, as soon as he had reached Padua, he stayed there but two days, [and] meanwhile no opportunity of meeting you was given. He therefore put it off to his return, until, the rest of Italy traversed, he should have to come back into Germany. But when he again approached your City, and busied himself to do what had before been omitted, he did not find you there; but your Mathematician, Galileo Galilei [Galilaeus de Galilaeis], reported that you had retired to your country villa for relaxation. And so, this matter failing beyond the expectation of us both, he returned to me (as I said). But since I must now answer the letter of the most excellent Lord Magini, which he brought me, I judged you too not to be passed over — that what before could not be done through our Tengnagel in person, may now be compensated by letter (since otherwise it is not allowed).

I think you have already in some way perceived from others how I turned from my native soil, and withdrew of my own accord into Germany, abandoning our Danish Uraniborg and bringing all its Astronomical apparatus with me abroad — since by certain men's envy and malevolence it came about that all the revenues assigned, for the support of that divine science of Astronomy, by King Frederick of never-sufficiently-praised memory, were withdrawn as soon as the new King here was crowned and applied to the administration of affairs. And likewise by the also-new Chancellor, whom I had diligently admonished by letter to obtain from this King that this sublime study might — according to the intention of the preceding King and the Senate-decree, at least for the honour of the fatherland — be preserved and perpetuated there (and I discreetly forewarned of what would follow, if it were not done) — it was written back to me that it was not in the King's power to contribute anything of his own to the support of my instruments serving this exercise. I therefore first withdrew to Copenhagen, the realm's metropolis, distant only a three- or four-hours' sea-journey from that Island of Uraniborg. There, when I had resolved to continue my studies at my own expense in a large house I had myself built by the rampart, and for that cause had conveniently arranged some of my instruments there, I was prohibited from doing so by the Court-Master, in the name of the Younger King (though he was then absent in Germany). Wherefore, seeing these excellent Arts so neglected there — nay, hated — that neither by public nor by private means was leisure and convenience granted to watch over them, I preferred to abandon my whole fatherland, and all the many there joined to me by blood, and my friends, rather than to behold the frustration and destruction of our Urania's labours. I halted, however, in the transmarine Duchy of Mecklenburg for a quarter of a year, and afterward in neighbouring Holstein (near the Cimbrians) a whole year — that, still studying the fatherland's [good], I might leave my countrymen enough time to deliberate the matter more rightly. When I saw this done in vain, and was meanwhile summoned thence into Bohemia by the most august and most powerful Emperor Rudolf II, my most clement Lord — my affairs in Germany composed and removed as soon as could be (my Instruments, with the rest of my household goods, [left] at Magdeburg on the Elbe) — I came to Dresden; where, after I had learned of the epidemic diseases raging popularly in Bohemia, and that the Emperor, on account of those perils, had left Prague and retired apart into a certain Castle — and was at the same time admonished by letter from the magnificent Lord Coraduz, Imperial Vice-Chancellor, of the Imperial Majesty's most clement wish, namely that I keep myself a while in the neighbourhood until that evil in Bohemia should abate and the Imperial Court reassemble — I waited a while in nearby Saxony, until I was again, by the Emperor's mandate, called to Prague by the said Lord Coraduz. Arriving thither last summer with my whole family, I was soon most kindly received by the Imperial Majesty, and provided with a sufficiently liberal annual salary, by which the study of Astronomy and the household might be cared for; and there was granted me a certain imperial Castle, Benátky — which, for the place's exceeding loveliness and the river Iser [Jizera] running around it, the natives call the Venice of the Bohemians — distant from the metropolis Prague only five German miles, or a six-hours' journey. This I preferred to three others likewise offered, because the place is quiet and pleasant, and at the same time quite high, and so more convenient for celestial Observations; where also buildings fit for arranging my Instruments in order and severally were at once erected, by the Emperor's order and expense, into which the apparatus brought with me into Bohemia has been placed, as use requires. The rest, from Germany, as soon as winter ends, brought hither likewise, will there be fitted for use — so that thus, the heavenly Maker favouring, the foundations of our Urania, which the fatherland repudiated, may be re-laid here, and the restoration of Astronomy be continued, for the honour of the Imperial Majesty and of the whole Empire, and completed as soon as may be. Which, after the providence of the Divine Godhead, is to be set to the account of this best Emperor, the most kindly Maecenas of all the liberal Arts, and especially of the Mathematical. For with clement hand He has made us this leisure [nobis haec otia fecit].

These things, most renowned Lord Pinelli, I wished you to know — if perhaps they were not before sufficiently known — that the vicissitude of my affairs, and the lodging most kindly restored to me in Bohemia by the Emperor, might not be hidden from you. As for the books which, dedicated to the Imperial Majesty, I most humbly offered when I first arrived — as to the two earlier ones, I suppose you have seen them. For [they] were reverently presented, on my behalf, by the aforesaid Youth, to the Prince [Doge] and Senate of Venice — who also honoured him with the dignity of a Golden Knight [eques auratus]. But if it has not yet befallen you to inspect them, you will easily obtain it (as I think) from the illustrious and renowned man Giovanni Francesco Sagredo. The third was on the restored motions of the Sun and Moon, and their comparison with the customary calculations [the Progymnasmata], which you will see elsewhere (as I hope).

Further, I beg that — by the occasion which now, because of the nearness of the Imperial Court, will occur more readily than before — you would not find it a burden to visit me with your learned letters, and to take candidly that these rough ones of mine have forestalled [you]. Farewell; and greet for me, most lovingly, for our conformable studies, the most excellent Professor of Mathematics there, Galileo Galilei [Galilaeo de Galilaeis]; who, if he had written to me (as he had resolved) through the said occasion, would not have found me difficult in replying. Yet this can perhaps be done at another time. And again farewell, illustrious and most celebrated man, and pass this Jubilee Year, and many others following, most happily.

Given from the imperial castle of Benátky, the 3rd day of January, the year 1600.

Apparatus

  • Recipient. Gian Vincenzo Pinelli (1535–1601), the great Paduan humanist, polymath, and book-/manuscript-collector, whose library was the hub of Padua's learned world (Galileo frequented it).
  • Significance — Tycho's fullest apologia/autobiography of the Hven departure, and the Galileo connection.
  • The exile narrative (the corpus's most complete). A detailed account of leaving Denmark: King Frederick II's astronomy revenues withdrawn as soon as the "new King" (Christian IV) took power; the "new Chancellor" replying that "it was not in the King's power to contribute anything"; Tycho's retreat to Copenhagen (the rampart house) and the Court-Master's prohibition "in the name of the Younger King"; the choice to abandon the fatherland; the quarter-year in Mecklenburg + year in Holstein "to leave my countrymen time to deliberate"; the imperial summons via Coraduz; the Dresden/Saxony wait during the Bohemian plague; and the grant of Benátky ("the Venice of the Bohemians," on the Jizera/Iser, 5 German miles from Prague), chosen over three others, with imperial-funded instrument-housings — "the foundations of our Urania, which the fatherland repudiated, re-laid here." Closes with the Virgilian echo nobis haec otia fecit (Ecl. 1.6) applied to Rudolf II.
  • The Galileo connection (rare and notable). Twice: Galileo ("the most excellent Professor of Mathematics there") reported Pinelli's absence to Tengnagel; and Tycho asks Pinelli to "greet Galileo most lovingly, for our conformable studies," adding that Galileo "had resolved to write to me" and "would not have found me difficult in replying." One of the very few documented Tycho–Galileo contact points — and a record of Galileo's intended (never realised) letter to Tycho.
  • The Venice book-presentation and Tengnagel's knighthood. Tycho's books dedicated to the Emperor (the Mechanica etc.) were presented to the Doge and Senate of Venice by Tengnagel, whom Venice honoured as a Golden Knight (eques auratus); copies obtainable from Sagredo. The "third book" is the Progymnasmata ("the restored motions of Sun and Moon").
  • The Magini link. Written alongside Tycho's reply to Magini (cf. brahe-t_16010220001), both carried in the same Italian exchange.
  • Persons & places named. Vincenzo Pinelli; Franz Tengnagel (now eques auratus of Venice); Galileo Galilei; Giovanni Antonio Magini; Giovanni Francesco Sagredo; King Frederick II & Christian IV of Denmark; the Danish Chancellor and Court-Master (unnamed); Rudolf II; Coraduz; the Doge and Senate of Venice. Places: Benátky ("Venice of the Bohemians," on the Jizera), Prague, Padua, Venice, Copenhagen, Hven/Uraniborg, Mecklenburg, Holstein, Magdeburg, Dresden, Saxony.
  • Cross-references. A keystone of the self-exile thread (cf. brahe-t_15980607001 to Grynaeus) and the principal Tycho–Galileo document; route through the Galileo/Padua and exile-narrative threads.
