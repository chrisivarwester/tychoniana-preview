The original

MAGNIFICO ET AMPLISSIMO VIRO, NOBILITATE GENERIS ATQUE EXCELLENTI ERUDITIONE IN PRIMIS CONSPICUO, DOMINO IACOBO CURTIO A SENFFTENAW, CÆSAREÆ MAIESTATIS CONSILIARIO ATQUE IMPERIJ PROCANCELLARIO PRUDENTISSIMO, AMICO SUO PLURIMUM HONORANDO, S.

QUAM mihi gratæ fuerint tuæ literæ, et præclaræ eruditionis et singularis erga me studij plenæ, quas cum honorificentissimo Cæsareæ Maiestatis Privilegio transmisisti, amplissime et nobilissime vir, dici a me paucis non potest. Nec invenio, quî possim pro merito satis magnas tibi agere gratias, tam quod magnificum illud Diploma mihi tua intercessione et autoritate impetrâris, in artis Astronomicæ laudem et promotionem scite et decore a teipso ordinatum, quam etiam quod tot tantisque publicæ administrationis, quæ tibi indies incumbunt, occupationibus detentus, tantum nihilominus otij tibi sumpseris, ut mihi luculenter admodum et amice rescribere non sis gravatus. Pro qua tua utraque benevolentia adeo tibi devinctus sum, ut si quid a me unquam proficisci queat, quod tibi acceptum aut honorificum sit, summa animi propensione memorem et gratum me in omnibus sis inventurus. Idque sincere de me confirmans et pollicens longiore gratiarum actione te non fatigabo, sed ad tuas literas paucis, ut tempus fert, respondebo.

Quod in principio tam benevolum erga me et rem Astronomicam, quam in hac Insula pro virili excolendam suscepi, declaras affectum, ut si per Cæsaris voluntatem id liceret, me hîc invisere et coram Astronomiæ repurgandæ elaborata adminicula inspicere deque his mecum conferre non grave duceres, id potius tuo singulari candori et in Astronomica propenso studio tribuendum censeo, quam quod de me meisque laboribus tale quid polliceri audeam, quod te tam illustri officio cum magno Reipubl. commodo occupatum huc in Daniam, per tot viarum terræ marisque anfractus allicere mereatur. Utinam tamen idipsum aliquando mihi evenire posset felicitatis, ut te unâ cum communi amico THADDÆO hîc coram intueri liceret, nihil sane mihi magis ex voto acceptiusque contingeret. Solent quidem invisere me in his Uraniæ dicatis ædibus plærique, qui in Daniam appellunt viri Principes aut eorum Legati, quemadmodum et proxime elapso vere serenissimus Scotorum Rex atque illustrissimus Dux Brunsvicensis diverso tempore, cum amplo et splendido comitatu, nostros hîc Lares accedere non dedignati sunt. Verum ut dicam, quod res est, paucissimi inter viros vel prosapiæ claritudine, vel officio aliquo eximio rebus Politicis addictos inveniuntur, qui hæc studia penitius excoluerunt aut solidum aliquem in his progressum assecuti sunt, utut videri volunt aliquatenus ea suspicere et nullo non in pretio habere. Ideo nostra inventa atque fabricas hîc intuendo, quorsum omnia spectent et quantam observationibus Astronomicis rite peragendis promptitudinem pari certitudine coniunctam præbeant, parum discernunt. Si itaque rerum harum intelligentes et gnaviter cupidi huc accederent (inter quos te præcipuum obtinere locum, et THADDÆUM etiam nostrum, satis mihi conscius sum) facile pro tua summa prudentia æstimâris, quam id foret mihi plurimis nominibus longe gratissimum. Sed ut vos hîc præsentes exoptare quidem mihi pervolupe sit, eius tamen me voti compotem fieri, amplissima et magnifica tua functio, qua assidue apud Cæsaream Maiestatem distineris, THADDÆIque ingravescens senium haudquaquam permittent. Ut tamen aliquatenus mea observatoria instrumenta noscere possitis, quorundam modellas (ut vocant) petente id a me per literas Domino THADDÆO, dum hîc moratur Tabellarius, confieri iussi, quas etiam vobis asportandas illi tradidi. Si quam mearum in his structurarum ideam inde concipere atque (modo ita libuerit) imitari detur, equidem libenter id video. Sed vereor, ut ex tam parvis iconibus ipsissimæ machinæ omnibus suis partibus satis absolute perspiciantur, cum singula tam exigua forma repræsentari nequeant.

Est deinde præter alia summopere hoc mihi gratum, quod literis tuis adeo candide de meis scriptis iudicium profers, omniaque in potiorem partem interpretaris. Existimas autem eos, qui pro tuenda Aristotelæa opinione mihi, quod Cometas in æthere procreari asseram, adversantur, id saltem in speciem, et dicis causa (ut Iurisconsulti loquuntur) facere, quo plura ad veritatem enodandam eliciant. Et solet quidem mos hic esse quorundam, qui ignorantiam aperte fateri erubescunt, ut veritati data opera contradicendo, id quod scire avent, oblique extorqueant. Suspicor tamen illos, qui se totos ARISTOTELI addixerunt, non eo nomine hoc agere, ut latentis certitudinis abscondita in apertum deducant, sed potius, ut præceptoris sui decreta, tot iam seculis pro authenticis habita, integra conservent atque propugnent. Satis enim vident, quod concesso hoc, Cometas in altissimo æthere versari, magnam partem Philosophiæ Aristotelicæ, præsertim quantum ad Cœlestia et Meteorica attinet (ut etiam revera plæraque in his prorsus falsa sunt) penitus collabascere, seque inde deceptos frustra ætatem talibus nugis consumpsisse. Sed utrum horum sit, in medio relinquens, ego quidem, quantum in me est, quæ cœlum ipsum per accuratas observationes Geometrice expensas ostendit, in publicum proferre, atque de eis apud intelligentes testificari non intermittam. Credant vel contradicant, qui volent, et quocunque tandem affectu, sive bono sive pervicaci, id fecerint, apud me perinde erit, modo ipsemet satis certo sciam, me nihil citra ratam experientiam atque infallibiles demonstrationes in medium adducere. Veritas enim seipsa contenta est, nec hominum opinionibus afficitur.

Porro videris mihi adhuc, amplissime Domine CURTI, nonnihil hæsitare, an omnes Cometæ ætherei sint, in qua ego prorsus persisto sententia, an vero nonnulli eorum sublunares concedi possint. Et quantum colligo, multum te movet eximij illius REGIOMONTANI, tum etiam fortasse VOGELINI Viennensis in duobus Cometis, quos elementares e suis observationibus concluserunt, autoritas. At firmam eorum placitis nequaquam inesse certitudinem, alias ostendere decrevi. Plausibile quidem est, Regiomontanianum Cometam sublunarem fuisse, ex quo motu diurno proprio circa medietatem apparitionis suæ 40 gradus circuli magni confecerit. Attamen id sufficienter non probat, infra ☾am necessario extitisse. Neque etiam satis video, quomodo hæc cohæreant, Cometam paulo ante medietatem Ianuarij incipientem et usque in finem Februarij perdurantem, non multo plus dimidio cæli ambitu emensum fuisse, cum tamen pro maiori itineris parte 40 gradus uno die absolvisse dicatur, siquidem hac concitatione intra quinos dies semicirculum complevisset. Nec moror, quod REGIOMONTANUS sex graduum parallaxin illi attribuerit, cum ex certis depromptam observationibus demonstrationem, quæ id necessario convincat, nequaquam proferat. Videtur autoritate ARISTOTELIS, ut plærique alij, fascinatus studiose ex erronea aliqua consideratione sic constituisse. Licet enim Mathematicus inprimis excellens fuerit, attamen in observationibus cœlestibus citra errorem peragendis non satis versatus erat, neque instrumenta ab omni erroris suspicione vindicata ad manus habuit, prout facile liquet ex ipsius quibusdam in ☉lis et Planetarum iam dudum publicatis observationibus. Si Cometa eius toto durationis tempore per sesquialterum mensem circuli in sphæra magni arcum motu suo proprio designabat, adeo ut integrum semicirculum et paulo ultra in hoc tramite expleverit (quemadmodum ipsemet fatetur) nequaquam elementaris naturæ, sed potius cœlestis particeps fuit, cum sublunaribus Meteoris talis per circuli maximi ambitum ordinarius incessus tanto tempore et in tot reiteratis per motum primum concitatissimis revolutionibus competere nequeat. Taceo, quod parallaxin admittere sex partium et orbitam circuli magni designare inter se dissentanea sint. Non enim in altiore et decliviore situ intra eiusdem circuli limites ob parallaxeos variam divagationem continebitur. VOGELINI demonstratio incertis nititur Dedomenis, nimis lubrica via et Quadrante puerili conquisitis. Nec motum Cometæ proprium, qui parallaxin plus iusto adauxit, discernebat, et temporis intervallo nimis arcto usus est, ut facile tum his tum alijs de caussis in tam enormi parallaxi isti Cometæ imputanda labi potuerit. Quin potius illum revera cœlestem fuisse tarditas motus proprij, quo non plene tres gradus in unica revolutione diurna absolvebat, testimonium non obscurum præbet. Sed hæc uberius convenientiore loco discutienda nobis erunt.

Quod novas meas cœlestium motuum Hypotheses tuo erudito suffragia approbas, plurimum mihi ipsi congratulor. Licet enim illæ a quibusdam, præsertim Aristoteleis, in orbium soliditatibus ipsa etiam iudicij præoccupatione induratis, impugnentur, prout fieri solet circa rem novam et nondum omnibus satis perspectam, ego tamen vel tui solius, qui totum negotium penitius introspicere et undiquaque ponderare optime nosti, de his censuram omnium istorum meris opinationibus præfero. Nec dubium erit, quin per has Hypotheses apparentijs cœlestibus abunde admodumque exacte satisfiat, quodque omnium ætatum observationibus congruant, quatenus per veterum non ubique satis subtiliter factas animadversiones id ullo modo præstare licebit. Nam et in ipso Ptolemæo, utut Ægiptiacis magnifice et Regijs sumtibus extructis organis uso, tam in fixarum quam in errantium stellarum observationibus plæraque ad præcisionem accuratiorem requisita desidero. Recentiorum, si quæ habentur circa cœlestia adversiones, dubiæ nimisque lato modo acceptæ sunt, adeo ut ne quidem magni illius COPERNICI circa has annotationibus satis tuto fidere liceat. Et quid facient, cum ne Solis quidem motum et affixarum stellarum ipsi cœlo congruentia loca hactenus rite explorare sustinuerint, ut de instrumentorum, quibus innituntur, fallacitate nihil dicam.

Sunt mihi in promtu plurimorum proxime antecedentium annorum exquisitæ in stellis, tam fixis quam erraticis, atque ambobus luminaribus observationes, quarum eæ, quas postremis duodecim, postquam in hac Insula solidiora et ampliora organa ad amussim elaborari curavi, nactus sum, eiuscemodi sunt, ut solidam Astronomiæ instaurationem illis superstruere tutum sit. Expecto adhuc nonnullas in tribus penderosioribus Planetis acronychas animadversiones, quo plurimis invicem collatis, in distantioribus præsertim eccentricorum locis, certius et exactius de Apogæis et eccentricitatibus pronuntiem. Deprehendo enim Apogæa Planetarum intra aliquot gradus non esse rite hactenus disposita, ut eccentricitates, quæ etiam paulo aliter se habent, iam præteream. Quamprimum autem Opus illud Recentiorum cæli Phaenomenωn, quod nunc præ manibus habeo, absolvero, statim ad alterum de mundo coævorum syderum revolutionibus, favente cœlestium Opifice, pertexendum me accingam. Sternent enim hæc præcedentia in plurimis ad sequentia viam, et hypotheseos nostræ certitudinem, eo præsertim quod cœlum realibus orbibus compactum non sit, ut hactenus frustra creditum est, confirmabunt, atque unâ Solis et fixarum stellarum restitutionem accuratam, sine qua cætera frustra tentantur, suppeditabunt. Maturabo autem, quo ad eius fieri poterit, et a divina clementia concessum fuerit, posterius illud astrorum perennium opus. Facile tamen per teipsum æstimare potes, tantum laborem modico tempore non exantlari. Fui hisce aliquot præcedentibus annis architectonicis curis et instrumentorum variorum fabricatione plurimum occupatus, adeo ut præter observationum, quas interea conquisivi, aliqualem copiam earundemque, tam inter se, quam cum aliorum inventis collationem et hypotheseos motibus apparentibus convenientem ordinationem nihil fere, quantum ad Tabularum structuram, moliri licuerit: quandoquidem etiam sine tabulis Astronomicis per solam triangularem supputationem Planetarum situs secundum aliquam datam hypothesin explorare non sit difficile. Quamprimum autem cætera, quæ prius conficienda suscepi, ad colophonem perduxero, tum demum hypothesium cum observationibus, tam veterum quam nostris, conciliationem et comprobationem Canonumque Astronomicorum hinc deductam calculationem, quæ plus laboris quam ingenij requiret, instituere opportunum erit. Interim si quæ (uti in rebus fit arduis) morula inciderit, æquanimiter ferenda est, et divinæ providentiæ, quæ actiones et successus rerum humanarum moderatur, omnia cum ipsius auxilij imploratione submisse et patienter committenda.

Quadrantis ultimo a te adinventa in singula minuta distributio admodum ingeniosa est, et si dextre perficiatur, non dubito, quin usui etiam accommoda sit. Videtur tamen in ipsa constructione paululo laboriosior, et tot alij Quadrantes, intra ultimum ducti, aliquantum spatij occupare necesse habent. Unde fit, ut intimus et centro proximus respectu ultimi plurimum de ambitu amittat, quod et de intermedijs quo ad magis vel minus intelligendum. Sicque divisionibus minimis rite discernendis non æque in ipsa praxi capaces erunt, ac si iuxta ultimum et maximum Quadrantem omnis subdistinctio perficeretur. Ipsa quoque fiduciæ linea non facile unum punctum ab altero discernet, nisi iustæ magnitudinis fuerit instrumentum. Certum tamen est, hanc tuam inventionem Nonianâ multis modis prævalere, imo et illi præferendam, quam ex tua indicatione CLAVIUS suo Scioterico libello in publicum protulit. Ut tamen id, quod res postulat, indicem: omnes eiuscemodi inventiones, licet admodum ingeniosæ sint, et quo ad speculationem ipsamque demonstrationem Geometricam rite constent, cum tamen ad praxin mechanicam atque ipsam in observationibus tractandis expeditionem perventumfuerit, tunc demum patefit, an id re ipsa præstent, quod prima fronte pollicentur. Noniana certe distinctio, etiam subtiliore quam ab ipso ratione et pluribus particulis administrata (modus enim, quo is utitur, non suppeditat omnia 90 graduum minuta) ijs, quæ autor pollicetur, nequaquam satisfacit, quod in duobus Quadrantibus orichalcicis tali methodo subtilissime elaborata divisione ipsa me dudum docuit experientia. Per hanc etiam rerum magistram vere id assecutus sum, ut nisi Quadrantes per se sufficientem habeant magnitudinem, utpote 4 vel trium ad minimum cubitorum, frustra in subdivisionum compendijs, quibus singula minuta scite exprimantur, laborari. In minoribus etenim organis punctationes, quacunque tandem solertia ordinatæ, plures simul quasi in eandem fiduciæ lineam incidunt, ut eas ab invicem discernere vix liceat, prout ipsamet experimentatio te satis edocebit. Ego in Quadrantum aut Armillarum et quorumvis arcuum in minimas quasque particulas distributione utor punctis quibusdam transversalibus sextam vel duodecimam graduum partem in singula minuta vel etiam horum aliquotam portionem subdividentibus, prout circa finem libri nostri secundi Æthereorum Phaenomenωn hunc gradus singulos minutatim subdividendi modum quodammodo denotavi, qui præ omnibus alijs et factu facilis et usui in primis accomodatus, tum etiam per se nihil vitij aut dubij suggerens diutina mea tractatione comprobatus est, quem etiam tanquam maxime expeditum et certum omnibus meis instrumentis adhibeo. Ideoque eum, si quando organa Astronomica confici curaveris, tibi commendatum volo. Experiêre utique multo operosioribus inventis, quo ad praxeos indubiam administrationem prævalere. Hoc quoque illustrissimus Princeps WILHELMUS, Landgravius Hassiæ, in suis Quadrantibus atque Sextantibus aliquot iam annis, postquam ex instructione WITICHIJ ad meorum imitationem sua instrumenta, tam quo ad graduum subdivisionem quam pinnacidiorum applicationem, renovavit, feliciter utitur. Habet insuper hæc ratio id peculiare, quod parvo spatio comprehensa Armillis etiam quibusvis applicari possit, quod in alijs, ubi plurimæ introrsum circinationes ducuntur, minus competit.

Postremo intelligo, te etiam in triangulorum erudita scientia egregie et solide versatum, et compendia in his numerorum tractationi faciliori inservientia ingeniose et utiliter adinvenire, quod facio, ut par est, plurimi. Quin et ego triangulorum supputationem compendiosiorem reddere non parum laboravi. Si tua etiam inventa et aliorum, quotquot nancisci liceat (plus enim vident oculi quam oculus) mecum per opportunitatem communicaveris, rem pergratam præstiteris.

Sed ut Epistolæ modum non excedam, nec te gravioribus publici officij oneribus occupatum nimium detineam, modo finem faciam, omnium Creatori Deo ex animo te commendans. Is te in Reipub. tam politicæ quam literariæ, ornamentum et emolumentum diutissime magnis donis et varijs bonis florentem conservare dignetur. Vale, arnplissime vir, atque hæc inter plurimas alias curas exarata pro tuo singulari candore æqui bonique consule, meque tua benevolentia atque amore, quod facis, in posterum complecti non intermitte. Ego vicissim, quantum in me est, quocunque genere officij liceat, te demereri conabor, atque ad gratificandum perpetuo quam paratissimum me præstabo.

Iterum, mi CURTI, amice eximie, vale.
Datæ Uraniburgi die primo Augusti veteri stylo Anno 1590.

To the magnificent and most distinguished man, conspicuous above all for nobility of birth and outstanding erudition, Master Jacob Kurz von Senftenau, most prudent Councillor of His Imperial Majesty and Vice-Chancellor of the Empire, his most honoured friend — greeting.

How welcome to me your letter was — full both of distinguished learning and of singular regard toward me — which you forwarded together with the most honourable Privilege of His Imperial Majesty, most distinguished and most noble man, cannot be told by me in few words. Nor do I find how I might render you thanks great enough to match the deserving, both because you obtained for me, by your intercession and authority, that magnificent Diploma — drawn up by yourself with skill and grace, to the praise and advancement of the astronomical art — and also because, detained though you are by so many and so great occupations of public administration, which press upon you day by day, you nonetheless took so much leisure for yourself that you were not reluctant to write me a reply so very luminous and so friendly. For both these kindnesses of yours I am so bound to you that, if anything could ever proceed from me that should be acceptable or honourable to you, you will find me mindful and grateful in all things with the utmost readiness of mind. And affirming and promising this sincerely concerning myself, I shall not weary you with a longer rendering of thanks, but shall answer your letter in few words, as the time allows.

As to your declaring, at the outset, so kindly a disposition toward me and toward the astronomical undertaking which I have taken up to cultivate on this island to the best of my powers — namely, that, if it were permitted by the Emperor's will, you would not count it a burden to visit me here and to inspect in person the elaborated aids for the purifying [reform] of astronomy and to confer with me about them — this I judge should rather be ascribed to your singular candour and to your studious inclination toward astronomical matters than that I should dare to promise anything of the sort concerning myself and my labours that would deserve to entice you, occupied with so illustrious an office to the great benefit of the Commonwealth, hither into Denmark, through so many windings of road by land and sea. Yet would that it might one day befall me as a piece of good fortune that I should be allowed to behold you here in person, together with our common friend [Tadeáš] Hájek — truly nothing could come to me more after my wish or more welcome. Indeed many of the princely men, or their envoys, who put in to Denmark are accustomed to visit me in this house dedicated to Urania — just as, this very spring last past, the most serene King of Scots and the most illustrious Duke of Brunswick, at different times, with an ample and splendid retinue, did not disdain to approach our hearth here. But, to say what the matter is, very few are found among the men devoted to political affairs, whether by the brightness of their lineage or by some eminent office, who have cultivated these studies more deeply or attained some solid progress in them — however much they wish to seem to look up to them somewhat and to hold them in no small esteem. And so, in looking upon our inventions and constructions here, they little discern to what end all things tend, and how great a readiness, joined with equal certainty, they afford for the duly carrying out of astronomical observations. If, therefore, men understanding of these matters and zealously desirous of them were to come here (among whom I am well aware that you hold the foremost place, and our [Hájek] too), you will easily reckon, in your great prudence, how this would be to me, on very many counts, by far the most welcome thing. But while it is most delightful to me indeed to long for you present here, yet your most ample and magnificent function, by which you are continually detained at His Imperial Majesty's side, and [Hájek's] advancing old age, will by no means permit me to obtain this wish. That you may, however, in some measure come to know my observational instruments, I have had certain little models (as they call them) made — Master [Hájek] having requested it of me by letter — while the courier was tarrying here, and I have handed these over to him to be carried to you as well. If from these any notion of my structures may be conceived and (provided it should so please) imitated, I for my part gladly see it. But I fear that from such small pictures the actual machines themselves may not be seen well enough in all their parts and entire, since the several details cannot be represented in so tiny a form.

Next, this is, beyond other things, exceedingly pleasing to me: that in your letter you set forth your judgement so candidly concerning my writings, and interpret everything in the better part. You consider, moreover, that those who oppose me — for the sake of upholding the Aristotelian opinion — in that I assert comets to be generated in the aether, do this only for appearance's sake, and (as the jurisconsults say) "for the sake of argument" [dicis causa], so as to draw out more toward the unravelling of the truth. And indeed this is wont to be the way of certain men who blush to confess ignorance openly: by deliberately contradicting the truth, they wrest out obliquely that which they are eager to know. Yet I suspect that those who have given themselves wholly over to Aristotle do this not on that account — to bring the hidden things of a latent certainty into the open — but rather to keep whole and to defend the decrees of their master, held now for so many ages as authentic. For they see well enough that, this once granted — that comets dwell in the highest aether — a great part of Aristotelian Philosophy, especially as regards the celestial and the meteorological matters (as indeed most things in these are utterly false), would wholly collapse, and that they themselves, deceived thereby, had vainly spent their lifetime on such trifles. But leaving it undecided which of these it is, I for my part, so far as in me lies, shall not cease to bring forth into public what the heaven itself shows through accurate observations geometrically weighed, and to testify of them among the understanding. Let those who will believe or contradict, and with whatever disposition in the end they shall have done it, whether well-meaning or obstinate, it will be all the same with me — provided I myself know with sufficient certainty that I bring forward into the midst nothing short of ratified experience and infallible demonstrations. For truth is content with itself, and is not affected by the opinions of men.

Furthermore, you seem to me still to hesitate somewhat, most distinguished Master Kurz, whether all comets are aetherial — in which opinion I altogether persist — or whether some of them may be granted to be sublunar. And so far as I gather, you are much moved by the authority of that eminent Regiomontanus, and then perhaps also of Vögelin of Vienna, in the two comets which from their observations they concluded to be elementary. But that no firm certainty resides in their assertions I have resolved to show elsewhere. It is plausible indeed that the Regiomontanian comet was sublunar, inasmuch as by its own diurnal motion it accomplished, about the middle of its appearance, 40 degrees of a great circle. Yet this does not sufficiently prove that it necessarily existed below the Moon [☾]. Nor do I see well enough how these things hang together: that the comet, beginning a little before the middle of January and lasting until the end of February, traversed not much more than half the compass of the sky, when nonetheless it is said to have completed, for the greater part of its journey, 40 degrees in a single day — since at this speed it would have completed a semicircle within five days. Nor do I dwell on the fact that Regiomontanus attributed to it a parallax of six degrees, since he by no means brings forward a demonstration drawn from sure observations that would necessarily prove it. He seems, bewitched by the authority of Aristotle — like most others — to have so determined it studiously, out of some erroneous consideration. For although he was a most excellent mathematician, yet he was not sufficiently versed in carrying out celestial observations free from error, nor had he to hand instruments vindicated from all suspicion of error — as is easily clear from certain of his long-since-published observations on the Sun [☉] and the planets. If his comet, throughout its whole duration of a month and a half, marked out by its own motion an arc of a great circle in the sphere, so much so that it filled out a whole semicircle and a little beyond on this track (as he himself confesses), then it was by no means of an elementary nature, but rather a partaker of the celestial — since such a regular procession through the compass of a greatest circle, over so long a time and in so many repeated, most rapid revolutions by the prime motion, cannot belong to sublunar meteors. I pass over in silence that to admit a parallax of six parts [degrees] and to mark out the orbit of a great circle are mutually inconsistent. For it will not be contained within the limits of the same circle, at a higher and a lower position, on account of the varying wandering produced by parallax. Vögelin's demonstration rests on uncertain data [Dedomena], obtained by too slippery a way and with a childish quadrant. Nor did he discern the comet's own motion, which increased the parallax more than was right; and he used too narrow an interval of time, so that he could easily, both for these and for other reasons, fall into error in imputing so enormous a parallax to that comet. Rather, that it was in truth celestial, the slowness of its own motion — by which it completed not fully three degrees in a single diurnal revolution — affords no obscure testimony. But these things will have to be discussed by us more fully in a fitter place.

That by your learned vote you approve my new Hypotheses of the celestial motions, I greatly congratulate myself. For although they are assailed by certain men — especially the Aristotelians, hardened in the solidities of the orbs by the very prepossession of their own judgement, as is wont to happen with a matter that is new and not yet sufficiently discerned by all — yet I prefer the verdict of you alone concerning these things, who know best how to look more deeply into the whole business and to weigh it on every side, to the mere opinionings of all those others. Nor will there be any doubt that by these Hypotheses the celestial appearances are satisfied abundantly and very exactly, and that they agree with the observations of every age — so far as it will in any way be possible to achieve this, given the observations of the ancients, not everywhere subtly enough made. For even in Ptolemy himself — though he used instruments magnificently and at royal expense erected in Egypt — I find wanting, both in the fixed and in the wandering stars, most of the things required for a more accurate precision. As for the more recent men, such observations of the celestial bodies as are had are doubtful and taken in too loose a manner, so much so that one cannot safely enough trust even the annotations of that great Copernicus concerning these matters. And what will they do, since they have not hitherto duly undertaken to explore even the motion of the Sun and the places of the fixed stars in agreement with the heaven itself — to say nothing of the fallibility of the instruments on which they rely?

I have ready to hand exquisite observations of very many of the immediately preceding years, in the stars both fixed and wandering and in both luminaries; and of these, the ones I have obtained in the last twelve years — after I had had more solid and ample instruments precisely fashioned on this island — are of such a kind that it is safe to build a solid restoration of astronomy upon them. I am still awaiting some acronychal observations in the three more ponderous planets, so that, with very many compared with one another — especially in the more distant places of the eccentrics — I may pronounce more surely and exactly concerning the apogees and eccentricities. For I find that the apogees of the planets have not hitherto been rightly disposed, within several degrees — to pass over for now the eccentricities, which also stand a little otherwise. But as soon as I have completed that Work on the Phenomena of the recent heaven [the comets], which I now have in hand, I shall at once gird myself, with the favour of the Maker of the celestial things, to weave through the other — concerning the world and the revolutions of the coeval heavenly bodies. For these preceding [works] will in many points pave the way to those that follow, and will confirm the certainty of our hypothesis — especially in this, that the heaven is not compacted of real orbs, as has hitherto been vainly believed — and will at the same time supply the accurate restoration of the Sun and of the fixed stars, without which the rest are attempted in vain. I shall hasten on, so far as it shall be able to be done and as it shall be granted by the divine clemency, that latter work of the perennial stars. Yet you can easily reckon for yourself that so great a labour is not drained out [completed] in a modest time. In these several preceding years I have been very much occupied with architectural cares and with the fabrication of various instruments, so much so that — besides amassing some store of observations, which I gathered in the meantime, and a collation of them, both with one another and with the inventions of others, and an ordering of them conformable to the hypothesis of the apparent motions — I have been able to undertake almost nothing as regards the construction of Tables: seeing that, even without astronomical tables, it is not difficult, by triangular computation alone, to explore the positions of the planets according to some given hypothesis. But as soon as I shall have brought to the colophon [completion] the other things which I undertook to finish first, then at last it will be opportune to set in train the reconciling and corroborating of the hypotheses with the observations, both of the ancients and our own, and the calculation of the Astronomical Canons [tables] deduced therefrom — which will require more labour than wit. Meanwhile, if any little delay should fall in (as happens in arduous matters), it must be borne with an even mind, and all things must be committed, with imploring of His help, submissively and patiently to the divine providence, which governs the actions and outcomes of human affairs.

The distribution into single minutes, lately invented by you, of the quadrant is exceedingly ingenious, and if it be rightly carried out, I do not doubt that it is also suited to use. Yet it seems in its very construction a little more laborious, and so many other quadrants, drawn within the outermost one, must of necessity occupy a certain amount of space. Whence it comes about that the innermost one, nearest the centre, loses very much of its compass with respect to the outermost — which is also to be understood of the intermediate ones, in greater or lesser degree. And thus they will not in actual practice be equally capable of duly distinguishing the smallest divisions, as if every subdivision were carried out alongside the outermost and greatest quadrant. The line of sight [line of confidence] itself, too, will not easily distinguish one point from another, unless the instrument be of a fitting size. Yet it is certain that this invention of yours prevails over the Nonian one in many ways — indeed is even to be preferred to that which, on your indication, Clavius brought forth into public in his little book on the sundial [the Scioterico]. But, that I may point out what the matter demands: all inventions of this kind, however ingenious they be, and however rightly they stand as regards speculation and geometrical demonstration itself, yet when it comes to mechanical practice and to expedition itself in handling observations, only then does it become plain whether they perform in fact what they promise at first sight. The Nonian distinction, certainly — even when administered by a subtler method than his own and with more particles (for the method he uses does not supply all the minutes of the 90 degrees) — by no means satisfies what its author promises; which experience itself long ago taught me by the division most subtly elaborated by such a method on two brass [orichalc] quadrants. Through this same mistress of things [experience] I have truly attained this also: that unless the quadrants have of themselves sufficient size — to wit, of four or at the least three cubits — labour is spent in vain on the contrivances of subdivision by which the single minutes might be skilfully expressed. For on smaller instruments the points [markings], ordered with whatever ingenuity in the end, fall together, several at once, as it were onto the same line of sight, so that one can scarcely distinguish them from one another — as experimentation itself will teach you well enough. I, in the distribution of quadrants or armillae and of any arcs whatever into all their smallest particles, use certain transversal points, subdividing the sixth or twelfth part of the degrees into single minutes or even into some aliquot portion of these — as, toward the end of our second book of the Aetherial Phenomena, I have in some fashion noted this manner of subdividing the single degrees minute by minute; which, before all others both easy to make and especially suited to use, and moreover of itself suggesting no fault or doubt, has been proved by my long handling of it, and which I also apply to all my instruments as the most expeditious and certain. And therefore I would have it commended to you, if ever you have astronomical instruments made. You will assuredly find by experience that it prevails over much more laborious inventions as regards the undoubted management of practice. This too the most illustrious Prince Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, happily uses in his quadrants and sextants for some years now — after, on the instruction of Wittich, in imitation of mine, he renewed his instruments, both as regards the subdivision of the degrees and the application of the sights [pinnules]. This method has, besides, this peculiar advantage: that, comprised in a small space, it can also be applied to armillae of any kind whatever — which holds less for others, where very many circlings are drawn inward.

Lastly, I understand that you too are excellently and solidly versed in the learned science of triangles, and ingeniously and usefully devise abridgements serving in these for the easier handling of numbers — which, as is fitting, I esteem very highly. Indeed I too have laboured not a little to render the computation of triangles more compendious. If you also communicate to me, as opportunity allows, your inventions and those of others, as many as can be obtained (for eyes see more than one eye), you will have done a most welcome thing.

But that I may not exceed the measure of a letter, nor detain you too long, occupied as you are with the heavier burdens of public office, I shall now make an end, commending you with all my heart to God the Creator of all. May He deign to keep you long flourishing, an ornament and benefit to the Commonwealth both political and literary, with great gifts and various good things. Farewell, most distinguished man, and take in good and equitable part these things written amid very many other cares, in keeping with your singular candour; and do not cease in future to embrace me with your kindness and love, as you do. I in turn, so far as in me lies, in whatever kind of service may be possible, shall strive to deserve well of you, and shall ever show myself most ready to do you a kindness.

Again, my Kurz, excellent friend, farewell.
Given at Uraniborg on the first day of August, Old Style, in the year 1590.

Apparatus

  • Sender. Tycho Brahe (1546–1601), writing from Uraniborg on Hven.
  • Recipient. Iacobus Curtius a Senfftenaw = Jacob Kurz von Senftenau (Jakob Kurtz; d. 1594), Imperial Councillor and Vice-Chancellor (Imperij Procancellarius) under Rudolf II at Prague, an accomplished mathematician in his own right. He had secured for Tycho the Imperial Privilege protecting his works (the Diploma) and corresponded with him on instruments and on the cometary question.
  • Edition of record. Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia, vol. VII (Epistolae). MS witness as given in the source header: Vienna, ÖNB, cod. Vindob. lat. 10686.61.
  • THADDAeUS — Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku (Thaddaeus Hagecius, 1525–1600), Bohemian court physician and astronomer at Prague, the "common friend" who linked Tycho and Kurz; he had requested the instrument models. Identified in brackets in the translation on each occurrence, as the source gives only the first name.
  • Distinguished visitors. serenissimus Scotorum Rex = James VI of Scotland (later James I of England), who visited Hven in spring 1590 during his Danish marriage journey; illustrissimus Dux Brunsvicensis = the Duke of Brunswick (Henry Julius of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel). "This very spring last past" (proxime elapso vere) = spring 1590, O.S. — flagged as in-letter dating, not reconciled.
  • Greek-letter genitives. Phaenomenωn (twice: Recentiorum caeli Phaenomenωn; Aethereorum Phaenomenωn) writes the Greek genitive-plural ending with the Greek letter omega (ω) — a learned typographic flourish for the Latinised Phaenomenon/Phaenomena. Reproduced verbatim in the Source block; rendered "Phenomena" in the translation. These name Tycho's projected/forthcoming works: the Opus Recentiorum caeli Phaenomenωn = his treatise on recent comets (published 1588 as De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis, of which the comet of 1577 is book II — liber secundus Aethereorum Phaenomenωn), and the planned companion de mundi … syderum revolutionibus (the never-completed Theatrum astronomicum / planetary theory).
  • Greek loan-word. Dedomenis (Dedomena, δεδομένα, "givens/data") — the technical term for the data of a problem; transcribed and glossed "data" in the translation.
  • Planet/luminary glyphs (named in English by this chapter's convention; glyph kept in Source block): ☾ (infra ☾am = "below the Moon," with the glyph inflected as a Latin accusative Lunam) → Moon; ☉ (in ☉lis = in Solis, "on the Sun['s observations]," glyph inflected as genitive Solis) → Sun.
  • Cometary debate. Cometas in aethere procreari — Tycho's central thesis (from De mundi … recentioribus phaenomenis, 1588) that comets are generated in the aether, above the Moon, refuting the Aristotelian doctrine of comets as sublunar exhalations (Meteora). REGIOMONTANUS = Johann Müller of Königsberg (Regiomontanus, 1436–1476), whose treatise on the comet (the comet of 1472) assigned it a 6° parallax and hence a sublunar location; VOGELINUS Viennensis = Georg Vögelin (Vogelin) of Vienna, author of a Significatio cometae on the comet of 1532, likewise concluding a sublunar (elementary) comet. Tycho's technical objections (the 40°/day proper motion, the semicircle-in-five-days reductio, the inconsistency of a 6° parallax with a great-circle path, Vögelin's "childish quadrant" and too-short time-base) are rendered literally; no astronomical "correction" is supplied.
  • Technical terms (editorial glossary–5.2). parallaxis → parallax; Apogaea → apogees; eccentricitates / eccentricorum → eccentricities / eccentrics; Hypotheses (motuum) → Hypotheses (of the motions); orbium soliditatibus / realibus orbibus compactum → "the solidities of the orbs" / "compacted of real orbs" (Tycho's denial of solid celestial spheres); acronychas animadversiones → acronychal observations (i.e. at acronychal/opposition rising); restitutio / instauratio astronomiae → restoration of astronomy; organa → instruments; Quadrantes / Sextantes / Armillae → quadrants / sextants / armillae; pinnacidiorum → sights (pinnules); Canones Astronomici → Astronomical Canons (tables); triangularis supputatio → triangular computation (trigonometric calculation).
  • Quadrant-subdivision passage. Quadrantis … in singula minuta distributio — Kurz's proposed method of subdividing a quadrant's arc to single minutes by nested concentric quadrant-arcs. Noniana … distinctio = the nonius subdivision of Pedro Nunes (Petrus Nonius), a nested set of 45 auxiliary arcs (not the later Vernier); CLAVIUS … suo Scioterico libello = Christoph Clavius, who published a version of the Nonius scale (on Kurz's indication) in his Gnomonices libri octo / sundial treatise (scioterico = relating to the sundial, σκιαθηρικόν). transversalibus punctis = Tycho's own transversal (diagonal) point subdivision, described "toward the end of book II of the Aetherial Phenomena." orichalcicis = of orichalc/brass. WITICHIUS = Paul Wittich (d. 1586), the Silesian mathematician who carried Tycho's instrument-design ideas to Kassel; WILHELMUS, Landgravius Hassiae = Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel.
  • Latin idiom. dicis causa — a legal phrase ("for form's sake / for the sake of argument"), explicitly flagged by Tycho as the jurisconsults' usage; kept and glossed. ad colophonem perduxero — "bring to the colophon," i.e. to completion (printer's metaphor). rerum magistra (sc. experientia) — "the mistress of things," experience. plus vident oculi quam oculus — proverbial ("more eyes see more than one eye"); kept as the sententia it is.
  • Calendar. Dateline Datae Uraniburgi die primo Augusti veteri stylo Anno 1590 = "Given at Uraniborg, 1 August, Old Style, 1590." Tycho's explicit veteri stylo (O.S.) is significant given that his Imperial correspondent at Catholic Prague would reckon New Style; the header date matches the dateline. No discrepancy with the catalogue label (1 August 1590).
