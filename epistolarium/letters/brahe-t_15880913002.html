Editorial note. A foundational document — Tycho's first long letter to Caspar Peucer (earlier than the undated reply of 1590), and the locus classicus of the autobiographical genesis of the Tychonic world-system: how, from a Leipzig boyhood with the cross-staff, through the deficiencies of both the Ptolemaic/Alphonsine and Copernican hypotheses, the Mars-parallax test of 1582 and the abandonment of the solid orbs (from the comets) led him to his geo-heliocentric arrangement. It also lays out the 7-book plan of his great Astronomical work, describes Uraniborg and Stjerneborg, casts (from old notes) Peucer's own horoscope, refutes Francisco Valles, and treats the Isaiah/Job fluid-heavens exegesis (overlapping the undated 1590 Peucer letter) and the Mars-parallax/system content (overlapping the Magini letter of 1 Dec 1590, the Landgrave letter of 16 Aug 1588, and the Hájek letter of 3 Aug 1590). Two declared interventions in the source block: (1) the digitised text layer-intruded apparatus "A" sigla (footnote-anchors to the witnesses) on some 90 words are removed as non-textual (as in the letters to Scaliger of 23 Aug 1598, to Peucer of 1590, to the Landgrave of 16 Aug 1588, to Herwart of 16 Nov 1599, and from Rothmann of 13 Oct 1588); (2) a genuine lacuna/anacoluthon in the source ("…circa Solem annua per totos orbes circumgyratione …" before "Habuit etiam quidam alius Mathematicus") is marked … and flagged. Tycho Brahe to Caspar Peucer. Sends greeting.

I have long had [it] in my wishes to contract a most welcome friendship with you — either in person, when I once passed through Wittenberg, or also afterward, absent, by letter — most celebrated man, excelling in varied learning and virtue; but since no convenience offered itself sufficiently opportunely meanwhile for effecting this, I was forced to defer it until the present time. But the occasion of at last in some part fulfilling this long-standing desire of mine was given by the letter of the most ample and most noble man Lord Heinrich Rantzau, Royal Vicar in Holstein, most ample in Mathematical matters. For there was added to it a certain transcript of those [things] which you had lately transmitted to him about my new invention of the disposition of the celestial revolutions; and at the same time he asked that I would be willing to meet your doubts about the delineation communicated to you by him — which, that I might perform it for the favour of you both, I judged that I must not omit to send this letter to you; in which, that your expectation and the resolution of [your] doubts may more fully be satisfied, I see that I must begin a little higher up — namely, from the very first occasion of the discovery of this hypothesis — and discourse somewhat more diffusely about this matter; and this I will now undertake.

Since from [my] entering age I was, among other parts of Philosophy, earnestly addicted — by a certain instinct of nature, even against the will and resistance of my very tutors — to the Astronomical study, and at once in boyhood, dwelling at Leipzig for studies' sake and being in the seventeenth year of my age, often scrutinized the courses of the Planets from the fixed stars by the Cross-staff [Radius Astronomicus] on serene nights, I noticed that their appearances did not agree precisely enough even with the Copernican calculation, much less the Alphonsine, which was now well-nigh obsolete. Thence I began long and much to ponder with myself whence this so evident discrepancy from the heaven itself arose — whether on account of observations not rightly instituted by the artificers, or also on account of the insufficiency and falsity of the assumed hypotheses. And when over some years I turned this matter variously over, my age now increasing and my judgment maturing more and more along with it, I inspected more diligently both the Alphonsine (or Ptolemaic) hypotheses and the Copernican, and inquired more deeply into their reasonings, and at last noticed that in those old hypotheses, deduced from Ptolemy and certain of his predecessors down to us — although for the most part they excused [saved] the celestial appearances — yet, because they admitted that the regularity of circular motion is made not about [the] proper but [about] some alien centre, [they] sin against the first principles of the art (which Copernicus himself too seems to have faulted in these); and besides, that the epicycles, so many and so great, which are there too assumed, occupy much space in the heaven and are superfluous, if the whole matter could be accomplished by fewer. And a great scruple they threw into me — that no necessary cause or natural combination underlay [the fact] why the superior Planets were so bound to the Sun that, conjoined to it, they always occupied the summit of [their] epicycles, but in the lowest place of the same were opposed to it; and the two which they call inferior always shared the same mean place with the Sun, and were joined to it at the Apogee and Perigee of [their] epicycles. These [things], I say, it was not possible to gather to have a sure and evident reason of combination — since these stars are distant from one another by so great an interval, so that, if this mutual concatenation were made precisely in that manner, that some competent necessity underlay [it], that everything in it should so happen, appeared to me less consonant; but rather, even for this sole cause, I suspected — not without reason — that something forced, dissonant, and superfluous lay hidden in these assumptions. But in the other, the neoteric distribution of that supreme Copernicus, the absurdity about the threefold motion of the Earth at once deterred [me] — but especially as to that annual declination of the centre and axis into contrary parts. For although it opportunely remedied the rest of [the things] which are found incoherent and superfluous in the Ptolemaic disposition, and offended nothing against Mathematical principles, yet, since it attributed a regular, perfect, and less-intricate motion to the Earth, a sluggish and ignobler body, this assumption was rendered not a little suspect — especially since the sacred writings in not a few places openly cry out against it. Hence I doubted not a little about both of these hypotheses, whether either of them stood rightly. But since no other was given which was consonant with the celestial appearances, and the figment of homocentrics devised by some [Fracastoro, Amici] was too violent, and through the inextricable retraction of many orbs this way and that was found absurd and even ridiculous, nor did it satisfy the appearances themselves in the approach and recession of the Planets to the Earth — therefore, long entangled in these doubts, since no innovation of hypotheses at once occurred (as I said) which might relieve all these requirements, I resolved at least to make a trial [of] which of those hitherto invented came nearer to the truth. And I trusted that I could fish this out chiefly through the star of Mars, when, namely, it should approach the Earth acronychally [at opposition]; for if it then came nearer to us than the Sun itself, [I judged] the Copernican speculation to prevail; if not, [I judged] one must rather stand by the Ptolemaic. And at last, with great diligence and no small expense, having procured various Astronomical instruments by which the motions of the stars could be explored centrally [with certainty] not only to the very minute but even to its half or quarter, I did not fear to undertake this subtle consideration — and that chiefly in the year 1582, when Mars, made all-night-visible, was opposed to the Sun in Cancer, and besides was rendered higher in [its] daily circuit by [its] northern latitude. Having had then several accurate observations, both about [its] rising and setting and its transit through the meridian, I found that Mars causes a greater parallax than the Sun itself, and therefore is made nearer to the earth when it is acronychal — its diurnal motion too agreeing, [and] consenting rather with the Copernican numbers, in that it stepped back into the preceding [direction] a little more swiftly, over a certain interval of days, than the reckoning of the Alphonsine [Tables], deduced from Ptolemy, would grant — and that on account of [its] smaller distance from the earth, which made its motion appear a little more intense. When I had this sufficiently explored in Mars, and the same too in Venus — observed by me several times by day, and indeed on the very meridian, and a comparison made near setting; nay, also by a certain peculiar method in the year 1587 about the 24th of February, when within one or another day I saw it both as evening and as morning [star] (which is otherwise very rare), comparing the apparent motion with that which would occur by reason of parallax — so that all these by no means agreed with the Ptolemaic hypotheses, I was impelled afterward more and more to attribute credit to the Copernican invention. Yet there stood greatly in the way the excessive absurdity about the Earth's revolution, so ordinary and perpetual, and besides the irrefragable authority of the sacred writings building the contrary. Wherefore, turning this business variously this way and that, I deliberated studiously with myself about another ordering of the hypotheses. At first I seemed to attempt an impossible thing; but at last, almost unexpectedly, it occurred [to me] — that if the Sun be constituted the centre of the five Planets, and nonetheless be revolved annually about the Earth resting in the middle of the universe (the circuits of the remaining five Planets accompanying [it] through the centre, and therefore also [through] the very circumference), so that the Earth furnish the centre at least for the revolutions of the Sun and the Moon and of the highest eighth sphere including all the rest — if, I say, the celestial revolutions be ordered in that manner, then all [the things] which fall out dissonant, idle, and superfluous in the Ptolemaic and Copernican assumption are removed and forestalled. But nonetheless it still threw in a scruple, that the Martian orb, relative to the Solar, was not found so great that it could totally include it, but necessarily penetrated it in two places. For I was still imbued with the long-received and by almost all approved opinion that the heaven is packed with certain real orbs carrying the stars around, and therefore I judged this incongruous penetration of orbs not to be admitted; and thence it came about that this my own invention was for some while suspect to me myself. But at last, when from the accurate examination of certain Comets, as to their motion and parallaxes, I had explored for certain that they completed the norm of their course in the heaven itself, far above the Moon, and were nonetheless subject to no corporeal and real orbs by which the Planets are believed to be carried, but had found a certain peculiar reckoning of motion from these — as is more amply and exquisitely demonstrated in our work on these ascititious phenomena of the heaven — I utterly cast aside that presumption about the hard and impervious matter of the celestial orbs. There was added this too, that two Comets, which were borne near the opposite of the Sun, showed evidently enough that the Earth is not really revolved annually — since its commutation detracted nothing from their predefined and proportionable motion, as comes about in the Planets, which on that account are thought by Copernicus to be driven backward. At last, therefore, both foundations — both the Copernican and the Ptolemaic — being overturned by experience itself, I confirmed in mind that this neoteric invention stood rightly beyond all doubt — especially because I perceived from the Comets (as I said) that the heaven consists of a most fluid and proper substance, and abounds in no hard and real orbs (as has been falsely believed now for so many centuries); so that on that account it presents no obstacle that the Martian imaginary orb does not totally include the Solar, but intersects [it] in two places — since those orbs are not corporeally in the heaven, but yet are understood to come about from the motion of the stars. Soon, within the next four years, I compared the more exact appearances in the motion of the five Planets with this our hypothesis, and noticed that they agreed with it most openly, and abundantly satisfied the ancients' observations too; nay, that something else lay hidden in it which excused a certain peculiar occasion of apparent inequality, especially in the motion of Mars (on account of the very sensible and great comparison of the Solar orb to its circuit), which could by no means be saved by the predecessors' assumptions — so that on that account, in some part, the course especially of Mars has hitherto remained utterly unexplored. Which was noticed by the ancients too, since Pliny does not hesitate to affirm that this star is unobservable. Wherefore, no longer hesitating that my neoteric invention stands rightly on every side, and suggests nothing dissonant either Mathematically or Physically, I resolved to build upon it alone the restoration of the celestial revolutions from the observations of the ancients and ours; yet I judged that its proof must be deferred to our Astronomical work, which, by divine help, I resolved to elaborate concerning the instauration of the whole art. But since the Comet of [15]77, which revolved about the Sun no less than the Planets themselves, invited me — and while I labour to save its appearances by a suitable hypothesis, I might lay open the reasoning of this [system's] hypotyposis in the world-frame by a more general judgment, until the publication of the Astronomical work — my opinion being changed, I judged it more advisable that the whole matter not be deferred. But since I had communicated to the aforesaid most noble Heinrich Rantzau, my singular friend, at his request, some copies of the second part of our work on the more recent Phenomena of the aethereal World, I understand that he transmitted the delineation of this hypothesis which is found there in chapter 8, excerpted, to you and certain other learned [men] in Germany — neglecting, however, the succinct explanation which was added to it; which, had he joined [it] at the same time, neither you nor the others would have fallen into doubts about this reasoning, so easily resolvable — which I will afterward dispatch with the least labour. But I send you this same book, from which the most noble Rantzau took this designation of the hypothesis, which treats amply of the Comet of [15]77. And it is the second of that work of which I spoke; for the first deals fully with that admirable New Star arisen by Cassiopeia in the year [15]72, where certain [things] are premised which lay the foundations for the instauration of the whole art — such as those which are proposed about the restitution of the Sun's course, accomplished for our times by sure observations [and] demonstrations, and about the exquisite certification of the fixed stars. In the same book too the observations and tenets of others about this star are recalled to the anvil of truth, and together your own, once had at Wittenberg, but with less suitable instruments. In the third tome I set forth my findings and decisions about certain other three Comets afterward observed, and confer [them] with others' tenets in the same, a diligent examination being applied in the singulars; to all which are at last subjoined certain great corollaries which can be derived from them, such as: that the heaven does not consist of real orbs and impervious substance; that no epicycles really exist; that the Earth is by no means revolved annually, as Copernicus wished; and that our ordering of the celestial hypotheses agrees with the truth of the matter on every side, and that no other is given which suffices for all requisites.

In such a way, then, through these three books of the neoteric and ascititious phenomena in the heaven, I wished to pave the way and lay the foundations for that work which will serve for restoring, from the heaven itself (the Maker of the heaven itself effecting [it]), the revolutions of the everlasting stars coeval with the world. And therefore I judged advisedly that this description of the wandering and secondary stars [comets, nova] should be sent ahead — and that not only on account of thoroughly knowing the position and motion of these ascititious stars, but much more on account of the occasions conveniently deduced thence for the re-integration of the whole Astronomical art. But the Astronomical work itself I resolved to distribute into 7 books. Of which, in the first, I will deal with the Mechanical part of this art, which will set forth the construction and use of the instruments devised by me — of which I now have more than 20 ready, incomparable not only in magnitude but also in solidity of material, variety of construction, and convenience and correspondence of use and certainty in observing. In the second, I am to treat of those [things] which come from the doctrine of triangles and numbers as aids to Astronomical matters, where certain compendia will be unlocked, greatly relieving the excessive and unwearied labours of our predecessors. These [things], I say, serving the rest, I judged it more expeditious to propose in the two prior books, lest afterward, in the remaining work — when I deal with limiting and saving the appearances themselves — they make trouble for me, which I see befell our other predecessors. The third book will contain the exquisite verification of the eighth sphere and the fixed stars. The fourth will supply the precise reckonings of the Sun and Moon, [their] settled circuits directed to the resting Earth. The fifth will unravel the appearances of the two Planets which circle the Sun but not at the same time the Earth — namely Venus and Mercury. The sixth will save the irregularities, falling under our sight, of the remaining three — namely Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn — which, circling the Sun, include the Earth and the whole elementary World. The seventh will scrutinize the latitudes of these five Planets, in which too no less than in the longitudes themselves much error still lurks — so much so that very often they are found sensibly northern in the heaven when in the numbers of the tables they are proposed southern, and the contrary. But the publication of this work still demands a procrastination of some years; for it can scarcely be completed within a quinquennium or sexennium — and that not so much on account of the immense and long labour which is required in constructing anew the tables of the mean motions, and especially of the prosthaphaereses [προσθαφαιρέσεων], in the single Planets, as because I still want more acronychal observations in the more remote Planets which circle the Sun, especially in the more distant places of the eccentric (to speak with the ancients, for our hypotheses remove not only epicycles, but also eccentrics and equants) — and that chiefly in Saturn and Jupiter, but most of all in Saturn itself, on account of the slowness of its motion — so that, a collation being made in all these with the ancients', but especially Ptolemy's, notations, it may be permitted to investigate, demonstrate, and recall under the knowledge of numbers, by exquisite search, the proportions of the circles and the congruence of the motions. Meanwhile, the divine Deity favouring, I will send ahead successively those [things] which shall seem to make for this whole business, and which, at the first occasion, since they do not require so long a delay, can be completed — such as those which will be contained in the 4 or 5 prior books. And I will take care that in this whole work everything correspond exactly to the celestial appearances, both of the ancients' age and ours (and on that account also of those to come) — which indeed I have no doubt you long since perceived has hitherto been accomplished by no one who has tried to drain so excellent a labour. For although that incomparable man Copernicus laboured to emend many [things] less rightly brought forward by the ancients, yet, on account of the less exact conformation of [his] instruments (as you too rightly suspect) and the not-wholly-infallible use [of them], he obtained void observations. Talent certainly and science for completing this knowledge and restitution were not lacking to him (as [not] to the other [Ptolemy]) — only the Mechanical means which he used did not stand rightly, and the places of the fixed [stars] had not first (as was needful) been rectified. I have his Parallactic instrument [triquetrum], sent me as a gift by a certain Canon of Ermland, which consists of fir-wood, and, by the divisions marked on it with ink, shows well enough that he did not have in these [things] the requisite diligence. But that you so beautifully support my assertion about the most fluid and most subtle matter of the heaven, and confirm it moreover by the testimony of the sacred writings, is most welcome to me; for I have long greatly esteemed your most weighty judgment about [these] matters and [your] high science in very many [things], and shall do [so]. Further, that passage which you cite from Isaiah 40:22 — Castellio (with whose version I am quite delighted) rendered [it] into Latin a little otherwise. He interpreted it thus: "He stretches out the heavens like a membrane, and expands [them] like a tent to dwell in." But the old exposition seems more probable, to which others too more closely subscribe. Castellio, studying elegance, seems sometimes to have deflected from the genuine interpretation of the words. The other passage, Job 37:18, by which the contrary seems at first sight to be asserted (because it says the heavens are hardened like steel [chalybs]), you rightly reconcile with the former, when you erudite[ly] and fittingly expound that there is regarded the solidity and firmness and perpetual constancy of the nature and revolution of the heaven, and of the bodies contained in it, rather than the framing of the matter. The aforesaid Castellio, by [his] inversion of this passage, repugns our opinion less than the ancient interpreter, when he renders it thus into Latin: "Did you draw out the aether with him so firm that it seems to be concreted?" But these [things] I leave to be discussed by those excellently skilled in the Hebrew tongue. A certain Spaniard, Francisco Valles [Vallesius] by name — a man, as appears from his writings, especially excelling in varied erudition — in the book which he entitled On Sacred Philosophy [De sacra philosophia], and which was published this year at Lyon, in chapter 51, from this very passage of Job (cited according to the old interpretation, which you too used), tries to confirm the opinion received from the ancient Philosophers Pythagoras, Parmenides, Plato, and Aristotle — namely, that the heaven consists of a certain most firm and most solid substance, and that the orbs themselves really inhere in it, harder and more solid than any adamant, so that they cannot be utterly divided and torn apart. But he understood this passage (as some others too) too literally, nor considered those [things] which are said to the contrary part by Isaiah and elsewhere. But what he disputes Philosophically against the heaven's most fluid and everywhere-pervious substance — by reason of rarity and density and the vacuum, and that the Stars cannot perpetually move regularly of themselves — is less valid, as we shall show elsewhere; and that will easily appear to you, when you have thoroughly inspected his argumentations brought forward there. But at last he even slips into the absurdity of not fearing that the framing of the whole aether consists of the four elements (which, however, to render the matter more plausible, he calls celestial). Nor [is it any] wonder, since the same author, in the first chapter of the same book — by those [things] which are said at the beginning of Genesis 2: "The heavens and the earth were perfected, and all their adornment, and God completed his work on the sixth day," and besides from this saying of the third chapter of Ecclesiasticus: "I have learned that all the works of God endure forever" — wrongly and against all experience builds [the claim] that the star which in the year 1572, by the seat of Cassiopeia (which he inconsiderately places back at her knees), arose first of all and at last disappeared, was by no means new; as neither [was] that which was noticed by Hipparchus (Pliny reporting) — about which indeed, since we do not have Hipparchus's own observations, I wish to determine nothing certain. But how far, in that new [star] by Cassiopeia beheld in the said year and the following, [he] has declined from the mark of truth, will become most clear from our first book of the recent Phenomena of the heaven. And how — without all sense and reason, I ask — does he establish that that star was always there from the world's beginning and still remains, but so small that it cannot be seen except faintly, yet was then seen to grow up to the first magnitude on account of some change of the medium, which occurred in the very orbs of the heaven (not everywhere equally thick), so that this star fell upon some part of the nearest heaven thicker than the rest, through which the condensed light exhibited for a while the appearance of a larger star, and thereafter, this [thick part] departing, [it was] rendered a smaller star? O speculation! — thus one absurdity begets another; for the heaven consists of no compacted orbs really existing there, much less that they be in some places of rarer, in some of denser substance, as certain effectors of homocentrics too dreamed. But let us grant that this very [thing] could be conceded — how then would it come about that the same star of Cassiopeia was not more often thus illuminated beyond the usual? and while that denser little part of the heaven passed across this [star], why did it not increase others nearby in the same way by a miraculous light? or why is the same not more often beheld in the remaining fixed stars? Why is there so great an abundance of them, and [such] a distribution throughout the whole heaven, that necessarily those parts of the orbs which are feigned denser must sometime slip past, by the continuation of their motion, under some of those [stars], and render them similarly far larger than usual? Which, however, for so many centuries now (if it be so) was not noticed in [the case of] Hipparchus alone, and in our age in that wonderful phenomenon by Cassiopeia. But these and many other [things] which that book — otherwise quite ingeniously written and crammed with manifold knowledge of various matters — proposes from the sacred oracles in too literal a sense, let us now dismiss, since they make us digress against the present business — especially since it is [my] intention to inquire elsewhere about most of [the things] contained in it and making chiefly for the Astronomical matter. Nothing, therefore, will stand in the way, either from the sacred Bible or from the decrees of the Philosophers, but that we surely establish the matter of the heaven [to be] most fluid and thinner and subtler than all air. For that Aristotle, although he labours greatly to defend the contrary parts, nonetheless contradicts himself, when he asserts that minds preside over the motion of the Stars, is most rightly inferred by you; for that is far other than to be revolved by the circumgyration of certain orbs to which [the stars] would be affixed. I see that you have moderately well perceived, from the bare delineation alone, the [outline] of my hypothesis — for in it, as to the five Planets revolved about the Sun as centre, and both Luminaries and the eighth sphere regarding the Earth itself resting in the middle of the universe, you have rightly set forth everything. But as to your doubt — why the picture disjoins the eighth sphere at the lowest part from the other stars by so great a capacity [gap] — the resolution is easy. For while all five Planets lead [their] gyres about the Sun, their leader and king, the centres of the imaginary orbs which they describe about it must be revolved annually together with it; and hence it comes about that always that part of the orbs which is opposed to the Sun, the Earth intervening, is more remote from the eighth sphere than the other; and although then a greater empty space appears there, yet, the Sun going around and being carried to the other part, the same is again filled, and that vacuity which is supposed is then on the other part; and so it comes about all around through the whole course below the eighth orb. For otherwise there would be no place, [given] that by the approach and recession of the Planets to the Earth, and their annual circumgyration about the Sun through whole orbs … [lacuna]. A certain other Mathematician, unknown to me, also had another doubt in this ordering of our hypothesis — about which he too wrote to the most noble Lord Heinrich Rantzau, and indicated [that] the hypotheses [had been] communicated to him two years ago through a certain fugitive servant of mine. He hesitates in this manner: if Mars and the Sun should sometime come into the same place where their orbs intersect each other, so that Mars wholly lacks latitude, how do they not impede each other, since two bodies cannot be in the same place? But this doubt introduces no great scruple, since what he fears can never happen; for it appears that he did not sufficiently perceive the inductions of our hypothesis. For how could it come about that, since Mars's circuit always regards the Sun as centre no less than the other Planets, it should ever meet it? For that is no more given than that the circumference of some circle be united with its centre and fall back into the same. He did not notice that now, when the Sun shall have come to that place where Mars's orb crosses its [the Sun's] course, the Martian course does not then cut it imaginarily at the same place, but everywhere stands as far from the Sun as before — so much so that Mars never becomes nearer to the Sun than its circuit about it requires, except in so far as those more particular motions in the two little circles [circelli] of which I spoke add or take away a little; which, however, compared to that greater and more general circumvolution, has not much moment. But these are better understood from a certain mechanical composition [model], such that the Sun and Moon are revolved in their orbs about the Earth, but the remaining Planets in their circuits about the Sun. For then, the Sun being carried around, it will appear how the orbs of the five Planets, going around, fill the whole space below the eighth sphere every year, and by what reckoning their retrogradations and stations are resolved through this concomitance of the centre of their orb with the Sun; and that the epicycles of the ancients come about per accidens — namely, from the annual motion of the centre of the orbs; and that there is no need for the Earth to move, and nonetheless [that] Mars is made nearer [to the Earth], in its anxious [eager] evening rising, than the Sun itself — which diligent experience has taught us, as I indicated before. The remaining more particular differences of apparent inequality, which the ancients excused by eccentrics and equants, [and] Copernicus by an eccentric with a small epicycle, we, in this hypothesis, will aptly defend by two little circles on the circumference of the Planets' orbs, which the star itself designates by a fixed law — except that in Venus and especially Mercury it is necessary besides to assume some particular gyrations. And so all the circuits of the Planets will turn out concentric, no otherwise than as Copernicus quite ingeniously and rightly speculated that the Moon's revolutions about the Earth come about in a concentric orb with two epicycles. For the circuits, both universal and particular, of the three superior Planets do not much differ from this, except that the centre of their orbs accompanies the Sun annually — as I will demonstrate more fully and exquisitely in that work of which I spoke, of the Astronomical instauration, by [its] very foundations and by sure observations both of the ancients and my own (the divine Deity favouring); and I will construct anew the tables both of the equal motions and also of the prosthaphaereses, founded for this hypothesis on the exquisite neoteric observations collated with the ancients'. And I will effect that the numbers correspond exactly to the ancient observations — but especially the Ptolemaic — and to the more recent too; which hitherto, now for so many centuries, has been wanting, to the great loss of this art as to [its] due precision. I marvel that that most learned [man], and most exercised in these studies and splendidly deserving of them, Reinhold, asserts in the preface of his Prutenic Tables that in the mean motions of the Sun some limitation is wanting, but that the tables of prosthaphaereses serve usefully for the whole duration of the world — since the one depends largely on the other, and, the proportions of the circles and the eccentricities being changed, it is necessary that the prosthaphaereses too stand otherwise. But sometimes even great Homer nods. But it is possible, by the benefit of certain compendia in triangles, from the given mean motions and the whole canon of triangles, to investigate the apparent positions of the Planets as quickly — if not more quickly — than by the tables of equations, as we shall open elsewhere. That you so greatly desire some time to see this work of ours — so much so that even for that reason you wish life divinely prolonged to you, and besides pray, by a pious and benevolent vow, the aid of the divine blessing for our endeavours — is [a thing] for which I give you the greatest thanks for this singular favour toward me and my studies, and [your] sincere judgment; and I will take care that, as far as in me lies, the elaboration of this volume be completed with the greatest maturation that can ever be [made]. May God the best and greatest meanwhile bestow on you firm vigour of mind and body, and preserve [your] life, [so that], using it as long as possible and faring well, you may be able to have leisure not only for these but for other labours of Philosophy too, and to promote them, and to bring forward your most skilful judgment about them. That you have for so many years lain hidden, detained in captivity (of which you make mention too in this letter), with great loss to good letters, I, with many other men favourable to you and to erudite studies, have always greatly grieved.

But since we have fallen upon mention of that custody, I will report those [things] which I find noted, in a certain Genethliacal book of mine, by your nativity-figure [Geneseos schema], by me, when at Leipzig about more or less twenty-four years ago I was giving my work to letters. For then, about boyhood, I was somewhat curiously addicted to Astrology and especially to Genethliacal predictions — which, however, afterward, my age and judgment increasing (especially because I saw that the very motions of the stars did not stand rightly in the tables), I set aside, and betook myself wholly, with great effort, to the observation and emendation of the motions. But I find there written thus of old: "Mercury [☿] in Capricorn [♑], the sign of Saturn [♄], and in the third house, oriental and free from the rays of the Sun, the Moon being in Gemini [♊], a Mercurial sign, and especially in the ninth house, over which Mercury too presides — designates a keen and profound talent, patient of labour, and an arduous [lofty] judgment." Afterward was subjoined: "But the Moon [☾] near the cusp of the ninth, afflicted by the square [□] of Saturn and the [imminent] trine [△] of Mars [♂], insinuates some great evil — and that by reason of religion (because of the ninth house), of talent (because of Gemini, a Mercurial sign), of some illustrious woman (because of the Moon itself, which is also joined to brighter but too-violent fixed [stars])." Then about the captivity I had written these words: "Mars, lord of the seventh place, set in the twelfth and in its detriment, and moreover injured by the antiscion-part of Saturn, signifies prison or long exile; and because Mars afflicts Mercury by a square from the twelfth, it denotes that harm by occasion of talent and studies; but when the Moon by the path of direction shall have reached the sextile [⚹] of Mars at 14° of Leo [♌] in the Midheaven [M.C.], he will be freed by death or by some Martial man, and restored to wholeness — and that about the 60th year of age." I had subjoined besides: "The Tail of the Dragon in the Midheaven, according to the tenets of the Arabs, denotes dejection from honours and dignities, especially since it is in a Solar sign [Leo], this being depressed, and no benefic stars are in the angles, but both [are] cadent." These [things], I say, I had then noted beneath the celestial figure of your nativity — which I now report not because I would wish to cast the causes of your long calamity onto the stars alone, attributing too much to these Astrological indications, but only that I might in passing signify to you what I once committed to paper about this matter, when I was more earnestly occupied with these predictions. I know how much is to be conceded to Astrological conjectures, and I neither esteem them greatly, nor too much despise [them] — in both of which most, even of the more erudite, sin. If this art be contained within its bounds, it supplies certain Physical significations not inept, nor at all frivolous, and not useless to know. Yet there are very many other concurrent causes of events, arising from the occasions of the lower world and of [the things] which are conducted tumultuously in it; but above all, God himself, the author of the universe, as the first and universal cause, acts most freely, and disposes and moderates the secondary causes, whether of the upper or the lower world, at his pleasure. But these I suggest in vain to you, who understand them more than sufficiently. Now I will return from Astrological [matters] to Astronomical.

I have ready to hand 24 years of observations of the stars, of which those taken in the latter 12 on this our island stand quite exact — after I took care that excellent and skilfully-built instruments be made here, with the greatest labour and expense, which I said above I have completed, very many [of them]; and they are elaborated with so great circumspection and diligence that, by whichever [of them] it shall please to investigate the true position of some star from the heaven, they fall back into entirely the same minute — even though they are diversely composed, and the observation be made by different and different observers — which evidently proves the perfect structure and exquisite division of the machines; so that on that account I am sufficiently certain that no fault lurks in my observations derived from the heaven by these instruments. Would that the ancients and our predecessors, especially the never-sufficiently-praised Copernicus (to say nothing now of Ptolemy himself), had applied so great diligence and precision in making Astronomical instruments and in exactly executing celestial observations! Indeed we should now be greatly relieved of the immense labour in restoring the motions of the aethereal bodies. Nonetheless the Mathematicians of our age are excusable, that they do not possess sufficiently great and apt instruments by which to scrutinize the motions of the stars, since their salaries and whole annual revenues scarcely suffice for rightly constructing and completing a single instrument. For I know that I have very many [instruments] of which the single ones far exceed some school-professor's annual stipend — even the greatest — by the cost of [their] making. So great is the illiberality of richer men, and especially of Kings and Princes, toward these sublime studies; for few now, or rather no King Alfonsos are found. And if provision had not been made for our family-estate, partly from our own goods and lands, partly through the Royal fiefs of this Kingdom, by the divine liberality, so that I could be equal to bearing these and other expenses, in vain indeed — even if everything else were at hand — should I attempt so great [things]. I have also, within this last decade, built up a certain castle on this island especially opportune for the instauration of Astronomy [Uraniborg], in whose middle and greater house certain Astronomical instruments are disposed in two little towers toward South and North, with their annexed appendages. Besides, not far outside the castle, on a certain little hill, I had another subterranean observatory [Stjerneborg] made within four years, in which 7 diverse instruments, exquisite both in magnitude and in form, are kept — so that each of them occupies its own crypt, which can be opened above, and the observation can be directed through the instrument into whatever part of the heaven it please, the other machines not standing in the way. For that place is so elevated, as is almost the whole island, that the visible horizon lies open freely with no intervening impediment; on account of which peculiar convenience too I made so many expenses and drained so great labours on this island rather than elsewhere. But of that castle here built I send you here a delineation annexed, as to those [parts] which lie open above the Earth and are seen externally, so that in some way, from its inspection, you may be able to recognize that I conduct the matter seriously, as far as restoring Astronomy to honour and splendour, and in that matter spare no expense or labour. The picture of those crypts, which is exhibited with difficulty on a flat [surface], I have not even had drawn. But all these, together with the construction and use of the instruments, I have resolved to set before the eyes and sufficiently declare, with suitable delineations, in the first tome of the work on restoring Astronomy, which will contain (as I said) its Mechanical part. Meanwhile I communicate the Design [Idea] of certain instruments which have hitherto been depicted and engraved. I have also annexed some copies of certain letters given to me by the most illustrious Prince Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse (most skilled in Astronomical matters among the Princes of Europe in this age — of whom you too made mention in this letter to Lord Rantzau) and by his Highness's most erudite Mathematician, Christoph Rothmann, together with our reply to each — in which very many [things] are referred making not a little for the whole Astronomical matter, and for instituting the observations themselves more rightly, and so for the re-integration of the art itself. And about this very question — concerning the motion of the heaven, [and] the nature and quantity of the refractions which appear in the position of the stars, and other occasions — it is copiously discussed between Rothmann and me. All which I wished to grant you to read and judge, asking lovingly that, when you have inspected and more diligently unrolled them, you would be willing to return [them] to me, lest they ever come into others' hands; and while you have them with you, that you communicate [them] to no one else — I ask you again and again. But above all I much desire from you that you would not be loath to write me, freely and amply, before winter, your judgment both about those [things] which are in that second book of the aethereal Phenomena [Phaenomeνων] now sent at the same time, and also [those] which are contained in these annexed letters. If anything has been erred in the transcription of those letters by my amanuenses, it will be yours to emend it by your skill; I had not leisure to read everything over. The most noble Rantzau wrote to me already before, and tries to persuade [me], by not-incongruous reasons, that I should subjoin, at the end of our work on the more recent Phenomena of the aethereal World, both these letters of the most illustrious Prince Wilhelm and those of his Mathematician and other most erudite men too, written to me about this whole Astronomical business, together with my reply to each — because they greatly illustrate the matter there treated and conduce greatly to the amplification of the whole art. In which matter I judged that his just and benevolent request must not be refused, relying also on your and other erudite [men]'s judgment and counsel. I have added besides to this letter the restitution of the Sun's course, deduced from my observations and demonstrations, for the next quinquennium, so that you may meanwhile obtain something of my emendations in the course of the celestial bodies, until time grant [me] to communicate more. The Sun's Apogee is here established near the end of the 6th degree of Cancer [♋], 3 degrees earlier than the Copernican; and the eccentricity is presupposed [at] 2° 9⅓′, of which the semidiameter of the eccentric is 60 — which much exceeds his [Copernicus's] finding. But why that great Copernicus deflected so greatly from the mark even in the retention of the Sun's course, I will show sufficiently elsewhere. But there is no [reason] for you to doubt that this our calculus differs not in the least, [that] which can fall under the senses, from the track which the aethereal Sun designates — as not only our observations of many years, but also the Landgravian [ones], especially after the renewed rectification of the instruments, attest. But how much it differs from the Alphonsine and Copernican computation, you yourself, a collation being made, will easily perceive — so [much so that] not even the Sun's motion, although it furnishes the norm for all the rest, has hitherto been sufficiently unravelled, much less [that] no fault lurks in the remaining Planets. But I see that I transgress the bounds of a letter, while I converse with you too avidly about these; it will therefore be yours to take in good part this prolixity, together with the book and the annexed papers. When the first and last tomes of the work which I now have in hand, on the more recent Phenomena of the aether, shall have been printed by our types, I will render you a sharer of them too. But that the first did not come forth earlier, the cause is the lack of the Gallican privilege, which I was daily expecting, to be added to the Imperial [one] obtained before. But since I have now received that Gallican [one], and shall shortly have the English too — the magnificent and most erudite man Lord Daniel Rogers, who was lately here with me, so procuring [it] with the most serene Queen [Elizabeth I] — nothing further will now stand in the way but that the first book be shortly brought to light. Meanwhile enjoy these, and fare as happily and as long as possible, most erudite Peucer, and most excellently deserving of the Republic of Letters; and do not cease to love me, most loving of you, in return (which you do), and to revisit [me] by letter before the threatening winter. But it will be most convenient for you to send them to the oft-said noble Lord Heinrich Rantzau in Holstein, who will not grudgingly procure that they be conveyed to me. Again, and often and much, farewell, man most worthy of long life and health.

Given at Uraniborg, the 13th day of September, in the Year 1588. These letters, once read, are to be sent at once to Lord John Dee, whom, God willing, we shall meet tomorrow morning before [his] departure.

Apparatus

  • Edition of record. Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia vol. VII (Epistolae); the letter is also preserved in P. J. Resen's Inscriptiones Hafnienses. MS witnesses: A = Mikulov (Nikolsburg), II 72 (copy); B = Paris, BN, Français 13040, fol. 162 (copy). Cross-check: page-collated at vol. VII pp. 127–141; every crux confirmed (anchors in the Editorial note), and additionally cross-corroborated by the Magini letter of 1 Dec 1590, the undated Peucer letter of 1590, the Landgrave letter of 16 Aug 1588, and the Hájek letter of 3 Aug 1590.
  • Sender / recipient. Tycho (Uraniborg) → Caspar Peucer (1525–1602), Melanchthon's son-in-law, Wittenberg polymath, imprisoned 1574–86 for Crypto-Calvinism (the "captivity" named). The friendship is opened via Heinrich Rantzau, who had forwarded Peucer an excerpt of Tycho's system-diagram (De recentioribus phaenomenis, ch. 8) without its explanation — hence Peucer's "doubts," which this letter resolves. (The later, undated Peucer letter is brahe-t_15900000001.)
  • The autobiographical genesis of the Tychonic system (the historic core). Tycho narrates: a Leipzig boyhood (age 17, c. 1563) at the cross-staff, finding neither Copernican nor Alphonsine tables precise → years weighing both systems → the ancients fault (circular regularity about an alien centre; superfluous epicycles; the arbitrary Sun-binding of the planets) and the Copernican fault (the triple Earth-motion absurdity + Scripture) → the decisive test by Mars: if Mars at opposition is nearer Earth than the Sun, Copernicus wins. The 1582 opposition in Cancer (Mars high in northern latitude) gave Mars a greater parallax than the Sun (with faster retrogression matching Copernican numbers); confirmed in Venus (incl. the rare 24 Feb 1587 morning-and-evening sighting). This drove him toward Copernicus — but the Earth-motion absurdity + Scripture barred it. Then, "almost unexpectedly," the geo-heliocentric arrangement: the Sun the centre of the five planets, itself carried annually round a resting Earth, the Earth the centre of Sun, Moon, and 8th sphere — dissolving both systems' absurdities. The remaining scruple (the Mars-orb intersecting the Sun's) fell once the comets abolished the solid orbs (comets above the Moon, carried by no real orb; and two comets moving opposite the Sun disproved the Earth's annual motion). He found a peculiar Mars inequality (Pliny: Mars "unobservable") explicable only on his system.
  • The publication architecture. The three "ascititious-phenomena" books (1 = the 1572 nova + foundations; 2 = the 1577 comet, carrying the system-diagram in ch. 8; 3 = three later comets) pave the way for the great 7-book Astronomical work: (1) Mechanics/instruments (20+); (2) triangles & numbers (prosthaphaeretic compendia); (3) the 8th sphere / fixed stars; (4) Sun & Moon (round the resting Earth); (5) Venus & Mercury (round the Sun, not the Earth); (6) Mars, Jupiter, Saturn (round the Sun, enclosing Earth + elements); (7) the latitudes. Needs 5–6 years (esp. more acronychal Saturn/Jupiter observations; his hypotheses "remove epicycles, eccentrics, and equants"). Copernicus's instruments were inexact — Tycho owns Copernicus's fir-wood triquetrum (gift of an Ermland canon), its ink-marked divisions betraying his lack of diligence. New tables of mean motions + prosthaphaereses to fit Ptolemaic and recent observations; the dig at Reinhold (Prutenic Tables preface) — "even great Homer nods."
  • The fluid-heavens exegesis (overlaps the undated 1590 Peucer letter). Peucer's Scripture confirmation welcomed: Isaiah 40:22 (Castellio's "membrane/tent" vs the old version), Job 37:18 (heavens "hard as steel" = firmness of the heaven's nature/revolution, not its matter). Francisco Valles (De sacra philosophia, Lyon 1588, ch. 51) is refuted at length for defending solid adamantine orbs (from Job, with Pythagoras/Parmenides/Plato/Aristotle) and for claiming (ch. 1, from Gen 2:1–2 + Ecclesiasticus 3) that the 1572 nova was not new but an old faint star magnified by a "denser orb-region" — Tycho's counter: no solid orbs exist; why then not other stars similarly lit, why only Hipparchus's and his own nova? Aristotle self-contradicts (minds rule the stars' motion ≠ solid orbs).
  • The system-diagram clarifications. Why the diagram leaves a gap below the 8th sphere: the planets' orb-centres revolve annually with the Sun, so the anti-solar part is farther — the void fills as the Sun moves. The "unknown mathematician's" objection (Mars and Sun colliding where their orbs cross) — refuted: Mars always keeps the Sun as centre (a circumference never meets its centre); the Sun reaching the crossing-point does not cut Mars's path there but stays equidistant; best seen on a mechanical model. Particular inequalities handled by two small circles on each planet's orb (Venus/Mercury need extra gyrations); all circuits become concentric (cf. Copernicus's Moon-theory). Note the Ursus allusion: the hypotheses were "communicated two years ago through a certain fugitive servant of mine" — Tycho's coded reference to the theft underlying the Ursus priority war (cf. the Craig letter of 14 Nov 1591 and the Herwart letter of 16 Nov 1599).
  • Peucer's horoscope (a rare document). From notes Tycho made at Leipzig c. 24 years earlier (c. 1564) under Peucer's nativity-figure: Mercury in Capricorn, 3rd house, free of the Sun → keen profound talent; Moon in Gemini, 9th house, afflicted by Saturn's square and Mars's trine → "some great evil" via religion / talent / "an illustrious woman"; Mars (lord of the 7th) in the 12th, in detriment, + Saturn's antiscion → prison or long exile, harm via studies; freed "about the 60th year" by death or a Martial man when the Moon's direction reaches Mars's sextile at 14° Leo in the MC; the Dragon's Tail in the MC → dejection from honours. (Peucer, b. 1525, was freed in 1586, ~age 60 — a striking match.) Tycho's measured caveat: astrology "within its bounds" gives "physical significations not inept," but "many concurrent causes" operate, and "above all, God, the first and universal cause, acts most freely."
  • Uraniborg & Stjerneborg (production-history). 24 years of observations (the last 12 on Hven exact to the minute, instruments cross-agreeing). The illiberality of princes ("no King Alfonsos now"); Tycho's self-funding (estates + royal fiefs). Uraniborg ("a castle … especially opportune for the instauration of Astronomy," instruments in two south/north towers) and the subterranean Stjerneborg ("another observatory on a little hill … 7 instruments each in its own crypt, opened above") built "within this last decade" / "within four years"; he encloses a delineation of the castle exterior (the crypts too hard to draw flat) and instrument Designs — all destined for the Mechanics tome. He sends the Landgrave Wilhelm + Rothmann letters (the Epistolae astronomicae core, incl. the heaven-motion/refraction debate) for Peucer's private judgment, and agrees to Rantzau's proposal to append them to De recentioribus phaenomenis.
  • The solar restitution & privileges. Encloses the 5-year solar course: Apogee near 6° Cancer (3° before Copernican), eccentricity 2° 9⅓′ (ecc-radius 60) — exceeding Copernicus's value, matching Tycho's and the Landgravian observations. The Progymnasmata Tome I was delayed for want of the Gallican privilege (to join the Imperial one); now obtained, with the English one coming via Daniel Rogers (procured from Queen Elizabeth I), so the first book will soon appear.
  • Persons & places. Caspar Peucer; Heinrich Rantzau (Royal Vicar in Holstein, the intermediary/postal channel); Copernicus, Ptolemy, "Alphonsus," Pliny, Aristotle, Reinhold; Sebastian Castellio; Francisco Valles (Vallesius, the Spanish physician-philosopher); Pythagoras, Parmenides, Plato; Hipparchus; Landgrave Wilhelm IV & Christoph Rothmann; Daniel Rogers (English diplomat); Homer (proverbial); the Ermland canon (donor of Copernicus's triquetrum); the unnamed "fugitive servant" (the Ursus theft) and the unnamed "other mathematician." Places: Wittenberg, Leipzig, Uraniborg/Hven, Stjerneborg, Lyon, Holstein, Ermland (Varmia).

  • Addressee of the postscript: John Dee. The closing instruction directs that the letters just read be forwarded at once to Dr John Dee, whom the writer expects, God willing, to meet the next morning before [Dee's] departure.
  • Implication for itinerary. Dated at Uraniborg 13 September 1588, the postscript places Dee in the vicinity of Hven at that moment, awaiting a meeting the following morning before leaving. This is corroborated by Wilhelm Norlind's Tycho Brahe (Lund, 1970), which cites this very Peucer letter to conclude Dee 'must have been on Hven in Sept. 1588' (Han torde ha varit på Hven i sept. 1588 — Tycho til Peucer sept. 1588), and by a later autograph note of Dee's own, dated Kassel 26 March 1589, recording his reception there of Christoph Rothmann — consistent with a continental itinerary passing near Denmark that autumn.
  • The plural 'literae'. In the postscript, 'literae' is plural — 'these letters', not 'this letter'; the translation preserves the plural.

(Dominum) is corrected to "Lord", matching the translation's own consistent rendering of the same abbreviation for D. Henricus Ranzovius and D. Daniele Rogersio earlier in the same letter. The identification of the addressee as John Dee is unaffected and remains correct.
