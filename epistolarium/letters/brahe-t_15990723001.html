The original

NOBILISSIMO DOCTISSIMOQUE VIRO DOMINO TYCHONI BRAHE, DOMINO A KNUDSTRUP, MATHEMATICORUM OMNIUM FACILE PRINCIPI, DOMINO ET AMICO SUO SINGULARI OBSERVANTIA COLENDO.

CUM hac ipsa hora, Vir nobilissime et Mathematicorum omnium facile Princeps, ex urbe Imperatoris literas acceperim, quibus certior factus sum te eo venisse a Caesarea Maiestate vocatum, et plane confidam Maiestatem suam pro singulari favore, quo Matheseos artem prosequitur, ut inde aliorsum te conferas, aegre laturam et vix atque adeo ne vix quidem esse passuram, omnino decrevi his literis de adventu isto tuo tibi et mihi gratulari. Quod si aliqua in re studia tua pro exilibus viribus meis iuvare possem, haberes me paratissimum. Mallem equidem hoc saltem primo alloquio, animi mei propensam in te voluntatem aliqua ratione potius declarare, quam quidquam a te petere, sed cum inpraesentiarum nihil istiusmodi occurrat, hoc tibi significandum duxi. Ex traditione antiquissimi Authoris Poëtae Romani, qui eo ipso tempore vixit et floruit, accepi anno ante CHRISTUM quarto inchoante, summi miraculi instar habitum fuisse passim ab omnibus, quod stella Veneris seu Lucifer, cum pro ratione motus sui, ipsissimo illo tempore, ante Solis ortum lucere debuisset, nuspiam in coelo visa fuerit, atque non apparente Lucifero dies illuxerit. Rationem esse ego conijcio, quod iam dicta Veneris stella tum temporis a stella Mercurij Eclipsin fuerit passa, nec enim video, quanam alia de caussa haec res prodigij loco posteritati tradita fuisset, Romae praesertim, ubi tum temporis quales quales saltem astronomi non defuerunt. Adhibui itaque calculum Prutenicum, et reperi, quod currente anno ante CHRISTUM quarto, die septima Ianuarij circa Solis ortum stella Mercurij subierit stellam Veneris, sed ex eodem calculo reperi quoque simul unâ, quod eadem Mercurij stella 2° 44' latitudine tum Borealior Venere fuerit. Quare despondissem animum nisi ex Epistolis tuis Astronomicis, et quae de Cometis scripsisti, animadvertissem, te latitudines praesertim ♀ et ☿ longe aliter observasse, quam calculus exhibeat. Rem itaque hanc omnino ad te deferendam esse duxi, cuius de ea iudicium audire percuperem, ut quod merito cuiusvis alterius sit praeferendum. Ad hanc meam petitionem accedit etiam alia. Soleo identidem Eclipses luminarium, quae ante CHRISTUM retro vel circa tempora CHRISTI, iuxta traditiones Authorum, contigerunt, ex tabulis Prutenicis supputare, et ea quoque, quae PTOLEMÆUS in magna Syntaxi hac de re tradit, conferre (quae quidem utplurimum calculo Prutenico consentire videntur). Attamen calculus iste non videtur satis exacte Eclipses exhibere. Intellexi quidem ex praefatis Epistolis tuis Astronomicis te ex nova restauratione cursus ☉ et ☾ Eclipses luminarium huius nostri seculi, longe aliter et exactius deprehendere, quam ullae tabulae hactenus exhibuerint. Utrum vero accuratissimae istae tuae observationes recentes eo rem deducant, ut etiam tempus et quantitatem illarum Eclipsium, quae circa tempora CHRISTI et ante illam aetatem retro contigerunt, exactius definire queas, id inquam, spero quidem, nescio tamen et non sine ratione addubitare mihi videor, cum veteres utique observationes desideres. Ideoque etiam atque etiam te rogo, ut hac quoque in re iudicium et prudentiam tuam mihi impertiri non dedigneris; summe enim desiderarem defectus luminarium vetustissimos quosque calculo (quoad eius fieri potest) praecisius assequi, et quanam ratione id fieri queat, rescire. Quodsi ullo genere officiorum studiorumque meorum hoc beneficium tuum in me collatum exaequare potero, semper ero promptissimus.

Vale feliciter, et has literas meas boni consule.
Datae Monachij 23. Iulij Anno 1599.
Dominationis Tuae Nobilissimae studiosissimus
IOAN. GEORGIUS HERUART ab Hohenburg,
Serenissimo utriusque Bavariae Duci a consilijs intimis, Praeses Schuabae et Statuum provincialium Cancellarius, manu propria.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the most noble and most learned man, Lord Tycho Brahe, Lord of Knudstrup, easily the Prince of all Mathematicians, his Lord and friend to be cultivated with singular respect.

Since at this very hour, most noble Man and easily Prince of all Mathematicians, I have received a letter from the Emperor's city, by which I am informed that you have come thither, summoned by the Imperial Majesty — and I fully trust that His Majesty, for the singular favour with which he pursues the art of Mathematics, will be loath that you betake yourself elsewhere thence, and will scarcely (nay, not even scarcely) suffer it — I have altogether resolved by this letter to congratulate you and myself on that coming of yours. And if in any matter I could aid your studies, according to my slender powers, you would have me most ready. I should indeed prefer, in this first address at least, to declare in some way my mind's inclined goodwill toward you, rather than to ask anything of you; but since at present nothing of that kind occurs [to me], I thought this should be signified to you. From the tradition of a most ancient Author, a Roman Poet who lived and flourished at that very time, I have learned that, at the beginning of the fourth year before Christ, it was held everywhere by all as a supreme miracle that the star of Venus, or Lucifer [the Morning Star] — when, by the reckoning of its motion, it ought at that very time to have shone before the Sun's rising — was nowhere seen in the sky, and, Lucifer not appearing, the day dawned. I conjecture the reason to be that the said star of Venus at that time suffered an Eclipse by the star of Mercury — for I see no other cause why this thing would have been handed down to posterity in place of a prodigy, especially at Rome, where at that time astronomers of some sort at least were not lacking. I therefore applied the Prutenic calculation, and found that, in the course of the fourth year before Christ, on the 7th of January about sunrise, Mercury passed beneath Venus — but from the same calculation I also found, at the same time, that Mercury was then more northern than Venus by 2° 44′ of latitude. Wherefore I should have despaired, had I not noticed, from your Astronomical Letters and what you have written about the Comets, that you have observed the latitudes especially of Venus and Mercury far otherwise than the calculation exhibits. I have therefore thought this matter should by all means be referred to you, whose judgement about it I greatly desire to hear, as one to be deservedly preferred to anyone else's.

To this request of mine another is also added. I am wont, again and again, to compute from the Prutenic tables the eclipses of the luminaries that occurred before Christ, back [in time], or around the times of Christ, according to the traditions of Authors; and to compare also what Ptolemy hands down on this matter in the Great Syntaxis [Almagest] (which indeed for the most part seem to agree with the Prutenic calculation). Yet that calculation does not seem to exhibit the eclipses exactly enough. I have understood from your aforesaid Astronomical Letters that you, from the new restoration of the course of the Sun and Moon, detect the eclipses of the luminaries of this our age far otherwise and more exactly than any tables have hitherto exhibited. But whether those most accurate recent observations of yours bring the matter to this — that you can also more exactly define the time and magnitude of those eclipses which occurred around the times of Christ, and before that age back [in time] — that, I say, I indeed hope, yet I know not, and not without reason I seem to myself to doubt, since you must in any case lack ancient observations. And therefore I ask you again and again that in this matter too you would not disdain to impart to me your judgement and prudence; for I should most greatly desire to attain more precisely by calculation each of the most ancient eclipses of the luminaries (so far as it can be done), and to learn by what method this can be done. And if by any kind of services and studies of mine I shall be able to match this benefit conferred by you upon me, I shall always be most ready.

Fare happily, and take this letter of mine in good part.
Given at Munich, 23 July, the year 1599.
Most devoted to Your Most Noble Lordship,
Johann Georg Herwart von Hohenburg,
privy councillor to the Most Serene Duke of both Bavarias, President at [Burghausen in] Swabia and Chancellor of the provincial Estates, with my own hand.

Apparatus

- Sender — and the opening of the Tycho–Herwart correspondence. Johann Georg Herwart von Hohenburg (1553–1622), the Bavarian chancellor and polymath (his titles given in the subscription: privy councillor to the Duke of Bavaria, Präsident at Burghausen (Schwaben), and Kanzler of the provincial Estates). This 23 July 1599 letter is the first of the long Tycho–Herwart correspondence on ancient eclipses and chronology (the other Herwart letters in the edition are all 1600: brahe-t_16000403001, brahe-t_16000504003, etc.). Survives in two witnesses — Herwart's own Munich draft (Kladde) and a Copenhagen copy in Resen's Inscriptiones Hafnienses (1668).
- Significance — the two requests (chronology & ancient eclipses; the edition's convention).
1. The "invisible Morning Star" of 4 BC. Herwart reports, from "a most ancient Roman Poet who lived at that very time," that at the start of the fourth year before Christ the Morning Star (Venus/Lucifer), which by computation should have shone before sunrise, was nowhere seen — held as "a supreme miracle." His hypothesis: Venus was occulted ("eclipsed") by Mercury. By the Prutenic tables he finds Mercury "passed beneath Venus" on 7 January (4 BC) at sunrise, but with Mercury 2° 44′ more northern in latitude — so no true occultation by the tables; he appeals to Tycho's Astronomical Letters and cometary work, which show Venus/Mercury latitudes differing "far otherwise than the calculation," and asks Tycho's verdict. (The Roman poet is unnamed; left unidentified per the no-fabrication rule.)
2. Dating ancient eclipses. Herwart routinely computes from the Prutenic tables the eclipses "around the times of Christ, and before," comparing Ptolemy's Almagest — but finds the calculation imprecise. He asks whether Tycho's new restoration of the Sun's and Moon's courses can fix the time and magnitude of the most ancient eclipses more exactly — doubting it, since Tycho "must lack ancient observations," but eager for his method. This is the chronological-astronomy programme (dating sacred and classical history by eclipse) that drives the whole Herwart exchange.
- Technical terms (kept;). calculus Prutenicus → the Prutenic Tables; magna Syntaxis → the Almagest (Great Syntaxis); Lucifer → the Morning Star (Venus); defectus / Eclipsis luminarium → eclipse of the luminaries; ♀ = Venus, ☿ = Mercury, ☉ = Sun, ☾ = Moon (glyphs kept in source).
- Editorial sigla. The transcription carries scattered superscript "A" marks — the edition's witness-sigla flagging readings of witness A (the Munich Kladde) against B (the Resen copy); they are apparatus markers, not text, and are disregarded in the translation (and noted here).
- Persons named. Johann Georg Herwart von Hohenburg (sender); Rudolf II (the Emperor); Ptolemy; the unnamed ancient Roman poet; the Duke of Bavaria (in the titulature).
- Calendar. Dateline Monachij 23. Iulij Anno 1599; Catholic Bavaria kept the Gregorian (New Style) calendar; marked [N.S.]. (The 4 BC date is Herwart's chronological reckoning, reproduced as he gives it.)
- Correction. Latin signature corrected 'HERWAUART' to 'HERUART' (Dreyer VIII p.158); English unaffected.
