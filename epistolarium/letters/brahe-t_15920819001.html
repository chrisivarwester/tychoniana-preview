The original

MAGNIFICO ET AMPLISSIMO VIRO, EXΙΜΙΑ TAM GENERIS QUAM DOCTRINÆ NOBILITATE INPRIMIS CONSPICUO, DOMINO IOHANNI MÆTELANO, SERENISS. SCOTORUM REGIS CANCELLARIO, AMICO SUO PLURIMUM HONORANDO

PERPAUCIS abhinc mensibus, Nobilissime et amplissime vir, literas tuas, quibus nostris superiore hyeme ad te datis respondes, iam pridem accepi. Quæ quod genuinum non modo raræ, quæ in te est, eruditionis specimen, inprimisque Romani sermonis nitorem, sed iucundissimam humanitatis fragrantiam affatim redolerent, gratissimæ mihi fuerunt. Ex quo itaque tuæ illæ ad me perlatæ essent, avide semper desiderabam, ut certo aliquo fretus internuntio ijs nonnihil responderem. Sed cum eorum, per quos meas ad te transmittere in animo haberem, præter meam opinionem navigatio iam tandem præpeditur, indeque accideret ut responsio ad tuas in hoc usque tempus dilata sit, idcirco minime intermittendum duxi, quin cum mercatoribus, qui hinc ad vos appellunt, ante ingruentem brumam paucis rescriberetur, qualemcumque gratæ mentis et memoriæ significationem apud te testatam relinquerem, meque vicissim tui amantem declararem. Agnosco equidem non exigua benevolentiæ erga me tuæ extare indicia, præsertim dum eruditis et argutis tuis versibus effigiem, unâ cum Urania nostra (ut superiora tua in me officia tacitus præteream) eleganter ornare non degravatus sis. Quo sane nomine gratias tibi ago, agamque perpetuo maximas. Unicum tamen est, quod in his non tam desidero quam liberius et iusto abundantius dictum sentiam, in quo etiam nimium Poëtarum indulsisse licentiæ videris, idque nimirum per terna illa verba, quibus tria ultima concluseris Epigrammata. Quæ cum saltem Creatori immenso et ineffabili Creaturis nequaquam competant, ea cuipiam conditæ naturæ asscribere, quocunque dicendi tropo excusentur, inconveniens, imo piaculum censuerim. Quare si loco horum quippiam aliud substitueris, quod fragilis humanæ conditionis metas non transcendat, rectius meo iudicio feceris, mihique rem magis gratam præstiteris. En quanta hîc utar erga te audacia et parrhisia, quam ingenuus candor ille tuus nostraque amicitia non difficulter (uti spero) excusabunt. Habeo insuper gratias, quod D. IOHANNEM CRAIGUM Medicum vestratem, vel ob meam commendationem cariorem habueris atque ad optima quæque promovere receperis, utut is nuper arrogans quoddam contra nostra Placita Scriptum tertio demum ab Apologiæ illi transmissæ anno parturierit, quod affectato et insolenti nomine Capnuraniæ restinctionem (proh Dædaleum inauditarum hactenus vocum fabricatorem) appellare non erubuit. In quo se omnia quæ in libro nostro secundo de Novis Æthereis Phænomenis atque Apologia ipsi hoc nomine destinata continentur, refutare atque infirmare inverecunda præsumptione iactavit, cum tamen ne minimum quidem eorum, apud eos, qui rem ipsam penitius introspicientes, usu eandem debite exercere didicerunt, nec præiudicio cæco impediuntur, vel semel vacillare faciat. Longe igitur consultius fecisset enervis iste Cometarum (qui revera cœlestes sunt, nec flagrantes in aëre fumositates, ut ille in suum nimium credens, ac si in hunc baptizatus fuisset, ARISTOTELEM, commentus est) restinctor, si proprium opinionum suarum incendium et æstuantem animi ardorem ac inflammatos affectuum motus extinguere didicisset. Veritatis, quam tuemur, invictam arcem et firma huius propugnacula, obtusis hisce, quibus eam invadere conatur, argumentorum telis, non magis lædet aut expugnabit, quam si æneum et insuperabilem HERCULIS clypeum plumbea confodere sarissa præsumpserit. Nostra enim ille aut parum minusque recte intelligit, aut ob præoccupatas opiniones intelligere nolit, aut ea distrahit, depravat, invertit, male accommodat. Imo cavillatur et calumniatur fere ubique effreni quadam petulantia, adeo ut ne quidem a scommatis, dicterijs, nænijs, superciliosis oblocutionibus alijsque id generis inflati et impotentis animi pomposis ineptijs sibi temperet, deque ijs, quæ nunquam viderit aut fundamentaliter hauserit, nedum ut usu diuturno sibi familiaria reddiderit, iudicium immaturum et præposterum futili potius quam subtili acumine interponere non vereatur. Hem egregiam a CRAIGO vestrate pro tot annorum in hac palæstra veritatis inquirendæ stabiliendæque causa exantlatis laboribus, proque perassiduis vigilijs, studijs indefessis, sumptibus inæstimabilibus, reporto gratiam. Certe si non maiorem a cæteris Europæ eruditis Mathematicis consequutus fuero, aut ego frustra tot tantaque egerim (dummodo nihil aliud quam hominum gratitudinem et non potius solam veritatem, quæ sibiipsi sufficiens meritum est, spectarem) aut illi valde ingrati reputabuntur. At nemo est ex omnibus Italiæ, Galliæ, Angliæ, Germaniæ, Bohemiæ et Poloniæ Mathematicis, qui, quantum ego sciam, nostra non libere et libenter approbet, ijsque (utut Peripateticorum in Scholis usitata dogmata in quibusdam convellant) grata mentis significatione subscribat. Solus hic vester præ omnibus alijs sibi sapiens veritatem, quam in lucem ex inveteratis opinionum et errorum tenebris producere elaboramus, offuscare, ut sic, quemadmodum iste ex devastato Dianæ templo, nomen sibi quoddam comparet, anticipata quadam philautia et præsumptione non indecens reputavit; quasi is solus penitius rem introspiciat, quam cæteri omnes, et cornicum oculos configere valeat. Verum eiusmodi cothurnis et sophisticis contradictionibus, veritati a nobis assertæ frustra obstrepentibus, nihil in posterum nostra Urania respondere dignabitur, ne bonas horas altioribus solidioribusque tribuendas usqueadeo inutiliter collocet. Abblandiatur et placeat is sibi inque suo cerebello triumphet, quantum allubescit. Veritas certissimis et infallibilibus observationibus (quamvis et has dentata sua strigili rodere et turgido ac temerario ausu improbare nefas haud existimarit, utut tales et his merito conferendas assequi et imitari vix sustineat) fundata et certis demonstrationibus Geometricis (quas ille non minus, sive per inscitiam sive affectati iudicij temeritatem, malitiose sugillat) in apertum deducta imbelles non moratur oppugnantis insultus. Ne autem quis suspicetur, me, quod causæ diffidam, responsionem aversari, mittam Capnuranicas ipsius commentationes, vel potius commenta, ad præstantes quosdam in Germania Mathematicos, observationibus cœlestibus accurate instituendis assuetos et in hac materia solide versatos (cæteris enim parum ista intelligentibus, fucatis, quibus scatet, sophismatis, facile imposuerit CRAIGIUS vester ille) qui controversiam hanc exquisita scientia et diuturno usu, decernere nôrunt, et singulis ad æquam trutinam expensis, citra affectus et præiudicium ullum, iustam ferent censuram. Forte etiam prodibit suo tempore amicorum vel discipulorum meorum aliquis, qui hanc controversiam dirimere et ad invalidos Craigicarum obiectionum ictus retorquendos otium sibi sumat. Sicque tandem infringat solido propria delusus ab arte. In me equidem non amplius inveniet Antagonistam. Præstat enim Tomis nostris Progymnasmatum Astronomicorum (quos nunc præ manibus habeo) absolvendis operam locare, quam talibus affectatis obmurmurationibus retundendis inutiliter occupari. Quorum non solum primus (qui de Nova Stella ubertim agit) CRAIGI circa hanc quoque ignorantiam et crassa errata abunde patefaciet, sed et reliqui duo de quinque Cometis ex professo tractantes, eosque omnes cœlestes, citra omne dubium, fuisse apertissime demonstrantes, quam perperam et frustra CRAIGUS hic nostris assertionibus refragetur, cuivis luce Meridiana clarius ob oculos ponent. Hinc, si volet, suæ evidentissimæ hallucinationis, ignorantiæ et ineptitudinis redargutionem depromat, atque demum melius perspectis ijs, quibus seducitur, deviationibus, in veritatis semitam (modo id fieri queat) revertatur.

Quæ licet ita se habeant, et CRAIGUS in ultimo suo scripto non solum veritatis, sed et modestiæ limites multifariam transilierit, parumque ob benevolentiam in illo amando, Regique et tibi eum commendando, non gravatim a me præstitam, grati vicissim animi retulerit: tamen tantum abest ut ipsi ob id male cupiam, quin longe potius etiamnunc, imo multo plus quam antea tibi commendatum velim, quo tua auctoritate et intercessione illius res familiaris augeatur et subsidia ipsi studiorum Mathematicorum plenius rectiusque instituendorum largius suppeditentur, ut id quod aliquot iam annis theorice contemplatus est, per ipsam exercitationem in usu explorare, veritatemque latentem ipsa Praxi eruere commodius valeat. Quæcunque enim per Experientiam in Pragmatiam ratam transferri debeant, longe maiores requirunt impensas et studiorum nervos, quam quæ otiosa quadam speculatione animo concipiuntur. Quæ cum forte huic CRAIGO non ita abunde (prout res ipsa postulat) suppetant, adeo ut ne quidem ea quæ olim in Germania Instrumenta (quæ satis paratu vilia fuisse non dubito) se habuisse iactat, nunc ad manus illi sint, ideo de rebus ipsis opinione quadam propria vel auctoritate aliorum falsam persuasionem audentius fovet et abortivum ingenij factum excludit. Rebus itaque iuvandus, quo rerum medullas promptius capiat, nec inanibus saltem verborum corticibus se, alias nudum, oppalliet. Atque hæc de illo satis aut forte ad te alijs distentum nimia. Ego nihilominus ut hæc omnia benevole accipias et quod iusto plus Craigicis hisce, mihi sane parum, tibi (quemadmodum existimo) longe minus gratis mecum motis controversijs, in tuum sinum, suadente id amicitia nostra, deponendis immorer, teque gravioribus Reipub. administrandæ munijs intente occupatum diutius, quam par est, detineam, in potiorem partem pro tuo candore interpreteris, te etiam atque etiam rogatum habeo. Vale, et amicitiam nostram per literas continuare non intermitte. Ego vicissim nec locorum nec temporum iniuria vincar, quo minus tui memoriam firmiter animo retinere, et data occasione Epistolis ad te scribendis (cum aliter non detur) in mentem revocare, studiose enitar.

Iterum quam felicissime vale.
Datæ Uraniburgi 19. Augusti Anno 1592.
Magnif. tuæ studiosiss.
TYCHO BRAHE.

To the magnificent and most eminent man, conspicuous above all for the exceptional nobility both of his birth and of his learning, Master John Maitland, Chancellor to the Most Serene King of the Scots, his friend to be most highly honoured.

A very few months ago, most noble and eminent man, I received — though it was already some while back — your letter, in which you reply to the one I sent you last winter. And because it breathed forth in abundance not only a genuine specimen of the rare erudition that is in you, and above all the polish of the Roman tongue, but also the most delightful fragrance of humanity, it was most welcome to me. And so, from the moment your letter was brought to me, I always eagerly longed to reply to it somewhat, relying on some sure messenger. But since the sailing of those by whom I had it in mind to send my [letter] to you is now at last impeded, contrary to my expectation, and from this it came about that my reply to yours was put off until this very time, I therefore judged it by no means to be neglected that, with the merchants who put in here on their way to you, a few words at least should be written back before the onset of winter, so that I might leave with you some attestation, of whatever kind, of a grateful mind and memory, and declare myself in turn one who loves you. I acknowledge indeed that there exist no small tokens of your goodwill toward me, especially in that you did not think it beneath you elegantly to adorn, with your learned and witty verses, my portrait, together with our Urania (to pass over in silence your earlier services to me). For which, to be sure, I give you thanks, and shall give the greatest thanks for ever. Yet there is one thing in them which I do not so much miss as feel to have been said too freely and more lavishly than is right, and in which too you seem to have indulged the licence of the poets to excess — namely, by those three words with which you concluded the last three epigrams. Since these can in no way belong to creatures, but only to the immense and ineffable Creator, I should reckon it unfitting — nay, a sin to be expiated — to ascribe them to any created nature, by whatever figure of speech they be excused. Wherefore if in place of these you substitute something else, which does not overstep the bounds of frail human condition, you will, in my judgement, act more rightly, and render me a more agreeable service. See how great a boldness and frankness [parrhisia] I here employ toward you, which that ingenuous candour of yours and our friendship will (as I hope) not find it hard to excuse. I owe you thanks besides, that you have held Master John Craig, a physician of your country, the dearer even on account of my commendation, and have undertaken to advance him to all the best things — although he has lately, in the third year after the Apology was sent to him, brought forth a certain arrogant writing against our tenets, which he did not blush to call, by an affected and insolent name, the Extinguishing of Capnurania (alas, what a Daedalus, a fabricator of words unheard-of until now!). In which, with shameless presumption, he has boasted that he refutes and overthrows all that is contained in our second book On the New Aethereal Phenomena and in the Apology destined for him under this title — though he does not make even the least of those things waver so much as once, in the eyes of those who, peering more deeply into the matter itself, have learned to ply it duly by practice, and are not hampered by blind prejudice. Far more advisedly, then, would that feeble extinguisher of comets (which are in truth heavenly, and not blazing exhalations in the air, as he, trusting too much in his own Aristotle — as if he had been baptised into him — has imagined) have done, had he learned to extinguish the fire of his own opinions, and the seething heat of his mind, and the inflamed motions of his passions. The unconquered citadel of the truth which we defend, and its firm bulwarks, he will no more wound or storm with those blunt weapons of argument by which he tries to assail it, than if he should presume to pierce the bronze and insuperable shield of Hercules with a leaden pike. For our matters he either understands little and less than rightly, or, on account of his preoccupied opinions, refuses to understand, or else he distorts, corrupts, inverts, and misapplies them. Indeed he quibbles and slanders almost everywhere with a certain unbridled insolence, so that he does not even restrain himself from gibes, sarcasms, doggerel, supercilious revilings, and other pompous follies of that kind, [the marks] of a puffed-up and impotent mind; and concerning things he has never seen nor drawn in at their foundations — let alone made familiar to himself by long practice — he is not afraid to interpose a judgement immature and preposterous, with an acumen futile rather than subtle. Ha! a fine thanks I get from your countryman Craig for the labours drained over so many years in this wrestling-school of seeking out and establishing the truth, and for unremitting vigils, untiring studies, and inestimable expense! Surely, if I shall not have obtained a greater thanks from the rest of Europe's learned mathematicians, either I shall have done so many and so great things in vain (provided I were to look to nothing other than men's gratitude, and not rather to truth alone, which is a sufficient reward to itself), or they will be reckoned greatly ungrateful. But there is no one among all the mathematicians of Italy, France, England, Germany, Bohemia, and Poland who, so far as I know, does not freely and gladly approve our matters, and (however much in certain points they overturn the dogmas usual in the Peripatetics' schools) subscribe to them with a token of a grateful mind. This countryman of yours alone, wise in his own eyes above all others, has thought it not unbecoming — out of a certain anticipated self-love [philautia] and presumption — to becloud the truth which we labour to bring into the light out of the inveterate darkness of opinions and errors, so that thus, just as that man [did] from the devastated temple of Diana, he might procure for himself some name; as though he alone peered more deeply into the matter than all the rest, and were able to peck out the eyes of crows. But to contradictions of this sort — tragic bombast and sophistical, clamouring in vain against the truth we have asserted — our Urania will henceforth deign to make no reply, lest she so uselessly spend good hours that ought to be given to higher and more solid things. Let him flatter himself and please himself and triumph in his little brain as much as he likes. The truth, founded upon most certain and infallible observations (although he has not thought it impious to gnaw even these with his toothed scraper and to condemn them with swollen and rash daring, however much he could scarcely manage to attain and imitate such [observations], or others worthily to be compared with these), and brought into the open by sure geometrical demonstrations (which he no less maliciously belittles, whether through ignorance or the rashness of an affected judgement), does not heed the unwarlike assaults of its attacker. But lest anyone suspect that I shun a reply because I distrust my cause, I shall send his Capnuranic disquisitions — or rather fabrications — to certain distinguished mathematicians in Germany, accustomed to setting up celestial observations accurately and solidly versed in this subject matter (for upon others who understand these things but little, that Craig of yours might easily impose with the painted sophisms in which he abounds), men who know how to decide this controversy by exquisite knowledge and long practice, and who, weighing each point in a fair balance, apart from any passion and prejudice, will pass a just verdict. Perhaps too there will come forth in his own time some one of my friends or pupils who will take it upon himself, at leisure, to settle this controversy and to turn back the invalid blows of Craig's objections. And so at last let him, deluded by his own art, be broken against the solid [truth]. In me, indeed, he will find an antagonist no longer. For it is better to bestow my pains on finishing our volumes of the Astronomical Preliminaries (which I now have in hand) than to be uselessly occupied in beating back such affected mutterings. Of these volumes, not only will the first (which treats copiously of the New Star) abundantly lay open Craig's ignorance and gross errors in this matter too, but the remaining two — treating expressly of the five comets, and most plainly demonstrating that all of them, beyond all doubt, were heavenly — will set before anyone's eyes, more clearly than the noonday light, how wrongly and vainly Craig here opposes our assertions. Hence let him, if he will, draw out the refutation of his most evident hallucination, ignorance, and ineptitude; and at length, having better discerned the deviations by which he is led astray, let him return into the path of truth (if only it can be done).

Though things stand thus, and Craig in his last writing has in many ways overleapt the bounds not only of truth but also of modesty, and has shown a grateful mind but little in return for the goodwill — bestowed by me not grudgingly — in befriending him and commending him to the King and to you: yet so far am I from wishing him ill on that account, that I would rather far more even now — nay, much more than before — have him commended to you, so that by your authority and intercession his household estate may be increased, and the means of pursuing mathematical studies more fully and rightly may be more liberally supplied to him, so that what he has now for some years contemplated in theory he may the more conveniently test in use through practice itself, and bring out the hidden truth by Practice itself. For whatever things must be carried over from Experience into established Practice require far greater expense and sinews of study than those that are conceived in the mind by a certain idle speculation. And since these things perhaps are not so abundantly at this Craig's command (as the matter itself requires) — so much so that not even those Instruments which he boasts of having once had in Germany (instruments which I do not doubt were cheap enough to procure) are now at his hand — he therefore the more boldly fosters a false persuasion about the matters themselves, whether from some opinion of his own or by the authority of others, and shuts out the miscarried product of his talent. He must therefore be helped with resources, so that he may more readily grasp the marrow of things, and not, being otherwise naked, cloak himself merely with the empty husks of words. And this about him is enough — or perhaps too much for you, distracted by other matters. I nonetheless beg you, again and again, to take all this kindly, and to interpret in the better part, according to your candour, the fact that I dwell more than is right on these Craigic controversies — stirred up against me, indeed, to my small profit and (as I think) far less to your liking — controversies to be laid into your bosom at the prompting of our friendship; and that I detain you, intently occupied with the weightier offices of administering the commonwealth, longer than is fitting. Farewell, and do not cease to continue our friendship by letters. I in turn shall not be overcome by the injury of places or of times, but shall strive zealously to keep your memory firmly in mind, and, when occasion is given, to recall it by writing letters to you (since otherwise it is not granted).

Again, may you fare most happily.
Given at Uraniborg, 19 August, the year 1592.
Most devoted to Your Magnificence,
Tycho Brahe.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Iohannes Maetelanus = John Maitland of Thirlestane (c. 1543–1595), Lord Chancellor of Scotland under James VI from 1587, and a Latin poet of some accomplishment. He had visited (or been associated with) Tycho's circle during the 1590 royal connections between Scotland and Denmark (James VI's marriage to Anne of Denmark; the Scottish party's contact with Hven). Maitland had written commendatory verses on a portrait of Tycho and on "our Urania" (see below).
  • Subject of dispute. D. Iohannes Craigus, Medicus vestratem = John Craig (d. 1620), Scottish physician and Aristotelian (later physician to James VI), an Edinburgh-trained Paduan graduate who maintained — against Tycho — the Aristotelian doctrine that comets are sublunary atmospheric exhalations, not celestial bodies. The exchange ran 1588–1592: Tycho sent Craig his second book De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis (the Novis Aethereis Phaenomenis named here) and an Apologia (1589, never printed); Craig replied in March 1592, in manuscript, with the polemic Tycho here mocks.
  • Capnuraniae restinctio — Craig's manuscript tract, fully Capnuraniae restinctio, seu cometarum in aethera sublimationis refutatio ("The Extinguishing of Capnurania, or a refutation of the raising-up of comets into the aether"). Capnurania is Craig's coinage from Greek kapnós (καπνός, "smoke") + Urania, i.e. "smoky-Urania" — a jeer that Tycho's celestial astronomy is built on (cometary) smoke. Tycho calls Craig a Daedalum … vocum fabricatorem, "a Daedalus, a fabricator of words," mocking the neologism. Rendered "the Extinguishing of Capnurania"; the wordplay is flagged, not flattened.
  • Greek loan-words in the Latin. parrhisia (παρρησία, "frank speech, candour") — kept and glossed "frankness [parrhisia]." philautia (φιλαυτία, "self-love") — kept and glossed "self-love [philautia]." Both are common in humanist Latin; no Greek script appears in the body.
  • ex devastato Dianae templo … nomen sibi … comparet — "from the devastated temple of Diana … procure for himself some name." A pointed allusion to Herostratus, who burned the Temple of Artemis (Diana) at Ephesus in 356 BC precisely to make his name immortal. Tycho casts Craig as a man seeking fame by an act of destruction. Kept as allusion and identified here.
  • cornicum oculos configere — "to peck out the eyes of crows," a proverbial phrase (cf. Cicero, Pro Murena 25) for outwitting the most cunning; ironic here. Kept as idiom.
  • aeneum … Herculis clypeum plumbea … sarissa — "the bronze shield of Hercules [pierced] with a leaden pike"; a sarissa is the long Macedonian pike. The futility-image is kept literal.
  • cothurnis — the cothurnus is the high boot of tragic actors; metonymic for inflated, tragic-style bombast. Rendered "tragic bombast."
  • dentata sua strigili rodere — "gnaw with his toothed scraper"; a strigilis is the bather's scraper. The metaphor of corrosive carping is kept literal.
  • Astronomy / the works named. libro nostro secundo de Novis Aethereis Phaenomenis = Tycho's De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis (Uraniborg, 1588), the second book of his planned trilogy, arguing the comet of 1577 was supralunary. Tomis nostris Progymnasmatum Astronomicorum = the Astronomiae instauratae progymnasmata (the "Astronomical Preliminaries"; Progymnasmata lit. "preliminary exercises," kept by sense as Astronomical Preliminaries), whose first volume "treats copiously of the New Star" (the 1572 nova, De nova stella) and whose projected further volumes were to treat the five comets. The translation preserves Tycho's claim that all five comets citra omne dubium … coelestes … fuisse ("were, beyond all doubt, heavenly") as his own assertion, with the hedge citra omne dubium kept as written.
  • Apologiae illi transmissae anno … tertio — "in the third year after the Apology was sent to him." Tycho dates Craig's polemic to the third year after the (1589) Apologia was transmitted, i.e. 1591/92; the manuscript reached Tycho in spring 1592 (so Norlind, after Dreyer VII). Reported, not silently reconciled with the catalogue year.
  • Urania nostra — "our Urania," the Muse of astronomy, figuring both Tycho's science and (in the portrait passage) the personified frontispiece-figure of his published astronomy; Maitland's commendatory verses had honoured her. Kept as "Urania."
  • Theological scruple. The terna illa verba ("those three words") with which Maitland's last three epigrams closed are not quoted in the letter; Tycho objects that they ascribe to a creature (conditae naturae) what belongs only to the Creator immenso et ineffabili — a piaculum ("a sin to be expiated"). The specific words are a crux: the source does not name them, so the exact phrasing Tycho found impious cannot be recovered from this text alone.
  • Negations checked. The clauses of praise-with-reservation were translated counting the negations: non … desidero quam … sentiam ("I do not so much miss … as feel"); non degravatus sis ("you did not think it beneath you"); non erubuit ("he did not blush"); ne minimum quidem … vel semel vacillare faciat ("he does not make even the least … waver so much as once"); non amplius inveniet Antagonistam ("he will find an antagonist no longer"); tantum abest ut … male cupiam, quin … commendatum velim ("so far am I from wishing him ill … that I would rather … have him commended") — the tantum abest ut … quin construction is reproduced with its full reversing force, not flattened.
