The original

DESCRIPTUM LITERARUM CLARISSIMI ET PRÆSTANTISSIMI MATHEMATICI DN. JOHANNIS ANTONII MAGINI PATAVINI BONONIA EODEM ANNO CIϽ. IϽ. XC. AD TYCHONEM BRAHE IN DANIAM SCRIPTARUM.
ILLUSTRIS VIR,

ACCEPI librum tuum eruditissimum ad me tuo nomine a studioso quodam Dano, qui olim tibi domesticus erat, transmissum; quo mihi abs te nihil gratius exhiberi potuit, cum ejusmodi scripta curiosissime conquirere soleam, ac libentissime perlegere. Vix enim aliâ viâ sperassem eum ad manus meas perventurum, cum perpauca istic excusa volumina soleant in Italiam importari propter longinquitatem itineris, ac vecturæ difficultatem: Eo accedit, quod elegantissimo hoc munere, quanti me faceres, præclare significasti: quo quidem nomine tibi gratias ago, quas possum maximas. Quod enim summopere expeto et studere soleo, ut mihi scilicet pateat aditus in gratiam tui similium; tu, quæ tua est singularis humanitas, id mihi ultro obtulisti. Hunc ego meum in te animum ne possem, ut decuit, atque ut optabam, statim per literas significare; id fuit caussæ, quod ad te cum meis literis nuper a me elucubratum Opus propediem edendum mittere constitueram: Superiore autem æstate, cum incidissem in morbum, egi diligenter cum eodem studioso, scriptis ad eum literis, ut a te meo nomine, quæ hîc subiciam, petere ne gravaretur. Habere igitur maxime cuperem compendiosam descriptionem stellarum fixarum, quemadmodum a te emendatæ sunt, ut mihi usui esse possit, in meis brevi ad prælum revocandis Ephæmeridibus, ad hæc notitiam aliquam Eccentricitatum Planetarum, itemque commensurationum uniuscujusque eorum orbis, si eas a Copernianis differre invenisti, ac correxisti. Quod si mihi gratificari non dedignaberis, efficiam profecto ut te non pœniteat beneficii in me tui: Namque eâ quæ humanos et liberalium artium Professores decet, ingenuitate, tuarum te rerum auctorem, laudare in meis scriptis mihi constitutum est. Meum vero sensum ac judicium, de tuo illo præclaro labore, circa Cometam anno 1577. conspectum, ne desideres: Sic habeto, vir Clarissime; Cum enim incredibilem in illo diligentiam tuam et accuratam observandi viam perspexerim, mihi persuadeo, fore, ut cælestium corporum motus emendare exactissime possis, fructuque operæ illius maximo omnium studio, atque expectationi cumulatissime satisfacias; unde nomini tuo sempiternum decus adjungatur. Cuperem tamen, te in motu Martis observando et examinando maxime incumbere, cum eum observari non posse exacte, vulgo persuasum sit: atque ego in eâ sum hæresi, ut putem immutari Eccentricitatem ipsius, periodumque suam habere, ita ut alia in eo introducenda sit æquatio Eccentricitatis: alioqui si tabula efficiatur pro maxima Eccentricitate, non poterit minimæ inservire, et contra. Ex quo videmus, Copernicum (etsi Eccentricitatem hujusmodi variari deprehendit) non idcirco tabulas condidisse, quæ singulis temporibus conveniant, quando Martis æquationes supputatæ ad Eccentricitatem a Copernico observatam partium 5.51'. 30''. aptari minime possunt, ipsius Ptolemæi temporibus, quibus partium 6. Martis Eccentricitatem definivit. Non possum non magnopere probare Systema Universi a te excogitatum, quamvis cuperem Solis Orbem ac Martis nequaquam sese intersecare. Quod si, ut a studioso isto cognovi, Martem acronycum terræ propius accedere, quam Solem, a te est observatum, intersecatio hujusmodi omninô admittenda est. In magna versor expectatione tuarum hujusmodi observationum et speculationum, quas et probare et sequi minime erubescam. Etsi in construendis Ephæmeridibus resolutisque tabulis a Copernianâ ratione, et tabulis Prutenicis ne latum quidem unguem deflexi. Spero etiam, quod vehementer cupio, primum tuum de mundi Ætherei phænomenis librum, quem iterum êditurum polliceris, hoc anno lecturum. Significavit mihi sæpe dictus studiosus abs te desiderari Verneri librum de motu octavæ Sphæræ, quem cum totâ Germaniâ conquisieris, nunquam tamen invenire potueris. Quare unum ego ipsi exemplar dedi, ut id ad te meo nomine transmitteret. Si quid erit aliud, quod tuâ caussâ efficere possim, perhonorificum ducam, ut a te mihi imperetur, cui velim omnia feliciter evenire, atque optime esse consultum. Hoc anno publice interpretaturus sum meas Cœlestium Orbium hypotheses: quæ mihi bona erit occasio, quæ in hoc genere a me êdita sunt, longe uberius explicare (ut jam facere aggressus sum) et commentariis ac Geometricis demonstrationibus, supputationibusque illustrare. Quæ tamen commentaria priusquam publici juris efficiantur; libenter ego lucubrationes tuas cognoscere, et intelligere velim, ut tuas quoque suppositiones iis inserere et explicare possim. Ac ne tibi prolixitate nimiâ sim molestior, hîc faciam scribendi finem;te rogans iterum atque iterum, ut meam in te observantiam benevolo excipias animo, bonique consulas.

Bononiæ Idib. Septemb. 1590.
Illustri dominationi tuæ Addictissimus
JO. ANTON. MAGINUS PATAVINUS.

A COPY OF A LETTER OF THE MOST RENOWNED AND MOST EXCELLENT MATHEMATICIAN MASTER GIOVANNI ANTONIO MAGINI OF PADUA, WRITTEN FROM BOLOGNA IN THE SAME YEAR 1590 TO TYCHO BRAHE IN DENMARK.
ILLUSTRIOUS SIR,

I have received your most learned book, sent to me in your name by a certain Danish student who was once a member of your household; than which nothing more welcome could have been presented to me from you, since I am wont to seek out writings of this kind most eagerly and to read them through most gladly. For I should scarcely have hoped, by any other route, that it would come into my hands, since very few volumes printed there [in Denmark] are usually imported into Italy, on account of the great length of the journey and the difficulty of carriage. To this is added that, by this most elegant gift, you have plainly signified how much you esteem me — on which account, indeed, I give you the greatest thanks I can. For that which I especially desire and am wont to pursue — namely, that there should lie open to me an approach to the favour of men like you — this you, with that singular kindness which is yours, have offered me unasked. That I was not able at once, by letter, to signify this disposition of mine toward you, as was fitting and as I wished — the cause of this was that I had resolved to send to you, together with my letter, a Work lately worked out by me [and] shortly to be published. But last summer, when I had fallen into an illness, I dealt carefully with the same student, by a letter written to him, [asking] that he should not be reluctant to seek from you, in my name, the things I shall here set down. I should therefore most greatly wish to have a compendious description of the fixed stars, as they have been emended by you, so that it may be of use to me in my Ephemerides, which are shortly to be recalled to the press; and besides this, some knowledge of the eccentricities of the planets, and likewise of the proportions of the orb of each of them, if you have found them to differ from the Copernican [values], and have corrected them. And if you will not disdain to oblige me, I shall assuredly bring it about that you do not regret your kindness toward me: for it is my fixed resolve, with that candour which befits men of letters and Professors of the liberal arts, to praise you in my writings as the author of your own discoveries. As for my opinion and judgement concerning that celebrated work of yours upon the Comet seen in the year 1577 — do not yearn for it [in vain]; have it thus, most renowned Sir. For since I have perceived your incredible diligence in that [work] and your accurate method of observing, I persuade myself that it will come to pass that you will be able to emend the motions of the heavenly bodies most exactly, and that by the fruit of that labour you will most abundantly satisfy the keenest zeal and expectation of all — whence an everlasting glory will be added to your name. Yet I could wish that you would apply yourself above all to observing and examining the motion of Mars, since it is commonly believed that it cannot be observed exactly; and I myself am of this heresy, that I think its eccentricity to be altered, and to have its own period, so that another equation of the eccentricity must be introduced for it; otherwise, if a table is made for the maximum eccentricity, it will not be able to serve the minimum, and conversely. From which we see that Copernicus (even though he detected that an eccentricity of this kind varies) did not for that reason compose tables that agree for every period, since the computed equations of Mars cannot in the least be fitted, for the [maximum] eccentricity observed by Copernicus of 5°51′30″, to the times of Ptolemy himself, in which he defined the eccentricity of Mars as 6 degrees. I cannot but greatly approve the System of the Universe devised by you, although I could wish that the Orb of the Sun and that of Mars by no means cut across each other. But if, as I have learned from that student, it has been observed by you that Mars at opposition [acronychal Mars] approaches nearer to the earth than the Sun, then an intersection of this kind must altogether be admitted. I am in great expectation of observations and speculations of this kind of yours, which I shall by no means blush either to approve or to follow — even though, in constructing Ephemerides and resolved tables, I have not deviated even a finger's breadth from the Copernican system and the Prutenic Tables. I hope too — what I vehemently desire — that I shall this year read your first book on the phenomena of the Aethereal world, which you promise to publish again. The often-mentioned student signified to me that Werner's book on the motion of the eighth Sphere is wanted by you, [a book] which, though you have searched for it through all Germany, you have nevertheless never been able to find. Wherefore I have given him a copy of it, that he might send it to you in my name. If there shall be anything else that I can accomplish on your behalf, I shall count it a great honour that it should be enjoined upon me by you — to whom I wish that all things may turn out happily, and that all may be most well provided for. This year I am to lecture publicly on my hypotheses of the Celestial Orbs: which will be a good occasion for me to explain far more amply the things I have published in this kind (as I have already begun to do), and to illustrate them with commentaries and geometrical demonstrations and computations. Yet, before these commentaries are made public, I should gladly wish to learn and understand your lucubrations, so that I may insert and explain your suppositions too among them. And lest I be too troublesome to you by excessive length, I shall here make an end of writing — begging you again and again to receive my regard for you with a kindly disposition, and to take it in good part.

At Bologna, on the Ides of September [13 September], 1590.
Most devoted to Your Illustrious Lordship,
Giovanni Antonio Magini of Padua.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Johannes Antonius Maginus Patavinus = Giovanni Antonio Magini (1555–1617), the Paduan-born mathematician and astronomer who held the chair of mathematics at Bologna (from 1588, having beaten Galileo for the post). A prolific ephemerides-maker and cartographer; the Patavinus ("of Padua") marks his birthplace, not his then-residence.
  • Edition of record. The letter survives because Tycho himself printed it in his Astronomiae instauratae mechanica (1598), under a chancery-style Latin rubric (DESCRIPTUM LITERARUM … = "A copy of a letter …"); reprinted by Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII. No manuscript witness is recorded in the edition.
  • studioso quodam Dano … domesticus — "a certain Danish student … a member of your household": an unnamed former Uraniborg famulus then in Italy who served as Magini's intermediary, carrying both the Mechanica-precursor gift-book to Magini and Werner's volume back toward Tycho. Identity not given in the letter; left unidentified rather than guessed.
  • librum tuum eruditissimum — "your most learned book": the work Tycho had sent. Most plausibly the De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis (1588, the comet book), to which Magini refers again below as primum tuum de mundi Aetherei phaenomenis librum; not named outright here, so rendered generically.
  • nuper a me elucubratum Opus — "a Work lately worked out by me": Magini's forthcoming volume, almost certainly his Novae coelestium orbium theoricae (Venice, 1589), the planetary-theory work he describes lecturing on below.
  • Astronomy — technical terms (editorial glossary): compendiosam descriptionem stellarum fixarum → "a compendious description of the fixed stars" (Tycho's emended star catalogue); Ephaemerides → Ephemerides; Eccentricitates Planetarum → "eccentricities of the planets"; commensurationum … orbis → "proportions of the orb"; aequatio Eccentricitatis → "equation of the eccentricity" (aequatio in the astronomical sense by the edition's convention); tabulae Prutenicae → Prutenic Tables (Reinhold's 1551 Copernican tables); resolutae tabulae → "resolved tables."
  • The Mars eccentricity figures. Magini cites Copernicus's Mars eccentricity as partium 5.51'.30'' and Ptolemy's as partium 6 — rendered 5°51′30″ and 6 degrees. The figures are reproduced exactly as printed; partium ("of parts/degrees") is the period idiom for degrees. This is the crux of Magini's "heresy": that the Martian eccentricity is not constant but varies with its own period, so that one table cannot serve both maximum and minimum — the very problem Kepler would later resolve. Magini's claim is preserved as his own hedged conjecture (in eâ sum haeresi, ut putem = "I am of this heresy, that I think"); no silent sharpening. [maximum] — editorial insertion (flagged). In the clause Martis aequationes supputatae ad Eccentricitatem a Copernico observatam partium 5.51'.30'' the Latin says only "the eccentricity observed by Copernicus," not maximam; maxima appears earlier in the letter (si tabula efficiatur pro maxima Eccentricitate) but not here. Magini's argument — that one table fitted to the maximum value cannot serve the minimum — implies that the 5°51′30″ figure he attributes to Copernicus functions as the maximum, so "[maximum]" is a defensible reading; but because the word is not in this clause it is bracketed as an editorial insertion rather than presented as translation.
  • Systema Universi a te excogitatum — "the System of the Universe devised by you": Tycho's geo-heliocentric (Tychonic) system. Magini approves it but objects to the intersection of the orbs of the Sun and Mars — cuperem Solis Orbem ac Martis nequaquam sese intersecare ("I could wish the Orb of the Sun and of Mars by no means cut across each other"). This is a real point of resistance: solid celestial orbs cannot interpenetrate, yet the Tychonic geometry forces the Mars-orb to cross the Sun's.
  • Martem acronycum — "acronychal Mars," i.e. Mars at opposition (rising as the Sun sets; Greek akrónychos, "at nightfall"). The observation that Mars at opposition comes nearer the earth than the Sun is precisely what compels the orb-intersection Magini dislikes; rendered "Mars at opposition [acronychal Mars]" to keep the technical term and gloss it.
  • Cometa anno 1577 — the Great Comet of 1577, the subject of Tycho's De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis; Tycho's parallax work on it placed it above the Moon, the same supralunary result that bears on the orb question.
  • Verneri librum de motu octavae Sphaerae — Johannes Werner's De motu octavae sphaerae (Nuremberg, 1522), on the motion of the eighth (fixed-star) sphere — trepidation theory. The literature (Mosley, Bearing the Heavens) confirms Tycho's avid, frustrated pursuit of this rare book; Magini's gift of a copy via the Danish student is the concrete favour returned for the Mechanica-precursor.
  • Coelestium Orbium hypotheses / theoricae — Magini's own planetary "hypotheses of the Celestial Orbs" (the Novae coelestium orbium theoricae), which he says he will expound publicly this year with geometrical demonstrations.
  • saepe dictus studiosus — "the often-mentioned student": saepe dictus is a fixed idiom ("the oft-mentioned / aforesaid"), referring to the Danish intermediary already named repeatedly in the letter (studioso quodam Dano … eodem studioso … studioso isto). The adverb saepe qualifies dictus (the student has been "often mentioned"), not the main verb significavit; rendered "the often-mentioned student signified to me," not "the student often signified to me."
  • Latin idiom. in eâ sum haeresi — literally "I am in that heresy/school of thought"; the word haeresis here carries its neutral Greek sense ("a chosen tenet, sect-opinion"), with a self-deprecating wink, not the theological charge — rendered "I am of this heresy" to keep the flavour. ne latum quidem unguem deflexi — "I have not deviated even a finger's breadth," a Ciceronian idiom (latum unguem, "a nail's breadth"), kept literal. lucubrationes tuas — "your lucubrations" (works composed by lamplight; scholarly labours), kept as the period term.
  • Epistolary formulae. Opening ILLUSTRIS VIR → "Illustrious Sir"; subscription Illustri dominationi tuae Addictissimus → "Most devoted to Your Illustrious Lordship" (dominatio tua, "Your Lordship," the courteous Italian-chancery form).
  • Calendar / dateline crux. Bononiae Idib. Septemb. 1590 = "At Bologna, on the Ides of September, 1590." Magini, in Catholic Italy, wrote on the Gregorian (New Style) calendar, ten days ahead of the Julian reckoning Tycho kept on Hven; the Roman idiom is kept and glossed, and not converted to Tycho's Old-Style equivalent (which would be 3 September O.S.). Per the edition's convention the style is flagged, the date not "corrected." Label-vs-dateline discrepancy (flagged, not reconciled): the edition label catalogues the letter "1590. 11. september n.s.," two days earlier than the dateline the text itself bears (the Ides = 13 September). Per the edition's convention the header therefore leads with the as-written date — 13 September [N.S.] — and appends the catalogued date (11 September); nothing in the source substantiates the 11th, which may reflect a separate cataloguing tradition or a slip. The discrepancy is recorded here and not silently resolved.
