TYCHO BRAHE AD RUDOLPHUM CORRADUCIUM.

ILLUSTRIS ET MAGNIFICE VIR, DOMINE ET AMICE OBSERVANDISSIME.

QUANTUM me sollicitum habuit vagus quidam rumor de adversâ Illris Dnis tuæ valetudine, tanto maiori gaudio sum affectus, postquam eam salvam et incolumem e longinquo et difficili itinere Italico, legatione perfunctam amplissimâ, in Bohemiam reversam accepi. Quo nomine Deo Opt. maximo gratias ago, eumque precor, ut in posterum quoque Dem Tam incolumem ac florentem Reip. et literis reservet. Non dubito, quin iam antea Do Ta ex alijs cognoverit, quomodo Clementissimæ voluntati Sæ Cæsæ Matis, quam per literas mihi, cum adhuc Witebergæ subsisterem, Do Ta, priusquam in Italiam iret, significavit, superiori Iulio (siquidem remoræ quædam inopinatæ id prius non permiserint) subiectissime paruerim, et postquam huc in Bohemiam veni, in Sæ Cæsæ Matis clientelam benignissime sim receptus. Quare huius rei prolixiori commemoratione apud Dem Tam alias occupatissimam supersedeo.

Id potius agam, ut Illrem Dem Tam debitâ cum reverentiâ obnixe rogem, ut in eâ benevolentiâ, quâ me nihil tale antea promeritum sic complexa est, ut Sæ Cæsæ Mati (quod et ultro facturam confido) me commendare meaque studia promovere non sit degravata (quamobrem etiam gratias ago quas possum maximas, et acturus sum, quoad vixero) in eâ, inquam, benevolentiâ in posterum quoque persistat, meque, sicubi fuerit opus, auxilio consilioque adiuvet. Nam licet de Cæsæ Matis, cuius Clementissimum erga me favorem ratis indicijs expertus sum, in me perseveranter fovendo constantiâ, quâ alias Heroice prædita est, nullatenus addubitem, cum tamen ego hîc peregrinus habear, et instituto vitæ, studiorum ratione, non minus quam locorum intervallo ab Aulâ vestra sim remotus: facile prævideo, quam multa incidere possint, quæ ab Illri De Tuâ, prudentiâ, rerum usu, authoritate, dignitate inprimis pollente, longe melius, quam per me ipsum caveri, et, prout res postulârit, emendari queant. Neque enim possum mihi omnino polliceri, tutiorem me hîc fore, quam antea in Daniâ, ab invidorum morsibus, eorumque, qui sinistre de ijs, quæ tracto, studijs loquentur et sentient, oblocutionibus. Etenim si isthic, ubi radices altius et firmius egeram, ac Consanguineorum, qui in præcipuâ dignitate ibidem constituti, non exiguâ manu stipatus et munitus eram, hoc tamen inertiæ et invidiæ virus amoliri non ita dabatur, quin hæc mea studia fastidiret et non ubique obvios plurimorum Annorum labores irritos reddere tentaret, aut (quod potius elegi) me meamque domum universam in peregrinas oras cum magnâ rei, maiori temporis irrecuperabilis iacturâ importunitate suâ migrare faceret: quanto igitur magis huic communi omnium, qui præter vulgarem consuetudinem aliquid arduum exantlare satagunt, fortunæ iam hîc in exteris locis expositus ero, nullâ familiæ avitæ potentiâ fultus, nisi mihi Deus Opt. max. de fidis, candidis, Dominationisque Tuæ similibus Amicis et fautoribus prospexerit, qui, aut quâ dexteritate cavendi, aut quâ lenitate consopiendi, aut quo animo excipiendi sint, si qui erunt eiuscemodi adversarij occulti vel aperti, oportune norint. Quod ut Do Ta tantâ promptitudine faciat, quantum ipsi est ad id faciendum facultatis, atque ut hoc, quod molior, Astronomicum exercitium, huc in honorem Cæsaris et Imperij a Tuâ inter primos Dne translatum, tutari etiam et promovere non desinat, id equidem rogarem illam operosius, nisi mihi de præclarissimâ et spontaneâ eiusdem voluntate iam antea liquido constaret. Quæ brevitatis caussâ hîc omisi, quodque ea calamus non facile sustineret, illa Do Tua, ubi vacaverit, oretenus audiet ab exhibitore harum, Nobili et Erudito Iuvene FRANCISCO TENGNAGEL, meo domestico, nuper ex Italiâ ad me redeunte, quem Illrem Dem Tuam iam antea Romæ proculdubio vidisse existimo. Qui cum Di Tæ, cuius est observantissimus, conditiones suas, et quid maxime optet, exponere cupiat, eius caussâ Dem Tuam etiam atque etiam quam officiosissime rogo, uti pariter et illum suâ benevolentiâ complectatur et ad optima quæque promoveat. Quicquid in ipsum beneficij contulerit, id veluti mihi quoque præstitum perpetuo gratâ mente agnoscam.

Valeat Illris Dno tua quam diutissime et felicissime, et hanc meam in scribendo audaciam ac prolixitatem pro suo candore sincere interpretetur.

Dabantur ex Arce Cæsareâ Benaticâ 8 Martij 1600.
Illri Doi Tuæ deditiss.
TYCHO BRAHE.

Mitto per prædictum TENGNAGLIUM duos libros ex meis unâ cum Globo quodam Cœlesti
ad restitutionem stellarum a me factam accurate elaboratum. Quæ ut Illris Dno Tua hac vice, donec plura in
hoc studiorum genere ad colophonem, volente Numine, diduxero, boni consulat,
reverenter oro.

TYCHO BRAHE TO RUDOLF CORADUZ.

Illustrious and magnificent man, Lord and friend most to be respected.

As much as a certain vague rumour of Your Illustrious Lordship's ill health held me anxious, with so much greater joy I was filled when I learned that you — safe and unharmed, having discharged a most ample legation — had returned to Bohemia from the long and difficult Italian journey. On which account I give thanks to God Best and Greatest, and pray him that in future too he preserve Your Lordship unharmed and flourishing, for the Commonwealth and for letters. I do not doubt that Your Lordship has already learned from others how, last July (since certain unexpected delays did not permit it earlier), I most submissively obeyed the most clement will of the Sacred Imperial Majesty — which Your Lordship had signified to me by letter, while I still stayed at Wittenberg, before you went into Italy — and how, after I came hither into Bohemia, I was most kindly received into the Sacred Imperial Majesty's clientage. Wherefore I refrain from a longer recollection of this before Your Lordship, otherwise most occupied.

I will rather do this: that I earnestly ask Your Illustrious Lordship, with due reverence, that — in that benevolence with which you have so embraced me (who had deserved nothing such before), that you did not find it burdensome to commend me to the Sacred Imperial Majesty and promote my studies (which I trust you will also do of your own accord — for which too I give the greatest thanks I can, and will give as long as I live) — in that benevolence, I say, you would also persist hereafter, and assist me with help and counsel wherever there be need. For although I in no way doubt the Imperial Majesty's constancy (with which he is otherwise heroically endowed) in perseveringly fostering me — whose most clement favour I have proved by sure tokens — yet, since I am held here a stranger, removed from your Court by my manner of life and pursuit of studies no less than by the interval of place, I easily foresee how many things may occur which can be guarded against, and (as the matter requires) amended, far better by Your Illustrious Lordship — pre-eminent in prudence, experience, authority, and dignity — than by myself. For I cannot altogether promise myself that I shall be safer here than before in Denmark from the bites of the envious, and the slanders of those who will speak and think ill of the studies I pursue. For if there — where I had struck roots more deeply and firmly, and was surrounded and fortified by no small band of kinsmen established in chief dignity — yet this poison of sloth and envy could not be so removed but that it disdained these my studies, and tried to render void the labours of very many years (not everywhere [easily] met), or (what I rather chose) by its importunity drove me and my whole house to migrate into foreign regions, with great loss of property and greater loss of irrecoverable time: how much more, then, shall I now be exposed, here in foreign places, to this fortune common to all who strive to accomplish something arduous beyond the common run — supported by no power of an ancestral family — unless God Best and Greatest provide me with faithful, candid friends and patrons, like Your Lordship, who may opportunely know with what dexterity such adversaries (hidden or open, if any there be) are to be guarded against, or with what gentleness lulled, or with what spirit met. That Your Lordship would do this with as much readiness as you have ability to do it, and would not cease also to protect and promote this astronomical exercise which I undertake — transferred hither, to the honour of the Emperor and Empire, by Your Lordship among the first — this indeed I would ask of you more laboriously, did not your most excellent and spontaneous goodwill already clearly stand established to me. What I have omitted here for brevity (and what the pen would not easily sustain), Your Lordship, when at leisure, will hear by word of mouth from the bearer of these — the noble and learned young man Franz Tengnagel, my servant, lately returning to me from Italy, whom I think Your Illustrious Lordship doubtless already saw at Rome. Since he desires to set out to Your Lordship (of whom he is most respectful) his own conditions, and what he most wishes, for his sake I ask Your Lordship again and again, most dutifully, that you would likewise embrace him with your benevolence and promote him to every best thing. Whatever benefit you confer on him, I will perpetually acknowledge, with grateful mind, as though performed to me too.

May Your Illustrious Lordship fare as long and happily as possible, and interpret sincerely, after your candour, this my audacity and prolixity in writing.

Given from the imperial castle of Benátky, 8 March 1600.
Most devoted to Your Illustrious Lordship,
Tycho Brahe.

I send, through the aforesaid Tengnagel, two of my books, together with a certain Celestial Globe accurately worked up according to the restitution of the stars made by me. Which [gifts], for this time — until, the Divine Will permitting, I shall have brought more matters of this kind of study to their conclusion — I reverently beg Your Illustrious Lordship to take in good part.

Apparatus

  • Recipient. Rudolf Coraduz (Coraduz), Vice-Chancellor of the Empire — one of Tycho's principal court patrons (the Coraduz/Barvitius axis; cf. brahe-t_16000324001, where Coraduz gives the "no equal in astronomy" testimonial; brahe-t_16000813001).
  • Significance — a patronage letter, and Tycho's eloquent statement of an exile's vulnerability.
  • Thanks & the chain of patronage. Tycho rejoices at Coraduz's safe return from a great Italian legation (after a false rumour of his illness), and recalls that it was Coraduz who, by letter to Tycho at Wittenberg (before going to Italy), conveyed the Emperor's summons — which Tycho obeyed "last July," and was "received into the Imperial Majesty's clientage." (This fixes Coraduz as the channel of the imperial call.)
  • The request — continued protection against detractors. The heart of the letter is a candid, moving plea. Though he trusts the Emperor's "heroic constancy," Tycho — "held here a stranger," far from the Court "by my manner of life and pursuit of studies no less than by the interval of place" — fears he will be no safer "from the bites of the envious" than he was in Denmark. There, "where I had struck roots more deeply … fortified by no small band of kinsmen in chief dignity," the "poison of sloth and envy" still disdained his work and drove him into exile "with great loss of property and greater loss of irrecoverable time"; how much more exposed now, "supported by no power of an ancestral family," unless God grant him "faithful patrons like Your Lordship" to guard against, lull, or meet "adversaries hidden or open." A first-rate self-portrait of the imperial exile's precariousness (cf. the same theme in the Ursus letter, brahe-t_16000328001).
  • Coraduz's role. Tycho credits him as among the first to have "transferred hither" the astronomical work "to the honour of the Emperor and Empire."
  • Tengnagel's suit. The bearer, Franz Tengnagel (lately back from Italy, whom Coraduz had seen at Rome), wishes to lay before Coraduz his own conditions and wishes — i.e. to advance his own court career (he would marry Tycho's daughter Elisabeth in 1601); Tycho commends him warmly, "whatever benefit you confer on him, I will acknowledge as done to me."
  • Persons named. Rudolf Coraduz; Franz Tengnagel; Rudolf II. The "kinsmen in chief dignity" allude to the Brahe/Bille connection in the Danish Rigsraad.
  • Calendar. Dateline 8 Martij 1600; New Style (imperial). Header agrees with the label.
  • What this is. A paragraph preceding the letter's close describing an actual gift-parcel — two of Tycho's own printed books plus a celestial globe embodying his corrected star positions — being carried to the recipient by a named courier, Tengnagel. This is the kind of concrete presentation-copy evidence that anchors the reception history of Tycho's works.
  • Franz (Frans) Tengnagel. Franciscus Gansneb Tengnagel van Camp (1573–1636), Westphalian nobleman, pupil and assistant in Tycho's household at Prague, engaged at this date to marry Tycho's daughter Elisabeth Brahe (the wedding followed in 1601); later an imperial courtier who managed the Brahe heirs' long dispute over Tycho's observational legacy after his death [Christianson, On Tycho's Island (2000); Christianson et al., Tycho Brahe and Prague (2002); Dreyer, Tycho Brahe (1890)].
  • "Restitution of the stars." Restitutio stellarum is Tycho's own standing technical term for his systematically re-derived, corrected fixed-star catalogue — the basis on which contemporary globe-makers (see also the 1594 Aslakssøn letter) were building improved celestial globes; rendered here as "restitution" to match established English usage in the secondary literature (Gingerich, Thoren, Mosley).
  • The recipient, Rudolf Coraduz. Rudolf von Coraduz (Corraduc, Corraducius), who became imperial vice-chancellor after the death of Jakob Kurz von Senftenau in 1594, was one of Tycho's closest contacts within Rudolf II's inner circle at Prague alongside Secretary Johannes Barvitius [Christianson, On Tycho's Island (2000); Christianson, Tycho Brahe and the Measure of the Heavens (2020)] — explaining the deferential, gift-bearing tone of this letter, written from Benátky in the first months of Tycho's Bohemian exile.
