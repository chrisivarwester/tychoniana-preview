THADDÆUS HAGECIUS AD TYCHONEM BRAHE.

ACCEPI tandem, nobiliss. et doctiss. TYCHO, exoptatissimas tuas epistolas, et multis nominibus admodum gratas, non solum quod abs te, omnium amicissimo, amicissime scriptae, sed etiam quod rerum Astronomicarum pulcherrima dissertatione refertae essent. In quibus dum primordia initae inter nos amicitiae suavissima recordatione commemoras, in eo tuae naturae bonitatem prodis. Ne vero tuo quantulocunque silentio igniculi illi amoris nostri mutui viderentur aliquo modo consopiti, idcirco humanissima iucundissimaque ista commemoratione contractae amicitiae et multarum rerum utilium doctissima expositione prudenter de novo eos excitare voluisti. Quae omnia adeo mihi grata et accepta advenerunt, ut his gratiora, hoc praesertim tempore, mihi obtingere non potuerint, tantum abest, ut prolixitas earum ulla ex parte mihi molesta fuerit: imo vero erudita illa abs te suscepta disputatio tam ardens in me eius rei auxit desiderium, mirificamque voluptatem in animo meo excitavit, ut non semel atque iterum, sed toties totam illam disputationem relegere non piguerit. Pro qua summa sane tua illa erga me humanitate et tam suavi iucundoque ac utili per literas colloquio maximas quas possum tibi ago et habeo gratias. Porro quod ex meis quaternis, quas te accepisse scribis, nullae vel interierint vel resignatione atque lectione gravitatem suam amiserint, id quoque iucundissimum lectu mihi fuit. Toto enim praeterito anno admodum anxius eram animi, an illas cum libro VERNERI acceperis. Iam vero liberatus suspicione quadam, quasi pravo studio cuiusdam mei aemuli interceptae et suppressae fuissent, loco illius anxietatis longe maiorem sentio voluptatem. Quod autem tardius responderis, in mitiorem interpretor id partem, quomodo sanctissima amicitiae iura postulant, ita ut nulla opus sit hac in re apud me excusatione: qui, si quis alius, quantisnam occupationibus distineare, optime novi, neque causam iustam habeo, ut ex tardiore responsione de tuo in me amore quicquam addubitem. Sed et diuturniusculum, liceat ita tecum loqui, illud silentium abunde compensasti, non tantum bene longa epistola, rebus pulcherrimis utilissimisque referta, sed duplicis quoque scriptionis labore: nimirum ideo, ut si alteram contingeret perire, altera saltem conservaretur et sarta tectaque redderetur. Accidit autem, quod commodum utraque felici cursu mihi reddita est, sed posterius illa, quam ad me vir clariss. D. VINSHEMIUS misit. Qui tuus labor iteratus merito a me requirit, ut ego quoque, pro eo, siquidem studij et benevolentiae plenus est, iterum tibi agam gratias. Huc accedit, quemadmodum ipse merito quereris, quod desint nobis certi homines, quibus nostra tuto et cito perferenda demus. Rarissimi enim sunt, qui vel hinc isthuc, vel isthinc ad nos commeent. Quod si interdum aliqui sese offerunt, tum vel negocia, vel aliae causae, puto absentia ab urbe, aut valetudo adversa, aut ignotus hospes atque viator, facultatem scribendi abripiunt: quomodo anno superiori mihi contigit, cum opportunitate illa scribendi ad te per nobilem quendam vestratem, qui a Rege vestro falcones Imperatori nostro attulerat, minime uti potuerim. Quamobrem inter nos id semper firmum ratumque esto: si deinceps aliqua cessatio ob quamcunque causam ex utravis parte obvenerit, nihilominus nos simus inter nos, quales esse debemus, amicissimo videlicet animo et constantissima voluntate coniuncti.

Verum hisce sive excusationibus intermissi officij, sive constantis amicitiae ac benevolentiae protestationibus omissis, ad alias potius epistolae partes, in quibus de rebus ipsis mecum agis et tam liberaliter disputas, iam accedo. Quae ut me mirifice delectârunt, ita mihi, scribo ingenue, ut alienus ab ostentatorum simulata sui extenuatione, ruborem et pudorem haud exiguum incusserunt. Nam primum quod tam multa in meam laudem dixeris, quae ingenij mei tenuitas sustinere vix, aut ne vix quidem potest, id ego totum amori in me tuo tuaeque praestanti eximiaeve humanitati adscribo, simul etiam gaudeo, me abs te sic diligi, et tuam tamque praestantem ac egregiam erga me voluntatem permagni facio. Sed rursum, ut, memor illius, quod descendisse de coelo fertur, Nosce te ipsum, haud parum erubescam, res ipsa cogit, et pudorem mihi vel invito extorquet. Etenim quis ego sum, mi optatissime TYCHO, qua doctrina, quibus ornamentis praeditus, cui ab homine nobilissimo, doctissimo et optimo, tantum laudis tribuatur? Si quid olim in me, sive studij, sive diligentiae in disciplinis Mathematicis, in quibus tu ita excellis, ut inter artifices et restauratores merito summum locum obtineas, aut fuit, aut etiamnum est, in ista ingravescente mea aetate, id fuit, profecto, estque hoc tempore, tam perexiguum, ut infra mediocritatem vix consistere queat. Ut taceam, quod curae domesticae, medica professio, ingravescens, ut dixi, aetas (superavi enim iam DEI beneficio annum magnum climacterem) multa ex eis mihi excusserunt. Itaque licet adhuc libere interdum meam sententiam de his profero: tamen mihi nihil arrogo, sed velut homo veritatis amantissimus rectius sentientibus non gravatim subscribo: cuius rei te unicum, instar omnium locupletissimum testem adduco. Quamobrem gratissimum fuit auditu et admirabile visum, quod ad alia, sicut scribis, te ex parallaxibus ♂, quando fuit in opposito ☉, viciniorem terris ipso Sole deprehendisse; quod non nisi ex COPERNICI hypothesibus, vel ex tuis, nuper abs te excogitatis, salvari potuisse asseris: idque non temere, sed certis rationibus confirmatus. Ad quam rem etiamsi non intenderim animum, tamen tibi assentiri potius quam tuas exquisitas observationes in dubium vocare malo, et dico cum SOCRATE, quem, cum legeret librum HERACLITI, dixisse ferunt: Quae intelligo proba sunt, credo et quae non intellexi. Tuo autem illi plagiario ut patrociner, absit, sed tantum gaudeo, quod eius plagium calcaris loco tibi ad rem tam praeclaram et te solo tantoque artifice dignam sit factum. Quod de tuis hypothesibus varij varia tulerint, aut etiamnum ferant iudicia, nil mirum. Nova enim omnia facile reprensionem incurrunt apud eos, qui alienis oculis vident, alienis auribus audiunt, alienis ratiunculis, de veritate parum soliciti, ducuntur et idcirco ab assuetis aegre se divelli patiuntur, imo ab illis discedere piaculum iudicant. Ego tamen in ea sum sententia, de aliorum laboribus non esse temere ferendum iudicium, quia doctorum virorum, ut libera esse solent, ita vera esse debent, de rebus praesertim abstrusis iudicia, quemadmodum hac in parte Magnificus D. IACOBUS CURTIUS, ab arcanis consilijs Imperatoris et Procancellarius Imperij dignissimus, dominus et amicus meus singulariter observandus, et alij quidam prudenter et sapienter se gesserunt, qua de re ipse D. CURTIUS plenius ad te perscribet. Est enim, ut in rebus omnibus, ita in hisce Mathematicis studijs vir iudicio acri et perspicacissimo praeditus.

De NICOLAO WINKLERO non est cur respondeam, vel tecum agam pluribus. Valeat, et negligatur sane. Nihil enim ab illo sperari potest: de quo, quod ne quidem prima elementa Astronomiae didicerit, satis liquet: cuiusmodi fere omnes aut saltem maior pars eorum sunt, isti triobolares Calendariopaei, vates mendacissimi. An iste peculiare aliquod scriptum de Cometa anni 80 ediderit, nunquam intelligere potui. Recitavit saltem illas absurdas parallaxes in vanissimo suo Prognostico anni 82. Illum etiam alterum perturbatum, perplexum, obscurum et arrogantem, et suas nugas pro magna sapientia et exquisita doctrina nobis venditantem, missum faciamus. Per nos suis nugis intricatissimis se oblectet. Utinam saltem eius amicitia tecum non sit fucata, et non ferat secum quasdam benevolentiae praestigias.

Quod MÆSTHLINUM contra me defenderis, sed ego illam defensionem impugnaverim, non feci id, mihi crede, mi TYCHO, vel dissensionis studio, vel quod veritatem agnoscere noluerim, aut quod existimaverim, te eam defensionem alieniore erga me animo suscepisse, aut denique quod MÆSTHLINO studiose contradicere in animum induxerim: quem ego non solum ob eius praeclaram eruditionem atque doctrinam et in Mathematicis studijs solidam peritiam, mirificamque in illis diligentiam, verum etiam ob eius erga me ignotum insignem humanitatem et benevolentiam, adeoque candorem, quod me benigne et perhumaniter in suo illo de Cometa anni 77 scripto monuerit, caput 9 meae Dialexis esse emendandum, ex animo complector atque exosculor, ac pro tam prompto et propenso eius in me animo iam non tantum ut amico, sed ut homini doctissimo, nunc quoque habeo gratiam. Nullo, inquam, alieniore animo vel abs te, vel a MÆSTHLINO dissensi, sed quod placuit libere meam sententiam proferre: ita tamen ut eam meliori iudicio submitterem, quemadmodum tute nosti meum ingenium, quod neque sua pertinaciter tueri, neque viris bonis et ingenio florentibus eorum doctrinae et celebritati famae detrahere soleat. Itaque quod tu in meis non nulla corrigenda et reprehendenda tibi sumpseris, MÆSTHLINUS quoque in quibusdam amanter et modeste monuerit, hoc corrigendi et reprehendendi officium ab utroque vestrûm, quoniam ab ingenuo et benevolo animo profectum esse novi, non potuit non esse gratissimum. Sicut contra non potui non iniquissimo animo ferre atrocia illa convicia et maledicta, quae in me impurissime et impudentissime evomuerunt mastygae illi mei, puta ille impurus, infamis et flagitijs contaminatissimus RAIMUNDUS, et alter ei non absimilis conviciator, GRAMINÆUS Verpus, huiusque succenturiatus LINDANUS, arrogans et petulans Episcopus: qui omnes omissa re, de qua disputatio instituta fuerat, toti ad convicia et maledicta sese converterunt. Sed DEI beneficio omnibus a me ora occlusa sunt, RAIMUNDO quidem terna responsione, GRAMINÆO vero et LINDANO Episcopo manu scripto mei Aquilonis Historici Prodromo, utrique iam ante 9 fere annos transmisso. Quamvis autem cum solo GRAMINÆO fuit mihi suscepta disputatio, siquidem Episcopi illius nuspiam, ne unico verbulo, mentionem feceram, tantum abest, ut alias uspiam aliquam ei inimicitiarum causam praebuerim, aut litem intenderim: tamen cum ipse GRAMINÆO, quasi non satis sufficienti ad maledicendum, se adiunxisset, ut coniunctis viribus me unum omni genere criminationum adorirentur adobruerentque, etiam Episcopo illi seorsim respondi, et pro suo merito hominem conspersit alicubi non lustrali sane aqua, sed salibus, non tam nigro Momi, quam candido Mercurij sale tinctis: et quia me sua reverentia claudum sutorem appellare dignata fuit, ex eadem officina sutoria sutorijs formis et calopodio afflixi caput eius sine metu et periculo excommunicationis. Ubique tamen luculente responsum est, in ipso Aquilone Historico, qui iam multis annis teritur in multorum manibus in Imperio. Est enim tribus secularibus Electoribus inscriptus. Sed huc usque publicari non potuit, clam meo fucato quodam amico Typographum, qui iam initium operis fecerat, ab editione deterrente. Verum apage mihi istos maledicos conviciatores a nostris amicis et homine ingenuo dignis disceptationibus. Quamobrem ad has redeo.

Non, non, mi TYCHO, mihi molestum vel fuit, vel est, vel erit, si a me in aliquibus dissentias. Par esto libertas pro veritate inter nos dissentiendi, donec hanc liquido assequemur. Cum igitur tu quoque impugnando licet me nondum mihi videaris satisfacere, amice et candide meam adhuc tibi exponam sententiam. Dixeram in meis ad te, et hanc thesim constitueram: Cometarum motus, quomodo Planetarum, certa lege astringi non posse. Tu contra, etsi fateris quidem Cometas non ita Solem respicere, quemadmodum reliqui Planetae eum respiciunt, et propterea digressiones ab eo non ubique consimili lege exhibere, imo, ut ipse ais, neque Lunam eam legem observare: negas tamen ex eo inferri posse, impossibile esse, Cometarum motus ad aliquam normam per convenientem hypothesim redigi posse. Difficultatem itaque in ea re confiteris, impossibilitatem vero negas, propterea quod ex aliqua portione circuli de tota circumferentia ratiocinatio sumi possit. Est omnino, ut dicis, mi TYCHO, quod quae difficilia sunt, ea impossibilia non sunt, quemadmodum et Comicus quodam in loco testatur: nihil tam arduum vel difficile esse, quin quaerendo investigari possit. Et ARISTOT. 1 Rhetor. illud difficile esse dicit, quod cum labore aut multo tempore fit. At cum eorum quaedam fint, quae summe difficilia sunt, ea certe pro impossibilibus et habita sunt semper, et habentur etiam hodie, quae tanquam deplorata reijci consueverunt. Sed cum tu de summa illa difficultate non loquaris, sed tantum de ea, quae aliquanto labore superari possit, cuperem sane, eam difficultatem aliqua possibilitate tot Cometarum per multa secula apparitionibus evictam fuisse, et luculente evinci. Nam quae tu de illa impossibilitate scripsisse et dubium illud amovisse scribis, ea non impugno, sed aliorum quoque eruditorum de ijs deque toto hoc negocio iudicium expecto expetoque, ac plurium Cometarum observationibus confirmari cupio, quod ubi factum fuerit, libenter tibi arena cedam.

Atque haec fuit estque prima mea ratio. Altera est haec: Si omnium Cometarum motus, quotquot hucusque vel emerserunt, vel emersuri sunt, ad certam aliquam formam redigendi sunt, ut certa illorum scientia haberi possit, prius singulorum Cometarum habendam esse cognitionem, quomodo Planetarum et fixarum stellarum. Universalia enim ex singularibus colliguntur: quae singularia cum sint infinita, persequi non possunt, et si unum deest individuum, non constabit universale. Tertia ratio, quia Cometae nova sunt lumina, et numero differunt, non uno et eodem coeli loco emergunt, aliquando uni loco coeli affixa et quasi stationaria visa sunt, aliquando lento, aliquando concitatissimo motu eoque inordinato feruntur et quasi rapiuntur, rarissimeque perfectos describunt circulos. Dices, haec omnia fere omnibus competere Planetis, nihilominus eorum periodos cognitas esse. Ita est sane, dilecte TYCHO: sed cum Planetae sint semper ijdem, certisque legibus ferantur, eorum integrae periodi observari poterant. Cometae vero quotquot huc usque fulgere visi sunt, alij et alij fuerunt nec statis temporibus effulserunt, nec integros perfectosque descripserunt circulos, neque id, quod sciam, a quoquam memoriae proditum est. In tanta diversitate quid obsecro facies? Etiamsi vero alicuius Cometae motus fuisset accuratius observatus, sive eius integra periodus, quod tamen nemo prodidit, sive aliqua saltem portio circuli, ex qua, quomodo in stellis inerrantibus, de integra eius revolutione iudicium sumi possit: tamen quis concesserit, ut hinc de quorumvis Cometarum motu constituatur? In stellis inerrantibus, quas tu pro confirmatione tuae thesis meae thesi contrariae adducis, nimirum quamvis illae nullam periodum a condito mundo absolverint et a tempore observationum vix sextam circuli partem huc usque emensae sint, nihilominus certa quaedam ratio motus apparentis explorari potuit. Hoc quidem ita se habere lubens concedo. Sed vide dissimilitudinem. Stellae fixae sunt semper eaedem et perpetuae. Quo loco coeli olim deprehensae sunt temporibus HIPPARCHI, illae eaedem deinceps consideratae sunt successivis seculis a PTOLEMÆO, ALBATEGNIO, COPERNICO, et ex illarum progressu in certo tempore de tota periodo ratiocinatum, adeoque et in circulo anomaliae velocitas et tarditas motus illarum deprehensa est. At quid his simile apparet in Cometis? Cum eorum certitudinem nullam habeamus, nec semper ijdem sint, quomodo stellae, nec statis temporibus emergunt, varij et incerti sunt, nec, ut dixi, integros describunt circulos. Si igitur tuae rationi locus dandus est, existimaverim primo omnium docendum esse, omnes Cometas, quos huc usque conspeximus, fuisse unum et eundem Cometam. Dices iterum: Considero unum Cometam, qui, exempli gratia, decurrit spatio unius diei 5 vel 6 partes circuli, et hinc ratiocinor de tota eius periodo, licet eam non absolvat, quomodo in stellis fixis. Sic cum scio, Lunam diebus singulis 12 partes in uno die percurrere, scio quoque, in quot diebus totum emetietur circulum. Ita quoque de singulis Cometis iudicandum, dum apparent. Respondeo, hoc quidem in Luna evidens esse, quia non tantum diurnus eius motus, sed etiam tota eius periodus deprehendi potuit, et deprehensa est verissime. De Cometis etsi similiter ex motu eius diurno de tota periodo ratiocinari possumus, tamen id erit solum in ratiocinio, non in rei verilate, cum nunquam integrum emetiantur circulum. Deinde cum, ut supra dictum est, inaequalem et inordinatum servent motum, quomodo de integra periodo iuxta rei veritatem constitues? Urges adhuc, inquiens, minorem in Cometarum motu extricando difficultatem subesse, quam in stellis fixis, quia ob celerem eorum motum in uno et brevi tempore facilius ab uno homine illorum motus percipi potest, in affixis vero ut eorum motus cognoscatur, aliquot secula requiruntur. Respondeo, verum quidem esse, quod motus Cometarum facilius, quantum ad unum hominem et temporis brevitatem, deprehendi possunt, quam motus stellarum fixarum, qui et plures homines et multas annorum centurias requirit. Sed ad integram periodum Cometarum et stellarum fixarum cognoscendam et constituendam eadem utrobique apparet difficultas. In Cometis quidem, quia cito evanescunt et nunquam integrum emetiuntur circulum. In fixis vero, quia nunquam ad primas suas sedes revertentur. Imo vero dixerim ego: cum stellarum motus iam propemodum a bis mille annis nobis perspectus sit, Cometarum non item, facilius nos de fixarum periodo quam de Cometarum ratiocinari posse. Vides, mi TYCHO, siquidem ego non erro et recte iudico, nos non tantum difficultate, sed etiam quasi impossibilitate prohiberi, quo minus motus Cometarum certis adstringantur legibus, doctrinaque generalis de eorum motu tradatur. Sed cum tu scribas, te demonstrasse in illo Cometa anni 80, ipsum centri loco habuisse Solem, et circa ipsum instar ♀ et ☿ obambulasse, ego haec accuratius in alijs quoque Cometis consideranda quam temere reprehendenda aut in dubium vocanda esse censeo, et cum SOCRATE, ut supra dixi, credam, quae non intelligam. Itaque nihil amplius his addam, sed pleniorem de his omnibus abs te disputationem expectabo. Interim ut hasce meas amicas nugas boni consulas, rogo.

Tabulas Astronomicas longe aberrare a vero, compertum est multis, Sed quae causa sit, quod iam a mensibus aliquot motus Saturni et Iovis reformatarum tabularum ab Alphonsino calculo differat paulo minus tribus integris gradibus, tu nobis aliquando in tuis novis hypothesibus explicabis. Ego profecto tantam differentiam in duobus illis Planetis huc usque nunquam animadverti. De Epocha commutationis mediae Cometae anni 77 ad diem 24 Novembris dicis, te eam, quam MÆSTHLINUS posuit, accepisse. At ego ex ipsius dedomenis, subducto calculo, deprehendi eam 15' minorem, quae in exacta supputatione non esse negligenda existimavi, cum ex Epocha constituta totum aedificium exurgat: ac feci id, ut non tantum aedificium a MÆSTHLINO extructum considerarem, sed etiam eius fundamenta examinarem. Sed cum tu differentiam illam pro pusillo errore ducas, ego quoque de ea contendere nolo. Porro differentia illa, quae inter nos est in prostaphaeresi, cum asseras eam provenire ex acceptione diversorum terminorum, nimirum meam respicere 24 Novemb., tuam vero 26 Ianuarij, in tua hac solutione lubens acquiesco. Mihi nunc de ijs novas considerationes suscipere et omnia denuo ad calculum revocare aliae occupationes vetant.

MUNNOSIJ libellum de nova stella Hispanico idiomate, ab ipsomet olim mihi missum et donatum, habeo, qui tamen eum Latinum faciat, habeo neminem. Sed et mihi nulla consuetudo cum Hispanis. CORNELIUS FRANGIPANUS Italice scripsit, sed quae ad nostrum propositum faciant, plane nihil habet. Alios scriptores novae stellae nullos novi, quemadmodum in mea recognita Dialexi intelligere potuisti, alioqui eos quoque produxissem in medium. APIANI libellum de Cometa anni 31 mitto. Ubi illum evolveris, quod intra unum et alterum diem fieri poterit, remittes ad me per hunc tabellarium. Et cum intelligam, te non amplius egere TRAPEZUNTIJ commentarijs, eos quoque ad me remittes. De bibliotheca WITYCHIJ egi cum quodam meo amico. Sed is nunc legatione fungitur in Poloniam. Ubi redierit, agam cum eo diligentius, teque certiorem faciam. IOHANNIS PÆNAe liber nunquam mihi contigit.

Quod ad Apologeticam responsionem tuam pertinet, hanc quoque perlegi diligentissime, eaque sane mirifice sum delectatus. Tam solide enim et tam luculente respondes, et obiectiones CRAIGIJ diluis, ut mirum sane videri possit, quid contra tam evidentia vel hiscere CRAIGIUS aut quivis alius possit. Quod si CRAIGIUS perget veritati obstrepere, in longe maiorem incurret reprehensionem apud veritatis amantes, quam quod ego in Nova Stella parum ex arte suo iudicio praestiterim. Sed sit ita, ut ille iudicat: ego certe illud parum multo gloriosius mihi esse duco, quam si manifeste veritati repugnarem. Thaddaeus Hagecius to Tycho Brahe.

I have at last received, most noble and most learned Tycho, your most longed-for letter — most welcome on many counts, not only because it was written most affectionately by you, the most affectionate of all [my friends], but also because it was crammed with the most beautiful disquisition on Astronomical matters. In which, while you recall, with sweetest recollection, the beginnings of the friendship entered into between us, you betray therein the goodness of your nature. And lest, by your silence (however slight), those little fires of our mutual love should seem in any way to have been lulled to sleep, you therefore wished prudently to rekindle them anew by that most kind and most delightful recollection of our contracted friendship, and by a most learned exposition of many useful matters. All of which came to me so welcome and so acceptable that nothing more welcome could have befallen me — at this time especially; so far is it [from being the case] that their prolixity was in any part troublesome to me. Rather, that erudite disputation undertaken by you so kindled in me a desire for that subject, and aroused so wondrous a pleasure in my mind, that it irked me not — once and again, nay so many times over — to read through that whole disputation. For which truly supreme kindness of yours toward me, and for so sweet and delightful and useful a conversation by letter, I give and hold you the greatest thanks I can. Furthermore, that none of my gatherings [quaterni], which you write you have received, either perished or lost their weight by being unsealed and read, was also a most delightful thing for me to read. For throughout the whole past year I was very anxious in mind whether you had received them along with Werner's book. But now, freed from a certain suspicion — as though they had been intercepted and suppressed by the depraved zeal of some rival of mine — in place of that anxiety I feel a far greater pleasure. And that you replied rather tardily, I interpret in the milder sense, as the most sacred laws of friendship demand — so that there is no need in this matter of any excuse with me, who, if anyone, know best by what great occupations you are held; nor have I just cause to doubt anything of your love toward me on account of a rather tardy reply. But that somewhat long silence (if I may so speak with you) you have abundantly compensated, not only by a good long letter, crammed with the most beautiful and useful matters, but also by the labour of a double writing — namely so that, if it should happen that one perished, the other at least might be preserved and rendered safe and sound. And it happened, conveniently, that both were delivered to me by a happy course; but the later one [reached me] which the most distinguished man, Master Vinshemius, sent to me. Which redoubled labour of yours rightly requires of me that I too, in turn (since it is full of zeal and benevolence), should again give you thanks. To this is added — as you yourself rightly complain — that we lack reliable men, to whom we may give our [letters] to be carried safely and swiftly. For very rare are those who travel either from here to there, or from there to us. And if sometimes some present themselves, then either business, or other causes — absence from the city, I think, or ill health, or an unknown host and traveller — snatch away the opportunity of writing: as happened to me last year, when I was quite unable to use that opportunity of writing to you through a certain nobleman of your country, who had brought falcons from your King to our Emperor. Wherefore let this always be firm and ratified between us: if hereafter any interruption, for whatever cause, should occur on either side, nonetheless let us be between ourselves what we ought to be — joined, that is, in most affectionate mind and most constant will.

But, these protestations being omitted — whether of excuses for an interrupted duty, or of constant friendship and benevolence — I now proceed rather to the other parts of your letter, in which you deal with me about the matters themselves and dispute so liberally. Which, as they delighted me wondrously, so — I write frankly, being a stranger to the feigned self-deprecation of show-offs — they struck no small blush and shame into me. For first, that you said so many things in my praise, which the slenderness of my talent can scarcely, or not even scarcely, sustain — all this I ascribe wholly to your love toward me and to your outstanding and exceptional kindness; and at the same time I rejoice that I am so esteemed by you, and I value most highly your so outstanding and excellent goodwill toward me. But again — that, mindful of that [saying] which is reported to have descended from heaven, "Know thyself," I should blush not a little — the matter itself compels, and wrings shame from me even against my will. For who am I, my most longed-for Tycho — with what learning, with what ornaments endowed — that so much praise should be bestowed [on me] by a man most noble, most learned, and best? If ever there was in me, or even now is — in this my failing age — any zeal or diligence in the Mathematical disciplines (in which you so excel that you deservedly hold the highest place among the artificers and restorers), it was, indeed, and is at this time, so very slight that it can scarcely stand below mediocrity. To say nothing of how domestic cares, the medical profession, [and] my failing age (as I said) — for I have now, by God's kindness, passed the grand climacteric year — have shaken many of those things out of me. And so, although I still sometimes freely offer my opinion about these matters, yet I arrogate nothing to myself, but, like a man most loving of truth, I subscribe without reluctance to those who judge more rightly — of which thing I bring you forward as a single witness, [yet] as rich as all [witnesses together]. Wherefore it was most welcome to hear, and seemed admirable, that — as you write — you have, among other things, discovered from the parallaxes of Mars [♂], when it was in opposition to the Sun [☉], that it is nearer to the Earth than the Sun itself; which you assert could be saved only from Copernicus's hypotheses, or from your own, recently devised by you — and that not rashly, but confirmed by sure reasons. To which matter, even if I have not bent my mind, yet I prefer to assent to you rather than to call your exquisite observations into doubt; and I say with Socrates — who, when he was reading the book of Heraclitus, is reported to have said: "What I understand is sound; I believe also what I have not understood." But far be it from me to plead for that plagiarist of yours; I only rejoice that his plagiarism has served you, in place of a spur, toward a matter so splendid and worthy of you alone, [and of] so great an artificer. That various men have brought, or even now bring, various judgments about your hypotheses — no wonder. For all new things easily incur censure among those who see with others' eyes, hear with others' ears, are led by others' little reasonings (little caring about the truth), and therefore suffer themselves to be torn from their accustomed [notions] with difficulty — nay, judge it a sacrilege to depart from them. I, however, am of this opinion: that judgment about others' labours must not be rashly passed, because the judgments of learned men, as they are wont to be free, so they ought to be true — especially about abstruse matters — just as in this respect the Magnificent Master Jacob Curtius [Jakob Kurz von Senftenau], most worthy privy Councillor of the Emperor and Vice-Chancellor of the Empire, my lord and singularly-to-be-esteemed friend, and certain others have conducted themselves prudently and wisely — about which matter Master Curtius himself will write to you more fully. For he is, as in all things, so in these Mathematical studies, a man endowed with keen and most perspicacious judgment.

About Nicolaus Winkler there is no reason why I should reply, or deal with you at greater length. Let him fare well, and be quite neglected. For nothing can be hoped from him: of whom it is clear enough that he has not even learned the first elements of Astronomy — of which kind are almost all, or at least the greater part, of them, those threepenny calendar-mongers, most lying soothsayers. Whether he has published any particular writing about the comet of [15]80, I have never been able to learn. He at least recited those absurd parallaxes in his most empty Prognostication of [15]82. That other [man] too — disordered, perplexed, obscure and arrogant, and peddling his trifles to us as great wisdom and exquisite learning — let us dismiss. Let him delight himself, for all we care, with his most intricate trifles. Would only that his friendship with you be not painted-over, and that it carry with it no sleights of benevolence.

That you defended Maestlin against me, but I attacked that defence — believe me, my Tycho, I did it neither from a zeal for dissent, nor because I was unwilling to acknowledge the truth, nor because I supposed that you had undertaken that defence with a mind rather estranged from me, nor, finally, because I had resolved to contradict Maestlin studiously: whom I embrace and cherish from my heart — not only for his splendid erudition and learning and solid skill in Mathematical studies, and his wondrous diligence in them, but also for the remarkable kindness and benevolence shown toward me (a stranger), and indeed [for] the candour with which he kindly and most humanely advised me, in that writing of his on the comet of [15]77, that chapter 9 of my Dialexis [is] to be corrected — and for so ready and well-disposed a mind toward me, I now hold him grateful thanks, not only as a friend but as a most learned man. With no estranged mind, I say, did I dissent either from you or from Maestlin, but because it pleased [me] to offer my opinion freely — yet in such a way that I submitted it to better judgment, as you yourself know my disposition, which is wont neither to maintain its own [views] obstinately, nor to detract from the learning and celebrity of fame of good men flourishing in talent. And so, that you took upon yourself to correct and reprehend some things in mine, [and] that Maestlin too, in certain [things], advised lovingly and modestly — this duty of correcting and reprehending, since I know it proceeded from an ingenuous and benevolent mind, could not but be most welcome from each of you. Just as, on the contrary, I could not but bear with a most resentful mind those atrocious revilings and slanders which those whips of mine spewed out against me most foully and most shamelessly — namely that impure, infamous, and most-contaminated-with-disgraces Raimundus [Annibale Raimondo], and another reviler not unlike him, Graminaeus the Verpus [a coarse insult], and his reserve-man Lindanus, an arrogant and petulant Bishop: all of whom, the matter being dropped about which the disputation had been instituted, turned themselves wholly to revilings and slanders. But by God's kindness their mouths have all been stopped by me — Raimundus, indeed, by a threefold response; but Graminaeus and Bishop Lindanus by the manuscript Prodromus of my Historical Aquilo, transmitted to each of them now nearly 9 years ago. And although the disputation undertaken by me was with Graminaeus alone — since I had nowhere, not by a single little word, made mention of that Bishop, so far was I from having anywhere given him any cause of enmities, or having brought a suit — yet, since that [Bishop] himself had joined himself to Graminaeus (as if [the latter were] not sufficient for reviling), so that with combined forces they might assail and overwhelm me alone with every kind of accusation, I replied to that Bishop too separately, and besprinkled the man, according to his desert, here and there not, indeed, with lustral water, but with salts dyed not so much with the black salt of Momus [the carping god] as with the white salt of Mercury [wit]: and because his Reverence deigned to call me a lame cobbler, from the same cobbler's workshop, with cobbler's lasts and a shoemaker's foot-block, I struck his head, without fear and danger of excommunication. Everywhere, however, an ample reply has been made, in the Historical Aquilo itself, which has now for many years been thumbed in many hands in the Empire. For it is inscribed [dedicated] to three secular Electors. But hitherto it could not be published, a certain painted-over [false] friend of mine secretly deterring the Printer (who had already made a beginning of the work) from the edition. But away with those slanderous revilers from our friendly disputations, worthy of an ingenuous man! Wherefore I return to these [matters].

No, no, my Tycho, it neither was, nor is, nor will be troublesome to me, if you dissent from me in some things. Let there be equal liberty, for truth's sake, of dissenting between us, until we plainly attain it. Since therefore you too, by attacking [me], do not yet seem to me to satisfy me, I shall still expound my opinion to you, in friendly and candid fashion. I had said in mine to you, and had established this thesis: that the motions of comets cannot, like those of the Planets, be bound by a fixed law. You, on the contrary — although you do indeed confess that comets do not regard the Sun in the way the other Planets regard it, and that therefore they do not everywhere exhibit their digressions from it by a like law (nay, as you yourself say, that not even the Moon observes that law) — yet you deny that it can be inferred from this that it is impossible for the motions of comets to be reduced to some norm by a suitable hypothesis. The difficulty in that matter you therefore confess, but the impossibility you deny — because from some portion of a circle a reasoning can be taken about the whole circumference. It is altogether [true], as you say, my Tycho, that things which are difficult are not [therefore] impossible — as the Comic [poet] too testifies in a certain place: that nothing is so arduous or difficult but that it can be tracked down by seeking. And Aristotle, in Rhetoric I, says that to be difficult which is done with labour or much time. But since there are certain of those [things] which are supremely difficult, these have certainly always been held, and are held even today, as impossible — which men are wont to reject as hopeless. But since you speak not of that supreme difficulty, but only of that which can be overcome by some labour, I should truly wish that that difficulty had been overcome, by some possibility, through the apparitions of so many comets across many centuries, and [that this] be amply proven. For the things which you write you have written about that impossibility, and [whereby you] have removed that doubt — these I do not attack; but I await and seek the judgment of other learned men too about them and about this whole business, and I desire [it] to be confirmed by the observations of more comets — which, when it shall have been done, I will gladly yield you the field.

And this was, and is, my first reason. The second is this: if the motions of all comets — as many as have hitherto either emerged or are to emerge — are to be reduced to some fixed form, so that a sure science of them can be had, [then] the knowledge of the individual comets must first be had, as [it is] of the Planets and the fixed stars. For universals are gathered from particulars: which particulars, since they are infinite, cannot be pursued; and if one individual is lacking, the universal will not stand. The third reason: because comets are new lights, and differ in number; they emerge not in one and the same place of the heaven; sometimes they have been seen affixed to one place of the heaven and as it were stationary; sometimes they are borne — and as it were snatched along — with a slow, sometimes a most rapid and (at that) disordered motion; and most rarely do they describe perfect circles. You will say: all these belong to almost all the Planets, and nonetheless their periods are known. So it is indeed, beloved Tycho: but since the Planets are always the same, and are borne by fixed laws, their whole periods could be observed. But the comets, as many as have hitherto been seen to blaze, were [each] different and different; nor did they blaze at fixed times, nor did they describe whole and perfect circles — nor (so far as I know) has this been handed down to memory by anyone. In so great a diversity, what, I beseech, will you do? But even if the motion of some comet had been observed more accurately — whether its whole period (which, however, no one has reported), or at least some portion of a circle, from which (as in the fixed stars) a judgment about its whole revolution could be taken — yet who would grant that from this [the motion] of any comets whatever be determined? In the fixed stars, which you adduce for the confirmation of your thesis (contrary to my thesis) — namely, although they have completed no period since the founding of the world, and since the time of observations have measured scarcely a sixth part of a circle down to now — nonetheless a certain sure reckoning of the apparent motion could be explored. This indeed I gladly concede to be so. But see the dissimilarity. The fixed stars are always the same and perpetual. In what place of the heaven they were once ascertained in the times of Hipparchus, those same [stars] were thereafter considered, through successive centuries, by Ptolemy, Albategnius [al-Battānī], [and] Copernicus; and from their progress in a certain time it was reasoned out about the whole period, and so too the swiftness and slowness of their motion in the circle of anomaly was ascertained. But what like these appears in comets? Since we have no certainty of them, nor are they always the same (as the stars [are]), nor do they emerge at fixed times, [but] are various and uncertain, nor (as I said) do they describe whole circles. If therefore place is to be given to your reasoning, I should judge that it must first of all be taught that all the comets which we have hitherto beheld were one and the same comet. You will say again: I consider one comet, which, for example, runs through 5 or 6 parts of a circle in the space of one day, and hence I reason about its whole period, although it does not complete it — as in the fixed stars. So, since I know that the Moon traverses 12 parts in a single day, I know also in how many days it will measure out the whole circle. So too one must judge about individual comets, while they appear. I reply: this is indeed evident in the Moon, because not only its diurnal motion, but also its whole period, could be — and most truly has been — ascertained. About comets, although we can likewise reason about the whole period from its diurnal motion, yet that will be only in reasoning, not in the truth of the matter, since they never measure out a whole circle. Then, since (as was said above) they keep an unequal and disordered motion, how will you determine [anything] about the whole period according to the truth of the matter? You press still further, saying that there is less difficulty in disentangling the motion of comets than [that] of the fixed stars, because, on account of their swift motion, their motions can be perceived more easily, in one and a brief time, by one man — whereas in the affixed [stars], that their motion may be known, several centuries are required. I reply: it is indeed true that the motions of comets can be ascertained more easily — as regards one man and the brevity of time — than the motions of the fixed stars, which require both several men and many centuries of years. But for knowing and establishing the whole period of comets and of the fixed stars, the same difficulty appears on both sides: in the comets, because they vanish quickly and never measure out a whole circle; in the fixed [stars], because they will never return to their first seats. Nay, I should even say: since the motion of the stars has now been perceived by us for nearly two thousand years, [but] that of comets not likewise, we can more easily reason about the period of the fixed [stars] than about [that] of comets. You see, my Tycho — if I do not err and judge rightly — that we are prevented, not only by difficulty but as it were by impossibility, from binding the motions of comets by fixed laws, and from a general doctrine about their motion being handed down. But since you write that you have demonstrated, in that comet of [15]80, that it had the Sun in the place of [its] centre, and that it circled about it after the manner of Venus [♀] and Mercury [☿] — I judge that these things are to be considered more accurately in other comets too, rather than rashly reprehended or called into doubt; and with Socrates, as I said above, I will believe what I do not understand. And so I shall add nothing further to these [matters], but shall await a fuller disputation about all of them from you. Meanwhile, I beg, take these my friendly trifles in good part.

That the Astronomical Tables stray far from the truth has been found by many. But what the cause may be that for some months now the motion of Saturn and Jupiter, [according to] the reformed tables, differs from the Alphonsine calculation by a little less than three whole degrees — you will some time explain to us in your new hypotheses. I, certainly, have never hitherto noticed so great a difference in those two Planets. About the Epoch of the mean commutation of the comet of [15]77 at the day 24 November, you say that you took the one which Maestlin posited. But I, from his own data [δεδομένα], the calculation having been worked, found it 15′ smaller — which I judged not to be neglected in an exact computation, since from the established Epoch the whole edifice rises up; and I did this so that I might consider not only the edifice built by Maestlin, but also examine its foundations. But since you regard that difference as a tiny error, I too am unwilling to contend about it. Furthermore, that difference which is between us in the prosthaphaeresis — since you assert that it arises from the adoption of different terms, namely [that] mine regards 24 November, but yours 26 January — in this your solution I gladly acquiesce. From taking up new considerations about these [matters] now, and recalling everything anew to calculation, other occupations forbid me.

The little book of Muñoz [Jerónimo Muñoz] on the new star, in the Spanish tongue, once sent and given to me by [the author] himself, I have; but one to make it Latin I have none. And besides, I have no familiarity with Spaniards. Cornelius Frangipanus [Cornelio Frangipane] wrote in Italian, but he has plainly nothing that makes for our purpose. Other writers on the new star I know none — as you could understand in my revised Dialexis; otherwise I would have brought them too into the open. I send Apian's little book on the comet of [15]31. When you have unrolled it (which can be done within a day or two), you will send it back to me by this courier. And since I understand that you no longer need the commentaries of Trapezuntius [George of Trebizond], you will send those back to me too. About Wittich's library I dealt with a certain friend of mine. But he is now discharging a legation into Poland. When he has returned, I shall deal with him more diligently, and inform you. The book of Johannes Pena never came my way.

As for your Apologetic response, I have read this too most diligently, and I was wondrously delighted with it. For you reply so solidly and so amply, and dissolve the objections of Craig, that it can truly seem a wonder what Craig — or anyone else — could even mutter against things so evident. And if Craig shall persist in clamouring against the truth, he will incur a far greater reproach among the lovers of truth than [I incurred], in [the matter of] the New Star, by having performed too little, in his judgment, "by [the rules of] art." But be it as he judges: I, certainly, hold that "too little" to be far more glorious to me than if I manifestly fought against the truth. I was the first — ignorant of what others had thought — who brought forward, with free voice and writing, that the New Star was not a Comet, nor in the elementary region, but in the aether: though I thought and brought forward that it can happen that comets arise in either region — namely the elementary and the aethereal — about which the later age will judge more rightly. I was also the first to shake the opinion of Aristotle about comets, which Craig retains tooth-and-nail, and fights for it as if for altar and hearth. They are, I confess, mine — the things I then brought forward — very slight, and, as to the doctrine of parallaxes, very many things are wanting in them. But these too I afterwards somewhat revised. You, however, have put the finishing hand to them. Wherefore I gladly yield you the field. To me, meanwhile, it will suffice — among the good and approved, for earning pardon (I do not say favour) — that, in a business assuredly difficult (which no one ever, so far as I know, dared to attempt, nor to depart from Aristotle's opinion, from fear of detractors and the envious, whom no good and learned man, more zealous for truth than for friendship, ever satisfied), I strove to labour to the best of my power, and was the first (as I said) in this corner to cut this ice, and to impress new tracks on an unknown road. But would that Craig had been able to overthrow those slight [contributions] of mine at least by a shadow of truth — I should have held him grateful. But since he could not, nor ever will be able, it is truly no wonder that he has not shaken your most firm and most solid [arguments] even in the least part. This one thing I see him to have done: that, the truth set aside, he might preserve and establish the opinion of Aristotle safe and sound. But the truth stands for us — though still deserted and poor, and not propped by any splendid patronage of the old or recent Philosophers; yet it will hereafter cast forth its rays more clearly and shine, however much it be now overshadowed by certain frivolous objections and sophistical little reasonings. Surely, if anyone shall bring forward better [things] than ours — which we have brought forward whether about the New Star or about Comets, and [which] have been brought forward far more solidly by you — we shall gladly embrace [them]. But he who neither brings better, nor solidly refutes another's, but only contradicts from a zeal for wrangling — that is the judgment of a malevolent and envious man. I marvel why Craig mixes my New Star with my Comet, since I myself frankly confessed that the certainty of my observations of the Comet cannot vie with the certainty of the observations of the New Star, and that I had neither those instruments at hand, nor those conveniences for observing the Comet, which I had in [the matter of] the New Star. You know too that I myself, both in the New Star and in the Comet — the truth being acknowledged — partly corrected certain things of my own accord, partly emended some, amicably advised about certain [things] by you and by Praetorius [Johannes Praetorius] and others. I marvel the more that Craig ascribes that change of opinion for the better — as if [it were] a reproach — to uncertainty and inconstancy, [a change] which ought to have been ascribed to virtue and ingenuousness. For it is not shameful to correct an error when the truth is acknowledged. But to defend an error against the truth, to please a man, is indeed most shameful. As if experience itself did not testify that earlier days are wont to be the disciples of later ones! But you see what those men do not dare, who have sworn upon the words of their master. He also accuses of absurdity all the Mathematicians who have asserted comets to be aethereal — not noticing that, while he tries to hold and defend Aristotle's opinion tooth-and-nail, and cannot overthrow ours, he himself becomes entangled in far greater absurdities, as is most learnedly proven by you. What [of this], that with great audacity, and indeed falsely, he accuses the splendid artificer Regiomontanus — [claiming] that hitherto it has been demonstrated neither by him nor by any of the Mathematicians how the distance of comets from the Earth is to be defined, since, namely, they advance with their own [proper] step, and that therefore Regiomontanus's demonstration goes off into absurdity? But, best Tycho, Craig in this matter is most shamefully deceived, and does great injury to the excellent artificer Regiomontanus, who, in that splendid little book of his on the Comet, in problems 2, 3, [and] 4, expressly advises that, in the investigation of parallax, account must be taken of the comet's own [proper] motion — as the reckoning of its motion was also had by you. Wherefore, while Craig charges these [things] upon all Mathematicians, he does no light injury to you too. I, indeed, freely confess that in that comet, and in the other one too, I knowingly and willingly neglected its proper motion. But lest from that neglect a notable and by-no-means-tolerable error should arise, I instituted my observations with a tiny interval of time interposed, in which no very perceptible variation of the comet's proper motion would occur; then I judged it enough to stand in the mean, and at least to take aim nearer to the truth than has hitherto been done by others — as Regiomontanus splendidly said: that we do not empty out the treasures of nature, but that, in most knowable things, it is granted to mortals to taste [something] near to the truth itself — as if he were saying that it is enough to subsist in that degree which does not utterly overthrow the truth. These [things] I wished to say in defence of Regiomontanus, and so of the truth. Nor yet do I deny but that some better reckoning can be found, whether by you or by others. Meanwhile, that the certainty of Regiomontanus's demonstrations will remain firm, I freely pronounce — [a certainty] which neither Craig, nor anyone else, will be able to convict of any absurdity. Regiomontanus assuredly scented something else about comets than what the schools publicly taught from Aristotle. But, on account of his [Aristotle's] authority, he dared not depart from the received opinion: who, if he now lived and had seen the New Star, would doubtless have blushed, and would have applied more accurate considerations of comets — nor is there any doubt with me but that, the truth being put first, he would have subscribed to our opinion. But why [do] I [say] these things to you, who have handled all these matters far more solidly, and have so dissolved all of Craig's reasonings and objections, that I do not see what more could be added to them for asserting the truth? Nothing else remains, except that, in accordance with your kindness and candour, you take these [things] of mine in fair and good part, and grant pardon to those [things] which have been either omitted by me, or less rightly understood.

Farewell, my sweetest Tycho, and see that you pursue me with your accustomed love.
Given at Prague on 11 June, New Style, which day was Whit Monday [the second feria of Pentecost], in the Year 1590.
Devoted to you from the heart,
Thaddaeus ab Hayck [Tadeáš Hájek of Hájek].

POSTSCRIPT.

I had already finished my letter to you when, behold, on the twenty-second of May, Old Style, the Nuremberg courier brought me yours, together with the description of the recent comet and the printed sheets of the Astronomical Letters, which I at once read through and studied most eagerly; and so that Lord Curtius might have, as it were, a spur to hasten that privilege and to dispatch a courier to you as soon as possible, I gave him all of it to read and peruse. And indeed that untiring zeal of yours for illustrating the science of astronomy pleased him wonderfully — he is, so help me God, so inflamed with love for you that he said nothing could happen to him more welcome and desired than if he could, for at least a few days, converse and confer with you; and certainly, were public business to permit it, he would hasten to greet you and embrace you as a friend, and I would join myself to him as his companion. That plan of yours, I say — indeed, already the carrying-out of that plan in the publishing of those letters — pleased him greatly, for many splendid things are drawn out in them by learned men, and truth is unearthed by disputation. All honour to that spirit of yours, which few men possess, and to your character, my Tycho; go forward with a fortunate step, wherever your virtue calls you.

I had sent you a very brief letter on the twenty-second of April, Old Style, by a certain student who said he was bound for Rostock. I enclosed within it your letter to Lord Chytraeus, and asked that he see mine carried on to you, which I do not doubt in the least was done, provided that student reached there. Mine contained nothing else but that, through a special courier — whom Lord Curtius himself said he would soon dispatch to you — we should write to you most fully about everything. I also asked, in that same letter, if it could be arranged, that you have made for me there, since you have craftsmen at hand, a model or pattern of your Sextant and Armillary [Sphere], and send it to me, so that afterward, on its pattern, I might have those same instruments made here at full size. When I mentioned this to Lord Curtius, he replied that it would be much more convenient if those instruments were made there, at the same size as your own, and sent here either by courier or by carter, and that he would most gladly defray all the costs. "But," he said, "a certain shame forbids me from asking this of Lord Tycho myself." I, however, earnestly beg you, if it can be done, to have those instruments made there — see to it that Lord Curtius has them, and oblige him in this. He will most generously pay all the expenses. I shall then have similar ones made for myself after their model. And if this comes about, we too shall be using the same instruments as you, and in our observations we shall be able to render each other mutual service.

That comet it fell to me to see only twice: first on the twenty-fifth of February, Old Style. On that day it stood between the first and second star of Triangulum, and appeared to lie in a straight line with the first and second stars in the horns of Aries and the first of Triangulum. It put out a tail, very thin and scarcely perceptible, like a rod or shaft, behind the second [star] of Triangulum, and pointed toward the left foot of Andromeda. I caught sight of it again on some other day, but not well enough, on account of intervening clouds. So, having taken up my Radius, I made ready to measure its distances from the neighbouring stars — but behold, clouds hid it from me, and afterward, on other days, I could not see it at all. I have, then, nothing further to write about it, nor have I read anything written about it by anyone, nor heard that it was observed by anyone.

I have several of your letters to me, but among them three deal with astronomical matters between us. I therefore send you their originals. In the letter of the year [15]80, as you will see, I have struck out a few things which I judged should either be omitted or altered. In my own [letters] to you, too, certain things will have to be removed — especially those recounting Scultetus's ill-will conceived against me — and the like, all of which I leave to your good judgement to remove or change. You will also correct, in those same letters of mine to you, whether written in my own hand or another's, certain grammatical faults committed through carelessness. But behold, while searching among my papers for something else, I find yet another letter of yours, by now almost torn to pieces, in which you deal with me about my observations — about the distance of the eleventh [star] in Cassiopeia from the pole star, about the meridian altitude of Spica, and about other matters. I have had that letter copied out. Do with it, as with the others, as you think best.

Toward the end of your letter, where you dealt with me about a more splendid privilege, you added that you were sending me a copy of the French Privilege. But know that none was enclosed with your letter. I easily persuade myself that, on account of your other most weighty occupations, you had forgotten it. Had we had a copy of it, it would indeed have been most welcome to us; we would have taken care that the Imperial one should not turn out humbler or less splendid than it. Nevertheless, even without having seen that French privilege, we reckon that, through the zeal and industry of Lord Curtius, and with due regard for your merit and learning — and indeed for your untiring labours in restoring and illustrating Astronomy — the Imperial one has been so drawn up that it cannot but be exceedingly welcome to you. Prague, the second feria of Pentecost [Whit Monday].

  • Recipient & occasion. Tycho, at Uraniborg. The letter answers a lost (or separately-transmitted) Tycho letter of c.1589 that had: (i) reported the Mars-parallax result and the Tychonic hypotheses; (ii) defended Maestlin against Hájek; (iii) argued (against Hájek's thesis) that comet-motions can be reduced to law; (iv) discussed the 1577-comet epoch and the prosthaphaeresis discrepancy; and (v) enclosed Tycho's completed Apologetica responsio to Craig.   • The Mars-parallax notice (historically the weightiest line). "te ex parallaxibus ♂, quando fuit in opposito ☉, viciniorem terris ipso Sole deprehendisse; quod non nisi ex COPERNICI hypothesibus, vel ex tuis … salvari potuisse" — Tycho's finding that Mars at opposition is nearer the Earth than the Sun, "saveable" only on the Copernican or his own (geoheliocentric) hypotheses. This is among the earliest correspondence-references to the result that grounded the Tychonic world-system (the Mars and Sun orbits intersecting); cf. the De mundi arguments in Opera vol. IV. Note the controlled term salvari = to save (the appearances), kept by this chapter's convention.
  • "That plagiarist of yours" (illi plagiario tuo). Nicolaus Reimers Ursus (Bär), whose Fundamentum astronomicum (Strasbourg 1588) published a geoheliocentric scheme Tycho held stolen from him during Ursus's 1584 visit to Hven. Hájek pointedly refuses to plead for him, while noting the "plagiarism" spurred Tycho to perfect his own system — the opening of the long Ursus priority-and-libel war that runs through the whole later correspondence (cf. the Kepler invitation 15991209001 and the Herwart letter 16000828002).
  • The comet-motion controversy (the scientific spine). The core exchange: Hájek's thesis that comet-motions cannot be bound by a fixed law as the planets are, against Tycho's counter that difficulty ≠ impossibility (a portion of a circle suffices to reason about the whole, as for the slow-moving fixed stars). Hájek defends his thesis with three reasons — (1) the difficulty is so great as to have always been reckoned impossible, and he wants it shown surmountable by many comets' apparitions across centuries; (2) a universal can only be gathered from all particulars, and comets (unlike the ever-same planets and fixed stars) are uncountable and non-recurring; (3) comets are new lights, differing in number, place, and speed, "most rarely describing perfect circles." He rebuts Tycho's planet- and Moon-analogies (the Moon's 12°/day and full period are both knowable; a comet's period is knowable "only in reasoning, not in the truth of the matter," since it never completes a circle), and his fixed-star analogy (Hipparchus → Ptolemy → al-Battānī → Copernicus traced the stars precisely because they are always the same and traverse a measurable "sixth part of a circle"). His verdict: comets are barred "not only by difficulty but as it were by impossibility." Yet on Tycho's demonstration that the comet of 1580 had the Sun at its centre, circling like Venus and Mercury, he defers — "with Socrates I believe what I do not understand." This is the geoheliocentric cometary orbit Tycho was developing (cf. Opera IV, the editor's note to p. 162 that "Tycho had begun to think seriously of such a hypothesis").
  • The technical sidebar. The Alphonsine tables now err by "a little less than three whole degrees" in Saturn and Jupiter — Tycho to explain in his "new hypotheses." The 1577-comet mean-commutation epoch (24 November): Hájek, recomputing from Maestlin's own data (dedomenis = δεδομένα), made it 15′ smaller, "since from the established epoch the whole edifice rises"; Tycho calls it a trifling error and Hájek lets it drop. The prosthaphaeresis discrepancy between them Tycho explained as arising from different reference terms — Hájek's reckoned from 24 November, Tycho's from 26 January — and Hájek "gladly acquiesces." (Prosthaphaeresis kept by this chapter's convention.)
  • The polemics (Hájek's own war over the 1572 nova). Three named opponents who reviled rather than argued: "Raimundus" = Annibale Raimondo of Verona, who attacked the supralunar reading and drew Hájek's threefold Responsio; "Graminaeus" = Theodor Graminaeus of Cologne (the abuse-word Verpus, lit. "the circumcised/the prick," is a coarse insult, kept and glossed not euphemised); and "Lindanus" = Wilhelm Damasus Lindanus (van der Lindt), the polemical Bishop (Roermond, later Ghent), Graminaeus's "reserve-man" (succenturiatus). Hájek answered Graminaeus and Lindanus in the manuscript Aquilonis Historici Prodromus (the "Prodromus of the Historical Aquilo"), a work dedicated to three secular Electors, "thumbed in many hands" but never printed — a "painted-over friend" having deterred the printer mid-press. The cobbler joke: Lindanus had called Hájek a "lame cobbler" (claudus sutor, alluding to the proverb ne sutor ultra crepidam, "cobbler, stick to your last"), so Hájek struck back "with cobbler's lasts and a shoemaker's foot-block … without danger of excommunication."
  • The Craig dossier. Tycho's Apologetica responsio answered the Scottish physician John Craig (Craige), who defended Aristotle's sublunary comets against Tycho and (in his Capnuraniae restinctio, the comet polemic) charged that comet-distances could not be determined. Hájek defends Regiomontanus — whose De cometae … problemata XVI (the sixteen problems on the 1472 comet) "in problems 2, 3, 4" does warn that a comet's proper motion must be allowed for in parallax-work — and recalls his own priority: "the first" to declare the 1572 star supralunar and "the first to shake Aristotle's opinion on comets … to cut this ice." The Regiomontanus sententia ("thesauros naturae nos non evacuare…") is kept as a maxim.

Hagecius's two brief sightings (25 February and one later day, O.S., near Triangulum/Aries, with a faint tail toward Andromeda's foot) are an independent minor observational record; separately, he notes that Tycho had promised, but failed to enclose, a copy of a French royal printing privilege meant as a model for the Imperial one Curtius was securing.
