The original

CLARISSIMO ET DOCTISSIMO VIRO DOCTORI PETRO SEVERINI, MEDICO REGIO, PHILOSOPHO EXIMIO, SUO AMICO. S.

BINAS a te charissime D. D. PETRE recepi literas, quibus interea nihil a me responsum est, non quod amicitiæ et officii immemor essem, sed quod multis negotiis et amicorum visitationibus interea (ut fit) distractus fuerim. In prioribus communis nostri fratris et amici immortali memoria dignissimi PRATENSIS casum et immaturum inexpectatumque a nobis discessum merito deploras. In posterioribus vero proprias, quibus conflictaris in vitæ cursu, difficultates doles.

Etsi vero PRATENSIS nostri abitus potius, quam obitus ab omnibus merito doleatur, utpote qui ob multarum scientiarum excellentem peritiam, vitæque et morum incomparabilem suavitatem, in omnium animis perpetuum sui desiderium reliquerit; tamen cum nos divinis oraculis et philosophia aliquatenus imbuti, statuamus eos, qui corpoream et visibilem hanc mundi formam, urgentibus fatis, reliquerunt, ad beatioris mundi incolas migrare et tanto beatius deinde vivere, quanto infinita felicitas finitam miseriam exsuperat; o Horizontum temporis et æternitatis imperscrutabile discrimen; cumque is, qui hinc discessit, non decessit PRATENSIS, fuerit et divina et humana Philosophia ita edoctus, ut in eius, per quem omnes misericordiam meremur, pia invocatione et officij sui in hac mortali vita fideli vocatione, in æternitatem migraverit, et dum fata sinebant, vitam hanc mortalem bene et laudabiliter transegerit; idcirco non dubitamus cum eo longe melius nunc actum esse, quam nobiscum, qui inter tot errorum et fluctuationum procellas misera spe, quod reliquum est in corpore vitæ, fovemus. Unde non est quod tam ipsius casum doleamus, quam proprium, quod tanto viro, tam præclaris animi dotibus ornato, tamque sincero et fido amico orbati simus. Verum cum et nos frustra hoc faciamus, utpote qui nullo luctu nullis lacrymis exoptatissimum amicum in hanc vitam revocare possimus, nostroque desiderio nulla ratione succurri possit, patienter feramus id, quod inexorabile fatum nulla impatientia mutari permittit. Unum restat, quo amici defuncti exuvias ornare possumus, nempe ut Epitaphium illi dignum confici curemus, quo eius memoria, alias quidem immortalis, sed pro civili et politica consuetudine ad posteros et eos, qui ipsum in vivis non norunt, conservari possit; huius, quantum ad sumtus attinet, ego eorum particeps ero, tu de inscriptione cogita. Id autem imprimis fieri oportet, ne, si diu differatur, in perpetuam (ut fit) oblivionem abeat. Dies enim diei memoriam delet.

Quantum vero ad posteriores tuas literas attinet, magis est quod nostram in hac vita sortem deploremus; quod tot assidue, dum hîc peregrinamur, itinerum difficultatibus et asperitatibus impediamur. Tu, dum necessaria Philosophiæ adminicula sectaris, libertatem Philosophicam in Aula aliquamdiu si non plane amittis, saltem in exilium abigis, unde eam revocare, ingentis, ut experiris, est difficultatis. Aula enim quosvis blando et benevolo vultu recipit, sed protervo et invito dimittit. Verum patientia, taciturnitate et consilio multæ difficultates tandem exsuperantur, tempore etiam aliam atque aliam fortunæ mutationem inducente. Sed danda est opera, ne, dum ab his fluctibus et tempestatibus evadere conamur, in novos impingamus et Charybdin evitantes in Scyllam devolvamur. Invia enim est inter tot scopulos, procellas et voragines ad Philosophiam via. Verum si cui auxilio Supremi portum attingere datur, is poterit magnum alterius spectare laborem, non quia vexari quenquam est iucunda voluptas, sed quibus ipse malis careas quia cernere suave est.

Petis insuper a me, charissime D. D. PETRE, ut hypotheseos COPERNICI in triplici motu Terræ explicationem tecum communicem; qua is solerti ingenio per solam Terram, tum primi mobilis rapidissimum motum, tum octavæ sphæræ inæqualem tarditatem, ipsius denique Solis annuum cursum salvari posse demonstravit. Primus est, quo Terra circa axem sui Æquatoris versus ortum spatio 24 horarum convolvi imaginatur, eaque ratione rapidissima illa primi mobilis circumgyratio, qua omnes secum inferiores sphæras in occasum convolvere a veteribus statuebatur, penitus excluditur. Alter motus est, quo centrum Terræ interea annuum cursum, quem veteres Soli attribuebant, absolvit, eaque via Eclipticæ ductum ijs in locis, ubi hi Solem locabant, designat. Tertius vero est, quo axem Æquatoris Terræ ad planum Eclipticæ, quod centrum ipsius (ut dixi) describit, variabilem et annuo hinc motu revertentem inclinationem habere oportet; alias enim apparentes Solis ab Æquatore declinationes, dierumque incrementa et decrementa cum umbrarum discriminibus hypothesi huic non congruerent. Sed et in hoc tertio motu mirabili industria per binos circellos circa axem Æquatoris Terræ descriptos, salvat inæqualitatem motus fixarum ideo nobis apparentem, quod Æquator Terræ inæqualiter in antecedentia promoveatur, alteramque Æquatoris mobilitatem, qua ad Eclipticam accedit et recedit, eodem artificio excusat. Verum hæc omnia, ut sunt ingeniose a solertissimo illo Artifice excogitata; ita, nisi machina talis extruatur, in qua oculariter demonstrentur, difficulter intelligi possunt. Id vero et plura alia, Deo conatus nostros adiuvante, aliquando facturus sum, ubi vulgi strepitu semoto mihi Philosophica quiete frui licuerit, et in ijs, quæ in motibus adhuc desiderantur, tunc diligentiorem trutinam adhibebo. Tu interim da operam, ut libertatem pristinam recuperare possis et tandem animum ad continuandum Philosophiæ studium componere, ni mavis sequi viam universæ carnis, cumque alijs reliquos frustrare inglorius annos. Vale et saluta magnificum Dominum Decanum meo nomine officiosissime.

Iterum terque quaterque vale.
Datæ Knudsdorp die 3. Septembris Anno 1576.
Spero me brevi vobis Haffniæ affuturum.
Tuus
Tycho Brahe.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the most renowned and most learned man, Doctor Peter Severinus, royal physician, distinguished philosopher, his friend — greeting.

I have received two letters from you, dearest Master Doctor Peter, to which meanwhile nothing has been answered by me — not because I was forgetful of friendship and duty, but because in the interval I was (as happens) drawn off by many affairs and by the visits of friends. In the earlier one you rightly lament the fall of Pratensis, our common brother and friend, most worthy of undying remembrance, and his untimely and unexpected departure from us. In the later one, however, you grieve over the difficulties of your own, with which you struggle in the course of your life.

But although the going-away rather than the death of our Pratensis is rightly mourned by all — inasmuch as, by reason of his excellent skill in many sciences and the incomparable sweetness of his life and character, he has left in the hearts of all a perpetual longing for himself — yet since we, imbued to some degree with the divine oracles and with philosophy, hold that those who, the fates pressing, have left this bodily and visible form of the world pass over to the inhabitants of a more blessed world and thereafter live the more blessedly, by as much as infinite felicity surpasses finite misery — O the inscrutable boundary of the horizons of time and of eternity! — and since he who departed hence, [yet] did not pass away, Pratensis, was so instructed in both divine and human philosophy that he passed into eternity in the devout invocation of Him through whom we all merit mercy, and in the faithful calling of his office in this mortal life, and, while the fates allowed, conducted this mortal life well and laudably: therefore we do not doubt that it now goes far better with him than with us, who amid so many storms of error and wavering cherish, with wretched hope, what remains of life in the body. Hence it is not so much his fall that we should grieve over as our own — that we have been bereft of so great a man, adorned with such illustrious gifts of mind, and so sincere and faithful a friend. But since we too do this in vain — inasmuch as by no mourning, by no tears, can we call back into this life a most longed-for friend, and our longing can be relieved by no means — let us patiently bear that which inexorable fate permits to be changed by no impatience. One thing remains, by which we can adorn the remains of our deceased friend: namely, that we see to it that a worthy epitaph be made for him, by which his memory — undying indeed in other respects, but, in accordance with civil and political custom, [to be preserved] for posterity and for those who did not know him among the living — may be preserved. Of this, so far as concerns the expense, I shall be a sharer in their part; do you give thought to the inscription. And this above all must be done, lest, if it be long deferred, it pass (as happens) into perpetual oblivion. For day deletes the memory of day.

As for your later letter, there is the more reason for us to deplore our lot in this life: that, while we sojourn here, we are so constantly hindered by the difficulties and harshnesses of [so many] journeys. You, while you pursue the necessary supports of philosophy, lose your philosophical liberty at Court for a time — if not wholly lose it, at least drive it into exile, whence to recall it is, as you find, of immense difficulty. For the Court receives anyone with a bland and benevolent face, but dismisses him insolent and unwilling. Yet by patience, silence, and counsel many difficulties are at length overcome, time too bringing on one change of fortune after another. But care must be taken lest, while we try to escape from these waves and tempests, we strike upon new ones, and, avoiding Charybdis, we tumble into Scylla. For the way to philosophy, amid so many rocks, storms, and gulfs, is hard to travel. Yet if it be granted to anyone, by the help of the Most High, to reach harbour, he will be able to look upon another's great toil — not because to see anyone harassed is a pleasant delight, but because it is sweet to discern from what ills you yourself are free.

You ask of me besides, dearest Master Doctor Peter, that I share with you the explanation of Copernicus's hypothesis in the threefold motion of the Earth, by which he, with skilful ingenuity, demonstrated that through the Earth alone could be saved both the most rapid motion of the primum mobile, and the unequal slowness of the eighth sphere, and finally the annual course of the Sun itself. The first is that by which the Earth is imagined to be revolved about the axis of its equator toward the east in the space of 24 hours; and by this means that most rapid whirling of the primum mobile — by which the ancients held that it carried all the lower spheres with it round to the west — is wholly excluded. The second motion is that by which the centre of the Earth meanwhile completes the annual course which the ancients attributed to the Sun, and by this path marks out the tracing of the ecliptic in those places where they located the Sun. The third, however, is that by which the axis of the Earth's equator must have, with respect to the plane of the ecliptic which its centre (as I said) describes, a variable inclination, returning by an annual motion from this [variation]; for otherwise the apparent declinations of the Sun from the equator, and the increases and decreases of the days together with the differences of the shadows, would not agree with this hypothesis. And in this third motion too, by marvellous industry, by means of two little circles described about the axis of the Earth's equator, he saves the inequality of the motion of the fixed stars that appears to us for this reason — that the Earth's equator is advanced unequally into the preceding [signs] — and by the same artifice he accounts for the other mobility of the equator, by which it approaches and recedes from the ecliptic. But all these things, ingeniously as they have been devised by that most skilful Artificer, can nevertheless hardly be understood unless such a machine be built up in which they may be demonstrated to the eye. This indeed, and many other things, I shall, with God aiding our endeavours, do at some time, when, the din of the crowd set aside, it shall be permitted me to enjoy philosophical quiet; and then I shall apply a more diligent scrutiny to those things which are still wanting in the motions. Do you meanwhile take pains that you may recover your former liberty, and at length settle your mind to continuing the study of philosophy — unless you prefer to follow the way of all flesh, and, along with others, to waste your remaining years inglorious. Farewell, and greet the magnificent Lord Dean in my name most dutifully.

Again, and a third and a fourth time, farewell.
Given at Knudstrup, 3 September, the year 1576.
I hope to be with you shortly at Copenhagen.
Yours,
Tycho Brahe.

Apparatus

  • Sender. Tycho Brahe (1546–1601), writing from Knudstrup (Knudsdorp in the source; Knutstorp, the Brahe family seat in Scania) in September 1576 — months after Frederik II's 23 May 1576 grant of Hven, and just before Tycho settled there. The closing "I hope to be with you shortly at Copenhagen" reflects this transitional moment.
  • Recipient. Petrus Severinus / Petro Severini = Peder Sørensen (Petrus Severinus, 1540/42–1602), the Danish Paracelsian physician and author of Idea medicinae philosophicae (1571), at this date royal physician (medicus regius) to Frederik II. The salutation's philosopho eximio marks his standing as a natural philosopher, not merely a court doctor.
  • Person — Pratensis. Pratensis = Johannes Pratensis (Hans Philipsen, 1543–1576), professor of medicine at Copenhagen and the intimate friend of both Tycho and Severinus, who had died suddenly (his death is dated to 1576; the works consulted confirm Tycho's Epitaphium D. Joh. Pratensi was printed at Uraniborg, 1584). The whole consolatory first half turns on him. Wordplay flagged: abitus potius, quam obitus ("a going-away rather than a death") and qui hinc discessit, non decessit ("who departed hence, [yet] did not pass away") are deliberate sound-figures contrasting abitus/obitus and discessit/decessit; the English keeps the antithesis but cannot reproduce the Latin assonance.
  • Person — magnificum Dominum Decanum. "the magnificent Lord Dean" — an unnamed dean (likely the dean of the Copenhagen medical faculty or a cathedral dignitary) whom Severinus is asked to greet; not further identifiable from the text, so left unidentified rather than guessed.
  • Place. Haffnia = Copenhagen; Knudsdorp = Knudstrup / Knutstorp (kept as in).
  • The Copernican passage (technical core). Tycho gives a careful exposition of the threefold motion of the Earth in Copernicus's hypothesis: (1) the diurnal rotation about the axis of the equator, eastward, in 24 hours, which removes (penitus excluditur) the primum mobile's westward whirl; (2) the annual revolution of the Earth's centre, reproducing the Sun's apparent annual course along the ecliptic (Ecliptica); (3) the third (declination/precession) motion, the conical motion of the equatorial axis relative to the ecliptic plane, which Copernicus modelled with binos circellos ("two little circles") to save both the unequal precession of the fixed stars (motus fixarum … in antecedentia, motion into the preceding signs) and the variation in the obliquity (qua ad Eclipticam accedit et recedit). Controlled-glossary renderings used: declinatio → declination, primum mobile kept as a technical term, Ecliptica → ecliptic, sphaera → sphere, motus → motion. Note on Tycho's stance: this is an expository, admiring account of Copernicus ("that most skilful Artificer") written before Tycho's own geo-heliocentric system; he calls for a physical machina (a model/orrery built up so the motions can be "demonstrated to the eye") and reserves judgement on "those things which are still wanting in the motions" (quae in motibus adhuc desiderantur) — his hedge is preserved, not sharpened into a critique he does not yet make.
  • Classical allusions. Charybdin evitantes in Scyllam devolvamur — the Scylla-and-Charybdis topos (cf. the same image in Wolf's 1571 letter). The closing sententia quibus ipse malis careas quia cernere suave est renders Lucretius, De rerum natura II.1–4 (Suave, mari magno … alterius spectare laborem); Tycho adapts the Epicurean lines, and the rendering keeps them as the moral aphorism Tycho intends, with the source flagged here rather than smoothed away. Viam universae carnis ("the way of all flesh") is a Scriptural turn (cf. Joshua 23:14 / 1 Kings 2:2).
  • Sententiae kept. Dies enim diei memoriam delet — "For day deletes the memory of day," kept as the free-standing maxim it is. Iterum terque quaterque vale — "Again, and a third and a fourth time, farewell" (the terque quaterque echoes Virgil, Aeneid I.94); rendered as the intensified valediction, distinct from a plain Vale.
  • Epistolary formulae. S. = salutem → "greeting"; D. D. PETRE = Domine Doctor Petre → "Master Doctor Peter"; Vale … Iterum … vale → "Farewell … Again … farewell". The doubled subscription (Tuus / Tycho Brahe) and the postscript-style line about Copenhagen are kept in their source order.
  • Calendar. Datae Knudsdorp die 3. Septembris Anno 1576 = "Given at Knudstrup, 3 September 1576" (O.S.; Denmark kept the Julian calendar). The dateline matches the catalogue label (1576. 3. september. Knutstorp); no date discrepancy.
  • Edition note. The source's edition field gives the Dänische Bibliothek (the 18th-century Copenhagen/Leipzig periodical of Danish-historical documents), the early printing recorded for this letter; it is also edited in Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII (Epistolae). The sole MS witness given is A = Odense, Karen Brahes bibliotek, 298 (857 b).
