The original

HENRICUS BRUCÆUS AD TYCHONEM BRAHE.

GRATISSIMÆ mihi fuerunt tuæ literæ cum literis ultro citroque ad Landgravium et eius Mathematicum scriptis, in quibus non sine voluptate legi vestrarum observationum consensum, tum in Cometa anni 85, tum in stellarum fixarum notatione longitudinum et latitudinum. Latitudines enim uterque mutari statuit, et sequi mutationem declinationis Eclipticæ, sive obliquitatis Eclipticæ variationem. Hæc cum maximâ, sententia COPERNICI, sit 24', quæ aliquot seculorum decursu fiat, et cum non multum inter PTOLEMÆUM et HIPPARCHUM et MENELAUM et TIMOCHARIN temporum intervallum intercedat, non mirum, latitudinum mutationem ab ijs non annotatam. Verum de COPERNICO miror, qui evidentem mutationem debuit animadvertere in tanta obliquitatis mutatione inter PTOLEMÆUM et COPERNICUM intercedente, si veræ sunt observationes, quæ tantam obliquitatis Zodiaci variationem tradunt. Quæ autem de refractione siderum tradis, subtilia nimis mihi videntur, et quæ sub regulam cadere vix poterint ob diversas aëris constitutiones, quæ varietatem refractionum in eadem siderum altitudine efficient. Quæ autem a doctissimo CHRISTOPHORO ROTHMANNO in sua de Cometa anni 85 tractatione commemorantur, velim legere. Ex hypothesi enim falsa verum sequi non dubito; scientiam autem fieri, nullus mihi persuaserit, si debeat ea oriri ex causis veris conclusionis. Videmus autem motum Solis tam per Eccentricum, quam per Concentricum cum Epicyclo tueri nos posse, cum vel neutra vel altera hypothesis vera esse queat, ut non satis intelligam, cur hoc in loco ARISTOTELES subtilitatis arguatur. Quæ de cancris fluvialibus scribis, dabo operam, quantum in me erit; verum video maiores difficultates, quam tu fortasse cogites, quod cancrorum emtio et navium in Daniam solutio raro in eadem tempora incidant. Attamen studium meum cum in hoc tum in alio genere cognosces.

Bene vale, mi TYCHO, et nos tui studiosissimos habeto.
Rostochij 28 Octobris Anno Domini 1587.

Heinrich Brucaeus to Tycho Brahe.

Most welcome to me were your letters, together with the letters written back and forth to the Landgrave and his Mathematician, in which I read — not without pleasure — the agreement of your observations, both in the case of the comet of [the year] '85 and in the recording of the longitudes and latitudes of the fixed stars. For each of you holds that the latitudes change, and that they follow the change in the declination of the ecliptic — that is, the variation of the obliquity of the ecliptic. Since this is, at its greatest, 24′ in Copernicus's opinion — a change that comes about over the course of several centuries — and since not much interval of time falls between Ptolemy and Hipparchus and Menelaus and Timocharis, it is no wonder that a change of latitudes was not noted by them. But about Copernicus I marvel: he ought to have observed an evident change, given the great change of obliquity falling between Ptolemy and Copernicus, if the observations that report so great a variation of the obliquity of the zodiac are true. As for what you set forth about the refraction of the heavenly bodies, it seems to me too subtle, and such as can scarcely fall under a rule, on account of the diverse constitutions of the air, which will produce a variety of refractions at one and the same altitude of the heavenly bodies. But what is recounted by the most learned Christoph Rothmann in his treatise on the comet of [the year] '85 — I should like to read it. For I do not doubt that a true [conclusion] follows from a false hypothesis; but that this constitutes [true] knowledge [scientia], no one will ever persuade me, if it [knowledge] must arise from the true causes of the conclusion. For we see that we can maintain the motion of the Sun as well by an eccentric as by a concentric with an epicycle, although either neither or [only] one of the [two] hypotheses can be true — so that I do not well understand why, in this place, Aristotle is charged with [excessive] subtlety. As for what you write about the river crabs, I shall do my best, so far as it lies in me; but I see greater difficulties than you perhaps suppose, because the buying of the crabs and the sailing of ships to Denmark rarely fall at the same season. Yet you will know my zeal both in this matter and in another kind.

Fare you well, my Tycho, and reckon us most devoted to you.
At Rostock, 28 October, in the Year of the Lord 1587.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Henricus Brucaeus = Heinrich Brucaeus (Hendrik van den Broeck, 1531–1593), of Alost (Aalst) in Flanders; professor of medicine and of mathematics/astronomy at the University of Rostock, and Tycho's former teacher there. The intimate, faintly tutorial tone of the close (mi Tycho) and the philosophical objection reflect that relationship.
  • ad Landgravium et eius Mathematicum — to Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel (1532–1592) and "his Mathematician," i.e. Christoph Rothmann, the Hesse-Kassel court mathematician (named explicitly later in the letter). The "letters written back and forth" (ultro citroque) are the Tycho–Kassel observational correspondence; Brucaeus had evidently been shown copies.
  • The comet of '85. Cometa anni 85 = the comet of 1585, observed both at Hven and at Kassel; the subject of Rothmann's treatise (Tractatus de cometa anni 1585) and of comparison between the two observatories.
  • Astronomy — obliquity/latitudes. declinatio Eclipticae … obliquitas Eclipticae = the declination / obliquity of the ecliptic (the tilt of the zodiac to the equator); latitudines / longitudines = (celestial) latitudes / longitudes of the fixed stars. Brucaeus reports that Tycho and Rothmann agree the stellar latitudes shift in step with a secular change in the obliquity. maximâ … sit 24′ — Copernicus put the total amplitude of this variation at 24 arc-minutes (24′) over several centuries (Copernicus's theory of the trepidation/variation of the obliquity, De revolutionibus III). The figure and the hedge ("at its greatest," maximâ) are kept.
  • Ptolemaeum … Hipparchum … Menelaum … Timocharin — Ptolemy (fl. 2nd c. AD), Hipparchus (2nd c. BC), Menelaus of Alexandria (fl. c. AD 100), and Timocharis of Alexandria (early 3rd c. BC): the ancient observers whose star-records bracket the question. Brucaeus's point is sceptical — the ancients' intervals were too short to register the latitude shift, yet Copernicus, far removed in time from Ptolemy, ought to have seen it if the reported variation is real.
  • Refraction. refractione siderum = refraction of the heavenly bodies; diversas aëris constitutiones = "the diverse constitutions [states] of the air." Brucaeus's objection — that atmospheric refraction is too variable with the air's condition to be reduced to a fixed table (sub regulam … cadere) at a given altitude (altitudine) — is a genuine and prescient methodological caution; rendered without sharpening.
  • The hypotheses passage (the philosophical core). Ex hypothesi … falsa verum sequi non dubito; scientiam autem fieri, nullus mihi persuaserit, si debeat ea oriri ex causis veris conclusionis. — "I do not doubt that a true [conclusion] follows from a false hypothesis; but that this constitutes [true] knowledge [scientia], no one will ever persuade me, if it must arise from the true causes of the conclusion." This is the classic "saving the phenomena" vs. demonstrative knowledge distinction: a hypothesis (geometrical model) may yield correct predictions yet be physically false, and so not true scientia in the Aristotelian sense (knowledge through true causes). scientia is rendered "[true] knowledge" with the Latin flagged, not modernised to "science." The example follows: motum Solis tam per Eccentricum, quam per Concentricum cum Epicyclo = the Sun's motion modelled equally well by an eccentric or by a concentric [deferent] with an epicycle — two geometrically equivalent constructions, of which (Brucaeus argues) at most one can be physically true. The clause cum vel neutra vel altera hypothesis vera esse queat is rendered "although either neither or [only] one of the [two] hypotheses can be true"; altera here = "(only) one of the two," the disjunction being "neither, or [at most] one" — i.e. not both. The two negation-bearing options (neutra / altera) are reproduced exactly, neither added to nor dropped.
  • hoc in loco ARISTOTELES subtilitatis arguatur — "in this place Aristotle is charged with [excessive] subtlety." subtilitatis arguatur = "is accused/charged on the score of subtlety"; the bracketed "[excessive]" marks that the genitive carries a pejorative sense (over-subtlety, hair-splitting) which is implied, not lexically present. The "place" (hoc in loco) is presumably a passage in Tycho's or Rothmann's letters where Aristotle's physics was faulted; Brucaeus defends Aristotle, since (on his view) the geometers cannot establish physical truth either.
  • River crabs. cancris fluvialibus = river crabs / crayfish (freshwater crustaceans, Astacus). A non-astronomical favour: Tycho had asked Brucaeus to procure crayfish; Brucaeus pleads the logistical mismatch between the buying of the crabs and the sailing of ships to Denmark (navium in Daniam solutio — solutio here = the loosing/setting-sail of ships), which rarely coincide in season. Rendered plainly; solutio glossed.
  • Epistolary formulae. Bene vale, mi Tycho = "Fare you well, my Tycho"; nos tui studiosissimos habeto = "reckon us most devoted to you."
  • Calendar / place. Rostochij 28 Octobris Anno Domini 1587 = "at Rostock, 28 October, in the Year of the Lord 1587" (O.S.; Denmark and Lutheran Rostock both kept the Julian calendar). The dateline agrees with the catalogue label (28 October 1587, Rostock) — no discrepancy.
