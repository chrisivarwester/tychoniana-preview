The original

[TYCHO BRAHE AD CHRISTINAM LINDENOV]

MIN gandske wenlig hilsen nu och allthiid forsend met Vor Herre. Kierre fru Chirstine och kierre modersøster, Gud allmechtigste beware eder nadelligen och well fran allt ont, och nest megen tacksigelse for allt bewist gode, huilket vdigien att forskylde ieg stedtze vill findis aff formuen willig. Giffuer ieg eder gandske wenligen thilkiende, att eders skriffuelße er mig thillhende kommen met min søn Thyge och derhos thuende breffue fra eder och eders datter, jomfru Elisabet, till eders søn Clauß. Saa kan jeg eder wenligen icke forholde, att samme eders søn er icke nu her hoß mig, mens der han haffde werrit her wed it fierring aar, bleff hand tillsindz att wille drage ind vdj Franckerig der att lerre sprockit, eptherdj han fornam, att samme sprock war nu icke saa møgit bruelligt vdj keyserens gaar som tillforn, eptherdj die thaller nu mest der jtaliensch och spansch, foruden tydsk och bemisch, och kom imod forgangen jeullethiid en dansk magister till mig ved naffn mester Niels Hammer, som thillforn haffde werrit y Franckerig och kunde sprogit och wiste der leyligheden, hannom thog han did ind met sig, huilket mig och søntiß gott att werre, paa det han icke skulde wandre allene; thi den dreng, han tog met sig aff Danmarck, bleff befengt och døde aff pestelenße, førend mand rett aff wiiste, thi herr war en megit skrøffuellige thiid y forgangen aar, och ieg haffde omhu noch for Clauß, for hand icke wille ware sig well, saa att han och icke achtede huad drengen skade, och mente, att det haffde ingen nød, intill det war forsent och slet vde met hannom. Siiden bleff samme drengs lig her nere vdj bøen hedellig begraffuit, och bleff mine folck so forferrit, att ieg fick strax att føre dennom hen paa itt andit af keyserns slott, ved 3 mill herfra liggendis, och Clauß fulde met oss, hor wij warre thillsammen ved 7 vger. Siden emod ieg achtid att drage hid vdigien, thog Clauß sig den reyse for, som sagt er, helst for han fornam, att keysern var icke snart ventendis till Prag vdigien, som var dragen der fran for bemelte farlig søgdom skyld thill en fast stad, som kaldis Pilsen, 10 mille derfran liggendis, hor hanß may. endnu er, och neppe kommer til Prag forend epther paaske. Jeg var thillsindz att wille haffue flid Clauß en god thieniste vdj keyserns gaar, menß der han fornam, att der holtz saa stor pracht och offuerflødighed, att mange therrit vell mehre end deris besolding kunde beløffue, som och well andensteds skier, och for dett fransøsiske sprock, som sagt er, nu icke der var sønderlig gengse, løstid hannom heller att drage ind vdj Franckerig, hor hand och nogenstund thillforn haffde werrit, och den forneunte magister, som fuldis med hannom, loffuit mig met hon och mund, so frembst han vilde haffue nogen thiid got enten aff mig eller nogen anden aff Clausis slecht eller wenner, att han wille tage flittellig vare paa hannom och werre hannom hold och tro y allemaade, som ieg och ingen thuill paa haffuer, att han io det flitelligen giørendis vorder.

Anlangendis die forbemelte breffue, som Clauß skulle haffue, huilke ankom nogit epther hand var affdragen, will ieg foruare thill Gud vill ieg kan faa att wijde, paa huad sted hand er y Franckerig att finde, och mig kan stedis wiß bod till hannom; hueß det icke inden pinsdag eller s. Hans dag ske kan, will ieg forbrenne begge breffuene, saa att ingen skall fo dennom att see eller laese. Eder steder snarer end mig bod fra Danmarck thillskuiß nu vdj angaendis sommer ind y Franckerig, naar y kan thilforn fo att wijde, hor Clauß er der att finde, som ieg tencker well, att hand skriffuer eder till med første mulighed. Gud beuare hannom och eders anden børn fra all wløcke. Och hueß ieg kan giørre, som nogen aff dennom till gode och forfremmelße kan werre, skulle y ingen tuill paa haffue, att ieg det io heller end gierne giørre will.

Hueß min thilstand och wilkor er vdj disse land tencker ieg vell, att y haffuer fornummitt aff hues ieg skreff min søster Sophi sistegang till derudom, och er allthingest, Gud ske loff, y en god wesen, forhobis att det kan herepther endnu bliffue bedre, saa att mig formedelst Gudz naade och forsøn ingenlunde fortrøder, att ieg met mine er vddragen aff Danmarck, wille kun, att det haffde lenge tillforn sked. Der er och got andenstes y werden att werre, naar man kan kun tro det och befalle Gud allting.

Kierre modersøster, vill ieg nu icke lenger opholde eder met denne skriffuelse, menß nu och allthiid haffue eder met allt, hueß y vell ville, Gud allmechtigste befalit. Giører vell och siger eders dater, jomfru Elisabet, mange gode netter paa mine vegne.

Daterit paa det keyserlige slot Benatick den 22 martij epther denne nø still anno 1600.

[TYCHO BRAHE TO CHRISTINE LINDENOV]

My wholly friendly greeting, now and always, conveyed with [the blessing of] Our Lord. Dear Lady Christine and dear aunt [mother's sister], may God Almighty preserve you graciously and well from all evil; and next [after] much thanks for all the kindness shown, which to repay I shall always be found willing according to my means. I give you quite amicably to know that your letter has come to my hand with my son Tyge, and with it two letters from you and your daughter, Mistress Elisabeth, for your son Claus. So I cannot amicably withhold from you that your said son is not now here with me: rather, when he had been here about a quarter-year, he resolved to go into France, to learn the language there — since he perceived that that tongue was now not so much in use at the Emperor's court as before, the people there now speaking mostly Italian and Spanish, besides German and Bohemian — and about last Christmastide a Danish Master came to me, by name Master Niels Hammer, who had formerly been in France, knew the language, and knew the lie of things there; him [Claus] took along with him, which seemed good to me too, that he should not travel alone. For the servant-boy he brought with him from Denmark was infected and died of the pestilence before one rightly knew it — for there was a very dreadful time here last year — and I had care enough for Claus, since he would not take good care of himself, so that he too disregarded what the boy was suffering, and thought there was no danger, until it was too late and quite over with him. Afterward the same boy's body was buried with dignity down in the town; and my people were so terrified that I had at once to take them away to another of the Emperor's castles, some 3 miles thence, where we were together about 7 weeks, and Claus came with us. Then, about [the time] I intended to come back here, Claus undertook that journey, as said — chiefly because he perceived the Emperor was not soon to be expected back at Prague, having gone thence, for the said dangerous sickness, to a strong town called Pilsen, 10 miles thence, where His Majesty still is, and scarcely comes to Prague before Easter. I had intended to procure Claus a good post at the Emperor's court; but since he perceived that such great pomp and extravagance is kept there, that many spend well more than their salary can amount to (as also happens elsewhere), and because the French tongue, as said, was now not much current there, he preferred rather to go into France, where he had also been some time before; and the said Master who went with him promised me, by hand and mouth — so far as he would ever have any good from me, or from any other of Claus's kin or friends — that he would take diligent care of him and be true and faithful to him in every way (of which I have no doubt that he will diligently do so).

Concerning the aforesaid letters meant for Claus, which arrived somewhat after he had departed, I will keep [them] until — God willing — I can learn at what place he is to be found in France, and a sure messenger to him can be had; if that cannot happen before Pentecost or St. John's Day, I will burn both letters, so that no one shall get them to see or read. You can get a messenger sooner than I, sent from Denmark this coming summer into France, when you can first learn where Claus is to be found — as I think he will write you at the first opportunity. May God preserve him and your other children from all misfortune; and what I can do to the good and advancement of any of them, you shall have no doubt I will do, more than gladly.

What my condition and circumstances are in these lands, I think you have perceived from what I last wrote to my sister Sophie about it; and everything is, God be praised, in a good state, [I] hoping it may yet grow better hereafter — so that, by God's grace and providence, I in no way regret that I, with mine, have departed from Denmark — would only that it had happened long before. There is also good [reason] to be elsewhere in the world, if only one can believe it and commit everything to God.

Dear aunt, I will not now detain you longer with this letter, but, now and always, commend you, with all you wish well, to God Almighty. Be so good as to wish your daughter, Mistress Elisabeth, many good nights on my behalf.

Dated at the imperial castle Benátky, the 22nd of March by this New Style, the year 1600.

Apparatus

  • Recipient & kinship. Christine (Kirsten) Lindenov, whom Tycho addresses as modersøster — "mother's sister," i.e. his maternal aunt (a sister of Beate Bille, married into the Lindenov family). Her son Claus and daughter Mistress Elisabeth are named. The MS is an autograph draft (Kladde).
  • A young kinsman "Claus" — identity to firm up (flagged). The Claus here (Christine Lindenov's son, who went to France) is most likely distinct from Claus Mule (son of Mette Kotte of Nislevgård; brahe-t_15990925001, brahe-t_15980910001) — different mother, different family — though both are young men of Tycho's orbit named Claus going abroad. The exact identification (and whether the two Clauses could overlap) remains open; left unconflated here.
  • Significance — a personal letter of the plague year (rich detail).
  • Claus's plans. Having spent ~a quarter-year with Tycho, Claus chose to go to France to learn French — Tycho noting that French was now little used at the imperial court, where Italian, Spanish, German, and Bohemian (Czech) prevailed (a vivid datum on the court's languages). He travelled with a Danish Master Niels Hammer, who knew France, and who pledged "by hand and mouth" to look after him.
  • The plague at Benátky (eyewitness). The servant-boy Claus brought from Denmark caught the plague and died before its danger was recognized; he was "buried with dignity down in the town," and Tycho's terrified household fled to another imperial castle c. 3 miles off for c. 7 weeks. A concrete account of the 1599 epidemic's impact on Tycho's own household (cf. the family letters brahe-t_16000321004, brahe-t_16000321002).
  • The court's "pomp." Tycho had meant to place Claus at court, but Claus saw "such great pomp and extravagance … that many spend more than their salary," and preferred France — a candid aside on imperial-court costs.
  • The letters for Claus. Two letters (from Christine and her daughter) arrived after Claus left; Tycho will hold them until he learns Claus's whereabouts in France, else burn them "so no one shall see or read" — a small note of discretion.
  • Exile sentiment. "I in no way regret that I, with mine, have departed from Denmark — would only it had happened long before."
  • Persons named. Christine Lindenov (Tycho's aunt) and her children Claus and Elisabeth; Master Niels Hammer; Tycho's son Tyge; his sister Sophie Brahe; Rudolf II.
  • Calendar. Dateline Benatick den 22 Martij efter denne nye stil anno 1600 — "by this New Style" (Tycho explicitly marking the imperial calendar for his Danish aunt). Header agrees with the label.
  • Correction. Latin closing formula restored to 'Gud allmechtigste befalit' (Dreyer TBOO XIV p.191), correcting the dropped 'all-' prefix; no change to the English, which already read correctly.
