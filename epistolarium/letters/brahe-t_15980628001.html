EPIGRAMMA AD OLIGERUM
ROSENCRANTZIUM SPONSUM.

Tene OLIGERE etiam materno
sauciat arcu

    Ille levis toto qui volat orbe puer?

Iste puer cæcus minimusque nec ardua curans

    Quem poteras alto despicere ingenio.

Omnia sed vincit, dices, cedamus amori

    Indomito; summos stravit et ante viros.

Est (fateor) fortis; Musæ, Dictinna, Minerva

    Sunt tamen e quibus is nulla Trophæa gerit.

Intimus es Musis, placet et Dictinna, tibique

    Artibus exculto culta Minerva favet:

Attamen aut Arte aut Pharetra, qui vincere nullas

    E tribus his potuit, te superavit amor.

Scilicet ut tandem miro te falleret astu,

    Miscuit inter se nomina nominibus,

Proque alijs Sophijs Sophiam ut tibi protulit unam

    Præ Sophia omni, unam, victus, amas Sophiam .

T. B.

To the man notably renowned for nobility both of genius and of birth, Lord Holger Rosenkrantz of Rosenholm, my kinsman, relative, and most upright friend.
Heartiest greeting.

Although I do not doubt, my most longed-for Holger, that both letters — namely those I sent with Mule, and those I afterward wrote to you — have been delivered to you, and that from them you have learned, among other things, what was written to me from Prague by my old friend Thaddaeus [Hagecius]: nevertheless, since a few days ago I received some letters from that Magdeburg friend of mine, whom you know is wont to write to me, treating of certain matters on the same subject, I thought a copy of these too should be shared with you, that you might more fully understand what the Imperial Majesty's disposition toward me is — especially since I must now reply to our Eske Bille about certain [letters] he sent me these days, and I have one of my men now at Lübeck who will direct these to you safely, when some convenient opportunity offers, while he is occupied carrying out my other business there. But I should wish that these very things I now write and send, you show to none but our intimates; you yourself know well enough which of them are faithful — though even they, as it happens, are few. I did not want the name of him who wrote these things to me appended; but you and Doctor Nicolaus will easily gather who it is. He is wont to write to me freely, though unknown to one unknown by face; I should therefore not wish these letters of his to lie open to others, chiefly on account of what he writes about a certain Prince otherwise well enough known. Either, then, keep these very secret, or hand them to Vulcan [burn them]. How easy it is for me to answer that Prince's objections, you yourself know well enough: for, deserted, I deserted — lest I myself be a spectator of the ruin of those things which in my fatherland I laboured at so long and with such effort; which, however, I judge is to be imputed neither to the Kingdom nor to the King — the authors of it are well enough known, and have deserved to be made yet more notorious. But that I add these things I now send to the earlier ones, my Holger, is done for this reason: that I may take timely counsel from you and other intimate friends — what you think should be done, when such a thing [the offer] is made to me. I understand, from those who know those places, that it is a splendid and pleasant castle, into which the Emperor himself was wont, a few years ago, sometimes to withdraw for recreation, and which he values most after the Prague one, and around which he was accustomed to hunt, while he delighted in these and the like; for now, within the last two or three years, they report he has kept himself only in the Prague castle. I, indeed, would rather be solicitous about obtaining some fief in Silesia, Moravia, Lusatia, or other dominions subject to the Emperor, than to dwell in so pompous a place of Bohemia; but one must comply with the Emperor's clement will, in whatever way at last he wishes to do good to me and mine, so that provision be also liberally made for the studies in which I am engaged — since our fatherland has so inconsiderately neglected this care. Speak of these things secretly with our Eske Bille, and also with Krag, our common friend, and signify by letter what counsel they give; but otherwise conceal these things, especially where it is less safe. My Franz Tengnagel has not yet returned hither, but I hope he will soon be happily present. I should also wish Mule would hasten his return, for I cannot at all determine about my affairs — how and when anything is to be done — before these two return. Meanwhile, however, I gird myself for the journey, as far as is granted, and commit all things to Divine providence, to which I also commend you with all yours.

Farewell.
Given in haste from Wandsbek, the 28th day of June, in the Year of the Lord 1598.
Your Tycho Brahe,
with my own hand.

[Postscript:] POSTSCRIPT. After I had written this to you, there came to me here Gabriel, son of Walter Schot of Hamburg — from whom your Mother and you are wont to buy cloth — and he said he would shortly come to Rosenholm. About the carriage too, which you are having made for me, he reported that your Mother said it would shortly be ready; wherefore he promised he would take it with him aboard ship from Aarhus to Lübeck; and when I enquired about you yourself, he supposed you had gone off into Zealand. And moreover [he said] that he had been at my brother Axel's, and had there learned that he is daily preparing for the weddings of the three daughters that are required, among which yours too will be celebrated, and he mentioned this would be six weeks from now. It came into my mind, then, that I had promised you a certain Epithalamium; and since, for the other occupations by which I am variously distracted, it can scarcely be done so quickly, have at least this short Epigram, which now came on the spur of the moment to my lips, here subjoined. May God, the author of the universe, bestow on you in this marriage all things prosperous and wholesome, according to your prayer. But do you bring it about that the love of this new Sophia does not weaken the love of that other, long-cultivated wisdom [sophia], but rather increases and confirms it. This is the task, this the toil; that goal has deceived many. / EPIGRAM TO HOLGER ROSENKRANTZ THE BRIDEGROOM. | Does that nimble boy who flies over the whole world / wound even you, Holger, with his mother's bow? / That boy, blind and tiniest, caring nothing for lofty things, / whom you could look down upon with your high genius. / "But Love conquers all," you will say; "let us yield to untamed / Love; he has laid low even the greatest men before." / Strong he is, I grant; yet the Muses, Diana, Minerva / are among those from whom he wins no trophies. / You are an intimate of the Muses, Diana too is pleasing to you, / and to you, refined in the arts, refined Minerva is favourable: / and yet, he who could conquer none of these three / by Art or by Quiver, Love has overcome you. / That is, so that at last he might deceive you by a wondrous wile, / he mingled names among names, / and instead of the other wisdoms [Sophiae] brought forth one Sophia for you — / so that before every Sophia, you love, vanquished, one Sophia. T. B.

Apparatus

  • Recipient. Holger Rosenkrantz "the Learned" (1574–1642), of Rosenholm — Tycho's consanguineus, affinis et amicus integerrimus ("kinsman, relative, and most upright friend"; the recurring Oligerus of the corpus, e.g. brahe-t_15980328001, brahe-t_15990307001). The MS is a later Langebek afskrift.
  • Significance — Tycho weighing the imperial offer (June 1598). A confidential, candid letter from the exile at Wandsbek, written as the imperial overture took shape. Tycho forwards (in copy) the latest from his anonymous Magdeburg correspondent on "the Imperial Majesty's disposition toward me," and asks Holger — with Eske Bille and Krag — for counsel on whether to accept "when such a thing is made to me." It is the deliberation behind the move that the 1599–1600 letters carry out.
  • The offered castle = Benátky. Tycho describes (from report) "a splendid and pleasant castle, into which the Emperor was wont … to withdraw for recreation … and around which he hunted," valued "most after the Prague one" — i.e. Benátky nad Jizerou, where Tycho would in fact be installed. Strikingly, Tycho says he would rather have a fief in Silesia, Moravia, or Lusatia than "so pompous a Bohemian place," but will comply with the Emperor's will provided his studies are "liberally provided for" — "since our fatherland has so inconsiderately neglected this care."
  • The "desertus deserui" epigram. Tycho's famous self-justification: "deserted, I deserted" (desertus deserui) — he left Denmark only after being abandoned, "lest I be a spectator of the ruin" of his life's work — laying the blame neither on the Kingdom nor the King, but on the "authors" of his troubles, "well enough known, and deserving to be made yet more notorious." (No silent sharpening: the careful exculpation of King and realm is kept.)
  • Secrecy. Aut … secreta habe, aut Vulcano trade — "keep these very secret, or give them to Vulcan" (burn them); show only "our intimates"; the anonymous correspondent's name withheld because of "what he writes about a certain Prince" — Tycho's tradecraft of confidential channels.
  • Persons named. Holger Rosenkrantz; Eske Bille (Tycho's kinsman-agent); Krag = Niels Krag (Nicolaus Cragius, 1550–1602, the Danish historian-diplomat; cf. brahe-t_15900228001), "our common friend"; Thaddaeus = Tadeáš Hájek (Hagecius), Tycho's Prague friend; Franz Tengnagel and Claus Mule (Mulaeus), Tycho's agents, both away (their return needed before Tycho can decide). Doctor Nicolaus — an intimate who, with Holger, will "guess" the Magdeburg correspondent; possibly Niels Krag (Nicolaus), though named separately as Kragius in the same letter, so left unidentified and flagged.
  • Calendar. Dateline Wandesburgo die 28. Iunij Anno Domini 1598; Old Style (the Danish-oriented exile context). Header agrees with the label.
  • §1 Dreyer collation. Closing postscript + a wedding EPIGRAM to Holger's bride Sophie (the Sophia bride / sophia wisdom pun), restored from Dreyer VIII p.79 (dropped by the working transcription).
  • A Virgilian tag before the epigram. Recovers Tycho's closing line to the prose passage introducing his wedding epigram for Holger Rosenkrantz: an adaptation of Virgil's famous "hoc opus, hic labor est" (Aeneid VI.129), repurposed here to describe the difficulty of the very thing Tycho has just urged — that love for a new bride ("this new Sophia") not weaken devotion to wisdom (sophia) itself. "Meta" (a race's turning-post/finish) and "fefellit" (has deceived/eluded) cast the marriage as a course "many" have failed to run without one love crowding out the other.
  • Literary self-consciousness. The tag functions as a bridge between the letter's prose and the Latin epigram that follows, and is itself a small piece of wordplay consistent with the whole letter's pun on Sophia (bride) / sophia (wisdom).
