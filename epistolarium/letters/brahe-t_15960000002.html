The original

HARDEWIGUS A DASSELL I. C. TYCHONI BRAHEN EQUITI, VIRO NOBILISSIMO.
S. D.

NOBILISSIME et amplissime vir, etsi singularis in te observantia ex ingenij tui praeclaris orta monumentis postulabat, ut aliquid ad te literarum darem, me tamen deterruit hactenus personae tuae dignitas, utpote hominis de facie mihi ignoti, verentem, ne talem virum tantarum rerum occupatione distentum inanibus appellare literis, pudentis hominis non esset. Nunc ut, quod sentio, dicam, paene accuso modestiam meam, quod, dum cogito ad te scribere, non tamen consilium explico. Interea tua fecit singularis humanitas, ut, dum Domini RANZOVIJ Producis Cimbrici literas ad me mittis, scribendo anteverteres.

Quanquam cur ego apud te maximarum virtutum laude praestantem, ad quas mihi adspirare non licet, plus residere officij moleste feram, cum praesertim hoc ipsum amoris in me tui, quo equidem antiquius nihil habeo, non obscurum argumentum esse videatur? Quocirca ut interdum me pudet pudoris mei, quod nihil ad te scribere sim ausus, cum tamen maxime cuperem, sic istâ voluntatis declaratione mirifice delector. Ego te, nobilissime vir, semper maximi feci, non propter illa, quae vulgi oculos in se convertunt, sed propter illa vere ac proprie tua, quae et te provexerunt ad istum, in quo es, dignitatis gradum, et provectura, ut spero, sunt etiam altius; etsi nihil aut tale effecissent, aut effectura essent, omni te tamen cultu atque observantiâ dignissimum reddidissent. Virtus enim et eruditio, vera mihi et solida videntur bona, eâque de causâ recte dicitur a Plutarcho: Τῆς ἀληθινῆς εὐδαιμονίας τὸ πλεῖστον ἐν ἤθει καὶ διαθέσει τῆς ψυχῆς εἶναι. Reliquum est, ut, cum tibi persuaseris me amantissimum tui esse, hoc mihi etiam des, ut in te expetendo, et tibi prospera omnia feliciaque optando concedam nemini.

Vale.
Luneburgi.

English translation machine translation, Probable

Hartwig von Dassel, Jurisconsult, to Tycho Brahe, knight, a most noble man. Greeting.

Most noble and most worthy man: although my singular regard for you, sprung from the illustrious monuments of your genius, demanded that I send you some letter, yet up to now the dignity of your person deterred me — being, as you are, a man unknown to me by sight — fearing that to accost such a man, stretched taut by the press of so great affairs, with empty letters would not be the part of a man of any modesty. Now, to say what I feel, I almost reproach my own modesty, in that, while I think of writing to you, I nonetheless do not carry out the design. Meanwhile your singular kindness has brought it about that, in sending me the letter of Master Rantzau, governor of the Cimbrian land, you forestalled me in writing.

And yet why should I take it ill that the greater part of the duty should rest with you toward me — a man pre-eminent in the praise of the greatest virtues, to which I am not permitted to aspire — especially when this very thing seems to be no obscure proof of your love toward me, than which indeed I hold nothing more precious? Wherefore, just as at times I am ashamed of my own shame — that I dared write nothing to you, though I most greatly desired it — so by this declaration of your goodwill I am wonderfully delighted. I, most noble man, have always esteemed you most highly, not on account of those things which turn the eyes of the common crowd upon themselves, but on account of those things truly and properly your own, which both have advanced you to that rank of dignity in which you stand, and are going, as I hope, to advance you even higher; and even if they had effected nothing of the kind, nor were going to effect it, they would nonetheless have rendered you most worthy of every honour and regard. For virtue and learning seem to me the true and solid goods; and for that reason it is rightly said by Plutarch: "That the greatest part of true happiness lies in the character and disposition of the soul." It remains that, once you have persuaded yourself that I am most loving toward you, you grant me this also: that in seeking after you, and in wishing you all things prosperous and happy, I yield to no one.

Farewell.
At Lüneburg.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Hardewigus a Dassell, I. C. = Hartwig von Dassel, styled Iuris Consultus (here expanded "Doctor of Both Laws" — I. C. = Iuris Consultus, a jurist/doctor of law). A Lüneburg jurist; he is otherwise little documented, and writes here as a man not personally acquainted with Tycho ("a man unknown to you by sight").
  • Recipient titulature. TYCHONI BRAHEN EQUITI, VIRO NOBILISSIMO — "to Tycho Brahe, eques (knight), a most noble man." Brahen is an accusative of the Latinised surname; eques renders Tycho's noble/knightly rank.
  • S. D. — salutem dicit, "sends greeting".
  • Domini Ranzovij Producis Cimbrici — "Master Rantzau, governor of the Cimbrian land": Heinrich Rantzau (1526–1598), royal Danish Statthalter (governor) of the duchies of Schleswig-Holstein, the great Holstein humanist-patron and astronomical correspondent of Tycho's. Produx Cimbricus (genitive Producis Cimbrici) is a humanist coinage for his office — produx "leader, governor" (a learned variant on dux/praeses), Cimbricus "of the Cimbrian Chersonese," i.e. of Jutland/Holstein, the standard humanist geographical name for the region. The phrase is rendered descriptively; the antiquarian "Cimbrian" geography is kept. The occasion of the letter is that Tycho had forwarded to Dassel a letter from Rantzau, which prompted (and pre-empted) Dassel's own first letter.
  • Greek (Plutarch). Τῆς ἀληθινῆς εὐδαιμονίας τὸ πλεῖστον ἐν ἤθει καὶ διαθέσει τῆς ψυχῆς εἶναι — "that the greatest part of true happiness lies in the character [ēthos] and disposition [diathesis] of the soul." A maxim attributed to Plutarch (a sententia in the moral-philosophical vein of the Moralia); transcribed verbatim and translated by the audit's rule. The clause is grammatically the accusative-and-infinitive completing recte dicitur a Plutarcho ("it is rightly said by Plutarch [that] …").
  • pudet pudoris mei — "I am ashamed of my own shame," a deliberate figura etymologica (the wordplay on pudor/pudet); kept as the rhetorical figure it is.
  • Dateline. The subscription gives only the place, Luneburgi = Lüneburg (Lower Saxony); no day or month is stated in the source. The edition assigns the year 1596 (the identifier's date-field reads 15960000, with zeros for month and day), consistent with the letter's appearance among the 1596-era pieces and with Rantzau's lifetime (d. 1598). The header therefore gives the year with an explicit note that day and month are not in the text; no date is asserted beyond what the source substantiates. Style (O.S./N.S.) is not determinable from the text and is not asserted.
  • Edition of record. Printed in Poematum libri IV (the letter survives as a printed prose piece within a poetry collection of this title; this is the source's edition field). No manuscript witness is recorded in the edition.
  • Correction. Three referent/lexical fixes in the Dassel-to-Tycho letter (TBOO VII p.376): (1) restored that Tycho, not Dassel, is 'unknown by sight', correcting a flipped mihi/tibi; (2) restored that the greater epistolary duty rests 'with you [Tycho]', correcting a flipped 'apud te'; (3) rendered 'I. C.' as Jurisconsult (Iuris Consultus) rather than the unsupported 'Doctor of Both Laws'.

The record's the page reference claims TBOO VII pp. 3-377 (30 pages) but this is a broken reference: visually inspected pages 1-4 and 27-30, which are Dreyer pp. 3-6 and 29-32 respectively — 1568-1576 correspondence (Tycho-Johannes, Tycho-Vellejum, Tycho-Pratensis) with no relation to this undated-1596 Hartwig von Dassel letter. The extract does not contain the target letter, so no Dreyer collation was possible; audited against the transcribed original alone. Two medium-severity pronoun/reference-flip mistranslation found ('mihi'->'to you'; 'apud te'->'with me'), each reversing a directional claim in the Latin, plus one low-severity title inflation. Flag for the process: this record's the page reference the page images is mismatched to its letter and should be regenerated.

3 defects found:
- mistranslation (medium). 'mihi' (dative, 'to me') is rendered as 'to you' and the referent is flipped from Tycho to Dassel: the Latin genitive-of-description 'hominis de facie mihi ignoti' describes Tycho ('you, being a man unknown to me by sight, i.e. we have never met'), agreeing with 'personae tuae' just before it. The English instead has Dassel himself as the one unrecognised, reversing who does not know whom. At: “being, as I am, a man unknown to you by sight”.
- mistranslation (medium). 'apud te' ('with you', i.e. with Tycho) is rendered as 'with me', reversing on whom the greater share of epistolary obligation falls. The letter's point is that Tycho, though the greater man, took on the courtesy-duty of writing first (unusual and flattering to Dassel); the English instead has the duty resting with Dassel, undermining the compliment the sentence is built to pay. At: “why should I take it ill that the greater part of the duty should rest with me toward you”.
- mistranslation (low). 'I.C.' is the standard early-modern abbreviation for 'Iuris Consultus' (jurisconsult/legal counsel), not 'I.U.D.' (Iuris Utriusque Doctor, 'Doctor of Both Laws'). The English supplies a specific academic title not warranted by the abbreviation. At: “Doctor of Both Laws”.

Repaired. This letter's own labelled extract (TBOO VII, nominally pp.3-377 — evidently a mislabelled 30-page span rather than a true per-letter extract) was not opened. Instead, its exact printed text was located, by a fortunate adjacency, at the tail of the neighbouring extract supplied for brahe-t_15960000001 (Dreyer TBOO VII p.376), which reproduces the Hartwig von Dassel letter in full, from its title through 'Vale. Luneburgi.' All three defect sites are confirmed there verbatim against the transcribed original (the title's 'I. C.', 'hominis de facie mihi ignoti', and 'apud te... plus residere officij' all match). All three defects fixed with full page collation.
