CLARISSIMO VARIOQUE DOCTRINARUM GENERE ERUDITISSIMO VIRO D. THADDÆO HAGGECIO AB HAYCK, PHILOSOPHIAe ET MEDICINAe DOCTORI EXIMIO, AMICO SUO INPRIMIS COLENDO.

QUATERNAS a te hac elapsa aestate, amantissime et eruditissime mi D. D. THADDÆE, accepi literas, primas Cal. Novembris veteri stylo anno 88 exaratas, 27. demum Aprilis huius anni me obtinere contigit; alteras 1O. Maij huius anni⟨⟩ scriptas ultimo Augusti recepi⟨⟩; tertias, quas 20. Maij novo stylo scripseras, circa medium Septembris obtinui⟨⟩; ultimae vero die 7. Septembris datae penultimo Octobris ad manus meas pervenerunt, idque (ut ubique antea) secundum veterem anni Iuliani numerationem, qua etiamnum nos utimur. Omnes tuae Praga erant missae, quas ego partim Hafniae, partim hîc Uraniburgi nactus sum. Singulae magnum illum tuum et constantem erga me meaque studia favorem affatim declarabant⟨⟩, et amicitiae inter nos olim Ratisponae ante annos circiter 14, cum modernus Imperator inauguraretur, percupide contractae sartam tectam continuationem et gratam conservationem, pluribus etiam interea literis confirmatam, non obscure testabantur⟨⟩. Pro qua tua singulari et plus quam benevola erga me affectione non possum non tibi gratias agere ingentes, pari vicissim te tuaque omnia amore et studio, quantum in me est, quoad vixero, prosequuturus et gratissimam tui memoriam perpetuo conservaturus⟨⟩.

Quod autem tuis illis eruditissimis pariter et amicissimis literis nihil tanto⟨⟩ iam elapso tempore responderim tuisque votis hac in parte non satisfecerim, ne⟨⟩ existimes, ulla oblivione tui vel etiam negligentia aut ob aliarum occupationum molem (licet hac praeterita aestate in varijs negotijs non exigua fuerit) id commissum esse, sed potius internunciorum penuriae, cum rarissime aliquis hinc ad vos abeat, cui tuto et certo literas concredere liceret, silentium illud diuturnum imputes velim. Nullus enim mihi incubuisset negotiorum tantus cumulus⟨⟩, neque ulla alia praevalere permisissem obstacula, quin aliquid ad te literarum dedissem, modo Grammatophorus certus et idoneus mihi interea temporis obtigisset. Et sane ut verum fatear, ne nunc quidem has ad te dedissem, ob instantem praesertim hyemem (quae maiora, quo minus tibi indubitanter tradantur, obstacula suggerit) nisi quartae illae tuae et ultimae nudiustertius supervenissent, et vehemens tuum, an priores a te missas acceperim, cognitionis desiderium unâ cum rescriptionis avida expectatione inculcassent⟨⟩. His enim acceptis, etsi non paucis hoc tempore implicer negotijs, sepositis tamen omnibus, non diutius responsione supersedendum duxi. Et licet non minor quam antea in mittendi occasione sit difficultas, imo potius ob hyemem, ut dixi, multo maior, attamen huic ita occurrendum censui, ut binas eadem continentes ad te exarari curarem, quarum unas Rostochium, alteras Lubecam vel Hamburgum translatas illac⟨⟩ tutius ad te perferri sperem; neque enim tunc ambas intercidere adeo in proclivi est.

Nunc itaque ut primis⟨⟩ tuis sufficienter respondeam, tempus postulat⟨⟩, siquidem ex quo illae a te scriptae sunt, hic ipse dies anniversarius sit, nimis magna interposita dilatione, quae tamen apud te omnia benevole et sincere interpretantem excusationem ob causas ante dictas⟨⟩ non difficulter⟨⟩ inveniet. Postea etiam ad alias nonnulla⟨⟩ subiuncturus idque paucioribus, cum et illae minus principalia tractent, et potissimum de primo missis⟨⟩ me admoneant. Ne igitur res ipsa diutius protrahatur, quae tuis primis⟨⟩ et prolixioribus ad ea quae continent, ordine respondenda nunc in mentem veniunt, sic habe. Binae illae, quarum istic ab initio mentionem facis, has Gallo cuidam Mathematico, illas Restochiensi Tabellario concreditas prorsus⟨⟩ (ut ipsemet recte coniectas) interciderunt, neque enim, si ullae illarum mihi allatae fuissent, rescriptionem tamdiu procrastinassem. Quod vero illae, quas ultimo ad te unâ cum libro nostro secundo aethereorum Phaenomenωn recenter conspectorum misi, optatum successum habuerint⟨⟩ et gratae oblectamentoque fuerint, plurimum mihi ipsi congratulor; quodque tam candide de scripto illo meo iudices, omniaque in potiorem partem interpreteris, est quod gratias tibi habeam permagnas. Nam licet tum ob aetatem, tum etiam curas domesticas et professionem medicam ab hoc sublimi studiorum genere, quo minus illi totus vacare possis, utplurimum, veluti ipsemet fateris, abstraharis, tamen ita iunioribus annis sedulo et feliciter in his versatus es, ut hac ingravescente aetate eo maturiore iudicio de ijs recte pronunciare possis. Et quamvis multa alia sint, quae subinde ab hac sublimi contemplatione te⟨⟩ avocent (quod et mihi non raro contingit) non tamen dubito, quin aliquot nonnunquam horis tantum tibi otij rapias⟨⟩, ut si non totaliter et ex professo, saltem aliqua et etiam bona ex parte his indulgeas⟨⟩. Neque enim amor ille tam arduus erga arduum etiam subiectum penitus deficere, vel ob curas rei familiaris aliaque studia, aut etiam senectae molestias ad nihilum redigi prorsusque aboleri poterit. Tuum itaque de meis studijs et scriptis solidum ac maturum iudicium pari aequanimitate coniunctum non saltem magnifacio, sed etiam multorum aliorum censurae praefero. Et cum hac materia, quam hisce prioribus Astronomiae instaurandae libris complector, plurimum tute multis abhinc annis afficiaris, eandemque non minima ex parte sedulitate tua et doctrina eximia illustrâris, eo etiam promptius tibi haec mea communico, et tecum de hoc utrique nostrum accepto negotio perlibenter confero, tuoque gravi iudicio omnia non gravatim subijcio. Et si quid in illis admonitione aut correctione dignum deprehenderis, non est quod ob amicitiam nostram et candorem tuum ingenuum, quo omnia in meliorem partem interpretaris, mihi nimium indulgeas aut nostris partibus plus aequo faveas. Amor enim et odium (ut est in Centiloquio) multorum iudicia pervertunt. Quin potius audacter et ingenue, si quid reprehensioni obnoxium inveneris, admone et libertate in his utere (more tuo) Philosophica: feceris mihi hac in parte rem non solum haud⟨⟩ ingratam, sed longe acceptissimam et ad veritatis in abscondito latentis penitiorem pervestigationem conducibilem. Invenies me animadversiones tuas et aliorum, qui acri iudicio valent, patienter et candide accipientem, et a te, qui etiam meas castigationes veritatis perquirendae amore in tuis scriptis admissas aequo et grato animo tulisti, non superatum iri.

Novam a me adinventam revolutionum coelestium dispositionem te nondum satis accurate perspexisse refers. De ea itaque recte facis, quod censuram non praecipitas, nam et haec in transmisso libro saltem generali indicatione (Cometa illo, qui non minus quam caeterae Planetae circa Solem ordinarie convolvebatur, sic ut hypothesis motus eius commodius et plenius constitui posset, exigente) consignata est. Pleraque enim adhuc restant particularia non cuivis obvia in ea ipsa explicanda, quae illic vel leviter attigimus, vel prorsus silentio, cum ex professo rem hanc ibi non tractaverim, praeterivimus, in alio quodam⟨⟩ et peculiari Astronomiae restituendae opere, favente Astrorum et omnis boni Authore, suo tempore enucleatius patefacienda et ex certissimis observationibus motuumque norma ipsi coelo analoga Geometrice demonstranda atque in numeros choream coelestem indubitatis vestigijs repraesentantes resolvenda. Interim ne sis nescius, me e Parallaxibus ♂tis in transitu acronycho⟨⟩ anno praesertim 82 diligentissime pervestigatis, cum solaribus aliquanto⟨⟩ maiores fuerint, tum etiam ex eius concitatiore in opposito Solis motu, atque fert Ptolemaica hypothesis, ipsum necessario, quando pernox est, terris plus, quam Sol unquam possit, appropinquare, solerti et saepius reiterata animadversione certo cognovisse scias. Atque hinc citra omnem refragationem consequitur, aut Terram annuo convolvi circuitu, quiescente circa medium Universi Sole, iuxta illam revolutionum coelestium distributionem, quam superioribus annis⟨⟩ ex veteri ARISTARCHO mutuatam⟨⟩ ingens ille et nunquam satis laudatus in apertum deduxit COPERNICUS, aut si id ob Physicas non leves sic⟨⟩ admissas absurditates, tum etiam sacrarum literarum non uno in loco refragantem auctoritatem concedi nequeat, nulla alia restat excusandarum Apparentiarum coelestium ratio, quae Geometriae et Arithmeticae legibus, uti oportet, subijciatur, quam ea ipsa ante sexennium⟨⟩ proxime in hypothesi systematis mundani conformanda a me excogitata⟨⟩. Sed et Terram nequaquam annuatim circumagi, ut vult Coperniana speculatio, aut Solis et octavae sphaerae motus per eam salvari posse, ut arduo et solerti ingenio idem tentavit Artifex⟨⟩, et maior in stellis, quae putantur affixae, celeritas, quam ipsius patitur Aequinoctiorum reciprocatio, et insuper⟨⟩ mutatae earundem latitudines a temporibus PTOLEMÆI hucusque iuxta proportionem alteratae declinationis Eclipticae ab Aequatore, satis convincunt; ideoque Terram immoto permanente Sole non designare annuo itinere Eclipticam, nec in hac sed potius Sole ipso istas differentias excusandas liquet⟨⟩. Accedit et hoc non obscuri testimonij loco, Terram instar Solis non moveri, quod Cometae circa acronychum⟨⟩ situm superioribus annis a nobis in altissimo aethere, summa diligentia quo ad omnes apparentias suas observati, motui annuo Terrae, qui tunc proprium illorum, quemadmodum in Planetis apud COPERNICUM sit, necessario vel retraheret vel saltem nonnihil retardaret, prorsus non paruerint, satis evidenti vel e solis Cometis indicio, terrestrem globum annuum circuitum nequaquam admittere⟨⟩. Cum itaque ob has et alijs etiam de causis Coperniana ratiocinatio in rei veritate nequaquam consistere possit, et vetus illa Ptolemaica dispositio ob Martem, ut dixi, aliquando Terras plus quam Sol appropinquantem (ut de Epicyclorum nimia superfluitate et Aequantum inconcinnis tricis, ac quibusdam alijs inconvenientijs nunc non dicam) locum non inveniat, sicque utraque positio antiquanda veniat, utique nostra haec neoterica inventio sola quadrabit, et in eorum locum hanc restitui veritatis circumspecta consideratio atque ab omnibus disconvenientijs vindicatio citra omne dubium postulat, quae tibi diligentius expendenda conferendaque relinquo. Omnium autem plenissime de hac hypothesi nostra, eam recte se habere iudicaveris, quando opus Astronomicum, quod auxiliante Deo ex professo hac de re luculenter tractabit, et figuris ac numeris rem ita se et non aliter habere evincet, laboribus nostris absolutum, publicam lucem viderit.

Sed est, quod obiter hîc conquerar. Quidam Dithmarsus ex Urso denominatus, cum ante quinquennium in cuiusdam nobilis et eruditi viri, ERICI LANGIJ nomine, mihi amantissimi, comitatu (illi enim tunc inserviebat) hîc unam atque alteram hebdomadam cum dicto Nobili hero suo commoratus fuisset, interea dum ego cum amico illo meo atque alijs nobilibus viris et Baronibus, quos secum huc adduxerat, hilarius convivo illis genialiter tractandis occupatus, iste Ursus clam chartas, instrumenta mea et quicquid oculis patuit, denotavit, schedas etiam, quascumque invenire potuit, furtim perlustravit. Inter alias vero unam, uti existimo⟨⟩, reperit, qua hypothesium nostra innovatio repraesentabatur, sed per incuriam aliquam non satis apposite formatam, siquidem Solarem orbem Martio totaliter includebat, ideoque tanquam inutilis inter alias quasdam chartas abiecta fuit. Ex hac iste procul dubio mearum hypothesium dispositionem hauriens, eam anno superiore in libello quodam, quem Fundamentum Astronomicum vocat, pro sua edidit, se hanc ante triennium in extremo quodam Poloniae angulo (ubi forte nunquam fuit) adinvenisse fingens, illustrissimoque Principi GULIELMO, Hassiae Landgravio, dedicare non est veritus. Sed ipsemet plagium hoc satis prodit, dum falso depictam figuram, quam forte⟨⟩ hîc reperit, imitatus Martium etiam orbem Solarem undiquaque ambire facit; ex quo enim vitium hoc per circinum commissum emendare non didicit, ignorans Solis orbem habere proportionem ad Martium sicut 4 ad 6 fere⟨⟩, et ob id Martem acronychum non excedere Solis ambitum, satis palam facit, se eius hypotheseos ne quidem solidam intelligentiam habere, nedum ut ipsemet eius inventor et author sit, ut perfricta fronte iactitat. Sic etiam multa alia in isto libello pro suis venditat, quae partim mihi, partim alijs praeripuit⟨⟩. Idem Astronomiae restitutionem insolenter pollicetur, cum et instrumentis et observationibus idoneis destituatur et insuper aliorum stipe vivat, ut de caeteris, quae in ipso ad tantum opus conficiendum⟨⟩ desiderantur, non dicam. Sic quoque in quibusdam alijs mihi contigit, qui hîc fuerunt et mea viderunt atque ex relatione nostra quaedam perceperunt, quaedam forte ex Chartis meis, qui⟨⟩ nunc in Germania partim conscribunt, partim alijs dictitant, ut vel hanc solam ob caussam consultum sit⟨⟩ operum meorum et inventionum publicationem maturare, ne tot a me suscepti in his studijs indefessi labores, tot vigilatae noctes, tot erogati sumptus (quae quartam Tonnae auri partem multum excedunt) ne tot, inquam, et plura alia Astronomiae instaurandae gratia a me vicenis iam annis exantlata, mihi ab alijs praeripiantur, ut si quid gratiae a ventura Posteritate sperandum esset, id illis cedat, qui ne minimum laboris, industriae aut impensarum huc contulerunt, accidatque mihi, quod de se olim festive cecinit MARO: Sic vos non vobis mellificatis apes etc. Sed valeant isti Plagiarij, satis etiam quibusdam in Germania probis et eruditis viris, quod alienis plumis instar corvi Aesopici sese ornent, cogniti.

Nunc ad tuas literas redeo. Quod Nobilissimo et amplissimo viro, Domino CURTIO, Caesareae Maiestatis Consiliario intimo⟨⟩ et Imperij Procancellario, viro etiam, ut audio, in Mathematicis excellenter perito, meum librum communicaveris, recte id a te factum est; multa enim de viri illius eximia prudentia et doctrina, pari virtute coniuncta, non solum abs te hisce⟨⟩ literis, sed etiam ab alijs iamdudum depraedicata sunt; quare et ego ipsum amare atque in pretio habere non omittam. Scribis illum, cum iudicium de ijs, quae libro meo continentur, ab eo⟨⟩ expeteres, respondisse, non temere de hypothesibus iudicandum, sed rem attentius considerandam esse, quae paulo ambiguosius a te referuntur, quam ut quid ille⟨⟩ senserit, inde⟨⟩ extricare queam; si enim me inconsiderate et non satis circumspecte hypotheses innovare existimat, et maiori attentione hac in parte opus fuisse, id illi forte ita videri potest, quod nondum omnia fundamenta et iustas rationes, quas in ista aliter quam ab antecessoribus nostris factitatum est, ordinanda hypothesi plenarie perspexerit; neque enim ex solo illo libro, quae me evidentia τεκμήρια⟨⟩ huc impulerunt, animadvertere potuit, cum illic omnia generali et latiori quadam indicatione breviter saltem indicem; neque enim ex proposito⟨⟩ ea de re in scripto aliquo Cometico agere placuerat, ideoque occasiones et ratiocinia, tum etiam particularia quaedam huc facientia⟨⟩ omittuntur, imo nullam forte eius inventionis in isto libro mentionem fecissem, ad opus Astronomicum eam, ubi convenientius locum mereretur, reservando, nisi quasi ex praesagio quodam id, quod mihi iam accidit, veritus fuissem, alios nempe mea mihi inventa praerepturos. Sin autem Dominus CURTIUS illa in hanc sententiam protulit, quod ipsemet non tam subito de mea hypothesium neoterica constitutione iudicium pronunciare velit, sed illud potius, donec res magis attente perspecta fuerit, differre, ea qua praeditus est prudentia eximia, admodum consulte⟨⟩ fecit; nam et plerique alias eruditi in Germania viri, dum re omni non satis provide et solerter expensa de meis hypothesibus iudicium praecipitârint, in varios errorum et praeiudicij scopulos impegerunt. Quidam ne Martia stella Soli in loco contactus orbium aliquando occurrat cursumque mutuum impediat, frustra veriti sunt, cum id nunquam contingere possit, siquidem, ubicumque Martis sidus in suo circuitu versatur, Solem pro centro respiciat. Circumferentiam vero et centrum circuli uniri quî fieri possit? Quidam vacuitatem, quae est in figura mei libri inter orbem ♄ et Fixarum sphaeram ab inferiori parte, minus concinnam reddere symmetriam suspicati, non animadverterunt, Sole in alteram orbis sui partem convoluto, quae prius vacua putabantur, adimpleri. Oportet enim, ut spatium undiquaque relinquatur satis amplum, quo Planetae superiores per quantitatem diametri orbis Solaris (qui Epicyclos veterum et motum annuum Terrae apud COPERNICUM excusat) respectu octavae sphaerae et Terrae nunc assurgant, nunc vero descendant. Quidam retrogradationes et stationes Planetarum non satis apposite sic salvari opinati sunt, cum nihil sit convenientius; nam quilibet superiorum Planetarum, dum Solem ubique loco centri respicit, per accidens Epicyclum motui proprio immiscet, et omnes istas passiones, quae per Epicyclos reales ab antiquis expediuntur, aut etiam per anniversariam Terrae convolutionem apud COPERNICUM, sic eadem forma, quantitate et eodem etiam tempore aptissime fieri possunt⟨⟩. Quidam Martis stellam terris aliquando, cum videlicet acronycha est, ipso Sole propiorem reddi, non satis tutum et conveniens esse timuerunt, cum id ipsa nos docuerit experientia. Unde etiam non minima caussa fuit, quae me ad hypotheses sic ordinandas adegit, ne Terra instar Astri triplici motu, cum sit unicum et simplex corpus admodumque grossum et grave, nec, ut aetherea illa lumina, ad perpetuo movendum habile in medio universi (ubi eam super stabilitate sua Opifex summus fundavit) ne moveatur in seculis, quiescere possit et tanquam patiens coelorum influentias activas in se recipere atque suo Archaeo cooperante, terrestria quaedam (ut sic dicam) astra, superioribus pulchre analoga indesinenter conformare, ut verum fiat illius HERMETIS TRISMEGISTI scitum dictum, etiam in toto Macrocosmo, quicquid est superius, idem esse inferius. Quidam etiam alias nonnullas circa nostram mundanorum corporum symmetriam et distributionem dubitationes moverunt, nullius tamen momenti, quas nimis longum foret nunc recitare⟨⟩; alias ubi meas resolutiones circa ea, quae opponi poterint, videris, haec omnia plenius cognosces. Si itaque illi omnes exemplo praedicti Domini CURTIJ censuram in re nondum penitius introspecta aliquandiu procrastinassent, multo sane prudentius⟨⟩ fecissent. Salutem quam ex ipsius ore mihi optas, eam ego vel millecuplam et illi et tibi vicissim precor, admodumque mihi grata est tanti viri erga me benevolentia, quam quocunque officij genere per me licuerit, non irrecompensatam relinquam. Consilium etiam eius, quo suadet, ne coeptum cursum interrumpam, quam arduo fieri poterit conamine, lubens sequar, sponte etiam mea huc propensus, ut si divinae clementiae ita visum fuerit, donoque suo mihi affulgere dignetur, Astronomiae instaurationem in ipsius gloriam et hominum f utilitatem Posteritati consecrem, ne frustra tandem hîc vixisse videar tanquam terrae inutile pondus, ut multis (dolendum) accidit. Et licet spontaneo ductu huc per mille anfractus et scopulos, alacri tamen et invicto animo (quod Deo acceptum fero) lubenter tendam, grata tamen nihilominus mihi est clarorum et eruditorum virorum cohortatio, quae validius addit incitamentum. Nam ut est in versu:

Fortis⟨⟩ et ad cursum⟨⟩ magno cum robore fertur,
Si tamen instiges, acrius ibit equus.

Addis insuper, saepedictum Dominum CURTIUM inter alia dixisse, me nimis multam fecisse mentionem et praedicasse nimium WINCHLERUM, hominem Astronomiae minus peritum. Atqui ego non tam ipsius scientiam in hac arte praedicavi, quam errores insulsissimos libello de Cometa isto ab ipso publicatos in apertum deduxi, et quaecunque ab illo imprudenter et inepte, tum etiam inscite prolata erant, redargui, adeo ut quibusdam, sed ijs potissimum, qui negotium hoc et errorum istorum foeditatem non perspiciunt, etiam nimius hac in parte fuisse videar. An hoc est hominem praedicare? Dicere eius argumentationes veritati vim facere, atque ipsius ratiocinationes saepenumero tot absurda committere, quot fere verba habent, tam multiplicem et prodigiosam subesse discohaerentiam, ut pudeat et pigeat referre, crassam insuper ignorantiam obicere, absurditates absurditatibus illum cumulare, ineptissimeque rem involvere usque ad nauseam, ipsius prolata sola reiectione ob discohaerentiam, aut commiseratione ob crassam ignorantiam potius quam longa refutatione digna esse, quod ab ipsius veritatis semita citra omnem verecundiam exorbitet, adhortari, ne tam absona in lucem edere amplius praesumat, ne seipsum suamque existimationem labe aspergat. Haec et plura eiuscemodi, quae invenies in ea tractatiuncula, qua WINCHLERI inepta et imperita placita cribravi, certe non prae se ferunt, me nimium illi tribuisse, aut ipsius doctrinam plus iusto extulisse. Neque etiam nimis ferociter aut rigide cum illo agendum duxi aut convicijs proscindendum, ut nunc quibusdam Theologastris pro more est, quemadmodum etiam tecum fecit impudens ille RAYMUNDUS. Sunt enim docti viri, utut in aliquibus graviter impingant, humaniter et moderate reprehendendi. Homines enim sumus, ab erroribus in hac caduca et multis obscuritatibus involuta ac inquinata vita prorsus immunes esse non possumus. Beatiores saltem, qui paucioribus et minoribus obnoxij sunt. Scio satis equidem WINCHLERUM plurima e tua Dialexi sibi arrogasse et non satis intellecta multipliciter depravasse, ideoque et ego pag. 446 idipsum brevibus indicare volui, et quae in Cometa anni 80mi ab illo ineptissime commissa in annotatiuncula quadam Dialexi tuae locupletatae circa Epistolam ad MILIUM et mihi communicatae addideras, satis animadverti. Verum ego de ista absurditate, qua Cometae illi Parallaxin 15 fere graduum assuit, et nihilominus longe supra Lunam collocat, nolui in libro secundo, ubi de Cometa anni 77 ex professo agitur, ullam mentionem facere, convenientius ducens, id in sequentem tertium Tomum, ubi aliorum de eodem anni 80mi Cometa sententiae sub incudem veritatis revocandae veniunt, reservare, ne confusio aliqua inordinata in his committeretur, cum methodice omnia tractanda sint. Illic, cum ipsius quoque prolata disquisivero, haec etiam nimia ineptitudo, quin indicetur, non praetereunda erit. Quod si quicquam⟨⟩ excogitare poteris, quod ad tuas assertiones stabiliendas faciat, non etiam id in mei gratiam intermittas. Quod MÆSTHLINUM ob egregiam Mathematum peritiam in pretio habes, adeo ut secundum a PRÆTORIO inter Germanos colloces, recte facis, eximij illius viri et doctissimi Astronomi ingenium atque industriam debita laude prosequendo. An vero is primus ante PRÆTORIUM, an vero secundus ab illo numerandus sit, ego non libenter dixerim. Est enim uterque in Mathematicis solide versatus, et ambo iudicio acri atque scientia perspicaci excellunt. MÆSTHLINUS tamen Astronomiam, quae omnium artium Mathematicarum Regina est, cui etiam reliquae ancillantur, plus excolere atque a mendis purgare laborat, cum PRÆTORIUS idipsum tum ob instrumentorum et aliorum adminiculorum defectum, tum etiam ob oculorum minus firmam valetudinem (ut ipsemet literis ad me non ita dudum perscriptis fatetur) tali opera supersedere cogatur. Utinam vero MÆSTHLINO satis solida et correcta non deessent organa, idque in aliqua copia, et sumptus otiumque illi suppeterent, sperandum foret, ipsum aliquid egregij in hac sublimi scientia moliri posse. At cum his subsidijs non ita, uti opus esset, instructus sit (nam instrumenta, quae hactenus illum habuisse ex scriptis eius colligo, tanto negotio rite exequendo nequaquam satis idonea et sufficientia sunt) non video, quomodo tam eximio labori ad optatum finem deducendo par esse possit; attamen in magnis voluisse sat est, et ut ille aiebat: »Est aliquid prodiret tenus, si non datur ultra«. Subiungis proinde, te id saltem nunc habere bona mea et MÆSTHLINI pace dicendum, quod ratio motuum Cometarum in hypothesin aliquam dirigi nequeat, quemadmodum in Planetis fieri potest, eo quod Planetarum motus integri sint mensurabiles, et quomodo accedant recedantque respectu Solis, pateat. In Cometis autem, cum non integras absolvant periodos, neque certa lege Soli appropinquent et ab eo expatientur, insolens tibi videtur iusta lege eorum motus astringere. Atqui hîc, dilecte THADDÆE, non satis considerate, quae in libro illo meo circa hoc ipsum negotium protuli, animadvertisse et expendisse mihi videris, siquidem illic hoc dubium et impossibilitatis suspicionem sufficienter resolvi atque amovi, imo ipsemet per hypothesin in illo Cometa extructam id fieri posse luculenter, tanquam in exemplo, ostendi. Fateor quidem, Cometas rarissime vel unicam per totum coelum revolutionem motu proprio absolvere, neque semper Solem instar centri, ut reliqui Planetae, respicere, ideoque digressiones ab eo non ubique consimili lege exhibere, quemadmodum nec Luna, quae Terram principaliter in sua convolutione observat. Attamen hinc non sequitur, prorsus impossibile esse, Cometarum motus sub normam aliquam per convenientem hypothesin redigere. Nam quod non totas periodos absolvere spectentur, difficultatem nonnullam in motu eorum regulando suggerit, possibilitatem omnem non excludit. Ex aliqua enim portione circuli data de tota eius circumferentia competenter ratiocinari adeoque centrum eius invenire non est adeo ἀδύνατον⟨⟩. Memineris eorum, quae ex Epistola quadam summi illius COPERNICI circa hanc rem citavi: »Ea quae aliquo modo determinata sunt, infinitam rationem habere non posse, sunt itaque comprehensibilia«. Et exemplum addit, quemadmodum si per tria puncta non secundum rectam lineam data circumferentia ducatur, non licet aliam superinducere, quae maior vel minor fuerit prius transmissa. Haec admodum scite et recte ab illo scripta sunt. Et si res ita se non haberet, quomodo affixarum stellarum motum unquam scrutabimur aut in rationem certam redigemus, ex quo ab eo tempore, quo observationes circa has innotuerunt, vix duo Dodecatemoria, sextam integri circuli partem emensae⟨⟩ sint? licet vero in earum curriculo explicando maior lateat difficultas, quam hactenus a quoquam animadversum est, attamen aliquam probabilem et apparentijs praeteritis satis consonam, ideoque et futuris, occasionem invenire, qua apparens in motu earum inaequalitas excusetur et numeratio observationi multis aevis consentiat, non duco inexplorabile, quin et longe minorem in Cometarum motu extricando difficultatem, licet et ea non exigua sit, subesse censeo, siquidem hi multo celerius sui cursus tramitem dirigunt, adeo ut nonnunquam intra duos vel tres menses quartam coeli partem, interdum plus, interdum vero minus, emetiantur, quae ab uno eodemque homine brevi tempore concipi possunt. In affixis vero sideribus multorum aevo opus est et plurimis plurimorum annorum intercapedine congestis observationibus, quae cum ab alijs atque alijs Artificibus non pari diligentia conquirantur, maximos hîc labyrinthos involvi video, licet et hi solerti iudicio et competenti adhibita limitatione extricabiles sint. Arduum itaque est et difficultatis non expers in Cometis, qui plenam revolutionem non perficiunt, motus harmoniam concinnare, at plane impossibile non est. Difficilia, quae pulchra, et viceversa quae pulchra, difficilia. Parallaxes Cometarum citra omnem erroris suspicionem persentiscere atque demonstrare grave est, et in hoc negotio inter praecipua reponendum. Neque enim, quae de his vel ab eximio illo REGIOMONTANO Franco vel nobili DIGESSÆO Anglo aut etiam a te et WITICHIO vel quibusvis, quod sciam, alijs in medium producta sunt, omnimode tam subtili inquisitioni satisfaciunt, idque eam praesertim ob caussam, quod motus proprij Cometarum rationem nullam habeant, quae neglecta Parallaxes inexplicabiles reddit, nisi admodum magnae forent, et motus ipse tardissimus; at plerumque Cometarum cursus genuinus adeo celer est, ut Parallaxes, quae per accidens tenorem inditum aliquantulum variant, respectu eius perexiguae sint. At licet hîc maior difficultas et pulchrior atque magis necessaria consideratio lateat, quam hactenus a plurimis, qui rem acu se tetigisse frustra existimârunt, exposita est (qua de re, si recte memini, te in quibusdam alijs literis aliquando admonui) longe tamen magis arduum, excellens utileque et pulchrum est Cometarum apparentem motum in certam normam instar astri alicuius perpetui redigere, ut demonstratio et numeri per totum eorum curriculum observationibus correspondeant, et motus circularis, vel quam minimum ab hoc deflectens illis attribuatur. Hoc opus, hic labor est, pauci hîc, quos ardens deduxit ad ardua virtus. Sic quod Solem non ubique respiciant crinitae, quemadmodum Planetae mundo coaevi, nihil probat eorum motus regulari non posse, nam et aliud quam circa Solem centrum habere queunt, utpote vel Terram observare aut quodvis aliud in universi capacitate punctum, cuius respectu cursus eorum ordinarius fiat. Accidit nihilominus aliquando, ut Solem quoque instar perpetuorum erronum centri loco habeat, quemadmodum in eo ipso Cometa, de quo nunc agimus, affatim a me demonstratum est, is enim circa Solem instar ♀ et ☿ obambulans neque etiam tam magnum designavit ambitum, ut Terram et Lunam simul includeret, et digressiones accessionesque ad Solem instar eorundem Planetarum oculis exhibuit. Cum enim circa principia suae apparitionis vix integro signo a Sole distaret prope primam triadem Novembris, deinde ad initia Decembris ab eo maximam elongationem assecutus, duobus quasi Dodecatemorijs removebatur, posteaque concinna lege huic successive denuo approximavit, ita ut circa ultimum suae evanescentiae limitem rursus non multo plus unico signo a Sole abscesserit, quam pulchram in motu eius respectu Solis harmoniam et egregiam cohaerentiam te ex observationibus et demonstrationibus meis libro⟨⟩ illo patefactis non satis perspexisse miror, nam et Cometa ille plane contrarium tuae sententiae hac in parte ostendebat. Omnia quae in coelo a mundi primordijs existunt corpora, motum regularissimum et perpetuum sortiuntur, quae vero ascititia sunt, si eum adeo absolute assequi nequeunt, attamen quam proxime aemulantur, ut ob id regulari omnimode non subterfugiant. Existimo autem Cometarum corpora, quae revera omnia ipso coelo procreantur, non esse ex tam solide concreta atque exaltata stabiliaque materia conformata, velut perennium illorum siderum. Ideoque non saltem consummationi obnoxia, sed etiam periodicum motum non continuant, nec eundem, quem exhibent, adeo praecise circularem aut forte e circularibus compositum, ut caeteri Planetae, praebent, interim tamen non multum a circularitatis perfectione degenerant, sed quoad fieri potest, Planetarum aemuli sunt, quae caussa me adduxit, ut illorum apparentem cursum in hypotheseos alicuius leges colligere non intentatum reliquerim.

Non igitur nimium tribuebam MÆSTHLINI hypothesi, sed quae in eo commendanda quaeque culpanda essent, reseravi, neque ob pulchritudinem et ingeniositatem veritati quidpiam detrahere volui, sed has per se seorsim praedicavi, quid verum vel falsum in illa hypothesi esset, alias pro virili indicans. To the most renowned man, most learned in every kind of doctrine, Master Thaddaeus Hagecius ab Hayck, distinguished Doctor of Philosophy and Medicine, his friend to be honoured above all.

Four letters from you reached me this past summer, my most loving and most learned Master Thaddaeus: the first, written on the Kalends of November Old Style in the year [15]88, I managed to obtain only on 27 April of this year; the second, written on 10 May of this year, I received on the last day of August; the third, which you had written on 20 May New Style, I obtained around the middle of September; and the last, dated 7 September, came to my hands on the second-to-last [day] of October — and this (as everywhere before) according to the old reckoning of the Julian year, which we still use. All of yours had been sent from Prague, and I came by them partly at Copenhagen, partly here at Uraniborg. Each one amply declared that great and constant favour of yours toward me and my studies, and bore no obscure witness to the unbroken continuation and welcome preservation — confirmed besides by several letters in the meantime — of the friendship eagerly contracted between us once at Regensburg about fourteen years ago, when the present Emperor was being inaugurated. For this singular and more-than-kindly affection of yours toward me I cannot but render you immense thanks, intending in turn, so far as is in me, to attend you and all that is yours with equal love and zeal as long as I live, and to keep a most grateful memory of you forever.

But as to my having sent no reply to those most learned and most friendly letters of yours over so long an elapsed time, and not having satisfied your wishes in this respect, do not suppose it was committed through any forgetfulness of you, or through negligence, or because of the mass of other business (though this past summer there was no small amount of it in various affairs); rather I would have you charge that long silence to the scarcity of messengers, since very rarely does anyone go from here to your parts to whom one might safely and surely entrust letters. For no such heap of business would have weighed on me, nor would I have allowed any other obstacles to prevail, but that I would have sent you some letter, had only a sure and suitable letter-carrier come my way in the meantime. And indeed, to confess the truth, not even now would I have sent these to you — chiefly on account of the impending winter (which throws up greater obstacles to their being delivered to you without fail) — had not that fourth and last [letter] of yours arrived the day before yesterday, and pressed home your vehement desire to know whether I had received those you sent earlier, together with an eager expectation of a reply. For these once received, even though at this time I am entangled in not a few affairs, yet, setting all aside, I judged that the reply should be deferred no longer. And although the opportunity for sending is no less difficult than before — indeed, on account of the winter, as I said, much greater — nevertheless I thought it must be met in this way: that I have arranged for two [copies] containing the same matter to be written out to you, of which I hope the one, conveyed to Rostock, the other to Lübeck or Hamburg, may by that route be carried more safely to you; for then it is not so likely that both will go astray.

Now then, that I may sufficiently answer your first [letter], time requires it, since the very anniversary of the day on which it was written by you falls now — too great a delay having intervened, which nonetheless, with you who interpret everything kindly and sincerely, will not find it hard to be excused for the reasons stated above. Afterwards I shall add some matter on the others too, and that in fewer words, since they both treat less principal things and chiefly remind me of what was sent in the first. So then, that the matter itself be not drawn out longer, hold this for what now comes to mind, to be answered in order, in reply to the things your first and more copious [letter] contains. Those two [letters], which you mention at the very beginning of it — the one entrusted to a certain French mathematician, the other to the Rostock postman — went utterly astray (as you yourself rightly conjecture); for had any of them been brought to me, I would not have put off my reply so long. But that those which I last sent you, together with our second book on the ethereal Phenomena recently observed, had the wished-for success and were welcome and a delight, I much congratulate myself; and that you judge so candidly of that writing of mine, and interpret everything in the better part, is something for which I owe you very great thanks. For although, both on account of age, and also of domestic cares and the medical profession, you are for the most part drawn away from this sublime kind of study (so that you cannot devote yourself wholly to it), as you yourself confess, yet in your younger years you were so diligently and successfully versed in these matters that, at this advancing age, you can with all the riper judgement rightly pronounce upon them. And although there are many other things that from time to time call you away from this sublime contemplation (as also happens to me not seldom), yet I do not doubt that you sometimes snatch yourself a few hours of leisure, so that, if not wholly and professedly, at least in some — and indeed a good — part, you indulge in these pursuits. For that love, so arduous toward an arduous subject, cannot wholly fail, nor be reduced to nothing and utterly abolished by the cares of household affairs and other studies, or even the troubles of old age. Your solid and ripe judgement, therefore, on my studies and writings, joined with equal even-mindedness, I not only set great store by, but even prefer to the censure of many others. And since you yourself have been much engaged for many years past with this subject which I embrace in these first books on the restoration of astronomy, and have illuminated it in no small part by your diligence and outstanding learning, the more readily do I share these things of mine with you, and most gladly confer with you about this task acceptable to us both, and submit everything without reluctance to your weighty judgement. And if you find in them anything worthy of admonition or correction, there is no reason why, on account of our friendship and your candid sincerity, by which you interpret everything in the better part, you should indulge me too much or favour my side more than is fair. For love and hatred (as it is in the Centiloquium) pervert the judgements of many. Rather, boldly and frankly, if you find anything liable to reproof, admonish me, and use in these matters (after your manner) a philosophical liberty: you will thereby do me a thing not only not unwelcome, but by far most acceptable, and conducive to a deeper investigation of the truth lurking in concealment. You will find me receiving your animadversions, and those of others who excel in keen judgement, patiently and candidly; and I shall not be outdone by you, who likewise have borne with a fair and grateful mind the corrections of yours that I admitted in your writings out of love of seeking the truth.

You report that you have not yet examined accurately enough the newly devised arrangement of the celestial revolutions [discovered] by me. Concerning it, then, you do rightly in not rushing your verdict, for it too was set down in the transmitted book only by a general indication (that comet requiring it, which no less than the other planets ordinarily revolved about the Sun, so that the hypothesis of its motion might be more conveniently and fully established). For very many particulars not obvious to everyone still remain to be explained in that very [arrangement], which there we either touched on lightly, or passed over in complete silence (since I did not treat this matter there professedly) — particulars to be laid open more fully in due time, with the favour of the Author of the stars and of all good, in another and special work on the restoration of astronomy, and to be demonstrated geometrically from the most certain observations and a norm of the motions analogous to the heaven itself, and resolved into numbers representing the celestial dance by undoubted traces. Meanwhile, that you may not be ignorant of it, know that, from the parallaxes of ♂ Mars, most diligently investigated in [its] acronychal transit, especially in the year [15]82 — since they were somewhat greater than the solar ones, and also from its swifter motion when in opposition to the Sun, as the Ptolemaic hypothesis holds — I have established with certainty, by careful and oft-repeated observation, that it necessarily approaches the Earth, when it is shining all night, nearer than the Sun ever can. And from this it follows, without any gainsaying, either that the Earth is revolved in an annual circuit, the Sun resting about the middle of the Universe, according to that distribution of the celestial revolutions which — borrowed from the ancient Aristarchus — that great and never-sufficiently-praised Copernicus brought to light in former years; or, if that cannot be granted on account of the not-slight physical absurdities thereby admitted, and also the authority of the Sacred Letters which gainsays it in more than one place, there remains no other account for saving the celestial Appearances, subject (as it ought) to the laws of Geometry and Arithmetic, than that very one which I devised about six years ago, in forming a hypothesis of the world-system. But that the Earth is by no means carried round annually, as the Copernican speculation will have it, nor that the motions of the Sun and of the eighth sphere can be saved through it, as that Artificer attempted by an arduous and ingenious mind — this both the greater swiftness in the stars that are reckoned fixed, than the reciprocation of the Equinoxes itself allows, and besides the altered latitudes of those same [stars] from the times of Ptolemy down to now, altered in proportion to the changed declination of the Ecliptic from the Equator, sufficiently prove; and therefore it is clear that the Earth, the Sun remaining unmoved, does not trace out the Ecliptic by an annual journey, and that these differences are to be excused not in it, but rather in the Sun itself. There is added too, in the place of no obscure testimony that the Earth does not move like the Sun, that the comets observed by us in former years in the highest ether about an acronychal position, with the utmost diligence as to all their appearances, did not at all obey the annual motion of the Earth — which would then necessarily either draw back their proper [motion], such as it is in the planets according to Copernicus, or at least somewhat retard it — by an indication sufficiently evident even from the comets alone, [showing] that the terrestrial globe in no way admits an annual circuit. Since, therefore, on account of these and other causes too, the Copernican reasoning cannot in any way stand in the truth of the matter, and that old Ptolemaic arrangement finds no place on account of Mars (as I said) sometimes approaching the Earth nearer than the Sun (to say nothing now of the excessive superfluity of epicycles and the inelegant tangles of equants, and certain other unsuitabilities), and so both positions come to be set aside — then assuredly this neoteric invention of ours alone will fit, and a circumspect consideration of the truth, and its vindication from all disagreements, demands beyond all doubt that this be restored in their place — which I leave to you to weigh and compare more diligently. But you will judge most fully of all that this hypothesis of ours rightly holds, when the Astronomical work — which, with God's help, will treat of this matter professedly and luminously, and will prove by figures and numbers that the thing is so and not otherwise — completed by our labours, has seen the public light.

But there is something I would here complain of in passing. A certain man of Dithmarschen, surnamed Ursus, when about five years ago he had stayed here a week or two in the company of a certain noble and learned man, Erik Lange by name, most loving toward me (for he was then in his service) together with his said noble master — meanwhile, while I, busied with entertaining genially that friend of mine and other noble men and Barons whom he had brought hither with him, was feasting rather merrily — this Ursus secretly took note of my papers, instruments, and whatever lay open to his eyes, and furtively scanned what sheets soever he could find. Among others he found one, as I think, on which our innovation of the hypotheses was represented, but through some carelessness not formed quite aptly, since it enclosed the solar orb wholly within the Martian one, and was therefore, as useless, thrown aside among certain other papers. From this, drinking in without doubt the arrangement of my hypotheses, he last year published it as his own in a certain little book which he calls the Fundamentum Astronomicum, feigning that he had himself devised it three years before in some remote corner of Poland (where perhaps he never was), and did not scruple to dedicate it to the most illustrious Prince Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse. But he himself betrays this plagiarism well enough, when, imitating the falsely drawn figure which he chanced to find here, he makes the solar orb encircle the Martian one too on every side; for in that he has not learned to correct this fault committed with the compasses — being ignorant that the Sun's orb has a proportion to the Martian roughly as 4 to 6, and that on this account acronychal Mars does not exceed the Sun's circuit — he makes it plain enough that he has not even a sound understanding of that hypothesis, far less that he is himself its inventor and author, as with brazen brow he boasts. So likewise he peddles many other things in that little book as his own, which he has snatched partly from me, partly from others. The same man insolently promises a restoration of astronomy, though he is destitute of suitable instruments and observations, and besides lives on others' alms — to say nothing of the rest that are wanting in him for the completing of so great a work. So too it has befallen me with certain others who have been here and seen my things and gathered some matters from my account, some perhaps from my papers, who now in Germany partly write [them] up, partly dictate them to others — so that for this single cause alone it is advisable to hasten the publication of my works and inventions, lest so many unwearied labours undertaken by me in these studies, so many sleepless nights, so much money spent (which much exceeds a quarter of a tun of gold) — lest, I say, so many things, and many others drained out by me now for twenty years for the sake of restoring astronomy, be snatched from me by others, so that, if any thanks are to be hoped from coming Posterity, it may fall to those who have contributed not the least bit of labour, industry, or expense hereto; and that there befall me what Maro once wittily sang of himself: Thus do ye bees make honey, but not for yourselves, and so on. But farewell to those Plagiarists — well enough known, too, to certain upright and learned men in Germany, for adorning themselves with others' feathers like the crow of Aesop.

Now I return to your letter. That you communicated my book to the most noble and ample man, Master Curtius, privy Councillor of His Imperial Majesty and Vice-Chancellor of the Empire — a man too, as I hear, excellently skilled in mathematics — you did rightly in this; for many things about that man's outstanding prudence and learning, joined with equal virtue, have long been proclaimed not only by you in these letters, but also by others; wherefore I too shall not fail to love and esteem him. You write that he, when you sought his judgement on the things contained in my book, replied that one must not judge of hypotheses rashly, but that the matter must be considered more attentively — which is reported by you a little too ambiguously for me to extricate from it what he meant; for if he thinks that I innovate hypotheses inconsiderately and not circumspectly enough, and that there was need of greater attention in this part, that can perhaps so seem to him because he has not yet fully grasped all the foundations and just reasons that are to be set in order in this hypothesis (devised otherwise than has been the practice of our predecessors); for he could not from that book alone perceive what evident proofs [τεκμήρια] drove me to this, since there I indicate everything only briefly, by a certain general and broader indication. For it had not pleased me to treat of this matter purposely in any cometary writing, and therefore the occasions and reasonings, and also certain particulars pertaining hereto, are omitted; indeed I would perhaps have made no mention of that invention in that book — reserving it for the Astronomical work, where it would more suitably deserve a place — had I not, as if by some presaging, feared that very thing which has now befallen me, namely that others would snatch from me my own inventions. But if Master Curtius brought those words forth in this sense — that he himself is unwilling to pronounce judgement so suddenly on my neoteric constitution of the hypotheses, but rather to defer it until the matter has been examined more attentively — then, with that outstanding prudence with which he is endowed, he acted most advisedly; for many otherwise learned men in Germany too, while precipitating judgement on my hypotheses without the whole matter having been weighed providently and carefully enough, have run upon various reefs of error and prejudice. Some have feared in vain lest the star of Mars should sometime meet the Sun at the point where the orbs touch, and impede their mutual course — since that can never happen, seeing that wherever the star of Mars is borne in its circuit, it regards the Sun as its centre. But how could the circumference and the centre of a circle be united? Some, suspecting that the void which is in the figure of my book, between the orb of ♄ Saturn and the sphere of the Fixed [stars] on the lower part, renders the symmetry less elegant, did not notice that, when the Sun is revolved into the other part of its orb, what were before thought void are filled up. For there must be left on every side a space ample enough, in which the superior planets, by the magnitude of the diameter of the solar orb (which excuses the epicycles of the ancients and the annual motion of the Earth in Copernicus), now rise and now descend with respect to the eighth sphere and the Earth. Some have thought that the retrogradations and stations of the planets are not aptly enough saved thus — though nothing is more fitting; for each of the superior planets, while it everywhere regards the Sun in place of a centre, incidentally blends an epicycle with its proper motion, and all those affections which by the ancients are dispatched through real epicycles, or by Copernicus through the annual revolution of the Earth, can thus be most aptly produced in the same form, magnitude, and likewise at the same time. Some have feared that it is not safe and fitting enough for the star of Mars to be sometimes rendered nearer to the Earth than the Sun itself (namely when it is acronychal) — though experience itself has taught us this. Whence too there was no slight cause that drove me so to order the hypotheses, that the Earth — being a single and simple body, and quite gross and heavy, and not, like those ethereal luminaries, apt for perpetual movement in the middle of the universe (where the supreme Maker founded it upon its stability, that it should not be moved for ages) — should rest, and as something passive should receive into itself the active influences of the heavens, and, with its own Archeus cooperating, should ceaselessly form certain terrestrial stars (so to speak), beautifully analogous to the superior ones, so that that knowing saying of Hermes Trismegistus might come true, [namely] that even in the whole Macrocosm, whatever is above, the same is below. Some have moved certain other doubts, too, about our symmetry and distribution of the world-bodies — of no moment, however, which it would be too long to recite now; another time, when you have seen my resolutions concerning the things that may be objected, you will know all these more fully. If, therefore, all those men had, after the example of the aforesaid Master Curtius, put off their verdict for a while on a matter not yet more deeply looked into, they would surely have acted far more prudently. The greeting which you wish me from his own mouth, that I in turn pray a thousandfold for both him and you; and most welcome to me is the goodwill of so great a man toward me, which I shall not leave unrequited by whatever kind of service may be in my power. His counsel too, by which he urges that I not interrupt the course begun — however arduous the effort it may take — I shall gladly follow, being of my own accord inclined this way too: so that, if it shall seem so to the divine clemency, and it deign to shine upon me with its gift, I may consecrate to Posterity the restoration of astronomy, to its glory and the use of men, lest I should seem at last to have lived here in vain, like a useless burden of the earth, as has befallen many (a thing to be lamented). And although of my own free guidance I gladly press hither through a thousand windings and reefs, yet with a brisk and unconquered spirit (which I count acceptable to God), nonetheless the exhortation of famous and learned men is welcome to me, which adds a stronger incitement. For, as it is in the verse:

The horse, though strong, and borne with great vigour to the course,
Will yet go the keener, if you spur him on.

You add, besides, that the oft-named Master Curtius said, among other things, that I had made too much mention of, and praised too highly, Winckler — a man little skilled in astronomy. But I did not so much praise his knowledge in this art, as bring into the open the most insipid errors published by him in his little book about that comet, and refuted whatever had been put forth by him imprudently and ineptly, and also ignorantly — so much so that to some, but chiefly to those who do not perceive this business and the foulness of those errors, I may even seem to have been excessive in this part. Is this to praise a man? To say that his argumentations do violence to the truth, and that his reasonings commit very often almost as many absurdities as they have words; that there underlies so manifold and prodigious an incoherence that one is ashamed and loath to report it; to charge him besides with gross ignorance, [to say] that he heaps absurdities upon absurdities, and most ineptly entangles the matter to the point of nausea; that his utterances deserve only rejection on account of their incoherence, or commiseration on account of his gross ignorance, rather than a long refutation; to exhort him, since he strays from the path of truth itself without any shame, not to presume to bring forth into the light anything so discordant any more, lest he besmirch himself and his reputation with a stain? These and many more of the kind, which you will find in that little treatise in which I sifted Winckler's inept and unskilled opinions, certainly do not show that I attributed too much to him, or extolled his learning more than was just. Nor did I think he should be dealt with too ferociously or rigidly, or torn apart with abuse, as is now the custom with certain theologasters — as that impudent Raymundus also did with you. For learned men, however gravely they stumble in some things, are to be reproved humanely and moderately. For we are men; in this fleeting life, wrapped and stained with many obscurities, we cannot be wholly free from errors. Those at least are happier who are liable to fewer and smaller ones. I know well enough, indeed, that Winckler arrogated to himself very many things from your Dialexis, and corrupted in manifold ways what he had not understood well enough; and therefore I too wished to indicate this same thing briefly on page 446, and I took sufficient note of the things most ineptly committed by him concerning the comet of the year [15]80 in a certain little annotation which you had added to your enriched Dialexis, around the Epistle to Milius, and communicated to me. But concerning that absurdity by which he sews onto that comet a parallax of nearly 15 degrees, and nevertheless places it far above the Moon, I did not wish to make any mention in the second book — where the comet of the year [15]77 is treated professedly — judging it more fitting to reserve that for the following third volume, where the opinions of others about the same comet of the year [15]80 come to be recalled to the anvil of truth, lest some disordered confusion be committed in these matters, since everything must be treated methodically. There, when I shall have examined his utterances too, this excessive ineptitude, that it be not indicated, will not be passable over. Only do you bring it about that I have his entire writing about the same comet, and also those of others, as many as you have so far obtained. Winckler, then, has been hewn down by me with a sufficiently strong axe, nor was he to be combed out with a sharper comb, unless it had been fitting to tear off his very skin and hair — which would be inhuman. Nor could I altogether pass him over (though he deserved it) on account of his unskilled and inept decision about that comet, since on the very frontispiece of the little book he pretended to Geometrical demonstrations, and within it put forward I know not what pompous argumentations out of Aristotle and parallaxes ill understood, by which he could throw dust in the eyes of the incautious and those who do not understand this matter deeply, and easily impose on them. May you, therefore, and other excellent men more knowledgeable in this matter, pardon me for having granted him the last place to close the rank — for I did this on purpose too, since he did not deserve to be set in a worthier place.

But I shall now let him too fare well, and proceed nearer to your own utterances, which are of greater moment and worthier of consideration. Most acceptable to me was that candour and integrity of yours, that you did not take it ill that I dissent from you in most things concerning that comet, and disapprove of some of your [opinions] by sure reasons. Indeed I knew well enough that you were endowed with this candid disposition, that you take it with no unwilling mind to be contradicted somewhere for the sake of eliciting the truth. You know well enough — our long-standing friendship demanding this very thing — that I wrote those things from no zeal of carping at you or belittling you, but only that I might vindicate that comet from the airy fumosities among which, in your earlier [book] about it, you had — against your own observations, through some carelessness — undeservedly placed it, and might deservedly set it among the brightest stars of heaven, in the most subtle purity of the ethereal region, where it truly displayed its courses; lest your singular learning and outstanding authority — which you have long since won for yourself among many in this matter too — should be an obstacle to the advancing and confirming of the truth. If you yourself acknowledge certain blemishes and errors in your writings, as you confess, do not forbear to wipe them away and clothe them in a more graceful form. For it is not so unbecoming to retract some things that were otherwise proposed without enough consideration; for this has been the practice before of many great and excellent men. One day teaches another; nor is the same man always endowed with the same circumspection of judgement.

What you report about that arranger of calendars — that he took your admonitions hatefully — I do not gladly hear, since it becomes learned men to bear the warnings of others moderately and patiently. I shall write to him at the first leisure, and among other things admonish him of his duty, and I shall be an exhorter that he love you and think honourably of you. You confess indeed, thereupon, that you neither can nor will defend your own slips — which you pronounce with a most candid and sincere judgement. Yet it appears that you are somewhat moved that I defended Maestlin against you, and you think that something can still be said to the contrary on your behalf, should you judge that my opinion ought to be opposed. But truly, my Thaddaeus, I wrote nothing for Maestlin in his favour that would prejudice you, nor did I do anything here from either hatred or friendship, but only that I might assert the truth inviolate. Maestlin I have never known by face, nor have I hitherto contracted any friendship with him by letters; indeed I have not heard anything reported about him by anyone else, so that he is quite unknown to me, save in so far as I have perceived his name from his learned writings; whereas you I have embraced most intimately, ever since familiarity first arose between us at Regensburg very many years ago, and how constantly I have preserved that same [friendship] by letters in the meantime — however we be sundered by the long interval of the roads — you yourself can be a witness fit enough; so that, if the truth were to be set aside for anyone's sake, it ought rather to be done for yours, on account of our long and close intimacy. But I judged that nothing here should be indulged to the affections, lest the truth, whose inner shrine it was my mind to open, be ruined — so that from this very thing it may be plain that I have brought nothing forward out of either hatred or even love for anyone, where the matter itself did not require it, but have striven, so far as was in me, to lay open the naked truth. But if you can devise anything that may serve to establish your assertions, do not on my account forbear that either. For if you oppose me in any part whatsoever, our friendship will not on that account waver in the least. To defend your own things, both is lawful and becomes you. Only see that you do it with just enough reasonings and confirmations. That you hold Maestlin in esteem for his outstanding skill in mathematics, so as to rank him second after Praetorius among the Germans, you do rightly, pursuing the genius and industry of that distinguished man and most learned astronomer with due praise. But whether he is to be reckoned first, before Praetorius, or second after him, I would not gladly say. For each is solidly versed in mathematics, and both excel in keen judgement and perspicacious knowledge. Maestlin, however, labours more to cultivate astronomy — which is the Queen of all the mathematical arts, and to which the rest are handmaids — and to purge it of faults; whereas Praetorius is compelled to forbear such work, both for the lack of instruments and other aids, and also for the less firm health of his eyes (as he himself confesses in a letter written to me not long ago). But would that Maestlin did not lack instruments solid and correct enough, and in some abundance, and that means and leisure were at hand for him: it would then be to be hoped that he could undertake something distinguished in this sublime science. But since he is not so equipped with these aids as would be needful (for the instruments which I gather from his writings that he has had hitherto are by no means suitable and sufficient for duly executing so great a task), I do not see how he can be equal to bringing so distinguished a labour to the wished-for end; yet in great things to have willed is enough, and as that man said: "It is something to advance just thus far, if it is not granted to go beyond." You add thereupon that you now hold at least this — to be said by your good leave and Maestlin's — that the account of the motions of comets cannot be directed into any hypothesis, as can be done in the planets, because the motions of the planets are wholly measurable, and how they approach and recede with respect to the Sun is plain; but in comets, since they do not complete whole periods, nor approach the Sun and depart from it by a fixed law, it seems to you unwonted to bind their motion by a just law. But here, beloved Thaddaeus, you seem to me to have noticed and weighed not carefully enough the things I put forth in that book of mine about this very business, since there I sufficiently resolved and removed this doubt and the suspicion of impossibility — nay, I myself showed luminously, as in an example, by the hypothesis constructed for that comet, that it can be done. I confess indeed that comets very rarely complete even a single revolution through the whole heaven by their proper motion, nor always regard the Sun as a centre, as the rest of the planets [do], and therefore do not everywhere exhibit their digressions from it by a like law — just as neither does the Moon, which principally observes the Earth in its revolution. Yet from this it does not follow that it is wholly impossible to reduce the motions of comets under some norm by a fitting hypothesis. For that they are not seen to complete whole periods suggests some difficulty in regulating their motion, [but] does not exclude all possibility. For from some given portion of a circle to reason competently about its whole circumference, and so to find its centre, is not so impossible [ἀδύνατον]. Remember those things which I cited about this matter from a certain Epistle of that supreme Copernicus: "The things which are in some way determined cannot have an infinite ratio, and are therefore comprehensible." And he adds an example: just as, if a circumference be drawn through three points not on a straight line, it is not permitted to superinduce another, greater or smaller than the one first traced. These things were written by him most skilfully and rightly. And if the matter were not so, how should we ever scrutinize the motion of the fixed stars or reduce it to a fixed reckoning, since from the time when observations concerning these became known, scarcely two Dodecatemoria — a sixth part of the whole circle — have been traversed? But although there lurks in unfolding their course a greater difficulty than has hitherto been noticed by anyone, nevertheless I do not hold it inexplorable to find some occasion, probable and consonant enough with the past appearances, and therefore with future ones too, by which the apparent inequality in their motion may be excused and the reckoning agree with observation over many ages; nay, I judge that a far smaller difficulty (though that too be not slight) underlies the unravelling of the motion of comets, since these direct the track of their course much more swiftly, so that they sometimes traverse, within two or three months, a quarter of the heaven — sometimes more, sometimes less — which can be grasped by one and the same man in a short time. But in the fixed stars there is need of the age of many [men], and of observations heaped up over a very great interval of very many years; which, since they are sought out by different and yet different Artificers with unequal diligence, I see the greatest labyrinths to be involved here, though these too be untanglable by keen judgement and with a fitting limitation applied. It is therefore arduous, and not free of difficulty, to frame the harmony of motion in comets which do not perform a full revolution; but it is plainly not impossible. The difficult things are the beautiful, and conversely the beautiful, the difficult. To perceive and demonstrate the parallaxes of comets beyond all suspicion of error is a grave matter, and to be set among the chief things in this business. For neither do the things produced about these — whether by that distinguished Regiomontanus the Franconian, or by the noble Digges the Englishman, or even by you and Wittich, or by any others, so far as I know — in every way satisfy so subtle an inquiry; and that especially for this cause, that they take no account of the proper motions of comets, the neglect of which renders the parallaxes inexplicable — unless they were very great, and the motion itself very slow; but for the most part the genuine course of comets is so swift that the parallaxes, which by accident vary somewhat the imparted tenor, are with respect to it exceedingly small. But although here there lurks a greater difficulty, and a more beautiful and more necessary consideration, than has hitherto been set forth by very many who vainly thought they had touched the matter to the quick (concerning which matter, if I rightly remember, I admonished you sometime in certain other letters), yet far more arduous, excellent, useful, and beautiful it is to reduce the apparent motion of comets into a fixed norm, like that of some perpetual star, so that the demonstration and the numbers correspond to observations throughout their whole course, and a circular motion — or one deflecting as little as may be from this — be attributed to them. This is the task, this the labour; few here, whom ardent virtue has led to arduous things. Thus, that the hairy ones [comets] do not everywhere regard the Sun, as do the planets coeval with the world, proves nothing of their motion not being able to be regulated; for they can have some other centre than about the Sun — namely, to observe either the Earth or any other point in the capacity of the universe, with respect to which their ordinary course may be made. It happens nonetheless sometimes that it has the Sun too, like the perpetual wanderers, in the place of a centre — as has been amply demonstrated by me in that very comet of which we now treat: for that one, going about the Sun like ♀ Venus and ☿ Mercury, did not designate so great a circuit as to include both the Earth and the Moon at once, and exhibited to the eyes its digressions and approaches to the Sun like those of the same planets. For since about the beginnings of its apparition it was scarcely distant a whole sign from the Sun, near the first triad of November, then, having attained its greatest elongation from it about the beginnings of December, it was removed by about two Dodecatemoria, and afterwards by a comely law again successively approached it, so that about the last limit of its vanishing it again withdrew not much more than a single sign from the Sun — what a beautiful harmony in its motion with respect to the Sun, and excellent coherence, [I marvel] that you did not perceive well enough from my observations and demonstrations laid open in that book, for that comet showed plainly the contrary of your opinion in this part. All the bodies which exist in heaven from the world's beginnings are allotted a most regular and perpetual motion; but those that are adventitious, if they cannot attain it so absolutely, nevertheless emulate it as nearly as may be, so that on that account they do not altogether escape being regulated. But I think the bodies of comets — which in truth are all procreated by the heaven itself — are not formed of so solidly concreted and exalted and stable a matter as those everlasting stars. Therefore not only are they liable to consummation, but also they do not continue a periodic motion, nor do they exhibit the [motion] they show as precisely circular, or perhaps composed of circulars, as the other planets [do]; yet meanwhile they do not degenerate much from the perfection of circularity, but, as far as can be, are emulators of the planets — which cause led me not to leave unattempted the gathering of their apparent course into the laws of some hypothesis.

You report, besides, that you do indeed assent to me in this — that I called Maestlin's speculation beautiful and ingenious — but that beauty and subtlety are not to be preferred to truth, which is the most beautiful virtue. Which I certainly nowhere, so far as I know, did; nay, everywhere, with what diligence was ever possible to me, I have laboured to dig out the hiding-places of truth, nor did I proclaim Maestlin's hypothesis as sufficiently ratified on account of its beauty and ingenious invention, but only wished to commend his industry, most worthy of all praise, by which he set about attempting that which all the rest reckon unwonted; nay, how much his hypothesis deflected from the very appearances of the comet — especially around the beginnings of its apparition, and much more yet near the end — I think no one has indicated more fully and surely than was done by me, even with Maestlin himself (as I hope) for judge. For I showed that that hypothesis, from the beginning, especially about the middle of November, by the reckoning thence deduced, exceeded by a whole degree the true place of the comet observed in the heaven. But at the end — namely about the middle of January and some days following, down to the 26th (on which that comet was still observed by us, though others thought it had ceased) — it retarded its course too much, so much so that not only by one or another [degree], but even, especially about the last days of January, it committed a deviation of nearly 3¼ degrees — which is surely intolerable. These and certain other things I examined about that hypothesis; nor yet was it on that account fitting to disparage Maestlin's excellent zeal and industrious labour in this invention, since no one among the other Germans has put forth things nearer to the truth — nay, indeed they have not even once dared to attempt so arduous a matter. For that, with Copernicus, he holds the Sun to rest and the Earth to be revolved annually, that comes to be easily pardoned him by mathematicians, since by this account too the appearances of the planets can be saved as nearly as possible, as that great Copernicus demonstrated. I did not therefore attribute too much to Maestlin's hypothesis, but laid open what in it was to be commended and what to be blamed; nor did I wish to detract anything from the truth on account of beauty and ingenuity, but proclaimed these in themselves separately, indicating elsewhere, to the best of my power, what was true or false in that hypothesis. That truth is the most beautiful and most excellent of all virtues I would readily grant you; for I too have always abhorred those who violate it of set purpose even in common conversation, and, so far as was in me, I have always striven so to inquire into it — not only in astronomical, but in chemical matters too, and indeed in all philosophy (to say nothing of theological) — that, by the granting of the divine goodness, I have ever desired to attain it as nearly as possible, sparing no labour or expense or even study in this matter. And therefore there is no reason for you to think that I set truth below beauty and ingenuity, nor did I wish to say that about Maestlin's speculation, as you gather from it. If my text be re-read, it will not show that; but since you will have it so, I will say something else, provided I be allowed to proceed paradoxically: Nothing is beautiful unless it be also true, nor true unless beautiful. But as to what you add — that neither Maestlin nor anyone else has attained, in the Theory of comets, the truth of which we speak, as is wont to be done in the motions of the other planets — here you seem to derogate too much, not so much from Maestlin as from me. The things wanting in his hypothesis of the comet I myself have brought sufficiently into the open, as I said a little before, and I showed in what place they were to be held. But I founded another account of the comet's revolution, by which — the Earth resting, that the physicists too might be satisfied — I have guarded its motion and appearances throughout the whole duration so precisely that nowhere has a digression of a single sixth of a degree been committed from the observations themselves. Whether any of all the planets has in its courses been brought, by any hypotheses hitherto given, to so great a precision of reckoning, anyone will discern who knows how to look into the heaven and the tables; for a deviation of many degrees is sometimes committed in these. Nay, not even the motions of the two luminaries have hitherto been so certainly unravelled, but that even in the Sun the heaven sometimes shows their appearances [to be] more than half a degree, and in the Moon more than a whole [degree], otherwise than the Canons — whether Copernican or Alphonsine — exhibit. Since, therefore, far greater agreement with the heaven has been worked out by my hypothesis as regards that comet than was ever before achieved in any of the planets, what, I ask, is the reason that you pass it by as less consonant with truth, or reject it altogether? Do not the numbers derived from the hypothesis on page 206 and the two following, when a comparison is made with those derived from observation on page 86 and the following, sufficiently prove that these things are so? If, therefore, you have anything that in this hypothesis of mine is dissonant from the celestial journey of the comet, bring it forth; and do not anticipate judgement on a matter not examined enough and less deliberated. I truly do not see what you can find wanting here, unless perhaps this moves you, that in the month of November I did not preserve precisely enough the uniformity of the motion in its circuit about the Sun. But how that came about, I myself sufficiently excused on page 133. And if it had pleased [me] to probe everything most subtly, it would certainly not have been impossible to maintain even this little inequality by a composite circular motion; but I deliberately passed it over, lest I entangle the matter too much, since it is also doubtful (as may be gathered from the foregoing too) whether comets exhibit a perfectly circular motion, like the rest of the planets, or not. How near, therefore, I have aimed at the mark, let those judge — if there are more, as you say, who tend with me thereto — provided they know how to discern the goal sought, by [the witness of] the heaven. The things, finally, which you object — that I erred in the numbers concerning the Maestlinian hypothesis — those, if by your good leave I may say it, my Thaddaeus, were rather committed by yourself, through some hindrances of business and a certain carelessness of deeper consideration; for first, as regards the Epoch of the mean commutation of the comet to the day of 24 November, which I establish the same as Maestlin [does], that stands well enough; and even if any very tiny fault lurked in it, I had nonetheless to assume the same [epoch] that he himself presupposes, because I wished to show that his hypothesis is not equally sufficient throughout the whole duration of the comet. The root of the motions established by him I was therefore not permitted to alter. In the rest, where you say that the equations computed by me do not agree with the Maestlinian and yours by a sufficiently evident discrepancy, this so seems to you in vain, while you wrongly apply my numbers — which I directed to 26 January according to the same hypothesis — to 24 November (from which I borrowed only Maestlin's Epoch, since this is the foundation of the remaining motions); a difference falling, as to time, of two months, in which interval the comet by its progression could by no means exhibit the same prosthaphaereses in excusing the inequality of motion. Re-read at least the things I left written on page 275 and some following, where I examine the Maestlinian hypothesis from the very demonstration and numbers: you will see that I adapt everything to the 26th day of January, so that I might show that, in its last apparition, the place of the comet diverged too much from his hypothesis. But the Maestlinian numbers, and yours which you adduce, regard 24 November, on which day Maestlin first used [it] in this Pragmatia. We are therefore in different terms, so that it is no wonder the numbers do not square with one another. But if you show that, on the very day to which I applied the Maestlinian hypothesis, I committed some fault in my numbers, I shall surely be grateful for your admonition. But if you find nothing, it will be superfluous to warn of these things. But it could easily happen to you, through some drowsiness amid perhaps other cares, that you did not notice that the reckoning you compare refers to different days. I, who am conscious enough to myself that the greatest diligence was applied in that calculation, as well as in that which the whole book contains, and that everything was twice or thrice repeated and diligently revised, could not wonder enough — when I first read your letter in the field (for I was, when I received it, gone out for a stroll for the sake of my mind, with two German students who had come from Germany and visited me at that same time), and when, while walking, we had come to the foot [of the letter], where you make mention of this discrepancy of numbers — [and] full of wonder I turned to the said students, bursting out into these words: "You will see, as soon as we have returned home, that that excellent old man has, in this diversity of the reckoning, taken one day for another, through the hindrances of business and other cares." Which also became plain enough, for, having returned home, presently snatching up my book, we sufficiently proved this very thing. If, therefore, you have nothing else, most excellent Thaddaeus, to object against the hypotheses established either by Maestlin or by me, or that may make for you against us, than can be gathered from these letters of yours, then assuredly even by your own judgement you now see well enough how little you have gained here. Nor did I disapprove of all the things put forth by you against Maestlin, but subscribed to those which seemed to merit just reproof; whereas those which were impugned by you on less just grounds I defended, that the inner shrine of truth might be preserved undefiled. And let these things suffice about these matters.

As regards the writers about the New Star, take pains that I may have at least the whole book of the Spaniard Muñoz, and, if it can be done, translated by someone there into Latin from the Spanish tongue; and also of those Italians whom you mentioned in your Dialexis, especially Cornelius Frangipani, and any others if there are, and that in good time, so that toward the end of the first volume I may be allowed to inquire into their opinions too. Apian's Astronomicum I have myself, and I know well enough that little or nothing can be gathered thence as regards shaking out the parallaxes of comets; but I was wishing for a particular book about a certain comet of the year 1532, formerly published by him — not so much because I hoped he had brought forth anything certain therein concerning the unravelling of parallaxes (for how too securely these have been passed over, not only by him but also by the rest of our predecessors, or at least too unsuitably treated, I have well enough perceived), but only that I might compare the apparent motion of the comet, doubtless described there by him with middling diligence, with Fracastoro's annotations in Italy about the same, briefly comprised in his book On Homocentrics, and hence elicit that the so monstrous parallax of Johann Vögelin of Vienna, which he strove to demonstrate in the same comet, was wholly erroneous; for that hairy [star] would have had to appear in Italy and Germany in sensibly different places of the heaven, if it had a parallax of nearly 35 degrees, as Vögelin will have it, or even 33, as you more precisely gathered by the assumption of the Earth's mobility from his own data. Wherefore, if you procure for me that little book of Apian from elsewhere now too, you will do a not useless thing. That Jean Pena the Frenchman wrote anything about comets I do not know, but that he treated excellently of the liquidity of the heaven, and of a nature not differing from airy subtlety, and certain other philosophical matters, I have learned from others, and also from the Bibliotheca Gesneriana. If, therefore, I receive this author too by your good offices, you will render me a most welcome thing, and perhaps not unfruitful for our common studies. The book which you had given me at Regensburg, I know not where and when I lost; but perhaps I shall have it from elsewhere — there is no reason for you to trouble yourself further in searching it out. For Werner's book On the Elements of Conics and the Motion of the Eighth Sphere, transmitted to me and obtained from Master Fabricius of good memory (for I hear that he has now yielded to the fates, which too I lament), I give you very many thanks; long and much was that book sought by me, nor was I ever before able to obtain it. Although, to say what is the case, as regards the treatise on the motion of the stars, it has not in the recess what it promises in the approach, as I shall show elsewhere, when occasion is given for dealing with this matter.

These things I had to answer to what is contained in the letters first sent to me. But satisfaction must be made too to the things written afterwards in the annexed slip. You rightly warn me there that, by the tenor of the Privilege obtained through you, I ought to send three copies of my lucubrations to the Imperial Chancery — which I shall also gladly do, when the whole work, or at least the first book, has come forth, to which that diploma, together with the rest obtained from other Kings, will be prefixed; for I did not think it necessary before this, since the tenor of the Privilege is not annexed to this second part [now] published, nor have I allowed copies to be communicated to others save very few — the rest, to the number of 1500, being still reserved until the first Volume has seen the public light. You have rightly, therefore, excused this delay, for it was not fitting to offer a maimed work to the Emperor; but I shall take pains, so far as is in me, that the first book be completed at our press this winter, and then there will be time enough for me to perform what I am bound to. You ask, moreover, in the same place, that I greet the above-named Master Curtius, when I shall have sent the copies, and visit him by letters, and contract a friendship with him through these — which I shall also most gladly effect, since I understand that man to be worthy to be loved, and excellently knowing in these studies too. Lest, therefore, there be any delay in my will, if perchance before the Frankfurt fair the first book cannot be wholly committed to the press, I have now written out these [lines], which I send you to be delivered to him, that he may the sooner know my zeal and service toward him; and if this winter he writes anything back and brings forth his weightiest judgement on our labours and studies, he will bring it about that I shall be the more eager to deal with him often by letters and to confer about this sublime art. But I see that I have perhaps spoken with you too prolixly, as regards the first Epistle. Now therefore I shall turn to the rest, in which I shall be briefer, since they too of themselves do not require so luminous a reply.

From your second letter I understand well enough, most loving Thaddaeus, how anxious you were about the earlier ones, brought to me together with that book of Werner's not quickly enough; where you also report (which I attribute to your goodwill toward me) that you eagerly awaited my first Volume of the recent celestial Phenomena at the spring fair, and even now at the most recently past autumn one. But the delays you have understood in some part from my last [letter] to you; the principal cause was that I was destitute of sufficient printing-paper, nor was that which I had procured from the neighbouring Duchy of Mecklenburg brought in time and abundance enough — and also because that very [paper] which came to me thence was less white, and not elegant enough, so that what had begun to be printed on it last winter, now that better and purer [paper] has been obtained, I judge it more advisable to reprint, even though some expenses may perhaps prove wasted. For I wish my things to come forth into the hands of the learned clean and beautiful, so far as it can be done. But that I be not entangled with so many further difficulties and delays in seeking paper from Germany, I took care this summer to have a mill for making paper built on this island, with the addition too of what serves for the grinding of flour and the preparation of skins — so that a single wheel, which equals about twelve ells in height, serves all these works; the water itself too [is brought] in sufficient abundance where before it was dry, derived hither through various channels and collected in great capacity before a dam quite high and broad, with very many fishponds disposed here and there for the same use too. All which are now completed, procured with labour and diligence of execution and no middling expenses, so that on that account I hope to have henceforth paper suitable for my Printing-workshop, in whatever form I shall have wished, sufficiently — lest the procrastination of others in sending it any longer be an obstacle to sending my labours into the light. For I wish that this whole island, however small it be, may by both land and water, and indeed by the very air, serve our Urania. And let this suffice about these things.

I grieve that that incomparable man Andreas Dudith has met his last day. I had resolved to contract friendship with him by letters, and, at Praetorius's request, to send to him one of my students with an instrument suitable for astronomical observations; for he, while he was alive, had asked this of Praetorius — but I have now learned that the fates have snatched him from us, not indeed without grave loss to the Literary Commonwealth, to which he was most profitable. You add that he had long ago dealt about Wittich's books with the latter's sister, who, however, being rather difficult, was unwilling to allow any of them to be sold off. But, since woman is ever a varying and changeable thing, it may be that she is now more amenable to selling them. I therefore most lovingly ask that you would, through some sure and faithful man dwelling at Wrocław, see that he transact with her in my name, so that I may obtain at a just price all her stock of books and papers; I shall take care that the money promised her be paid in good time and surely — for I should not wish the books of that distinguished mathematician to perish, or to come into the hands of those who do not hold them in esteem. His Copernicus, and what he wrote about Triangles, I would seek before the rest, and also any other manuscripts there may be. There will be found among the other books that Astronomicum of Apian's too, of which mention was made before, which, bought for 20 florins, I gave to him as a gift while he was here, nor do I reclaim it after his death, save as bought at a just price. If his sister grants me this together with the rest, I will make you a present of that Astronomicum of Apian's, provided it still be there; for I myself have another copy at hand, as I indicated before too.

Your third letter contains almost nothing that much requires a reply, except that you remind me about that astrological book formerly given me by you — what the author's name was, and with what diligence (though in vain) you inquired; in which [matters] too you excuse yourself as to why that noble sent to the Emperor returned to me without your [letters], which I had however received from elsewhere before he returned hither. The fourth and last [letters], which I obtained two days ago, treat of whether the earlier ones were procured, and whether I received them. To these there is no need to reply at greater length here, since you have now sufficiently learned what you desire. About my earlier book too, when it will be published — which you long to know, at the desire of Master Curtius and your own as well — I have already said enough. As for the Commentaries of Trapezuntius, especially as regards the two first books, I would not indeed have thought it useless to publish them, but neither very necessary, since they contain nothing peculiar that was not almost said before. My Printing-house scarcely suffices for striking off what I myself have written, far less should I waste time and pains on the works of others. If you ask back that book, and someone is willing to bring it to you, I will gladly send it back on some occasion given. The things which you afterwards add in your last letter, about the new rumours destructive of politics — or rather of politicians — I do not wish to reply to, since I would rather philosophize with you. Let there be left to the Politicians their own eccentricities and anomalous motions, which they will never, in this confusion of an unclean world, regulate by any hypothesis of prudence, or render concentric; so that I cannot but marvel and at the same time grieve enough that men, microcosms, for whose sake the macrocosm with all its adornment is believed to have been chiefly founded, so greatly stray, that they can never be reduced to any fixed harmony. For one wiser than the wise errs, and there is almost nowhere any place for truth or repentance; but since God bears these things, who could amend them, why should we not patiently endure, since we cannot correct them? This at least, as regards politics, I will add: that I gladly learned from you that the most illustrious Archduke Maximilian has passed the Polish borders safe and unharmed, and returned safe to his own. For many and excellent things in praise of that Prince have been proclaimed to me by Austrian Barons and nobles who have often visited me here; and as Maro said: "More pleasing is virtue coming from a beautiful body."

But I see that, while I labour at a reply to each of your letters in this single one, I exceed the laws of an Epistle by excessive prolixity — which, on account of our friendship (which makes it that I gladly deal at length with you), and also because I wished now to recompense my so-long-interrupted reply by some little abundance, I trust will be easily excused with you. Now therefore I shall not detain you longer with my writing, but I have you commended, now and ever, to God Best and Greatest, who governs and preserves you and all your actions, with your whole family, happily and long unharmed. And if you receive these letters written out twice in the same tenor, deem it done for the cause stated at the beginning, and — I would ask you — interpret everything, according to your goodwill and love toward me, in the best part, as you are wont.

But one thing more I will say. Since I know that you take an intense delight in this subject, which treats of astronomical matters and comets, I have added to these an Apologetic reply, which a few days ago I wrote against the objections of a certain most learned man and distinguished mathematician, Doctor John Craig the Scot, practising medicine at Edinburgh — by which [objections] he attempted to overthrow most things, indeed the chief things, in our second book (especially as regards the ethereal position of the comet which we maintain, and as regards our hypothesis), in letters sent to me this summer, a copy of which I have had transcribed [and enclosed] together [with this]: so that — being a man otherwise most learned and endowed with sagacious judgement — he might patronize his Aristotle, to whom he seems too much addicted, as regards the sublunar generation of comets from dry fumosities, and the reality of the celestial orbs. I therefore, having got some breathing-space from the summer cares — though still distracted by very many and various occupations — yet did not think it should be wholly omitted, but that I should meet his objections by this Apologetic writing, and guard the truth once asserted and set down in writing against any assaults whatsoever, and establish it more fully. Do you, therefore, at your leisure read these things, and, if it please, re-read them — for mention of you too is made therein here and there — and afterwards open to me by letter your weightiest judgement on these matters, and, as arbiter, settle this philosophical dispute, with the weightiest censure of Master Curtius adjoined, if it be not troublesome to him. For since he is excellently skilled both in Jurisprudence and in mathematics, he will of all men best be able to take cognizance of this case and decide it with an even scale. Adjoin too the opinions of other learned men, exercised in this art and excelling in judgement, as many as you have near at hand; and if there shall be any who are willing to confer with me by letters about this arduous business — whether known, or before unknown — they will do me a most acceptable thing in this part, and one not useless to astronomical studies and to the unravelling of this question about comets. But this also I would have you besides advised of: that I have been asked by others, but chiefly by the most noble and ample Master Heinrich Rantzau, our King's Vicar in Holstein and the annexed territories, to permit the letters of the most illustrious Prince Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, and of other learned men in Germany — of which, dealing with me about this subject, I have very many — together with my reply to each, to be made public; which too, since it makes not a little for the illustrating of this matter, I thought by no means to be refused. For I have resolved to publish separately, in a certain fourth Volume, all these — and any others about this business that I shall have obtained from the learned — together with our replies and Apologies, should any presume to contradict anything further. This I wished to indicate here for this reason: that everything be written to me about this matter, attentively and deliberately and luminously enough, whether by you or by others through you — which I also advised before, if I rightly remember. But it is time that I now at last draw in the sails and let the pen here rest. Wherefore again and again, fare you most happily, my most loving Thaddaeus; and see that I be revisited, before Easter, by your most learned letters, and also by those of that distinguished Master Curtius, if it be so convenient for him; and now a third time and more, farewell.

Given at Uraniborg on the very Kalends of November, Old Style, in the year 1589.
TYCHO BRAHE, in his own hand.

POSTSCRIPT.

Since we have happened upon mention of that Imperial Diploma, there is something about which I should like to treat further with you concerning it, if it seem good to you. For that privilege seems to me too brief, and not ample or handsome enough for the magnificence of the art and the matter being treated; and I have another, granted me by the King of France, which is much more capacious and splendid — written, moreover, on parchment, and signed below by the royal hand — a copy of which I send you. Daniel Rogers too, a distinguished man and among the foremost in letters, the second envoy of the Queen of England into Denmark, when he was lately here with me, promised that he would obtain for me a privilege likewise from his most serene Queen, which, he said, would be drawn up so elegantly and honourably that it would far excel even the French one, and would likewise be engrossed on parchment, subscribed by the Queen's own hand. So too from the King of Spain, unless the excessive distance of places delay our wishes, I hope for an ample and magnificent privilege; I should not wish, therefore, that His Imperial Majesty, who holds the first place among the Kings of Europe, should suffer the privilege he has clemently granted me to be comprised in terms smaller and briefer than theirs. For that seems to me neither honourable enough to His Majesty himself, nor sufficient to the excellence of so sublime an art.

I ask, therefore, by our friendship, that you be willing to bring it about that in the Imperial Chancery a new privilege be drawn up for me, still ampler and more elegant, and that it be engrossed on parchment — if that is ever the custom — subscribed by the Imperial hand. The Magnificent Lord Curtius will readily lend both you and me his help in this matter; and since he is especially knowledgeable in, and fond of, this art, he will be able to sprinkle in something to the honour and commendation of this art and these labours, of a kind to rouse in many Kings and Princes, and in others too, love and favour for this sublime study and its votaries — which I leave to be arranged at the discretion of his own prudence and of his goodwill toward these arts and toward me (though unknown to him) and my labours.

And if it should also seem good to you both, I do not think it so necessary to mix in much mention of Religions and occasions of scandal, which might perhaps prove odious to some; for I gladly abstain from such things as could give offence to any party in Religion; nor do I willingly take upon myself judgment concerning these matters — let that rest with the Creator of all, who best knows how to separate the kids from the lambs, hypocrites from those who worship him sincerely, since he searches the hearts of all. The tumultuous World already has heresies and confusions enough; there is no need that I should found new ones, or stir up and reopen those introduced by others. The World, since it loves darkness rather, on account of its evil works, does not suffer open truth to be declared in these matters. Nor is it fitting for anyone to play the part of an Apostle unless he has been sent by divine [commission] and not by men — lest that saying apply to those who arrogate this to themselves from the light of nature alone, which is in the Prophet: "You have run, and no one sent you; you have cried out, and no one opened your mouth for you." But this is beside my purpose.

You see now, my Thaddaeus, what it is, and why I should wish certain things in that privilege to be changed, enlarged, and amplified, and then also to be written out on vellum, so that they may be kept safer and longer. Yet I ask nothing here except what is fitting, and what you may hope to obtain in my name without difficulty or trouble. For the last one sent to me on paper I sent twelve florins as payment to the scribe, according as the Assessor had reckoned. If a new and more magnificent one is again to be engrossed on parchment, I shall be ready, even now, to recompense the labour of writing more liberally than the Assessor shall judge fitting. Yet in this whole matter I commit everything to be done, or left undone, to your counsel and help. And if it seem to you advisable, and you obtain the new privilege without difficulty, I should wish you to see to it that it be sent to me here in Denmark, even at my own expense, before the coming Frankfurt fair, so that, having received it early enough, I may be permitted to prefix it to the frontispiece of the earlier volume, and then at last see to it that both parts, once printed, are conveyed to the Emperor's Chancery, as I have undertaken to do. Again, farewell, and happily.

I ask you too, my Hagecius, that if you have kept copies — as I suppose you have — of any of the letters I have sent you, you would have them transcribed and share them with me. For I have set these aside for a volume that is to contain Astronomical Letters. Wherefore, if you send them soon, you will do me a thing especially welcome.

T. BRAHE.

Correspondent. Thaddaeus Hagecius ab Hayck = Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku (Thaddaeus Hagecius, 1525–1600), Bohemian court physician, astronomer and astrologer to Maximilian II and Rudolf II at Prague, author of the Dialexis de novae … stellae … apparitione (1574). Tycho and Hagecius met at Regensburg in 1575 at the coronation/inauguration of Maximilian II as King of the Romans ("about fourteen years ago, when the present Emperor was being inaugurated") and corresponded for the rest of Hagecius's life.

This is the foundational document of the celebrated Tycho–Ursus plagiarism controversy; Tycho here gives his own narrative (the 1584 visit in Erik Lange's retinue; the discarded diagram with the solar orb wrongly enclosed within the Martian one). - Header dateline. The subscription reads Datae Uraniburgi prim. Cal. Novemb. stylo veteri Anno 1589. Cal. Novemb. = the Kalends of November = 1 November; prim. is read as prima(s)/primis ("on the very Kalends"), giving 1 November 1589 Old Style, in agreement with the edition label "1589. 1. november." (Note: pridie Cal. Novemb. would mean 31 October; the abbreviation here is prim., not prid., and the label confirms 1 November — so the Kalends reading is followed and no discrepancy arises.) Tycho explicitly states he writes stylo veteri (O.S.); Denmark used the Julian calendar.
- In-letter dates are reported, not converted. Tycho carefully tracks the style of each incoming letter: Hagecius's first written Cal. Novembris veteri stylo anno 88 (1 Nov. 1588 O.S.), received 27 April [1589]; the second written 10 May [O.S. implied], received 31 August; the third written 20. Maij novo stylo (20 May N.S.) — Hagecius in Imperial/Catholic Prague used New Style, and Tycho notes the contrast — received mid-September; the fourth dated 7 September, received 30 October ("the second-to-last [day] of October"). Tycho stresses that his own datings are secundum veterem anni Iuliani numerationem, qua etiamnum nos utimur ("according to the old Julian reckoning, which we still use"). The 10-day O.S./N.S. gap is exactly the calendar matter flags; no date here is silently reconciled — they are reproduced as Tycho gives them.

Named cruxes.
1. The Werner title (liber Werneri de elementis cornicis). The phrase de elementis cornicis is obscure; cornicis (gen. of cornix, "crow," or of cornicula) does not yield an obvious sense for a Werner mathematical tract. Werner's known work is De motu octavae sphaerae (Nuremberg 1522), which Tycho names in the same breath. cornicis may be a corruption/abbreviation in the witness, or a reference to a now-obscure subsidiary tract. Rendered tentatively as "On the Elements of the Cornicula" with the Latin kept; flagged, not smoothed.
2. "prim. Cal. Novemb." abbreviation. Read as primis/prima Calendis Novembris = 1 November; secure given the label agreement, but the unexpanded prim. is noted for the record (it is not pridie).
3. Identity of Raymundus. The impudens Raymundus who attacked Hagecius is named only by this single word; the specific person is not securely fixed here.
- What the recovered text is. The bulk of the postscript to Tycho's 1 November 1589 letter to Thaddaeus Hagecius, entirely absent from the prior transcription (only the closing valediction after it survived as the published tail).
- Recipient. Thaddaeus Hagecius ab Hayek (Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku, 1525–1600), Emperor Rudolf II's imperial physician at Prague, Tycho's friend and correspondent since they met at Rudolf's 1575 coronation as King of the Romans; Hagecius had earlier given Tycho his treasured manuscript of Copernicus's unpublished Commentariolus [Christianson, On Tycho's Island; Christianson, Measure of the Heavens].
- Subject. Tycho's campaign to secure printing privileges (early-modern copyright protections against piracy) from multiple European monarchs for his forthcoming publications. He already held one from the King of France, on parchment with the king's own signature, and was expecting one from the King of Spain; Daniel Rogers, styled here "the Queen of England's second Legate" to Denmark, had promised to obtain an English one — this matches Thoren's account that "Tycho entrusted his hopes for an English copyright to Daniel Rogers, who came to Hven in 1588" pursuing exactly this [Thoren, Lord of Uraniborg]. Rogers died in 1590, within a year of this letter. Tycho now asks Hagecius to use his friend "Magnificus Dominus Curtius" to obtain a grander Imperial privilege from the Prague Chancery, on parchment, under the Emperor's own hand — this is very likely the same Curtius (probably the Bohemian chancellor Jacobus Curtius) in whose Prague house Tycho would later live and die, per Dreyer, Tycho Brahe (1890).
- Religious reticence. Tycho explicitly asks that the privilege not dwell on "Religionibus et scandalizationibus" — a rare direct statement of his deliberate avoidance of confessional controversy in his printed apparatus, leaving judgment of hypocrites and true worshippers to God alone; he closes the point with an unattributed prophetic saying about running unsent and crying out unheard, which echoes (without being verbatim) the Vulgate topos of the false, unsent prophet, cf. Jeremiah 23:21 ("non mittebam prophetas, et ipsi currebant") — flagged as a crux below.
- The Epistolae astronomicae. Tycho asks Hagecius to return transcripts of his own letters "for a volume that is to contain Astronomical Letters" — a direct, seven-years-early reference to the Epistolae astronomicae, printed at Uraniborg in 1596 [Nielsen, Dansk Bibliografi, no. 430], documenting the collection process for that book.
- Cost of the document. Tycho records paying 12 florins to the scribe for the paper copy of the privilege, per a "Taxator" (official assessor)'s valuation, and offers to pay more liberally for a parchment original — a concrete data point on the cost of engrossing an official instrument in Tycho's world.
- Correction. Restored Dreyer's reading in three places on TBOO VII pp.213 and 218: 'conicis' for the corrupted 'cornicis' (Werner's treatise on the conic elements, not an invented 'Cornicula'), 'numero 1500' for an erroneous 'numero 150' in the count of Progymnasmata copies still held back from circulation, and 'Astronomicas' for the ungrammatical 'Astronomicus'. The English translation was corrected to match: the fabricated title 'the Cornicula' now reads 'Conics', and the reserved-copy figure now reads 1500.
