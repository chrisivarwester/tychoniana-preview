The original

TYCHO BRAHE IOH. ISACIO PONTANO. S.

BINAS iam nuper a te, dilecte mi IOHANNES ISACIDE, intra paucos dies accepi literas. Ex quibus cognovi te salvum ad patrem Amstelrodamum hinc redijsse, quod et libenter audio. Respondebo vero ad utrasque hisce unis, et ad priores primum. Indicas illic statim ab initio, commilitonem IOANNIS ANTONIJ tecum fuisse, literas accepisse, contenta earum placuisse, ideoque ipsum itineri confestim se accinxisse. At vero, nec is nec eius socius, tanto interim elapso tempore, huc advenit. Si postmodum id fiet, mihi non erit ingratum. De Sphaeropaeo quod scribis, illum proprijs literis suam sententiam mihi aperturum, nihil tamen etiamnum ea in re effectum est. Nam nec literas nec globos exinde ab eo obtinui. Videtur avaritia nimium fascinatus, a qua Astrali infectione, sive coeli sive soli vitio, plerique eorum, qui caetera propemodum religionis quodam affectu effectuque superare vel didicerunt vel videri volunt, se vindicare non sustinent. Si is promissis non steterit, erit forte alius, qui lubens hanc conditionem arripiet. De doctis vestratibus quae scribis, mihi inprimis accepta sunt. Magnus ille SCALIGER si huc in Daniam (uti significas) advenerit, haud vulgaris felicitatis loco, si me invisere non degravatus fuerit, duxerim. Virum enim illum, etsi absentem, mihique de facie adhuc ignotum, semper (uti etiam eius meretur virtus et doctrina incomparabilis) in precio habui, suspexique plurimum. DOUZAM filium summae expectationis iuvenem, quem a vobis in Germaniam adeoque vicinam nobis Cimbriam discessisse, meque, si alia non obstiterint, hîc alloqui decrevisse ais, nondum vidi. Si is deinceps accesserit, erit mihi ipsius congressus perquam iucundus. Nam et eius ingenium et ad solidam eruditionem arduum enixum diu multumque magni feci. R. SNELLIUM, eximium illum Lugdunensium Mathematicum, quod meo nomine conveneris, et consilium de filio isthuc mittendo aperueris eiusque disciplinae commendando, gratum est; longe vero adhuc gratius, quod iam in tantum ipsi placuerit, ut filium suum huc vicissim mittere, et meae fidei concredere (dummodo de meâ voluntate prius certior redditus fuerit) animum induxerit. Ego meum TYCHONEM isthuc sub initia futurae aestatis, ut studijs ingenuis operam navet, amandare constitui, et SNELLIJ disciplinae morumque inspectioni submittere, nihil de ipsius fide et integritate addubitans, quin seduli et fidelis praeceptoris ac moderatoris munus sit obiturus. Si ille suum GILLEBRODUM, aut antea vel etiam post, huc ad me miserit, erit is mihi pariter serio curae, tam quoad liberalem institutionem et honestatis morum inspectionem, quam caetera ad decentem educationem sustentationemque necessaria. Intelligo ipsum esse ferme eiusdem aetatis cum meo filio, id est, iam primos pueritiae annos, sive secundum septenarium excessisse, atque in hac tenera aetate Arithmetices et Geometriae studijs initiatum esse, idque non saltem ex tuis literis, sed et a FRANCISCO TENGNAGEL, qui nunc mecum est, a DOUZA iuniore mihi commendatus, perlubenter cognovi. Erit itaque is, ubi huc appulerit, mihi loco mei filij, quemadmodum spero, optimum illum SNELLIUM meum quoque vice sui habiturum; sicque quisnam maiori affectu suum adoptivum prosequi pergat, invicem certabimus. De sumptibus, quae ad honestam mei filij sustentationem requiruntur, haud gravatim providebo. Quae omnia ut ad ipsum Dominum SNELLIUM meo nomine referas, te rogatum habeo. Scripsissem ipsemet illi, modo suppeteret otium, verum fiet id alio tempore commodius. Literas eius, quas illum ad me misisse innuis, necdum accepi. Sed sive is scribat sive non, nihilominus mea de ipso sincera persuasio ac fiducia sartae tectae manebunt. Ipsum esse quam occupatissimum, tam in privatae suae scholae, quam publicis Academiae laboribus exequendis, et ab alijs et a praedicto TENGNAGELIO iam antea satis percepi, ut ob id eum, quod scriptione supersederit, non invite excusatum habeam. De re pecuniaria patri tuo scribo, quare hîc subticeo. Nunc ad alteras tuas.

Iconem mei isthic expressam unâ accepi, licet et antea eadem per CUNRADUM nostrum (qui hanc Coloniae sculptam existimavit) huc delata sit. Non satis placet, quod duobus haemisphaerijs imaginem istam inniti fecerit artifex. Videtur id minus gravitatis prae se ferre. Is quidem forte suo quodam consilio, nec sine industrio labore sic faciendum censuit. At mihi, si rotundas istas picturas omisisset vel adhuc in aliqua altera (si sic volet) imitatione omiserit, rem magis arridentem praestiterit: sic quoque pomposa illa circumscriptio nimia est, neque enim tanto me dignor honore. Opera nonnulla ex meis, de quibus solicite inquiri asseris, brevi (uti spero) lucem publicam videbunt. Nactus enim iam tandem sum chartopaeum. Ideoque papyrus Typographiae nostrae inserviens (cuius defectus editionem hactenus retardavit) nunc indies in propria hîc moletrina conficitur. Ad ea, quae de SNELLIO et utriusque nostrum filijs in his posterioribus repetis, iam antea responsum est. SCALIGERI de Emendatione Temporum praestans illud opus recognitum, ubi illud consummatum fuerit, si mihi transmiseris, recte feceris. Quoniam vero de anni Iuliani cum coelesti comparatione ad amussim praefinienda is inibi non minima ex parte agit, quo epocham antiquissimam illam Iudaicam a se productam vel quamvis aliam cum aequinoctijs hoc aevo coelitus quam accuratissime depromptis penitius conferat (siquidem ea, quae ex libris et tabulis quibusve petuntur, nimium devia sunt) additi quaterniones aliquot primi Capitis Progymnasmatum nostrorum Astronomicorum, quod de Solari curriculo tractat. Isthic pagina 13 utriusque aequinoctij rata momenta certis annis applicata inveniet; quae cum vetustissimis illis pariter, respectu bisexti Iuliani, se habentibus non inutiliter componet, verioremque anni coelestis et Iuliani ad invicem commensurationem quam antea hinc extricabit. Quamvis autem ea, qua ego isthic utor, pro hisce proximis seculis anni aequinoctialis Solarisque quantitas saltem ab Epocha centum annorum derivata sit, observationibus videlicet REGIOMONTANI eiusque discipuli GUALTERI, idque peculiari quodam consilio, ut inaequalitatis labyrinthos, a COPERNICO et alijs nimia tum (uti ego sentio) credulitate et licentia introductos isthic loci evitarem: attamen si comparatio cum observationibus ante plurima secula habitis rite instituatur, ijs praesertim, quae fidem indubitatam merentur (quales ego HIPPARCHI prae ceteris esse iudico, qui ad utriusque luminaris apparentias diligentissime attendit) vix annua quantitas a nobis illic praedefinita in pauculis, utpote 5 vel 6 scrupulis secundis deficiet, ideoque annorum determinationem nequaquam attinget. Disparitatem vero, tam in anni quantitate, quam in motu fixarum stellarum, non adeo esse magnam tantique momenti, prout suspicantur Astronomi, meae observationes selectioribus vetustis applicatae demonstrant. Haec per occasionem ad Dominum SCALIGERUM ut referantur, efficies. Alibi pluribus, quid in his expertus sim sentiamque, declarabo. Tu autem illustrem illum virum, nostri seculi decus, ex me quam officiosissime saluta, resalutaque. Tum quoque doctiss. Dominum SNELLIUM, DOUZAM enim ad vos nondum reversum existimo. Verum antea eius hîc praesentiam avide expecto. Vale mi IOANNES, et de mea erga te benevolentia nihil ambige, quam semper tibi probatam relinquam, praesertim si (quando ad nos redieris) mea opera tibi uspiam commodare possit.

Iterumque vale.
Raptim Uraniburgi 5. Septembris Anni 95.
Tycho Brahe,
manu propria.

Tycho Brahe to Johannes Isaacius Pontanus — greeting.

A short while ago, within a few days, I received two letters from you, my beloved Johannes Isaacides. From them I learned that you had returned safely from here to your father at Amsterdam, which I am glad to hear. I shall answer both in this one letter, and the earlier one first. You indicate there, right at the outset, that the comrade-in-arms of Joannes Antonius had been with you, had received the letters, that their contents had pleased him, and that he had therefore at once girded himself for the journey. But in fact, in all the time that has since elapsed, neither he nor his companion has arrived here. If it happens later, it will not be unwelcome to me. As to what you write about the Sphere-maker [Sphaeropaeus] — that he would open his mind to me in a letter of his own — nothing has yet been accomplished in that matter. For I have obtained from him neither letters nor globes. He seems too much bewitched by avarice — that Astral infection from which (whether by the fault of the heavens or of the soil) most of those who have either learned, or wish to seem, to surpass [other men] in well-nigh everything else by a certain disposition and effect of religion cannot bring themselves to free themselves. If he does not stand by his promises, there will perhaps be another who will gladly seize this offer. What you write about the learned men of your country is especially welcome to me. That great Scaliger, if he comes here to Denmark (as you intimate), I should reckon it a piece of no common good fortune, should he not think it beneath him to visit me. For that man — though absent, and as yet unknown to me by face — I have always held in esteem and looked up to greatly (as indeed his worth and incomparable learning deserve). Dousa's son, a young man of the highest promise, who you say has departed from you for Germany and so for Cimbria neighbouring us, and has resolved to address me here, if other things do not stand in the way — I have not yet seen. If he comes hereafter, meeting him will be most delightful to me; for I have long and much prized both his talent and his strenuous striving toward solid erudition. That you met R. Snellius, that distinguished Mathematician of Leiden, in my name, and disclosed the plan of sending [my] son thither and commending him to his instruction, is welcome to me; but far more welcome still is that it has now so pleased him that he has made up his mind to send his own son here in turn, and to entrust him to my good faith (provided only that he be first assured of my willingness). I have determined to send my Tycho thither at the beginning of next summer, that he may apply himself to the liberal studies, and to submit him to Snellius's instruction and to his oversight of his conduct, in no way doubting his good faith and integrity, but that he will discharge the office of a diligent and faithful preceptor and guardian. If he sends his Gillebrodus here to me — whether before, or even after — that boy too shall be in equal earnest my care, both as regards a liberal upbringing and the oversight of an honourable conduct, and the other things necessary to a fitting education and maintenance. I understand that he is of nearly the same age as my son — that is, that he has now passed the first years of boyhood, or the second seven-year span — and that at this tender age he has been initiated into the studies of Arithmetic and Geometry; and this I learned with great pleasure not only from your letter, but also from Franciscus Tengnagel, who is now with me, commended to me by the younger Dousa. And so, when he arrives here, he shall be to me in place of my son, just as I hope my son will have that excellent Snellius to himself in place of his own [father]; and so we shall contend with one another, [to see] which of us shall press on to cherish his adoptive [son] with the greater affection. As for the expenses required for the honourable maintenance of my son, I shall not grudgingly provide them. All of which I ask you to report to Master Snellius himself in my name. I should have written to him myself, had I but the leisure; but that will be done more conveniently at another time. His letter, which you hint he sent to me, I have not yet received. But whether he writes or not, my sincere conviction and trust regarding him shall none the less remain whole and intact. That he is exceedingly busy, both in carrying out the labours of his own private school and the public labours of the University, I learned well enough already, both from others and from the aforesaid Tengnagel, so that on that account I readily hold him excused for having forborne to write. Concerning the money matter I write to your father, wherefore I keep silent about it here. Now to your other letter.

The portrait of me engraved there [isthic] I received together [with the letter], although the same had also earlier been conveyed here by our Cunradus (who reckoned it had been engraved at Cologne). I am not altogether pleased that the craftsman has made that image rest upon two hemispheres. It seems to carry less of dignity. He indeed perhaps, on some plan of his own and not without industrious labour, judged that it ought to be done thus. But to me, if he had omitted those round pictures — or shall yet omit them in some other rendering (should he so wish) — he will render the thing more pleasing. So too that pompous inscription is excessive, for I do not deem myself worthy of so great an honour. Some of my works, about which you assert that anxious inquiry is being made, will shortly (as I hope) see the public light. For I have at last secured a paper-maker; and so the paper that serves our Press (the lack of which has hitherto delayed the edition) is now being made daily here, in [our] own mill. To the things you repeat in this latter [letter] about Snellius and the sons of us both, an answer has already been given above. That excellent revised work of Scaliger's On the Emendation of Times — when it is completed, you will do rightly if you transmit it to me. For since he deals there, in no small part, with precisely determining the comparison of the Julian year with the celestial [year] — in order more deeply to compare that most ancient Jewish epoch which he has put forward, or any other, with the equinoxes most accurately derived from the heavens in this age (since those that are sought from books and tables of whatever kind are too far astray) — I have added several gatherings of the first Chapter of our Astronomical Progymnasmata, which treats of the Solar course. There, on page 13, he will find the established moments of either equinox applied to certain years; and these, set beside those most ancient ones, likewise reckoned with respect to the Julian bissextile, he will not unprofitably compare, and from this will work out a truer mutual commensuration of the celestial and the Julian year than before. But although the quantity of the equinoctial and Solar year that I use there, for these next centuries, is derived only from an epoch of a hundred years — namely from the observations of Regiomontanus and of his pupil Walther — and that by a particular design, that I might in that place avoid the labyrinths of inequality introduced by Copernicus and others with too much (as I judge) credulity and licence: nevertheless, if a comparison be duly instituted with observations made many centuries before — especially those that deserve undoubted trust (such as I judge Hipparchus's to be above the rest, who most diligently attended to the appearances of both luminaries) — the yearly quantity predetermined by us there will scarcely fall short by a few, namely 5 or 6 seconds [scrupulis secundis], and therefore will in no way reach [the magnitude of] the determination of years. But that the discrepancy, both in the quantity of the year and in the motion of the fixed stars, is not so great and of such moment as Astronomers suspect, my observations, applied to the choicer ancient ones, demonstrate. You will see to it that these things are reported, as occasion offers, to Master Scaliger. Elsewhere, at greater length, I shall declare what I have found and think in these matters. But do you, in my name, greet and greet again that illustrious man, the glory of our age, most dutifully. And likewise the most learned Master Snellius — for Dousa, I think, has not yet returned to you; but I eagerly await his presence here beforehand. Farewell, my Johannes, and have no doubt of my goodwill toward you, which I shall always leave proven to you — especially if (when you return to us) my service can anywhere be of use to you.

And again, farewell.
In haste, at Uraniborg, 5 September, the year [15]95.
Tycho Brahe,
in his own hand.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Joh. Isacius Pontanus = Johannes Isaacius Pontanus (Johannes Isaksen, 1571–1639), Dutch-Danish historian, geographer and physician, son of Isaac Pietersz, the Dutch diplomatic resident at Elsinore; he had stayed on Hven shortly before this letter (Mosley, Bearing the Heavens, records that he later "followed [Tycho] in reprinting Tycho's remarks"). The Greek-style patronymic Isacides / vocative ISACIDE = "son of Isaac."
  • Ioannis Antonii — "Joannes Antonius": almost certainly Giovanni Antonio Magini of Bologna (cf. glossary), whose commilito ("comrade-in-arms," here a fellow-traveller/associate) was expected; left as named in the letter, the identification flagged as probable.
  • Sphaeropaeus (also spelled Sphaeropoeus) — literally "globe-/sphere-maker"; a craftsman from whom Tycho expected celestial globes (globos). Not a proper name but an occupational designation; kept and glossed. Tycho's complaint of avaritia ("avarice") names no individual securely.
  • Magnus ille Scaliger … illustrem illum virum, nostri seculi decus — Joseph Justus Scaliger (1540–1609), the great chronologist, then at Leiden; Tycho had not yet met him face to face. His opus de Emendatione Temporum recognitum = the revised (second) edition of the De emendatione temporum (1st ed. 1583; the revised edition appeared 1598). The literature confirms Tycho still pressing this chronological dispute with Scaliger as late as 7 Jan 1600 (Benátky letter, on the Dionysian epoch).
  • Douza filium … Douza iuniore — Janus Dousa the Younger (Jan van der Does, 1571–1597), son of the Leiden statesman-humanist Janus Dousa the Elder; here "Dousa's son … the younger Dousa," travelling toward Cimbria (Jutland / the Danish lands, see below).
  • R. Snellius … Lugdunensium Mathematicum — Rudolph Snellius (Rudolph Snel van Royen, 1546–1613), professor of mathematics at Leiden (Lugdunum Batavorum), father of Willebrord Snell. The reciprocal-fosterage plan: Tycho would send his son Tycho the Younger to Snellius at Leiden, and Snellius would send his son Gillebrodus = Willebrord Snell (Snellius, of Snell's law) to Hven. The phrase secundum septenarium excessisse = "[the boy] has passed the second seven-year span," i.e. is about 14; an astrological-physiological reckoning of life in seven-year stages (cf. the climacteric in the 1571 Wolf letter).
  • Franciscus Tengnagel — Frans Gansneb Tengnagel von Camp (c. 1576–1622), Tycho's Westphalian assistant (later his son-in-law and literary executor), then resident on Hven; "now with me," commended by the younger Dousa.
  • Cunradus noster — "our Cunradus": a member of Tycho's household/circle (likely Conrad Aslaksen / a familiaris), who had earlier brought the engraved portrait and supposed it cut at Cologne (Coloniae); left as named.
  • Icon … duobus haemisphaerijs … pomposa illa circumscriptio — Tycho criticises an engraved portrait (icon) of himself: he dislikes the device of resting the image on two hemispheres and finds the encomiastic inscription (circumscriptio) too grand. (This bears on the De Gheyn / Cologne portrait tradition; the editor holds the Altena study of the De Gheyn engraving.) No silent identification of the print is made here.
  • chartopaeum … in propria hîc moletrina — Tycho reports securing a paper-maker (chartopaeus, Gk.-Lat. coinage) so that paper for his Press (Typographia) is now produced "in [our] own mill" (moletrina) on Hven — the documented Uraniborg paper-mill, whose earlier lack had delayed publication.
  • Progymnasmatum nostrorum Astronomicorum … primi Capitis … pagina 13 — the Astronomiae Instauratae Progymnasmata (Tycho's "Preludes," pr. 1602/1610), here cited while still in press; the first Chapter "on the Solar course" (de Solari curriculo) and the table of equinoctial moments on page 13 are given as a specific citation. Quaterniones = printer's gatherings (quires).
  • Astronomy / chronology. anni Iuliani cum coelesti comparatio = the comparison of the Julian year with the celestial (tropical) year; bisextus Iulianus = the Julian bissextile (leap-day); anni aequinoctialis Solarisque quantitas = the length of the equinoctial / solar year, here derived from a hundred-year epocha on the observations of Regiomontanus (Johannes Müller of Königsberg, 1436–1476) and his pupil Bernhard Walther (Gualterus, 1430–1504) of Nuremberg. inaequalitatis labyrinthos … a Copernico et alijs … introductos = the "labyrinths of inequality" (the trepidation / variable-precession schemes) which Tycho judges Copernicus and others adopted with "too much credulity and licence." Hipparchi … apparentias utriusque luminaris = Hipparchus's observations of the appearances of "both luminaries" (Sun and Moon), which Tycho rates above all ancient data.
  • scrupulis secundis — "seconds" (of time/arc); scrupulum secundum is the period term for the second sexagesimal subdivision; rendered "seconds," term flagged. The clause annorum determinationem nequaquam attinget ("will in no way reach the determination of years") is a compressed astronomical claim — Tycho's point is that an error of only 5–6 seconds in the annual quantity is far too small to affect the dating of years; rendered closely with the literal sense kept and the implication bracketed.
  • sartae tectae — the legal-proverbial phrase sarta tecta ("[kept] in good repair, whole and intact"); rendered "whole and intact."
  • Places. Amstelrodamum = Amsterdam; Lugdunum (Batavorum) (Lugdunensium) = Leiden (Tycho's Lugdunensis Mathematicus is the Leiden professor, not Lyon); Cimbria = the Cimbrian peninsula / Jutland (the Danish lands generally), "neighbouring us"; Colonia = Cologne; Uraniburgum = Uraniborg on Hven.
  • Edition-field anomaly. The source's edition field reads "Contra vitam monasticam ad Bern. Mattium epistola" — this is a separate Pontanus work, not the edition of record for this letter, and appears to be a edition mis-tag. Per the edition's convention the edition of record is Dreyer, Opera Omnia (the Epistolae, vols VII–VIII; this Tycho→Pontanus letter is in TBOO VIII). The witnesses field is empty; no MS shelfmark is supplied by the source.
  • Calendar / place. Raptim Uraniburgi 5. Septembris Anni 95 = "In haste, at Uraniborg, 5 September [15]95" (O.S.; Denmark kept the Julian calendar). Dateline and catalogue label agree (5 Sept. 1595). manu propria = "in his own hand."
  • Spelling note. The source prints Sphaeropaeo (l. 4 of para 1) and the variant elsewhere; SGALIGERI / SCALIGER / SCALIGERUM vary in the source; praesfinienda (for praefinienda) and praestito terit (for praestiterit) are printed thus and kept verbatim in the Source block, normalised silently only in sense in the translation.
  • Correction. Latin corrected against Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII p.373: 'praestito terit' (wrongly split) -> 'praestiterit'; 'SGALIGERI' -> 'SCALIGERI'; 'praesfinienda' -> 'praefinienda'. None of these affect the published English translation, which was already correct.
