The original

ERUDITISSIMO ET PRÆSTANTISSIMO VIRO, DOMINO IOHANNI KEPLERO, ORDINUM PROVINCIALIUM STYRIÆ MATHEMATICO EXIMIO, NUNC GRATZIÆ DEGENTI: AMICO SUO INPRIMIS DILECTO. GRÂITZ.

QUOD prioribus tuis ante duos Menses ad me datis, Eruditissime et dilectissime Magister KEPLERE, nihil antea responderim, nulla tui, nulla negotij, de quo inter nos actum, oblivione, sed potius, ut temporis oportunitati, quæ in hac Aula non semper suppetit, me accommodarem, atque eo promptius atque felicius omnia exequerer, factum esse credas. Vix enim aliqua interea commoditas intermissa, sed et ultro aliquoties captata. Quod ut rectius percipias, quæ interea acta sunt, brevibus narrabo. Paulo postquam Cæsarea Maiestas, Pilsena relicta, huc Pragam in arcem solitæ residentiæ reversa esset, me Benatica ad se evocavit, ante hebdomadas plus minus septem. Pauculis autem diebus postquam hîc comparuissem, ad audientiam clementissime vocatus, in conclavi suo de varijs mecum loqui ultra sesquialteram horam dignabatur, et postea eodem die etiam per dimidiam circiter. In primo vero accessu, postquam ipsius Maiestas ea, quæ ex me scire averet, proposuisset, iussit, ut, si quæ forent, quæ in rebus meis non ita succederent, prout ab initio mandârat, et si quæ præterea desiderarem, libere exponerem. Ego submisse actis gratijs, me talia in chartula aliqua denotaturum atque Domino BARVITIO traditurum respondi: quod et placuit, imo, ut hoc facerem, mandavit. Cum autem viderem tam exoptatam cum ipsius Maiestate de rebus necessarijs loquendi mihi ultro benignissime oblatam occasionem, de Te quoque, quæ in animo habebam, proloqui non intermittendum duxi, sed arrepta aliqua ansa, ex ea, quæ de instrumentis meis et siderum observationibus atque Astronomia instauranda, incidebat mentio: me solum tanto oneri, licet plurima iam huc conducentia in promptu habeam, non sufficere, sed collaboratoribus opus habere aiebam, ut citius et plenius res tanta in ipsius Maiestatis atque Imperij honorem et commodum absolveretur. Respondit eius Maiestas, se ut votis meis hac in parte satisfiat provisuram. Ibi cum inter alia adminicula huc facientia de te quoque huc e Styria accersendo et per unum atque alterum annum sustentando, tuo etiam discrete addito nomine insinuassem, ipsius Maiestas mox benignissime annuit, atque ut inter cætera, quæ desiderarem, id quoque chartæ mandatum Domino BARVITIO traderem, iussit, nec ullam hac in re difficultatem movit. Locutus sum postea particularius cum Illustri et Magnifico Domino CORRADUCIO, hac ipsa de re, et conditiones tuas illi sigillatim aperui, atque hoc solum pro tempore rogavi, ut literas a Cæsarea Maiestate impetrare vellet ad Principem tuum et Amplissimum Ordinem Styriacum, quibus rogarentur, ut per biennium in gratiam Cæsareæ Maiestatis hîc mihi præsto adesse tibi liceret, et nihilominus annuo isthic frui stipendio, quod per Cæsaris liberalitatem centum florenis annuatim augeri exoptarem, siquidem omnia hîc cariora sunt, quam in Styria. Is vero se lubentissime negotium hoc prolaturum, atque ut exoptatum eventum sortiatur, curaturum benigne pollicitus est. Id saltem suasit, ut denuo rem inter cætera, quæ requirerem, Cæsari, sive oretenus, sive per literas, subiectissime proponerem, atque ut ad se eius executio devolveretur, impetrarem, tunc suo auxilio et consilio nequaquam nobis defuturum. Interea dum ea, quæ ipsius Maiestati submisse proponere constitui, animo volvo, et calamo concipio, iniunxit ipsius Maiestas mihi peculiare quid, pro se, quam primum fieri possit, efficiendum. Eram adhuc in publico hospitio apud Insigne aurei Gryphei, ubi cum nulla tranquillitas eiuscemodi quid agendi concederetur, conduxi aliam, non longe a Generoso Domino HOFFMANNO, habitationem minus irrequietam, in quam vix migrâram, et res meas utcunque disposueram, cum denuo me in arcem accersit Cæsar, et quærit, an ea parata sint, quæ a me fieri voluit. Ego vero per hospitij incommoditatem et migrationem in alias ædes, me excusavi, et nacto iam meliori otio, omnia quam primum fieri possit, intra duas vel tres hebdomadas effecturum promisi, cui etiam responso ipsius Maiestas clementissime acquievit. Interim vero dum hoc, uti pollicitus sum, ago, quod sane non parum requirit diligentiæ et laboris, non audeo ab ipsius Maiestate audientiam humillime petere, et de ijs, quæ in votis habeo, etiam me ipsum non parum concernentia sollicitare, ne videar propria potius curare quam ea, quæ ipsius Maiestas a me requisivit, suadentibus etiam alijs, ut propria interea differam, quo absolutis et exhibitis ijs, quæ ipsius Maiestas a me desiderat, eo facilius, captata sic benevolentia, id quod exopto, assequar.

Atque hæc præcipua causa est, cur responsionem ad priores tuas hactenus distulerim: nolui enim quippiam ad te scribere, antequam res omnis ex voto peracta esset, et literæ, quibus huc honorifice vocareris, conscriptæ. Interim vero ante paucos dies superveniunt alteræ tuæ, quæ licet prioribus non dissimilia ab initio referant, tamen circa calcem catastrophen quandam insperatam habent, qua priora consilia non saltem tua, sed et mea non parum collabascunt. Intelligo enim inde Salarium Styriacum tibi, uti et alijs quibusdam, renunciatum, et insuper exilium impositum, nisi decreto istic promulgato parueris, ut ob id in Wirtenbergensem Ducatum, Patriam tuam, redire malis. At quia in hac rerum tuarum conturbatione et prioris sententiæ frustratione, non habeo præsentius consilium, quod ex re nata tibi dem, quam ut, postposita omni mora, te huc conferas, sive solus, uti antea, sive etiam advecta simul coniuge, unâ cum supellectile et rebus necessarijs, pro quibus omnibus facile, uti arbitror, viaticum invenies, urgente præsertim sic necessitate instanti. Neque enim tibi deerunt tui Mecænates et fautores; aut si opus fuerit, pecuniam, quæ ad iter requiritur, alicubi interea mutuo sumes: ubi adveneris, tum præsentes rectius et tutius inter nos agemus, et rationes inveniemus, quibus hisce difficultatibus subveniri poterit, atque tibi et tuis rebus convenienter prospici, ne tibi opus sit tam cito in Patriam tuam redire, aut alio declinare. Interim nullam occasionem præterlabi sinam, quin te, non saltem intercedente autoritate Domini CORRADUCIJ et Domini BARVITIJ, sed et apud Sacram Cæsaream Maiestatem propria mea intercessione ad optima quæque promoveam, et rebus tuis fluctuantibus atque afflictis subveniam. Imo si cætera omnia, quod tamen non spero, frustranea fuerint, ego ipsemet tibi non sum defuturus, sed in ipso effectu ostensurus, ea quæ tibi me scripsisse memini, cum adhuc prope Hamburgum hærerem, antequam nos invicem de facie novissemus, apud me adhuc rata esse, et constanter servari. Tu saltem nihil hæsita, sed confidenter, quantocius poteris, advola: tum coram de omnibus, quæ requiruntur, expeditius agemus, et tutius quam per literas: nec diffido, quin, ubi adveneris, viam iam præparatam invenies. Hæc summa sit consilij, quod etiam is, qui te mihi commendavit, maxime probat.

Plura nunc non addo, neque enim ea, quæ in utrisque literis fuse attigisti, cum ad negotij promotionem, sic stantibus rebus, minus conducant, repetere volo: uti neque de Mathematicis, utrobique a te motis, quippiam addere, cum ea sint alterius atque maioris otij. Eclipsis tamen Solaris, cuius in ultimis tuis mentionem facis, et observationem qualemcunque assignas, calculum, e tabulis nostris renovatis, communico, ut tibi in conspectu sit, atque cum tua conceptione conferre queas. Quantitas deliquij hîc 5 digitos vix attigit, ut ob id apud Vos 6 excedere non potuerit: dimidium digitum istic plus de Sole tectum fuisse concessero, ob minorem Lunæ parallaxin; sed vix amplius. Diligenter a nobis antequam Benatica discederemus, observata est hæc Eclipsis: aderat adhuc M. CHRISTIERNUS, qui postea a me libere et liberaliter dimissus in Patriam abijt. Dominus a TZERNEMEL, qui tibi fecit spem aliquam ante quinquennium, hîc Pragæ est a Cæsare citatus, et forte aliquando hîc manebit, uti sunt admodum ..... quod indicandum duxi, ne frustra alibi ab eo opem et promotionem exspectes. Ego Bibliothecam meam et instrumenta, quæ Benachiæ habui, sic suadente Domino BARVITIO, huc transtuli: quantum enim intelligo, cupit Cæsar, ut in propinquo habitem, et iam de loco commodo, qui civitati conterminus est, deliberatur, qua de re, ubi accesseris (neque enim abfuturum dubito) tibi, uti spero, non displicitura cognosces.

Vale.
Dabantur Pragæ, die 28 Augusti 1600.
Tui amantissimus
TYCHO BRAHE, m. pr. scripsi.

POST SCRIPTA. FACIS in ultimis literis tuis mentionem Scoti istius Capnuranici, quod in ipsum antapologiam, perlecto præsertim libro nostro secundo de Cometa, mediteris: quare te rem gratam et tuæ promissioni meæque expectationi analogam præstiteris. Addixisti etiam, te, quæ in famoso isto scripto Ursino, Mathematica inveniuntur, refutaturum, quatenus rite non constant, quod etiam te effecturum confido. Imprimis autem averem, ut ea quæ inventioni nostræ circa Hypothesin novam fucato et fraudulenter obijcit, eam nimirum ex COPERNICO vel APOLLONIO Pergæo desumptam adhuc dilucidius et plenius quam antea a te refelli, atque ut eam mihi, uti par est, asserere, prout probabilibus rationibus iam antea fecisti, non molestum duceres. Institui contra illum actionem iuridicam, postquam huc Pragam veni, intelligens mihi aliquandiu hîc commorandum, et periculum esse in mora, ast siquidem graviter decumberet: et impetravi a Cæsarea Maiestate (utut quibusdam, quamdiu poterant, remoram inijcientibus) ut Commissarij quatuor deputarentur, duo Barones et duo Doctores iuris, qui sententiam hac de re pronunciarent. Siquidem is per emissarios meos quæsitus, ea, quæ scripserat, retractare noluit, sed potius ad ius provocavit. Verum accidit, ut eadem hora, qua citatio illi insinuanda fuit, extingueretur; at nihilominus ius meum inchoatum prosecuturus sum, siquidem lis iam quasi contestata erat, et actio non plane personalis, sed etiam realis, ob librum famosum, qui perpetuo loco sui autoris loquitur, ut me et meos a calumnijs, quibus scatet, legitime vindicem, et iam a Cæsare Illustrissimo Domino Archiepiscopo commissum est, ut omnia exemplaria, quæ hîc reperiuntur, conquirat et igni tradi faciat, atque Typographum puniat. Decretum etiam Dominus CORRADUCIUS a Cæsare promulgatum pollicitus, ut per totum Imperium liber iste annulletur, et tanquam famosus et scurrilis irritus reddatur. Ego vero postea totum hunc processum unâ cum Commissariorum sententia et Cæsareæ Maiestatis decreto in publicum edere constitui, libello quodam peculiari, qui ad mediocrem excrescet magnitudinem. Quod et ex consensu clementissimo Cæsaris et approbatione Domini CORRADUCIJ mihi iam perspecta, Deo volente, facturus sum. Atque in altera eius parte ad ea, quæ Mathematica sunt, atque Hypotheses concernunt, responsurus, quare tuas quoque de his conceptiones mature obtinere averem; aut si quam citissime (uti spero) accesseris, poterit id differri in tuum adventum. Vale. Datum ut supra in literis. T. B.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the most learned and excellent man, Lord Johannes Kepler, eminent Mathematician of the Provincial Estates of Styria, now dwelling at Graz, his especially beloved friend. Graz.

That to your earlier [letter], sent me two months ago, most learned and most beloved Master Kepler, I made no answer before, believe was done by no forgetfulness of you, nor of the business treated between us, but rather that I might accommodate myself to the opportunity of time (which in this Court is not always at hand), and so the more readily and happily carry everything through. For scarcely any convenience meanwhile was let slip — nay, several times one was even sought out. That you may the more rightly perceive what has meanwhile been done, I will narrate it briefly. A little after the Imperial Majesty, leaving Pilsen, had returned hither to Prague, to the castle of his usual residence, he summoned me from Benátky to him, about seven weeks ago. A few days after I had appeared here, called most clemently to audience, he deigned to speak with me, in his chamber, of various things, for more than an hour and a half — and afterward, the same day, for about half [an hour] more. At the first approach, after His Majesty had proposed what he wished to know from me, he ordered that, if there were any things in my affairs not succeeding as he had at the beginning commanded, and whatever else I desired, I should freely expound them. I, having submissively given thanks, answered that I would note such things on some paper and give it to Lord Barvitius; which pleased him — nay, he commanded that I do it. But when I saw so longed-for an occasion of speaking with His Majesty about necessary matters most kindly offered me of his own accord, I judged that I should not omit to speak out about You too, what I had in mind; and, seizing some handle, from the mention that fell of my instruments and star-observations and the restoring of Astronomy, I said that I alone do not suffice for so great a burden (though I have very much already conducing hither at hand), but have need of collaborators, that so great a work be completed sooner and fuller, for the Imperial Majesty's and the Empire's honour and advantage. His Majesty replied that he would provide that my wishes in this part be satisfied. There, when among other aids conducing hither I had insinuated about summoning you too hither from Styria, and supporting you for a year or two — discreetly adding your name — His Majesty soon most benignly assented, and ordered that I put this too on the paper to Lord Barvitius, among my other desires; and he raised no difficulty in this. I afterward spoke more particularly with the illustrious and magnificent Lord Coraduz about this very matter, and opened your conditions to him in detail, and asked only this for the present: that he would obtain letters from the Imperial Majesty to your Prince and to the most ample Estate of Styria, by which they should be asked that for two years, for the Imperial Majesty's sake, you be allowed to be present here to assist me — and nonetheless to enjoy your annual stipend there, which I should wish augmented, by the Emperor's liberality, by a hundred florins yearly (since all things are dearer here than in Styria). He benignly promised that he would most gladly forward this business, and take care that it have the longed-for outcome. He only advised that I again propose the matter, among the other things I sought, to the Emperor (whether orally or by letter) most submissively, and obtain that its execution be devolved to him; that then he would in no way fail us with his help and counsel. Meanwhile, while I turn over in mind, and conceive with the pen, what I have resolved submissively to propose to His Majesty, His Majesty enjoined on me a particular thing to be done for him as soon as possible. I was still at the public inn at the Sign of the Golden Griffin, where, since no tranquillity for doing such a thing was allowed, I hired another, less restless lodging, not far from the noble Lord Hoffmann; into which I had scarcely moved, and arranged my things as I could, when the Emperor summoned me again to the castle and asked whether the things he wished done by me were ready. I excused myself by the inn's inconvenience and the move into another house, and, having now got better leisure, promised to do everything as soon as possible, within two or three weeks; with which answer too His Majesty most clemently acquiesced. Meanwhile, while I do this (as I promised), which indeed requires no little diligence and labour, I do not dare to beg an audience of His Majesty most humbly, and to solicit about the things I have in [my] wishes (concerning me too not a little), lest I seem to care for my own affairs rather than those the Imperial Majesty required of me — others too advising that I defer my own meanwhile, so that, once the things His Majesty desires of me are completed and shown, I may, the goodwill being thus won, the more easily attain what I long for.

And this is the chief cause why I have hitherto delayed the answer to your earlier [letter]: for I did not wish to write anything to you before the whole matter was accomplished according to [my] wish, and the letters, by which you should be honourably called hither, were written. But meanwhile, a few days ago, comes your second [letter] — which, though at the start it reports things not unlike the earlier, yet near the foot has a certain unhoped-for catastrophe, by which the prior plans, not only yours but mine too, no little collapse. For I understand thence that the Styrian salary has been renounced to you (as to certain others too), and exile besides imposed, unless you obey the decree promulgated there — so that on that account you would rather return into the Duchy of Württemberg, your fatherland. But since, in this disturbance of your affairs and frustration of the prior resolution, I have no more present counsel to give you, from the case as it stands, than that, all delay set aside, you betake yourself hither — whether alone, as before, or even bringing your wife with you, together with household goods and necessaries — for all which you will easily, as I judge, find travel-money, especially with present necessity so urging. For your Maecenases and patrons will not fail you; or, if need be, you will meanwhile borrow somewhere the money required for the journey. When you have arrived, then, present, we will deal between us more rightly and safely, and will find ways by which these difficulties may be relieved, and provision conveniently made for you and your affairs — so that you need not so soon return to your fatherland, or turn aside elsewhere. Meanwhile I will let no occasion slip but that I promote you to all the best — not only by the intervening authority of Lord Coraduz and Lord Barvitius, but also by my own intercession with the Sacred Imperial Majesty — and succour your fluctuating and afflicted affairs. Nay, if all else (which yet I do not expect) prove vain, I myself will not fail you, but will show in the very effect that what I remember writing to you — while I still lingered near Hamburg, before we knew each other by face — is still ratified with me, and constantly kept. Only do you hesitate at nothing, but confidently, as soon as you can, fly hither; then, in person, we will deal of all that is required more expeditiously, and more safely, than by letter; nor do I doubt that, when you arrive, you will find the way already prepared. Let this be the sum of [my] counsel — which too he who recommended you to me most approves.

I add no more now; for I do not wish to repeat what you touched on fully in both letters (since, as things stand, they conduce less to the promotion of the business), nor to add anything about the Mathematical matters you raised in both — since these are [for] another and greater leisure. Yet the Solar eclipse, of which you make mention in your last, and to which you assign some observation: I communicate the calculation, from our renewed tables, that it may be before your eyes, and you may compare it with your conception. The magnitude of the eclipse here scarcely reached 5 digits, so that on that account it could not have exceeded 6 with you; I will grant that half a digit more of the Sun was covered there, on account of the smaller lunar parallax — but scarcely more. This Eclipse was diligently observed by us before we left Benátky; M. Christiernus [Longomontanus] was still present, who was afterward freely and liberally dismissed by me and went away to his fatherland. Lord von Tzernembl, who gave you some hope five years ago, is here at Prague, summoned by the Emperor, and will perhaps stay here for a time, since they are quite … [lacuna] — which I thought should be indicated, lest you vainly expect help and promotion from him elsewhere. I have transferred my Library and the instruments I had at Benátky hither, on Lord Barvitius's advice; for, so far as I understand, the Emperor desires that I dwell near by, and a convenient place, bordering the city, is now being deliberated — about which, when you have come (for I do not doubt you will), you will find things not displeasing to you, as I hope.

Farewell.
Given at Prague, the 28th day of August, 1600.
Most loving of you,
Tycho Brahe, written in my own hand.

[Postscript:] POSTSCRIPTS. You make mention in your last letter that you are meditating an anti-apology against that Capnuranic Scot, especially now that you have read through our second book On the Comet; whereby you will have done a thing welcome to me, and answerable to your promise and to my expectation. You also engaged that you would refute the Mathematical matters found in that infamous writing of Ursus, in so far as they do not duly hold good — which too I am confident you will accomplish. But above all I should wish that the things which he objects, with painted falsehood and fraudulently, against our invention concerning the New Hypothesis — namely that it was taken over from Copernicus or from Apollonius of Perga — be refuted by you yet more clearly and fully than before, and that you would not find it troublesome to claim it for me, as is fitting, just as you have already done before with probable arguments. I have instituted a legal action against him, after I came hither to Prague, understanding that I must stay here for some time, and that there was danger in delay should he lie gravely ill; and I have obtained from the Imperial Majesty (however much certain men, as long as they could, threw in obstruction) that four Commissioners be deputed — two Barons and two Doctors of law — to pronounce sentence in this matter. For when he was sought out by my emissaries, he was unwilling to retract what he had written, but rather appealed to the law. But it came to pass that, at the very hour when the citation was to be served on him, he was extinguished [i.e. died]; yet nonetheless I shall prosecute the suit I have begun, since the litigation was, as it were, already joined, and the action is not wholly personal but also real, on account of the infamous book, which perpetually speaks in place of its author — that I may lawfully vindicate myself and mine from the calumnies with which it teems. And it has now been committed by the Emperor to the most illustrious Lord Archbishop, that he gather up all the copies found here and have them given to the fire, and punish the Printer. Lord Coraduz has also promised a decree to be promulgated by the Emperor, that throughout the whole Empire that book be annulled and rendered void, as infamous and scurrilous. And I have resolved afterward to publish this whole process in public, together with the Commissioners' sentence and the Imperial Majesty's decree, in a certain particular tract, which will grow to a moderate size. This too, by the Emperor's most clement consent and the approbation of Lord Coraduz, now clearly seen by me, I shall, God willing, do. And in the other part of it I shall reply to the things which are Mathematical and concern the Hypotheses; wherefore I should wish to obtain your conceptions about these in good time — or, if you come (as I hope) very speedily, it can be deferred to your arrival. Farewell. Given as above in the letter. T. B.

Apparatus

  • Recipient. Johannes Kepler, at Graz ("Mathematician of the Provincial Estates of Styria"). The formal letter calling Kepler to Prague after his Styrian exile — the immediate precursor to Kepler's reply of 17 Oct 1600 (brahe-t_16001017001) and to his permanent arrival.
  • Significance — the sealing of the Tycho–Kepler collaboration (a keystone document).
  • The audiences with Rudolf II. A vivid first-hand account: c. 7 weeks after the Emperor returned from Pilsen, he summoned Tycho and conversed with him in his chamber for over 1½ hours (+ ½ hour the same day) about astronomy and Tycho's affairs, inviting him to set his wishes on a paper for Barvitius.
  • Securing Kepler's appointment. Seizing the opening, Tycho told the Emperor "I alone do not suffice for so great a burden … I have need of collaborators," and "discreetly added Kepler's name" — proposing to summon Kepler from Styria and support him for a year or two. The Emperor assented; Tycho then arranged with Coraduz for imperial letters to Kepler's Prince (Württemberg) and the Styrian Estates, asking that Kepler assist Tycho two years while keeping his Styrian stipend, augmented by 100 florins ("all is dearer here than in Styria").
  • The court delays. The Emperor enjoined a special task on Tycho; lodged at the Golden Griffin inn (too noisy), Tycho moved to a quieter lodging near Hoffmann; he dares not press his own requests until the imperial task is done — hence the delay in answering Kepler.
  • The "catastrophe." Kepler's second letter brought the blow: the Styrian salary withdrawn and exile imposed (the 1600 Counter-Reformation expulsion), pushing Kepler toward Württemberg.
  • Tycho's warm pledge. The heart of the letter: Tycho urges Kepler to "come hither, all delay set aside — alone or with your wife and goods," promising patrons, loans, and intercession (Coraduz, Barvitius, the Emperor) — and, most movingly, "if all else fail, I myself will not fail you," recalling the promise he wrote Kepler "while I still lingered near Hamburg, before we knew each other by face." "Fly hither … you will find the way already prepared."
  • The July-1600 solar eclipse. Tycho sends his eclipse calculation from his "renewed tables," noting c. 5 digits' magnitude at Prague (≤6 at Graz, +½ digit for smaller lunar parallax), observed at Benátky with Longomontanus (who has since "been freely and liberally dismissed" home — i.e. left Tycho's service).
  • Von Tzernembl & the move. Warns that Georg Erasmus von Tzernembl (a Kepler patron of five years before) is now at Prague; and reports the library/instruments moved from Benátky to Prague, with a city-adjacent residence being arranged (the coming Curtius house).
  • Cross-references.
