The original

HENRICUS BRUCÆUS AD TYCHONEM BRAHE.
S. D.

LEGI responsionem tuam ad meas literas de Cometa, in qua non eo candore usus videris, quem expectabam, nec eo animo nostra accepisse, quo scripta sunt. Miraris, me tuam Hypothesin probare, cum veterum autorum errores mordicus tueri soleam. Quid ita? An quia infinitis tenebrionum erroneis sententijs non assentiar, miraris? Ego vero levitatis magnae, ingenij parvi, imbecillis iudicij arbitror, non recte expensis adversariorum opinionibus, quibus calumniandi quam recte sentiendi maior est cura, subito assentiri. Soleo ego diu multumque premere, quae contradicuntur, et quomodo refelli queant, cogitare, tum vel in priore sententia persistere, aut etiam aliter sentientibus adhaerere. Non me movent convitia, quibus tenebriones veteres tam Medicos quam Philosophos obruunt, et hac ratione turbae indoctae fucum faciunt. Mihi veritatis semper magnum fuit studium, dissentiendi levitas semper invisa. Quod vero Dithmarsus iste tuam Hypothesin surripuerit, non est, quod hoc te admodum moveat. Cum enim observationibus destituatur, e quibus Hypothesis confirmatur et motuum ratio construitur, nihil profecerit, alienis plumis se ornasse, neque intelligentibus persuaserit, suum esse inventum. Perge in opere inchoato, et macte animo prosequere institutum. Haec ad immortalitatem via.

Quod vero de Lunae maxima latitudine scribis, quam 15 scrupulis statuis Ptolemaica maiorem, non facere magnum in eclipsibus discrimen, quae circa nodos incidunt, verum est; attamen in maxima distantia a nodis, in qua eclipses incidere possunt, aliquid, credo, dabit. Sed quare me in tenebrionis sententiam trahis, qui sidera humore vesci credidit, cum alio nostra spectet oratio? Videbimus, quam solide a te eorum sententia refellatur, qui sentiunt, hunc aërem undiquaque permeabilem coelestem regionem implere. Quantum enim ex literis ad PEUCERUM datis colligere possum, statuisti omnium sententias secus sentientium refellere. Hypothesin quoque tuam explicas. Verum ego prius de ea cum quodam, qui apud te fuit, contuli, et videor assecutus. Quod autem scripsi totam tractationem de Cometis circa individua versari, quorum cognitio unâ cum ipso Cometa intercidit, verum est, cum nec motus idem, nec corporis forma eadem unquam sit reditura. Non ivero tamen infitias, multa cum his tradi posse, ut non pauca tuo operi sunt inserta, quae ingeniose inventa et cum iudicio accommodata ad alia plura transferri commode possint. Quare non est, quod sphaericorum libellorum aetatem superatura scribas, quorum plerique non Bosphorum visent, ut de suis poëmatis prodidit HORATIUS, sed pharmacopolia et cauponas, ut habeant, in quod piper, thus, lardum et alia eius generis reponant. Atque utinam Sphaericis id tantum libellis usuveniret, et non maximae parti scriptorum, quae hisce temporibus in lucem eduntur, nisi forte in Bibliothecis aliquandiu delitescant blattis et tineis exposita. Quid itaque de meis mihi pollicear, ipse intelligis; spem magnam immortalitatis suorum scriptorum alijs relinquo. Non est autem, quod graviter feras, quod de Cometae distantia scripsi. Sic enim acceperam: post enim animadverti, de apparente te loqui.

Quod ad postremas tuas literas attinet, quibus nostri sat Boreaae datum meministi, quid aliud tibi respondeam, quam quod summis tam Medicis quam Philosophis visum est, non habeo, quorum ea fuit sententia, coelum sequi hominum naturas. Durum et asperum dura et aspera ingenia ferre, moderata temperatum. Id naturalibus rationibus posset confirmari, sed supersedeo. Quod si aliqui studijs liberalibus exculti, aut bonorum consuetudine humaniores et civiliores evaserint, hoc veterum sententiam minime evertit. Ipse si progrediare versus Boream, sic re ipsa deprehendes. Nec volo comparationem, quae semper est odiosa, instituere. Sufficit, utplurimum ita se habere. Possent enim incidere causae, quae coeli sive aëris constitutionem nihil morentur. Nam et Scythae non pauci temperata satis loca incolunt, barbari tamen et inhumani permanent, sic a puero educati. Et alij Regum aut Magistratus studio ad humanius vitae genus transferuntur. At Philosophi hasce causas restringunt, et quid aër per se efficiat, referunt, Boeotoque aëre natum scribunt.

Sculptorem conveni, is se adfore aperto mari pollicebatur et in Insulam tuam traiecturum; verum non apparuit. Ex hisce literis et chartulis adiunctis, intelliges, quid actum sit; quid fieri insuper velis, perscribes.

Bene vale, amantiss. TYCHO.
Rostochij 23. Martij An. 1589.
Henricus Brucaeus,
plane tuus.

English translation machine translation, Secure

Heinrich Brucaeus to Tycho Brahe.
Sends greeting.

I have read your reply to my letter on the comet, in which you do not seem to me to have shown the candour I expected, nor to have taken my words in the spirit in which they were written. You wonder that I approve your hypothesis, when I am wont to defend the errors of the ancient authors tooth and nail. Why so? Is it because I do not assent to the countless erroneous opinions of the obscurantists that you wonder? For my part, I judge it a mark of great fickleness, of small wit, of feeble judgement, to assent suddenly without having rightly weighed the opinions of one's opponents — opinions in which the care to slander is greater than the care to think rightly. I am wont to press long and hard upon things that are disputed, and to consider how they may be refuted, and then either to persist in my earlier opinion, or even to side with those who think otherwise. I am not moved by the abuse with which the obscurantists overwhelm the ancients, both physicians and philosophers, and by this means throw dust in the eyes of the unlearned crowd. The pursuit of truth has always been a great thing with me; the fickleness of dissent always hateful. But that that fellow from Dithmarschen has filched your hypothesis — there is no reason this should much trouble you. For since he is destitute of the observations from which the hypothesis is confirmed and the account of the motions is constructed, he will have gained nothing by decking himself in others' feathers, nor will he have persuaded men of understanding that the invention is his own. Press on in the work begun, and with good heart pursue what you have set in hand. This is the road to immortality.

As for what you write about the Moon's greatest latitude — which you set at 15 minutes greater than the Ptolemaic value, [saying] that it makes no great difference in the eclipses that fall near the nodes — that is true; yet at the greatest distance from the nodes at which eclipses can fall, it will, I believe, yield something. But why do you drag me into the opinion of that obscurantist who believed the stars feed on moisture, when my discourse looks to something else? We shall see how solidly you refute the opinion of those who hold that this everywhere-permeable air fills the celestial region. For as far as I can gather from the letter you sent to Peucer, you have resolved to refute the opinions of all who think otherwise. You also set out your hypothesis. But I had previously conferred about it with a certain man who had been with you, and I think I have grasped it. As for my having written that the whole treatment of comets turns upon individual things, the knowledge of which perishes together with the comet itself — that is true, since neither the same motion nor the same bodily form will ever return. Yet I do not deny that much can be handed down along with these things; so that not a few matters are inserted into your work which, ingeniously discovered and judiciously adapted, can conveniently be carried over to many other matters. Wherefore there is no reason for you to write that [your work] will outlast the age of the little books on the sphere — most of which will not visit the Bosphorus, as Horace declared of his own poems, but the apothecaries' shops and the taverns, to serve as wrapping in which to store away pepper, frankincense, lard, and other things of that kind. And would that this befell the little books on the sphere alone, and not the greatest part of the writings that are brought to light in these times — unless perhaps they lie hidden for a while in libraries, exposed to bookworms and moths. What, then, I may promise myself of my own [writings], you yourself understand; I leave the great hope of the immortality of their writings to others. There is, moreover, no reason for you to take ill what I wrote about the comet's distance. For so I had understood it; but afterwards I noticed that you were speaking of the apparent [distance].

As for your last letter, in which you recall that enough was conceded by me to the North, what other answer am I to give you than what has seemed [right] to the greatest of physicians as well as philosophers — whose opinion was that the heaven follows the natures of men[?] [It is] harsh and rough natures that bear the harsh and rough [climate], temperate natures the temperate. This could be confirmed by natural arguments, but I forbear. But if some, refined by the liberal studies, or grown more humane and civil by the company of good men, have turned out so, this in no way overturns the opinion of the ancients. If you yourself should travel northward, you will discover it so in the very fact. Nor do I wish to set up a comparison, which is always odious. It is enough that for the most part it is so. For causes might intervene that pay no heed to the constitution of the heaven or of the air. For not a few Scythians too inhabit lands temperate enough, and yet remain barbarous and uncivil, having been so reared from boyhood. And others are brought over to a more humane way of life by the zeal of their kings or magistrates. But the philosophers limit these causes, and report what the air of itself effects, and write that [such a one] was born under a Boeotian air.

I have met with the sculptor; he was promising that he would come, once the sea was open, and cross over to your island; but he has not appeared. From this letter and the little papers enclosed you will understand what has been done; what you wish to have done besides, you will write [to me].

Fare you well, most loving Tycho.
At Rostock, 23 March, the year 1589.
Heinrich Brucaeus,
wholly yours.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Henricus Brucaeus = Heinrich Brucaeus (Hendrik van den Broucke, 1530/31–1593), Flemish-born professor of mathematics and then of medicine at the University of Rostock, and an elder correspondent and early supporter of Tycho. The letter is his reply in the running 1588–89 exchange occasioned by Tycho's De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis (Uraniborg, 1588), the comet treatise.
  • Recipient / salutation. S. D. = salutem dicit, "sends greeting". The closing apostrophe Bene vale, amantiss. Tycho ("Fare you well, most loving Tycho") addresses Tycho in the vocative; plane tuus = "wholly yours."
  • Dithmarsus iste … tuam Hypothesin surripuerit — "that fellow from Dithmarschen has filched your hypothesis": Nicolaus Reimarus Ursus (Nicolai Reymers Bär, "Ursus," c. 1551–1600), born in Dithmarschen, whom Tycho accused of stealing his geo-heliocentric world-system after a visit to Uraniborg; Ursus had published a like system in his Fundamentum astronomicum (Strasbourg, 1588). The literature (Gingerich, Annotated Census of De revolutionibus) confirms the identification "Nicolaus Raimarus Ursus Dithmarsus." Brucaeus's point — that without observations the borrowed scheme is worthless — is rendered without softening Tycho's own charge of theft.
  • Hypothesis — kept as "hypothesis" throughout (the world-system / geometric model); motuum ratio = "the account of the motions."
  • Astronomy (Moon's latitude). de Lunae maxima latitudine … quam 15 scrupulis statuis Ptolemaica maiorem = "the Moon's greatest latitude, which you set at 15 minutes [of arc] greater than the Ptolemaic [value]"; scrupulus = arc-minute, here "minute(s)." quae circa nodos incidunt = "[eclipses] that fall near the nodes"; nodus → node, eclipsis → eclipse. maxima latitudine / maxima distantia: the qualifier "greatest" is the source's own (maxima), not an editorial insertion.
  • qui sidera humore vesci credidit — "[the obscurantist] who believed the stars feed on moisture": the old doctrine (Stoic / Aristotelian-meteorological) that the heavenly bodies are nourished by exhalations drawn up from below; Brucaeus protests that this is not the doctrine Tycho should impute to him.
  • hunc aërem undiquaque permeabilem coelestem regionem implere — "this everywhere-permeable air fills the celestial region": the fluid-heavens question (whether one continuous, permeable air-like medium extends through the heavens, as against solid celestial spheres) which Tycho's comet work pressed; Brucaeus expects Tycho to refute the solid-orb party.
  • literis ad Peucerum datis — "the letter sent to Peucer": Caspar Peucer (1525–1602), Wittenberg physician-astronomer and Melanchthon's son-in-law, another node of this correspondence-network.
  • individua … quorum cognitio unâ cum ipso Cometa intercidit — "individual things, the knowledge of which perishes together with the comet itself": individua in the logical sense of unrepeatable particulars; a comet, being a transient singular, leaves no recurring motion or form, so (Brucaeus had argued) the science of it dies with it — qualified, as Brucaeus does, by his concession that transferable method survives.
  • sphaericorum libellorum — "the little books on the sphere": the elementary Sphaera textbooks (in the tradition of Sacrobosco), here disparaged as ephemeral.
  • Allusion (Horace). non Bosphorum visent, ut de suis poëmatis prodidit Horatius, sed pharmacopolia et cauponas … in quod piper, thus … reponant — Horace's conceit (Epistles I.20; cf. Ars Poetica) of the worthless book ending as wrapping-paper for grocers' pepper and incense. The grim list (piper, thus, largum) is kept; largum is read as "bulk/ample goods" of the same shop-stock kind, paralleling alia eius generis. blattis et tineis = "bookworms and moths."
  • nostri sat Boreaae datum — "enough was conceded by me to the North": refers back to Tycho's previous letter on the old climate-theory of national character (the Boreas, the north wind / the North). Boreaae is the manuscript form (with doubled vowel) for Boreae. The whole third paragraph debates the classical-medical doctrine — coelum sequi hominum naturas, "the heaven follows the natures of men" — that climate shapes temperament: northern peoples harsh because their climate is harsh.
  • Scythae … barbari … Boeotoque aëre natum — the Scythians as the type of the cold-dwelling barbarian; Boeoto aëre natum = "born under a Boeotian air," the proverbial dull/stupid air of Boeotia (cf. Boeotum in crasso … aëre, Horace, Epistles II.1.244). Kept as allusion; the philosophers' position (that air of itself dulls or sharpens wit) is distinguished from the king-and-magistrate counter-causes Brucaeus allows.
  • summis tam Medicis quam Philosophis — "the greatest of physicians as well as philosophers": the Hippocratic–Galenic and Aristotelian authorities behind the climate doctrine. Punctuation: the source runs the rhetorical question quid aliud tibi respondeam … non habeo together; an editorial [?] and [It is] are bracketed to carry the sense without recasting.
  • Sculptorem … in Insulam tuam traiecturum — "the sculptor … would cross over to your island": a craftsman (engraver/carver) Brucaeus had engaged on Tycho's behalf, to come to Hven once the sea was open (i.e. ice-free) in spring; aperto mari = "the sea being open." chartulis adiunctis = "the little papers enclosed."
  • Dateline. Rostochij 23. Martij An. 1589 = "at Rostock [Rostochium], 23 March 1589" (Old Style; Denmark and the Lutheran north kept the Julian calendar). The header date matches the dateline; the edition label ("1589. 23. marts. Rostock") agrees, so no discrepancy arises.
  • Editorial insertions. Bracketed words ([saying], [other], [value], [such a one], [right], [It is], [?], [your work], [yet], [to me], [distance]) supply syntax implied but not lexically present in the source; the source's own hedges (credo "I believe," videor assecutus "I think I have grasped," Non ivero infitias "I do not deny") are preserved unsharpened.
  • Correction. Latin corrected against Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Opera Omnia VII, page 167: "largum" corrected to "lardum" (lard/bacon). The English's bracketed guess "[other] bulk goods" replaced with "lard".

1 defect found:
- text error (low). The transcribed original misprints 'lardum' (lard/bacon, a stock item merchants wrapped in waste paper, per the Horace/spice-shop trope Brucaeus is invoking) as 'largum', an adjective that cannot function as a listed noun; the English's odd bracketed '[other] bulk goods' is a downstream guess at the corrupted word rather than a rendering of 'lardum'. Dreyer prints: in quod piper, thus, lardum et alia eius generis reponant.

Repaired. The single defect site was collated against Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Opera Omnia volume VII, page 167 (page image, not the digitised text layer). Confirmed: "ut habeant, in quod piper, thus, lardum et alia eius generis reponant." No other cruxes were found on the sampled pages.
