The original

CLARISSIMO VIRO, DOCTRINA ET VIRTUTE PRÆSTANTI, D. CHRISTOPHORO ROTHMANNO, ILLVSTRISSIMI PRINCIPIS WILHELMI LANDTGRAVII HASSIÆ, ETC. MATHEMATICO EXIMIO, AMICO SUO PLURIMUM DILECTO, S. D.

STATVERAM quidem hoc tempore, doctissime et charissime ROTHMANNE, plenius copiosiusque ad proximas Illustrissimi Principis tuasque literas respondere, sed cum plurimæ, tum publicæ, tum priuatæ curæ inciderint, et minus etiam firma vtar valetudine, a proposito desistere vel inuitus cogor. Accedit et hoc, quod plures in Veneris Stella, nunc quoque interdiu apparente, Obseruationes vespertinas expectem, quas cum matutinis, Anno præsertim antecedente in Augusto habitis, denuo conferam, vt quinorum illorum Minutorum, quibus in Fixarum Longitudinibus discrepamus, vberior examinatio instituatur. Quamprimum vero opportunius otium, commoditatemque exoptatam nactus fuero, et illas, quas dixi, in Venere Obseruationes perfecero, non intermittam, quin abunde et ex voto, tum ad Illustrissimi Principis, tum etiam tuas literas respondeam. Quod te rogo, vt tuo Principi meo nomine indicare velis, et responsionis moram ob caussas antedictas excusare. Interim mitto tibi exemplar secundi libri Operis mei de Mundi Ætherei recentioribus Phænomenis, quem diligenter per otium velis euoluere, et de eo tuam ad me sententiam perscribere. Tractat de Cometa anni 77; Primus enim est de Stella Noua ad Cassiopeam: Tertius et vltimus de tribus alijs minoribus Cometis. Poteris autem vel ex hoc vnico, quid de Cometarum negotio penitius introspecto sentiam, non obscure colligere. Inuenies etiam ibidem circa initium capitis octaui, nouam a me non ita dudum adinuentum cælestium Reuolutionum Hypothesin, quam veteri illa Ptolemaica et recentiore Copernicea multas ob causas veriorem esse non dubito, eamque apparentijs apprime consentaneam deprehendo. Tute diligenter omnia in hac circumspicito, nihilque concinnius, aut veritati conuenientius adferri posse, non ambigo quin vltro concessurus sis. Cuperem etiam, vt eam Illustriss. Principi tuo exponeres, eiusque C. de hac iudicium mihi significares. Constitui super hanc, emendationem motuum cælestium fundare, sicque totam Artem Astronomicam e certis Obseruationibus, quasi ad prima incunabula reuocare, redintegrareque; modo Numen diuinum conatibus nostris aspirauerit, et vires ac commoditatem tanto labori exequendo suppeditârit. Recensui etiam in hoc libro, Illustrissimi tui Principis de eodem Cometa Obseruationes mihi transmissas, easque sub incudem numerorum diligenter reuocaui, et a meis Inuentis non admodum dissentaneas adinueni: Neque enim vlla perceptibilis Parallaxis istius Cometæ inde colligi potest, sed omnia in contrarium potius deueniunt, vt ex ipso scripto videre licet. Spero Illustrissimo Principi tuo hanc suarum in Cometa hoc Obseruationum euulgationem, accuratamque considerationem non futuram ingratam; siquidem ad rei inquirendæ veritatem confirmandam plurimum conducat. Quamprimum reliqui duo Tomi absoluti fuerint, ijsque Typographus extremam manum imposuerit, te eorum etiam reddam participem. Hunc etiam præsentium exhibitorem, GELLIVM SASCERIDEM nomine, qui annis aliquot, tum in Astronomicis, tum etiam Pyronomicis exercitijs egregiam apud me nauauit operam, velis tuo Illustrissimo Principi commendare: cui etiam priuatim quædam significaui, tibi exponenda, vt ea ad Illustrissimum Principem tuum per occasionem referas, quod vt diligenter exequaris, te etiam atque etiam rogatum habeo. Sperabamus nos antea, si successissent Comitia Hamburgensia, neque tantus nostræ patriæ luctus (proh dolor) ex inopinato incidisset, Illustrissimum Principem tuum Hamburgum venturum. Quod si contigisset, istuc conueniendi eius Celsitudinem gratiâ me contulissem, si propius huc non accessisset, quo de Astronomicis rebus coram plenius perfectiusque cum eius Celsitudine conferendo ex voto agerem. Sed quoniam ista nobis occasio nimium lugubri obstaculo (quodque animus meminisse horret luctuque refugit) prærepta est, ad literas recurrendum esse video, quas etiam prima quaque opportunitate, ijs quas vltimo ab Illustrissimo Principe et te accepi, affatim respondentes, (vt supra etiam pollicitus sum) breui mittam. Interim moram hanc non ægre feras, eamque apud Illustriss. Principem tuum diligenter excuses.

Vale.
Datum Vraniburgi circa Solstitium Æstiuum, Anno 1588.
TYCHO BRAHE.

English translation machine translation, Probable

To the most distinguished man, eminent in learning and virtue, Master Christoph Rothmann, outstanding mathematician of the Most Illustrious Prince Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, etc., his most dearly beloved friend — heartiest greeting.

I had indeed resolved at this time, most learned and most dear Rothmann, to answer the most recent letters of the Most Illustrious Prince and your own more fully and copiously; but since very many cares, both public and private, have come upon me, and I am also enjoying less than firm health, I am compelled, however unwillingly, to give up that purpose. There is added this too, that I am awaiting several evening observations of the planet Venus — now visible by day as well — which I shall compare again with the morning ones, made in particular in August of the preceding year, so that a fuller examination may be set on foot of those five minutes by which we differ in the longitudes of the fixed [stars]. But as soon as I have come by a more convenient leisure and the long-wished-for opportunity, and have completed those observations of Venus of which I spoke, I shall not fail to answer abundantly and as I would wish both the Most Illustrious Prince's letters and your own. And I ask you to be willing to make this known to your Prince in my name, and to excuse the delay of my reply for the aforesaid reasons. Meanwhile I send you a copy of the second book of my work On the More Recent Phenomena of the Aethereal World, which I should like you to peruse carefully at your leisure, and to write me your opinion of it in full. It treats of the comet of the year [15]77; for the first [book] is on the New Star by Cassiopeia, the third and last on three other lesser comets. From this one [book] alone you will be able to gather, not obscurely, what I think about the question of comets, once it has been more deeply looked into. You will find there too, near the beginning of the eighth chapter, a new Hypothesis of the celestial Revolutions, devised by me not so long ago, which I do not doubt to be — for many reasons — truer than that old Ptolemaic one and the more recent Copernican one, and which I find supremely consonant with the appearances. Examine everything in it carefully for yourself, and I do not doubt that you will of your own accord concede that nothing more elegant, or more agreeable to the truth, can be brought forward. I should wish also that you would expound it to your Most Illustrious Prince, and signify to me His Highness's judgement on it. I have determined to found upon this [hypothesis] the emendation of the celestial motions, and so to recall and restore the whole Art of Astronomy from sure observations, as it were to its first cradle — provided the divine Power shall favour our endeavours, and shall supply the strength and the means for accomplishing so great a labour. I have also reviewed in this book the observations of the same comet sent to me by your Most Illustrious Prince, and I have carefully recalled them to the anvil of numbers, and have found them not greatly at variance with my own findings; for indeed no perceptible parallax of that comet can be gathered from them, but everything comes out rather to the contrary, as may be seen from the writing itself. I hope that this publishing-abroad of his observations on this comet, and their accurate consideration, will not prove unwelcome to your Most Illustrious Prince, inasmuch as it may contribute very greatly to confirming the truth of the matter under inquiry. As soon as the remaining two volumes have been completed, and the printer has set the final hand to them, I shall make you a partaker in these too. This same bearer of the present [letter], Gellius Sascerides by name, who for some years has done excellent service with me in exercises both astronomical and pyronomic [chemical], I should like you to commend to your Most Illustrious Prince; to whom I have also privately communicated certain things to be laid out to you, that you may convey them on occasion to your Most Illustrious Prince — which that you carry out diligently I do most earnestly ask of you. We had hoped before now, if the Diet of Hamburg had gone forward, and so great a grief had not (alas!) fallen unexpectedly upon our country, that your Most Illustrious Prince would come to Hamburg. And had that happened, I would have betaken myself thither for the sake of meeting His Highness — if he had not come nearer here — that I might attend, as I would wish, upon discussing astronomical matters in person, more fully and more perfectly, with His Highness. But since that opportunity has been snatched from us by a too-mournful obstacle (and which my mind shudders to recall and shrinks from in grief), I see that I must have recourse to letters, which too, at the very first opportunity, fully answering those I last received from the Most Illustrious Prince and from you, I shall send shortly (as I have promised above also). Meanwhile, bear this delay without resentment, and excuse it diligently to your Most Illustrious Prince.

Farewell.
Given at Uraniborg around the Summer Solstice, in the Year 1588.
Tycho Brahe.

Apparatus

  • Sender / recipient. Tycho Brahe, writing from Uraniborg on Hven, to Christoph Rothmann (Christophorus Rothmannus, c. 1550/60 – c. 1600), the mathematician-astronomer of Wilhelm IV at Kassel and Tycho's principal scientific correspondent of the late 1580s. The Illustrissimus Princeps Wilhelmus Landtgravius Hassiae of the address is Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel (1532–1592), Rothmann's patron and himself an observing astronomer.
  • The book sent. secundi libri Operis mei de Mundi Aetherei recentioribus Phaenomenis = Book II of Tycho's De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis liber secundus (Uraniborg, 1588), his treatise on the comet of 1577. As Tycho explains, the projected work was tripartite: Book I on the New Star (nova) of 1572 by Cassiopeia (Stella Nova ad Cassiopeam), Book II (the one here transmitted, the only one printed in Tycho's lifetime) on the 1577 comet, and a planned Book III on three lesser comets — the reliqui duo Tomi ("remaining two volumes") not yet finished by the printer.
  • The world-system. nouam … caelestium Reuolutionum Hypothesin … capitis octaui — the famous announcement, near the start of chapter 8 of the comet book, of Tycho's new Hypothesis of the celestial Revolutions (the geo-heliocentric "Tychonic" system), claimed veriorem ("truer") than both the Ptolemaica and the Copernicea systems and apparentijs apprime consentaneam ("supremely consonant with the appearances"). Tycho's epistemic hedges are preserved: he writes non dubito ("I do not doubt"), not a flat assertion, and grounds the claim in fitting the apparentiae.
  • Tycho's programme. totam Artem Astronomicam e certis Obseruationibus, quasi ad prima incunabula reuocare, redintegrareque = "to recall and restore the whole Art of Astronomy from sure observations, as it were to its first cradle" — the instauratio / restitutio astronomiae programme; redintegrare rendered "restore." ad prima incunabula ("to its first cradle/swaddling-clothes") kept as the metaphor it is.
  • Technical / observational. Veneris Stella … interdiu apparente = "the planet Venus … visible by day"; quinorum illorum Minutorum, quibus in Fixarum Longitudinibus discrepamus = "those five minutes [of arc] by which we differ in the longitudes of the fixed [stars]" — a daytime-Venus campaign used to resolve a 5′ disagreement with Rothmann in stellar longitudes. Parallaxis istius Cometae = "parallax of that comet": the absence of perceptible parallax is the crux of the cometary argument (a comet without measurable parallax must lie far beyond the Moon, dissolving the solid celestial spheres). sub incudem numerorum … reuocaui = "recalled to the anvil of numbers," i.e. subjected Wilhelm's observations to numerical recomputation; the smith metaphor kept.
  • Bearer. Gellium Sasceridem = Gellius Sascerides (1562–1612), the Danish scholar then in Tycho's service, here carrying the letter; in Astronomicis, tum etiam Pyronomicis exercitijs = "in astronomical and also pyronomic exercises" — Pyronomica ("fire-governing") is Tycho's term for the alchemical / chemical (laboratory) work at Uraniborg; rendered "pyronomic [chemical]" rather than modernised.
  • The "grief of our country." tantus nostrae patriae luctus (proh dolor) ex inopinato incidisset and nimium lugubri obstaculo refer, unnamed, to the death of King Frederik II of Denmark (d. 4 April 1588), Tycho's patron — which is why the hoped-for Comitia Hamburgensia ("Diet of Hamburg") meeting with Wilhelm IV did not come about.
  • Allusion (kept as a half-line of verse). quodque animus meminisse horret luctuque refugit — a near-verbatim echo of Virgil, Aeneid II.12 (quaeque ipse miserrima vidi … et quorum pars magna fui … animus meminisse horret luctuque refugit, "things which my mind shudders to recall and has shrunk from in grief"); set off in italics in the translation to mark the parenthetical quotation, as in the source's own parentheses.
  • Idiom. extremam manum imposuerit = "[the printer] has set the final hand," i.e. finished/perfected the work (the standard ultima/extrema manus idiom); eius C. / eiusque C. = eius Celsitudo "His Highness," the Landgrave's style (the edition's convention, Celsitudo).
  • Calendar / dating crux. The dateline reads Datum Vraniburgi circa Solstitium Aestiuum, Anno 1588 — "Given at Uraniborg around the Summer Solstice, in the Year 1588," an approximate date (the Old-Style summer solstice fell c. 11–12 June 1588 by the Julian reckoning Denmark kept). The edition's catalogue label "11. juni" supplies a precise civil day (consistent with, and presumably derived from, the solstice indication), but the source text gives no day-of-month: the header therefore leads with the as-written "around the Summer Solstice 1588" and records the catalogued 11 June in parentheses, by the edition's convention No silent conversion is made; O.S. throughout (Denmark, Julian).
  • Edition note. The letter was printed by Tycho himself in the Epistolae astronomicarum libri (Uraniborg, 1596), the edition of record cited above; it is reprinted in Dreyer, Opera Omnia VI. No manuscript witness is recorded in the edition.
