Your letter, Most Noble and Most Excellent Master Tycho, which you had despatched on 21 February, we received scarcely on 17 August — that is, six days after you had written and sent to me the other one. But although I am now pulled this way and that by various occupations, I judged that I should answer you in a few words: especially since I see that you have understood my earlier letter — perhaps on account of your various occupations and also your illness — far otherwise than I had written it. For you charge me with things that can never be gathered from the words of that letter, and that never even entered my mind. For where did I detract anything from the authority of the sacred Scriptures? Are not the things I said said also by you yourself, and by all the theologians who interpret the sacred Scriptures? Unless perhaps you reckon that, when two say the same thing, it is not the same thing — and that therefore that is to be censured in me which could readily be granted to others. If you read Augustine, you will find him speaking far more freely about the sacred Scriptures. When I lately set out the same opinion before our Most Illustrious Prince and our theologians, men of the highest rank — lest I make this quarrel my own (for in our [book on the] Observations I assigned it a chapter of its own, where I showed that the sacred Scriptures do not stand in the way of our opinion concerning the fluidity of the celestial matter) — neither the Prince nor the theologians disapproved it, nor did they find anything in it to be censured. And you too will have to do the same, if only you wish to defend that quintessence of yours. The glory of God alone is set before me in all my actions: do not think anything impious of me. About the twilights, too, do not make me out to be quite blind. For I am not so rash as to write anything in these matters that I have not tested by experience. I know that twilight ends when the Sun is sunk 17 degrees below the horizon, if you take "twilight" in the common sense for the doubtful light. But I defined twilight [as lasting] up to the point where no further trace of whitening air appears — which is altogether necessary, if you wish to determine more exactly the outermost surface of the dense air that can be illuminated. Consider, then, the air around the place of the Sun's setting, once the common twilight has ended, and compare it with the other places of the horizon round about — as toward the East, toward the North, toward the South — and you will quite certainly find a difference of colour and those traces of whitening air. But I do not know why you also detract from my refractions. For how else can I find them, or even extract them, than as the instruments themselves furnish? Am I to do this to please you? You demand of me what is surely unjust, if you demand this. But I have not faulted the title of your volumes "On the new generations of the aetherial world," nor is it to be faulted, nor does it need your excuse: since the things that come into being are also said to be generated; and comets are indeed generated in the heavens. I put those things forward not assertively, but rather doubtfully and by way of question, chiefly so that you might explain to me your opinion concerning your generation and corruption. The things I also wrote about the comet's tail, those too I rather submitted to your judgement than urged as true. And our Most Illustrious Prince readily deserves to be excused, since at that time he was scarcely beginning to deal with the observations, had not yet corrected the places of the fixed stars, nor was he practised in observations — and I should not wish you to publish that observation. But neither should you mix my opinion concerning the generation of comets into your writings. For if I had judged it should be published, I should myself have published it long ago. You would be fighting by that argument with one who is dumb, since no one yet knows that opinion of mine. After my writings have been made public, you will be able to examine them in your writings at your pleasure, since they will then be common property; but concerning things not yet published no prejudgement is to be brought in. Last summer, when I reflected on the matter of the stars, I fell into a different opinion: I found, too, that the things I had put forward in chapter 7 about the running beams are utterly false. Wherefore I am going to alter that whole chapter on the matter of comets and their illumination, and to treat it on a far different principle, if I shall yet observe still another comet besides, and inspect and consider it with all diligence. As for that the planets hang in the most subtle and purest air, I will not deal with you any further. I have treated this opinion of mine in our work on the Fixed Stars, and have not mixed you in, but have treated the matter itself. The same you too will have to do, namely to defend your opinion by whatever means you can. The most learned mathematicians will judge afterwards whose opinion is the true one. For we ourselves cannot in this matter sustain at once the part of plaintiff and of defendant and of judge; rather, for pronouncing sentence a third person is required, a lover of truth [φιλαληθὴς] and bewitched by no prejudice and affection whatever. The Mystery of the Trinity has truly been imparted by God to created things, and in this lower world all earthly things are found to consist of Earth, Water, and Air — as I signified also in my earlier letters — or rather of an earthy matter, a watery glue, and an airy spirit; and this matter, thus disposed, the celestial Heat or the Sun, approaching, then floods with light by its own specific and inborn virtue, rouses and quickens. Of the soul of man I say nothing, which is a particle of the divine breath. But these things I have treated elsewhere. I see that I have at last won this point with you, that the transparency of the pure air differs in nothing from the transparency of the matter poured around the planets: as for the remaining opinion, also without doubt true, truth, the daughter of time, will teach it you. Concerning the hypotheses of Ptolemy and of Copernicus, which of the two is true, we shall one day discuss in a writing of its own, nor will we set up any further disputation here. For neither will that comet of the year [15]85, seen in opposition to the Sun, be able to teach us anything certain here, even though it was not rendered subject to the motion of the earth. For if its motion could surpass the motion of the other planets by so many degrees, it could also exclude the annual motion of the earth. To this is added that the nature of motion is compounded of circular motions, so that all are in relation to one another [πρὸς ἄλληλα], and however you transpose the eccentrics and epicycles, yet the same motion and the same distance from the earth comes out, its own manner being kept — as is clear from Copernicus, Book 3 of the Revolutions, chapter 20, Book 4 chapter 3, and Book 5 chapters 4, 22, and in other places, as we have set forth elsewhere. As regards your hypotheses, you rightly conjecture that that general delineation of the world was displayed to our Most Illustrious Prince by that Thersites. For so had he, together with our clockmaker, by this triviality of his occupied His Highness in my absence (for I was away abroad on travels), that when I returned and learned these things from the Most Illustrious Prince, I had business enough to expose its falsity to the Most Illustrious Prince. For the true inversion of Copernicus was well enough known to me from my Astronomical Elements, which neither then nor even now has our Most Illustrious Prince seen. And I said openly that that man did not have these things out of his own brain, since he was utterly ignorant of the Doctrine of Triangles [trigonometry]; and since it lay hidden that he had acted as a collector of letters [a typesetter] in your printing-house with you, I said he had learned these things from no one else but you. Yet I could not altogether persuade our Most Illustrious Prince that these things were false, although I adduced everything both from Ptolemy and from Copernicus — so much had His own Highness been occupied by the clockmaker. Yet at last, when that instrument had been constructed which that little man had delineated in that writing, both the Most Illustrious Prince and the clockmaker saw with their own eyes that those hypotheses could not be true, especially from Mars. For they assumed only a little eccentric of the Sun, and within it carried round the eccentrics of the remaining planets, the reckoning of the equants being omitted — or, if one is to speak according to Copernicus, those little circles being utterly omitted. Nor did that man know the ratios and quantities of the diameters and eccentricities, but our clockmaker took these from Copernicus, who heard well enough from me, in the presence of the Most Illustrious Prince, how the matter was to be handled. Nor should I have said anything about these things, had it not been my duty to draw back my Most Illustrious Prince from approval (for that most shameless man was seeking from His own Highness a testimonial of approval) — from approval, I say, of a false thing. And as often as I conferred with His own Highness, the clockmaker too was always present, who played the man's part. For only in my absence had that man been admitted to the Most Illustrious Prince; but afterwards he was never admitted again, for which cause he also covertly inveighs against me in his preface. And how could I commend him to our Most Illustrious Prince, when he understood neither that delineation itself nor anything much about this matter? Nor yet had our clockmaker rightly grasped from me how the matter was to be handled; but after that man's departure I explained to the Most Illustrious Prince how all the data and the ratios of the diameters in the circles were to be assumed and transposed from Copernicus — which the clockmaker then readily seized upon, to whom that Ursus had translated Copernicus into the German tongue after a fashion. So afterwards our Most Illustrious Prince had made for himself, through the clockmaker, a most perfect automaton, perfectly exhibiting the motion of all the planets according to the calculation of Copernicus, about which I also wrote in my earlier letters. For that which that man delineated in his writing is of no moment, and he did not leave it here but carried it off with him, interpreting Copernicus for the clockmaker for his construction — from whom too [the clockmaker] he learned my compendious methods in some way or other; and in that writing, with the most manifest malevolence, he attributes [them] to the clockmaker, an unlettered man and an idiot, whom nevertheless I was the first to teach the demonstration of Euclid I.47, now reaping a serpent's wage [for it]. That obliquity of the Zodiac, transcribed from my observations, our clockmaker transmitted to him even down to the seconds; just as other things too were filched from my observations through the clockmaker's collusion. But I will have nothing to do with that impure fellow and our clockmaker: learned men will easily judge whence most things flowed, and what is to be attributed to each. That he omitted my name, he did me a most welcome service. If it shall seem good to you, you will be able to vindicate your hypotheses from him. You can scarcely believe how those two men would assail with insults both you and me and that wretched Ptolemy and wretched Copernicus and all the learned, and call [them] stupid asses understanding nothing — vaunting themselves as the suns and lights of the world: so that nothing whatever is more intolerable or more unjust than an unlettered man who scarcely understands the first rudiments. But these things are to be reckoned by us as nothing and to be despised. I have nevertheless written fully about this matter, last May, to my singular friend, a man in every kind of learning both most excellent and most versed, Master John Dee, and signified to him that he [Ursus] not only attributed my [work] to an unlettered clockmaker and idiot, but had even filched from you that system of the world: now craftily seeking to snare the learned, whom nevertheless he had before pursued, together with the clockmaker, with a thoroughly Vatinian hatred. "Every complexion and condition and circumstance became Aristippus." I wrote also that he really is such as he described himself in his preface. But I will have nothing to do, as I said, with that man, nor is he worthy that his name be brought into our writings. The learned will easily see that he has that system from you, since he himself confesses that he learned the Doctrine of Triangles from our clockmaker. But how, without the Doctrine of Triangles, could he have known anything about that system and the ordering of the celestial circles and their quantity? I too shall attribute that system to you in my Astronomical Elements, just as I have also restored to you the design of our instruments in our work on the fixed stars. But enough of this. Our Most Illustrious Prince had it in mind to reply to your letter; but, setting out on a journey in sudden haste, he made for Schmalkalden, so that by this circumstance the reply has been hindered. Just as he is also greatly hindered around this time by the fact that he is now devoting his pains to spearing the harts that pursue the work of Venus [are in rut]. Nevertheless, that His own Highness awaits a letter from you and often thinks of you, you will be able to gather from the enclosed bundle of letters which His own Highness gave to me last July from the county of Homberg; according to the tenor and command of which I also handed to Frischlin letters to be conveyed to you. What you wrote to me in your latest letter about your buildings and your summer occupations — namely that you have built a mill for making paper — I reported to my Most Illustrious Prince, to whom the matter was very pleasing. Those letters which you had written to Gellius Sascerides I lately handed to the tutor of the most noble Rosenkrantz, who greeted me most kindly both on your behalf and on behalf of the most noble Master Dee, my singular friend, and undertook that he would send or carry these too, along with other letters, to the same Gellius. Lately Master Victorinus Schönfeld, professor of mathematics among the Marburgers, asked of me, through Christoph Harsach, a councillor of our Most Illustrious Prince, that I commend his son to you, and [asked] that you learn the matter from him — whether you could receive him for some time among your students and instruct him in these arts; and if this seemed good to you, that he would send him to you next summer. If, therefore, this is convenient to you, you will be able to declare your will to me by the next Fair, so that he may know what he is to do. These things, now in greatest haste, distracted by various occupations, I wished to answer briefly; and I pray you again and again that you take them in the best part. I shall write more, when I have got the occasion and the leisure. Farewell, then, Most Noble and Most Excellent Master Tycho, and love me as you do. At Kassel, 22 September, the year [15]89. Yours wholly, Christoph Rothmann.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Christophorus Rothmannus = Christoph Rothmann (c. 1550/60 – c. 1600), mathematician-astronomer at the court of Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel; Tycho's chief correspondent on observational and cosmological method. This is one of the more pointed letters in the exchange: Rothmann defends himself against Tycho's charges (on Scripture and the fluidity of the heavens, on twilight, on refractions) and then narrates the Ursus affair at length.
  • Recipient / persons.
  • Illustrissimus Princeps noster = Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel (1532–1592), Rothmann's patron.
  • Thersites … Ursus … horologiopaeus … homo ille / homuncio — the central polemic. Ursus is Nicolaus Reimers Baer (Nicolaus Raimarus Ursus, c. 1551–1600), whom Rothmann brands "that Thersites" (Homer's lowborn railer, Iliad 2) and the "little man / fellow." The horologiopaeus ("clockmaker," from Greek hōrológion + -poios) is Wilhelm IV's instrument-/clock-maker Jost Bürgi, here Ursus's collaborator in presenting a "world-system" delineation to the Landgrave. Rothmann's allegation — that Ursus had worked as a typesetter (collectorem literarum) in Tycho's Uraniborg printing-house and there pilfered Tycho's geo-heliocentric system — is the documentary seed of the Tycho–Ursus priority quarrel that culminated in Tycho's Apologia and Kepler's Apologia Tychonis contra Ursum. Identification flagged: the letter never names Ursus except as "Thersites"/"Ursus"; "Bürgi" is supplied editorially and is not in the Latin.
  • D. Ioannem Dee / D. DEE = John Dee (1527–1608/9), to whom Rothmann says he wrote in May denouncing Ursus — a notable datum for Dee's continental network in the late 1580s.
  • Gellius Sascerides = Gellius Sascerides (1562–1612), Danish physician-astronomer, Tycho's former assistant on Hven.
  • Praeceptori … ROSENCRANTZII = the tutor of a young Rosenkrantz (Danish noble family) then travelling; not further specified in the text.
  • Victorinus Schonfeldt = Victorinus Schönfeld (1525–1591), professor of mathematics at Marburg; he seeks a place for his son among Tycho's students. Christophorus Harsachius = Christoph Harsach (Harssach), a councillor of the Landgrave. Frischlino = (likely) Nicodemus Frischlin or a courier of that name; identification not secured by the text.
  • Places. Cassellis = Kassel. Smalcaldia = Schmalkalden. comitatu Hombergico = the county/district of Homberg (Hesse). Marpurgenses = Marburg. Uraniborg / typographia tua implied — Tycho's printing-house on Hven.
  • Technical terms (per editorial glossary–5.2). Crepusculum → twilight; Refractiones → refractions; eccentricos / epicyclos / eccentriculum / aequantium ratione / circellos → eccentrics / epicycles / little eccentric / the reckoning of the equants / little circles; Obliquitas Zodiaci → obliquity of the Zodiac; in secundis scrupulis → down to the seconds (of arc); Stellarum Fixarum loca → the places of the fixed stars; Instrumenta → instruments; Doctrina Triangulorum → the Doctrine of Triangles (= trigonometry; kept literal with gloss). liquiditate materiae caelestis → the fluidity of the celestial matter (Rothmann's fluid-heavens doctrine). quinta Essentia → quintessence (Tycho's retained Aristotelian fifth element, which Rothmann challenges). Cometa Anni 85 = the comet of 1585.
  • Astronomical-source citations. Copernico lib. 3 Reuol. cap. 20, lib. 4 cap. 3, et lib. 5 cap. 4, 22 = Copernicus, De revolutionibus, Book 3 ch. 20, Book 4 ch. 3, Book 5 chs. 4 and 22 — given as Rothmann wrote them, untranslated as references.
  • Works named. Tomorum tuorum de nouis Mundi Aetherei generationibus = Tycho's De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis (1588), here paraphrased by Rothmann as "your volumes on the new generations of the aetherial world." nostris Obseruationibus / opere nostro Stellarum Fixarum / Elementa mea Astronomica = Rothmann's own (largely unpublished) works on observations, the fixed stars, and astronomical elements. demonstrationem 47 p. 1 Eucl. = Euclid, Elements I.47 (the Pythagorean theorem).
  • Greek (transcribed and translated).
  • φιλαληθὴς (philalēthḗs) = "lover of truth," qualifying the disinterested third-party judge required to settle a scientific dispute.
  • πρὸς ἄλληλα (pròs állēla) = "in relation to one another" / "mutually" — that the circular components of planetary motion are interrelated, so eccentric/epicycle transpositions preserve the same motion and distance.
  • Allusions.
  • diuinae particula aurae = Horace, Satires II.2.79 ("a particle of the divine breath"), of the human soul.
  • Omnis Aristippum decuit color et status et res = Horace, Epistles I.17.23 ("Every complexion, condition, and circumstance became Aristippus") — kept as the quoted sententia, applied ironically to Ursus's chameleon adaptability.
  • Vatiniano … odio = "Vatinian hatred," proverbial extreme hatred (after Catullus / Cicero on P. Vatinius).
  • mercedem anguinam = "a serpent's wage" — the proverbial ingratitude (a snake biting its benefactor) Rothmann says he got for teaching the clockmaker.
  • ceruis … qui rem Veneream sectantur, figendis — a courtly euphemism: the Landgrave is busy "staking out" (hunting) the harts in rut; rendered with the period decorum kept.
  • Epistolary formulae. Opening LITERAS tuas … accepimus (no salutation rubric in this printed text). Closing Vale … Cassellis 22. Septemb., Anno 89. T.T. — Vale → Farewell; T.T. = Tuus Totus ("Yours wholly"), expanded in translation.
  • Calendar / DATING CRUX (flagged, not reconciled). The dateline as printed reads "Cassellis 22. Septemb., Anno 89" = 22 September 1589 (O.S.; Hesse-Kassel kept the Julian calendar at this date). The edition's catalogue label field reads "1589. 22. august. Kassel" — i.e. 22 August, one month earlier, contradicting the letter's own dateline. Per this chapter's convention the header leads with the as-written September date and appends the catalogued August date; the discrepancy is recorded, not resolved. The August reading is unsupported by the source text and is most plausibly a catalogue slip (or a confusion with the "17. Augusti" receipt-date that opens the letter). Internal date data are consistent with September: Rothmann says he received Tycho's 21 February letter "scarcely on 17 August," reports writing to Dee "last May," and refers to letters the Landgrave gave him "last July" — all anterior to a September composition.
  • Source-text artefacts (working transcription). The verbatim Source block reproduces the local working transcription (harvested from the modern critical edition, normalising the 1596 Epistolae). Stray superscript-A tokens appended to words (LITERASA, etA, morbumA, quamA, TheologiA, meamA, definiuiA, MeridiemA, hocA, eaA, rationeA, examinareA, StellarumA, insuperA, affectuA, terrenaA, significauiA, aurae.A, compendiaA, satis. A, Septemb.A) are editorial footnote/variant anchors carried over from the source apparatus, not part of Rothmann's Latin; they are left in the Source block (this chapter's convention forbids silent alteration of the transcription) and are ignored in the translation. Likewise the run-together HorologiopaeoCopernicum, the variant spelling ipius for ipsius, and the compressed closing …ama.Cassellis…Anno 89.T. T.CHRISTOPHORVS ROTHMANNVS. are transcription artefacts; word-division is restored only in the translation.
  • Negation check. The dense polemical clauses were checked for negation count: e.g. Vbi enim quicquam detraxi…? (rhetorical "where did I detract anything…?"); ne quid impium de me cogites ("do not think anything impious of me"); ne me prorsus caecum effice ("do not make me out quite blind"); nec velim Obseruationem illam publices / nec … sententiam scriptis tuis immisce (two distinct prohibitions: "I should not wish you to publish that observation" and "do not mix my opinion into your writings"); nec dignus est, ut nomen ipsius scriptis nostris inferatur ("nor is he worthy that his name be brought into our writings"). Each negation in the source is reproduced once in the translation.
  • Correction. Two low-severity translation fixes: 'de vestra generatione et corruptione' restored to 'your generation and corruption' (2nd-person 'vestra', not 3rd-person 'their [the comets']'); 'ceruis...figendis' restored to 'spearing' (the gerundive of figere, active spearing/shooting) in place of 'staking out'.

2 defects found:
- mistranslation (low). "vestra" is a 2nd-person possessive ("your") consistent with Rothmann's tu/tuus address of Tycho throughout the letter; the translation silently recasts it as 3rd-person "their [the comets']", shifting whose opinion is being solicited. At: “chiefly so that you might explain to me your opinion concerning their [the comets'] generation and corruption”.
- mistranslation (low). "figendis" (gerundive of figere, to pierce/transfix/shoot) denotes active spearing or shooting of the rutting stags, i.e. hunting them; "staking out" implies passive surveillance rather than the kill, softening the verb's sense. At: “he is now devoting his pains to staking out the harts that pursue the work of Venus [are in rut]”.

Repaired. No Dreyer extract was available for this letter (no page reference), so no text error-type claim could be checked against the edition of record; there were none to check. Both defects are mistranslation-type and were verified from the letter's own the transcribed original, which is internal evidence independent of Dreyer: 'vestra' is unambiguously a 2nd-person possessive (contrasts with 3rd-person eorum/sua) and 'figendis' is the gerundive of figere (to spear/transfix), not a verb of passive watching. Both fixes are grammatically certain from the source text as published. Nothing else in the letter was touched.
