The original

TYCHO BRAHE AD THADDÆUM HAGECIUM.

PLURIMUM sane gavisus sum, optime mi THADDÆE, quod ex literis tuis, his ipsis diebus mihi redditis, perceperim te adhuc mediocriter bona, pro hac ætate, frui valetudine. Verebar enim ne gravissimi illi morbi, quos te diu multumque infestasse, ultimis significasti, sæviores adhuc redditi, te nimis valetudinarium lecto detinerent, vel forte humanitus tibi quid interea accidisset, præsertim ex quo meis literis præcedente anno per civem quendam Gorlicensem ad te datis nihil tanto tempore responderis. Itaque valde etiam nunc addubito, an has recte acceperis, siquidem nullam mentionem in his literis facias, vel de contentis earum vel de Tomo nostro primo Progymnasmatum Astronomicorum, quem unâ Magnifico Domino IACOBO CURTIO et tibi inspiciendum dijudicandumque misi. Imo iam bis tibi eiusdem libri exemplar communicari curavi, posterius tamen locupletius auctum et in quo pauca admodum desiderarentur, quo minus publici iuris fierent. Mirari autem satis nequeo, quod in nullis literis interea ad me datis huius rei mentionem facias, quare ad aliorum manus pervenisse hos libros et alibi detineri suspicor. Id equidem invite ferrem. Quod Astronomica studia in hoc senio apud te conticescant, quodque Chymica ob vanitatem neglexeris, recte fieri censeo, his enim nimium indulgere, est, ut plurimum, oleum et operam perdere, illis penitius tractandis valetudinis in hac ingravescente ætate afflictio non sustinet; optime igitur feceris, si supramundanis, ut te etiam facere dicis, contemplandis et valetudini curandæ solummodo intentus fueris. Privilegium Cæsareum ultimum illud et quadam in parte locupletatum Doct. SAGERUS Lubecensis iam dudum ad me misit, pro quo etiam in ultimis literis et Ampliss. D. CURTIO et tibi gratias obnixe egi, quod et nunc facio.

Cometam quendam in Iulio et Augusto superioris anni illuxisse certum quidem est ex multorum relatione, at nos eum hîc Uraniburgi, utut diligentius forte quam alij cœlestibus attendentes, non conspeximus, tum quod rarissima ijs temporibus hîc fuerit serenitas, tum quia versus Septentrionem, si quando cœlum per intervalla sudum esset, chasmata crebra viserentur, quæ Cometam istic versantem per se, uti audio, admodum tenuem existentem, facile visui subduxerunt. Invigilavimus ijsdem mensibus omni serena nocte (quæ tamen uti dictum est crebra non fuit) Martis potissimum stellæ ad acronychium situm tendentis observationibus, ut discrimen calculi Alphonsini et Copernicei, quod in ipsa oppositione 9 gradus (maiori quam unquam alias intervallo) excessit, cœlitus dirimere liceret, utque hanc acronychiam Martis observationem cæteris per plurimos antecedentes annos habitis coniungerem. Deprehensus autem est Mars (ut et hoc obiter hîc indicem) quando Soli e diametro opponebatur, numeros COPERNICI 4½ gradibus excedere, Alphonsinos vero integris quinis gradibus antevertere, quæ differentia maior quam alias in ♂, cum pernox est, obveniens propter vicinitatem Perigæi eccentrici (ut communiter loquar) contigit, illic enim error in vero Apogæi loco, qui apud COPERNICUM quinos gradus attingit, admodum fœcundus existit, ut de eccentricitate, quam is suis hypothesibus patrocinari volens nimium coarctavit, ut de alijs quæ huc requiruntur nunc non dicam. Interim vero (ut ad rem redeam) dum Martis observationi, quoties noctes defæcatæ id permiserunt, gnaviter insistentes, Cometam illum, de quo iam rumor increbuerat, uspiam videre nequivimus, utut data opera illam cœli plagam circa finem Augusti crebrius inspicientes, quemadmodum et alij, qui in vicina Haffnia aliquas noctes huic inspiciendo vigiles traduxerunt, voto suo frustrati sunt. Perscriptum nihilominus ad me est, quod studiosus quidam Danus CHRISTIERNUS IOHANNIS RIPENSIS nomine, qui antequam in Germaniam concessisset, aliquot annis in exercitijs Astronomicis hîc mihi inservierat, confecto uno vel altero ad imitationem meorum instrumentulo, eius observationi, qua licuit accuratione, incubuisse, neque tamen aliquid certi nisi bis, ob exilitatem Cometæ et serenitatis penuriam denotasse. Quicquid tamen id fuerit, is brevi e Cimbria Patria sua (nuper enim in Daniam reversus est) huc ad me veniens secum adferet. Tunc si viderim operæ pretium esse, observationes istas examinabo atque an Parallaxes inde scrutari liceat, tentabo, ut vel sic, id quod nobis invitis neglectum est, quodammodo resarciam, atque hunc Cometam quinis illis maxima diligentia a me Cœlitus denotatis atque secundo Tomo Progymnasmatum Astronomicorum descriptis adiungam, mutuando mei quondam discipuli observationes atque eas loco mearum usurpando.

Quæ cl. ille PEUCERUS tibi de hac Crinitâ per literas significavit, nihil ferme ad rem faciunt, nullas enim habet observationes, quæ locum eius apparentem prodant, nedum quæ Parallaxibus enodandis sufficiant, imo ne quidem diem rectum annotat, quo illum viderit, neque ista invicem congruunt, quod ab initio dicat, se saltem bis eum vidisse in Asterismo Ursæ maioris et postea circa finem subiungat, quod a Capellæ et Hædorum sidere non procul constiterit; hi enim duo asterismi ultra duodecimam cœli partem inter se distant. Qualis igitur quæso hæc fuerit observatio etiam ex solo visu petita? Ista igitur otiosa et mentione indigna uti fuere, ita etiam nunc sint, relinquamus.

Gaudeo et præstantissimo illi D. CURTIO communi amico nostro congratulor, quod Sextantis instrumentum ex officina Lantgraviana adeptus sit, quo siderum intercapedines infallibiliter rimari possit. Est procul dubio ad imitationem cuiusdam ex meis factum, quo per unicum observatorem distantiæ mensurantur, licet nonnihil maiori negotio quam alijs Sextantibus, qui duos requirunt collimatores, quod si sic est, habet rimulas iuxta centrum prioribus pinnacidijs parallelas et divisionem per puncta transversalia nobis usitatam, tunc quoque fulcrum, cuius beneficio in omnem planitiem versus quascunque stellas commode dirigi queat: atqui hæc VITICHIUS Lantgravio apud me visa præordinavit. Verum ipsemet Princeps loco cochleæ, qua ego in laxando vel coarctando instrumenti angulo utor (quam VITICHIUS indicare neglexerat) catenulam rotulis inhærentem addidit. Poterit D. CURTIUS periculum fieri curare, an intermediante hoc suo novitio Sextante eædem prorsus quarundam fixarum remotiones deprehendantur, quales pag. 30 et sequenti in eo fragmento Epistolarum Astronomicarum ad Lantgravium scriptarum, quod iam dudum tecum communicavi, nostris Sextantibus cœlitus designatæ, exponuntur; eas enim scio exactas esse, quibus si Curtiani Sextantis inventa ad amussim corresponderint, non potest non eius perfecte elaboratus esse, cum omne verum suo vero consonum sit. Exoptarem autem, ut Quadrantem quoque iustæ magnitudinis et e solido metallo constantem pariter divisum et pinnacidijs instructum in promptu haberet, quo per altitudines meridianas Solis et stellarum declinationes unâ cum Poli sublimitate exquisite scrutari commodum foret, quamvis et hoc eodem Sextante intra sublimitatem sexaginta graduum ad æquilibrium Horizontis sit dispositum atque in plano meridiani directo id ipsum præstare non admodum sit invium, qua de re eum admonere potes.

Gratum insuper mihi est, quod Globum magnum sexpedalem in diametro construi curet, prospiciat autem diligenter ne in rotunditate eius undiquaque procuranda plus quam existimaret difficultatis lateat, præsertim ut ea in materiâ ligneâ utut varie composita, mutationi tamen auræ suaque ipsius natura instabilitati obnoxia, diuturna et constans sit. Consultius itaque duxerim, ut Globum istum ad rotunditatem accuratam, quoad fieri potest, deductum, permittat primum unius ad minimum æstatis calorem et hiemis frigus, varia ijs intercedente auræ mutatione, experiri, donec ad consistentiam aliquam deducatur, tumque denuo globositatem eius examinet, atque ubi opus fuerit, emendari curet, postmodum, ubi sensibiliter nihil remittere et variationem nullam suscipere animadverterit, laminis orichalcicis lævigatis et subtiliter adunatis superinduci faciat, et tunc demum in rotunditatem exquisitam omni ex parte redigi, politaque superficie subdivisionibus graduum in singula minuta per puncta transversalia commoditatem præbente elaborari provideat, alias enim stellas nostras, quas circiter octingentas cap. 2 prioris Tomi in ipso minuto adeoque nonnunquam semisse unius diligentissime quo ad longum et latum cœlitus derivatas recensuimus, vix eâ quâ opus fuerit subtilitate et correspondentia Globo insigniverit. Haud ignota loquor, nam et is noster orichalcicus Globus, qui sex pedes in diametro adæquat, aliquot annorum laboriosam difficultatem obiecit, antequam ad omnimodam perfectionem perductus sit, ut de sumptibus nihil dicam, qui si numerati sigillatim essent, forte mille Ioachimicorum expensam æquarent aut superarent.

Quod tam amice atque honorifice sæpedictus D. CURTIUS me ad comitia Imperialia, quæ proxime Ratisbonæ celebrabuntur, invitat, est cur illi officiose gratias agam, et si res meæ multipliciaque, quibus occupor, negotia id ferrent, eius benevolæ petitioni equidem non [renuntiarem]. Verum cum mihi in hac insula, utut non admodum magna, maximis tamen occupationibus distento, vix tantum supersit otij et temporis, ut in vicinas Daniæ oras ad consanguineos et amicos vel etiam propriorum negotiorum causa nonnunquam excurrere liceat, si itaque tam longinquum in Germaniam iter cum iactura et dispendio rerum, quas hîc tracto et urgeo, suscipere nequeam, habebit me D. CURTIUS pro congenita sibi humanitate et iudicij dexteritate, uti spero, sufficienter excusatum. Scripsissem vero ipsemet nunc illi, si tantum superesset opportunitatis, et nuncius in vicinam Germaniam properans abitum non adeo festinaret. Quod vero nunc hac in parte prætermissum fuerit, alias oblata commodiore occasione affatim compensare non supersedebo. Resaluta itaque meo nomine Excellentissimum D. CURTIUM tui uti video meique amantissimum.

Iamque uterque vestrum bene valete.

Tycho Brahe to Thaddaeus Hagecius.

I was indeed very glad, my excellent Thaddaeus, that from your letter — delivered to me these very days — I gathered that you still enjoy moderately good health, for your age. For I had feared that those most grievous illnesses, which (as you signified in your last) had long and sorely afflicted you, had grown still fiercer and were keeping you confined to your bed, too much an invalid, or that perhaps in the meantime some human accident had befallen you — especially since, to my letter sent to you last year by a certain citizen of Görlitz, you made no reply in so long a time. And so even now I am much in doubt whether you received that letter rightly, since in this present one you make no mention either of its contents or of our first volume of the Astronomical Preliminaries [Progymnasmata Astronomica], which I sent for the Magnificent Master Jacob Curtius and yourself together to inspect and judge. Indeed I have now twice had a copy of that same book conveyed to you — the later one, however, more richly enlarged, and in which very few things were lacking that would keep it from being made public. But I cannot wonder enough that in none of the letters sent to me in the interim do you make mention of this matter; wherefore I suspect that these books have come into other men's hands and are being detained elsewhere. That I should indeed take amiss. That astronomical studies should fall silent with you in this old age, and that you have neglected the chemical [studies] for their vanity, I judge to be rightly done; for to indulge too much in these is, for the most part, to waste oil and labour, while the affliction of your health in this advancing age does not permit the deeper handling of those [astronomical] matters; you will do best, therefore, if you are intent solely upon contemplating supramundane things — as you say you also do — and upon caring for your health. That last Imperial privilege, enlarged in a certain part, Doctor Sagerus of Lübeck sent to me some while ago; for which, in my last letter, I rendered earnest thanks both to the most worthy Master Curtius and to you, as I do now also.

That a certain comet shone forth in July and August of last year is indeed certain from the report of many; but we did not catch sight of it here at Uraniborg — though attending to the heavens perhaps more diligently than others — both because the clear weather was exceedingly rare here in those times, and because toward the North, whenever the sky was at intervals cloudless, frequent chasms [aurorae] were seen, which easily withdrew from view the comet lingering there — being of itself, as I hear, very faint. Throughout those same months, on every clear night (which, as I said, were not frequent), we kept watch chiefly on observations of the star Mars as it was tending toward its acronychal position, that we might settle from the sky itself the discrepancy of the Alfonsine and the Copernican calculation — which at the very opposition exceeded 9 degrees (a greater interval than ever before) — and that I might join this acronychal observation of Mars to the rest taken over very many preceding years. Mars was found (let me note this too here in passing), when it stood diametrically opposite the Sun, to exceed the numbers of Copernicus by 4½ degrees, but to outrun the Alfonsine ones by full five degrees; which difference, greater than at other times in Mars when it is visible all night long, came about on account of the nearness of the eccentric perigee (to speak in the common way), for there the error in the true place of the apogee, which in Copernicus amounts to five degrees, proves very fertile [of error] — to say nothing now of the eccentricity, which he, wishing to support his own hypotheses, contracted too much, nor of the other things that are required here. Meanwhile, however (to return to the matter), while we were diligently pressing on with the observation of Mars whenever clear nights allowed it, we were nowhere able to see that comet, of which the rumour had now grown, although we deliberately inspected that quarter of the sky rather often around the end of August; just as others too, who passed several nights watchful in nearby Copenhagen to inspect it, were frustrated of their wish. Nonetheless it has been written to me that a certain Danish student, Christen Hansen of Ribe by name, who before he withdrew into Germany had for some years served me here in astronomical exercises, having made one or another little instrument in imitation of mine, applied himself to the observation of it with what accuracy he could — yet noted nothing certain except twice, on account of the faintness of the comet and the scarcity of clear weather. Whatever it may have been, he will shortly bring it with him, coming here to me from his fatherland Cimbria [Jutland] (for he has lately returned to Denmark). Then, if I see it to be worth the trouble, I shall examine those observations and shall try whether parallaxes may be investigated from them, so that in this way I may in some measure repair what was, against our will, neglected, and join this comet to those five [comets] noted by me from the sky itself with the greatest diligence and described in the second volume of the Astronomical Preliminaries — borrowing the observations of my one-time pupil and using them in place of my own.

What that distinguished Peucer signified to you by letter about this hairy [star / comet] contributes almost nothing to the matter, for he has no observations that would yield its apparent place, far less such as would suffice for unravelling parallaxes; indeed he does not even note the right day on which he saw it, nor do those [statements] agree among themselves, since at the beginning he says he saw it at least twice in the asterism of the Great Bear, and afterward, near the end, he adds that it stood not far from the star of the She-Goat and the Kids; for these two asterisms are more than a twelfth part of the heaven apart from each other. Of what sort, then, I ask, can this observation have been, sought even from mere sight alone? Therefore, as those things were idle and unworthy of mention, so let them now be too; let us leave them.

I also rejoice and congratulate that most excellent Master Curtius, our common friend, that he has obtained a sextant instrument from the Landgrave's workshop, by which he may infallibly probe the intervals of the stars. It is without doubt made in imitation of one of mine, by which distances are measured by a single observer — albeit with somewhat greater trouble than other sextants, which require two sighters. If it is so, it has little slits beside the centre, parallel to the forward sights, and the division by transversal points that we use; then too a support, by whose aid it may be conveniently directed in every plane toward any stars whatever: and indeed these features Wittich devised beforehand, having seen [them] with me, for the Landgrave. But the Prince himself, in place of the screw which I use in loosening or tightening the angle of the instrument (which Wittich had neglected to point out), added a little chain attached to small wheels. Master Curtius will be able to have a trial made, whether by means of this novel sextant of his exactly the same distances of certain fixed stars are found as those set out on page 30 and the following, in that fragment of the Astronomical Letters written to the Landgrave (which I shared with you some while ago), designated by our sextants from the sky itself; for I know those to be exact, and if the findings of the Curtian sextant correspond to them to the line, then it cannot but be that his is perfectly wrought, since every truth is consonant with its own truth. But I could wish that he also had at hand a quadrant of just size and made of solid metal, likewise divided and furnished with sights, by which it would be convenient to investigate exactly, through the meridian altitudes of the Sun and stars, the declinations together with the elevation of the Pole — although this too may be done with the same sextant, when it is set to the balance of the horizon within an elevation of sixty degrees, and to do that very thing in the direct plane of the meridian is not very impracticable; about which matter you can advise him.

It is moreover pleasing to me that he is having a great globe, six feet in diameter, built; but let him diligently take care lest in procuring its roundness on every side there lie more difficulty than he would suppose — especially that it be lasting and constant, [being made] in wooden material which, however variously put together, is yet liable to the change of the air and by its own nature to instability. I should therefore have judged it more advisable that, having brought that globe to an accurate roundness so far as can be done, he should first let it experience the heat of at least one summer and the cold of [one] winter, with the varied change of air coming between them, until it is brought to some settled state; and then examine its globosity afresh, and where there is need have it corrected; thereafter, when he perceives that it sensibly slackens nothing and takes on no variation, let him have it overlaid with sheets of brass, smoothed and finely joined, and only then provide that it be reduced on every part to an exquisite roundness, and that its surface, once polished, be worked with subdivisions of the degrees into single minutes by transversal points, affording convenience; for otherwise he will scarcely mark out upon the globe, with the fineness and correspondence that will be needed, our stars — which, about eight hundred, we have catalogued in chapter 2 of the first volume, derived from the sky itself most diligently as to length and breadth, to the very minute and so sometimes to the half of one. I do not speak of things unknown [to me], for that brass globe of ours too, which equals six feet in diameter, set before [me] a laborious difficulty of several years before it was brought to perfection of every kind — to say nothing of the costs, which, were they to be reckoned item by item, would perhaps equal or surpass the outlay of a thousand joachimsthalers.

That the oft-named Master Curtius so kindly and honourably invites me to the Imperial Diet, which will shortly be held at Regensburg, is reason for me dutifully to render him thanks; and if my affairs and the manifold business with which I am occupied allowed it, I would indeed not [decline] his kind request. But since to me on this island — though not very large, yet [I being] stretched by the greatest occupations — there scarcely remains so much leisure and time that I may sometimes make an excursion to the neighbouring shores of Denmark to my kinsmen and friends, or even for the sake of my own affairs; if, then, I cannot undertake so long a journey into Germany with the loss and expense of the matters which I here handle and press forward, Master Curtius will hold me, by the humanity innate in him and the dexterity of his judgement, as I hope, sufficiently excused. I would indeed have written to him myself now, if only there were so much opportunity left, and the messenger hastening into nearby Germany were not hurrying his departure so much. But what has now been left undone in this respect, I shall not forbear amply to make up another time, when a more convenient occasion is offered. Return the greeting, therefore, in my name to the Most Excellent Master Curtius, most loving — as I see — of both you and me.

And now fare you both well.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Thaddaeus Hagecius = Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku (Thaddaeus Hagecius ab Hayek, 1525–1600), the Bohemian court physician, astronomer and alchemist at Prague, and a central figure in the European network that disputed the parallax of the 1572 nova; Tycho's long-standing correspondent and his principal channel to the imperial court of Rudolf II. The letter survives only as Tycho's own draft (Kladde) in the Vienna codex.
  • Date. The source carries no internal dateline; the catalogue assigns it to 1594 (identifier …15940000001), consistent with the internal content — Mars's opposition observed at Uraniborg and the "comet of last year, July–August," i.e. the comet of 1593 (C/1593 O1). Header dated "1594 [O.S.]" on that catalogue basis, with the year not asserted from any dateline the text itself supplies; the absence of a written dateline is itself noted here. Denmark was on the Julian calendar (O.S.) throughout.
  • Place. Uraniburgi — Uraniborg, Tycho's observatory on the island of Hven; the catalogue place-label "Uraniborg" matches the internal hîc Uraniburgi ("here at Uraniborg").
  • Persons named.
  • IACOBUS / D. CURTIUS = Jakob Kurz von Senftenau (Jacobus Curtius, d. 1594), imperial vice-chancellor under Rudolf II, an amateur of astronomy who had commissioned a sextant from the Hessen workshop and a six-foot globe; Tycho's dedicatee-in-prospect and patron at court. The recurring "common friend."
  • cl. ille PEUCERUS = Caspar Peucer (1525–1602), Melanchthon's son-in-law, physician and astronomer; here cited (and dismissed) for unreliable naked-eye notes on the 1593 comet.
  • Doct. SAGERUS Lubecensis = Doctor Sagerus of Lübeck (Caspar/Kaspar Sager), the intermediary who forwarded the renewed imperial printing privilege; identification by name and city as given.
  • VITICHIUS = Paul Wittich (c. 1546–1586) of Wrocław, the mathematician whose instrument designs Tycho says were realised at Kassel; Tycho repeatedly claimed (as here) that the Hessen sextant derived from his own instruments by way of Wittich's intermediary visit — a sensitive priority point in the Brahe–Wittich–Kassel triangle.
  • CHRISTIERNUS IOHANNIS RIPENSIS = Christen Hansen (Christiernus Iohannis) of Ribe, a former Danish assistant of Tycho's who attempted to observe the 1593 comet; "of Ribe" = the Jutland cathedral town. (e Cimbria Patria sua names Jutland/Cimbria as his homeland.)
  • Places.
  • Gorlicensem — a citizen of Görlitz (Lusatia), the courier of the previous year's lost-or-delayed letter.
  • Haffnia — Copenhagen.
  • officina Lantgraviana / Lantgravio — the Landgrave's workshop and Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel (d. 1592); the Kassel observatory's instrument shop.
  • Ratisbonae — Regensburg, site of the forthcoming Imperial Diet (comitia Imperialia; the Diet of 1594).
  • Cimbria — Jutland (the Cimbrian peninsula).
  • Works. Progymnasmata Astronomica (Tomus primus / secundus) = Tycho's Astronomiae Instauratae Progymnasmata — rendered "Astronomical Preliminaries"; vol. 1 (the nova/refraction/solar theory) was in circulating proof, vol. 2 was to treat the comets. fragmentum Epistolarum Astronomicarum ad Lantgravium = the printed gathering of the Epistolae Astronomicae (Liber I, the Hessen correspondence) Tycho had already shared with Hagecius; "page 30 and the following" cites that fragment.
  • Astronomy — technical terms.
  • acronychium situm / acronychiam … observationem — acronychal position/observation, i.e. Mars at opposition, rising as the Sun sets; kept as the technical term.
  • oppositio → opposition; Soli e diametro opponebatur → "stood diametrically opposite the Sun" (the same configuration).
  • calculus Alphonsinus / Copernicus → the Alfonsine and Copernican computation/tables; Tycho reports the sky falling 4½° from Copernicus and 5° from the Alfonsine numbers — the figures are reproduced exactly as written (9°, 4½°, 5°), with no silent reconciliation of the "9 degrees" total against the two component errors.
  • Perigaei eccentrici / vero Apogaei loco / eccentricitate → eccentric perigee / true place of the apogee / eccentricity; hypothesibus → hypotheses. Tycho's epistemic hedge (ut communiter loquar) "to speak in the common way" is preserved.
  • Parallaxes → parallaxes — the distance-test for whether the comet was sub- or supralunar.
  • chasmata crebra → "frequent chasms": aurorae / auroral rifts ("chasmata" is the period term for auroral phenomena toward the North); glossed [aurorae] rather than silently modernised.
  • Crinita → "the hairy [star]," a standard Latin epithet for a comet (stella crinita); kept as the image and glossed.
  • Asterismus Ursae maioris → "the asterism of the Great Bear"; Capellae et Haedorum sidere → "the star of the She-Goat and the Kids" (Capella and the Haedi, in Auriga). duodecima coeli pars → "a twelfth part of the heaven" (= 30°), left as written.
  • Poli sublimitas → "the elevation of the Pole" (polar altitude = latitude); altitudines meridianas … declinationes → meridian altitudes … declinations.
  • Instruments.
  • Sextans / Sextantibus → sextant(s); Quadrans → quadrant; Globus … orichalcicus / sexpedalis → a six-foot globe sheathed in orichalcum (brass / latten).
  • pinnacidia / pinnula → sights / pinnule; collimatores → "sighters" (the two observers a two-man sextant needs); rimulae → "little slits" (sighting slits); divisio per puncta transversalia → transversal-point division (Tycho's diagonal-dot scale subdivision).
  • cochlea → "screw"; catenula rotulis inhaerens → "a little chain attached to small wheels" (the Landgrave's chain-and-pulley fine-adjustment substituted for Tycho's screw); fulcrum → "support" (the instrument's mount/stand).
  • ad amussim → "to the line" (exactly, by rule); ad aequilibrium Horizontis → "to the balance of the horizon" (levelled).
  • Symbol. ♂ (in maior … in ♂) = Mars; the glyph is retained in the Source block and named "Mars" in the translation. Mars is elsewhere written out (Martis) in the same passage.
  • Money. mille Ioachimicorum → "a thousand joachimsthalers" (Joachimsthaler / thaler, the Bohemian Joachimsthal silver coin); kept as the period unit.
  • Chemistry / programme. Chymica ob vanitatem neglexeris → "you have neglected the chemical [studies] for their vanity"; supramundanis … contemplandis → "contemplating supramundane things" (matters above the world — devotional/theological, as opposed to alchemy). oleum et operam perdere → "to waste oil and labour," the proverbial tag, kept as idiom.
  • Editorial insertion in the source. eius benevolae petitioni equidem non [renuntiarem] — the bracketed verb [renuntiarem] ("[I would] not decline/renounce") is an editorial supply in the source (the draft left it incomplete); the brackets are reproduced as printed and carried into the translation as "[decline]." The negation non + the supplied verb yields "I would not decline," i.e. Tycho would accept were he free — the one negation in the clause is preserved.
  • Epistolary formulae. Salutation header AD THADDAeUM HAGECIUM kept as title-line. Closing Iamque uterque vestrum bene valete → "And now fare you both well" (plural, addressed to Hagecius and Curtius jointly — uterque vestrum); Resaluta … meo nomine → "Return the greeting in my name." No S./S.P.D. opening appears (it is a draft).
