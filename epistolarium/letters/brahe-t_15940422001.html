The original

GENERIS NOBILITATE, OMNI
DOCTRINARUM ET VIRTUTUM EXCELLENTIA PRÆSTANTISSIMO VIRO D. TYCHONI BRAHE, HWÆNE
INSULÆ, DOMINO ILLUSTR. REGIS DANIÆ CONSILIARIO, DOMINO ET AMICO SUO
HONORANDO.

EXTINCTA nunc prope Nobiliss. D. TYCHO tuarum de me literarum
memoria, quæ in huiusmodi rebus admodum labilis esse solet, et de humanitate tua
ea concepta opinione, ut apud ipsum quoque offensiones deferbuisse existimem, ad
amicitiam redeundum videtur. Quod hoc tempore vir præstantissimus Dominus
IUNIUS, qui nunc legatum ad vos agit,
diligenter egit, qui ut utrique nostrum plurimum favet, ita animorum
coniunctionem, quæ inter nos fuit, minime dissolvi cupit, neque ego fore
speravi, ut tantum laxaretur. Si ulla mea disputatione id evenire posse,
suspicatus fuissem, vel mihi indixissem silentium vel dissentientem, quia aliter
non ratio passa est, non disserentem habuisses amicum, cum hoc amicitia nostra
non ferre videatur. Quod ad scriptum nostrum attinet, defensionem eius nolo
agere, hoc tantum profiteri libet, scriptori nec voluntatem nec propositum
fuisse tui vel minimum commovendi. Tacuissem libenter, et ita res meæ suadebant,
nam Mathematica vix unquam cum ullo commodo tractavi, sed silere non passus es,
assentire non potui, nisi assentari voluissem; si absque argumento sententiam
dixissem, non acquievisses; candide et sincere tecum agi petijsti, gratam tibi
fore nostram operam pollicitus es. Occurrebant in Apologia tua, quæ stomachum
cuivis commovere potuissent, ut imperitiæ, ignorantiæ, mancipati iudicij et
similium insimulari, absque causa absque ulla offensione data. Itaque voluntatem
tuam meis rebus cœpi præferre, et illa qualicunque disquisitione tibi
gratificari libuit. Amicitia me dirigente pro veritate et Uraniæ tuæ dignitate
quædam attuli, quæ eo acceptiora fore credideram, quo magis pro his facerent,
quia has Deas tanto studio et industria colis, ut nemo hoc nostro sæculo pari,
superioribus nemo magis; ut nostra defensione offensum te iri, ne suspicatus
fuerim, præsertim si absque contumelia id agerem, a qua certe ita cavi, ut ab
igne, neque enim Philosophum eiusmodi contentionem decere existimavi, neque ullo modo nostræ convenire amicitiæ. Nonnulla certe res ipsa
a me expresserat, quæ in quæstionem tantum directa, in offensionem non erant
rapienda. Si tua perverti, distraxi, male accommodavi et cætera, quæ nunc et
excidere et si hærerent non repetenda sunt, loca modo
designes. Quæ feci inscius, non invitus mutabo. Brevitati equidem studui, quæ
copiose et pluribus locis a te allata sunt, ne prolixitas tædium pareret,
contraxi, sensu tuo religiose observato, in nudam argumenti speciem redegi, ubi
dissentiendum fuit, partim serio, partim etiam per iocum ostendi; idque
doctissimorum et optimorum, in simili negocio, exemplo, ut inde offensum te iri
nequaquam crediderim, præsertim cum nihil iniuriose fieret, aut alioqui
cogitatum sit. Tua quoque me nemo magis admiratus est semper, illorumque iusta
æstimatio nunquam passa est amorem tui minui, tantum abest, ut ijs quicquam
voluerim detractum, quin potius indefessum tuum et vix imitabile in cœlum
studium in cœlum semper, ubicunque licuit, tulerim; neque enim si in re una
dissentimus, eaque non tanti ponderis, in cæteris similiter, sed ubi ex apodicto
agis, me tuis subscribentem inprimis habes. Ac in illa re, quæ a me hactenus
allata sunt, tuo subijcio iudicio, tuo etiam permitto arbitrio, salva veritate,
quam scio te salvam velle, mutes, expungas, cancelles quæ libet; privatim tecum
egi, nec nostra ad alios referenda fuere, qui parum illa vel faciebant, vel
sapiebant; mihi uno verbo si constitisset, molestius te nostra capere, quæ in
gratiam tuam fuere scripta, in eandem quoque abolita fuissent. Grati animi
officia a me requiris, nec alia a me unquam profecta sunt, eaque ex amore magis
quam debito, nisi communi nomine deberi possint, quod in commune Reipub.
literariæ incrementum a te immensa præstita sint, quo nomine patiar et me
teneri. Quod ad factam mei mentionem apud Regem et ad D. Cancellarium
attinet, in eo quoque benevolentiam tuam agnosco et amplector, sed nec petij nec
opus fuit: Serenissimum Regem ipse et omnes
mei, quamvis aulam non sectemur, clementissimum semper experti sumus, quem Deus optimus max. conservet. D. Cancellarius, antequam ulla inter vos esset notitia, meis rebus
favit plurimum, et aliqua sangvinis ratio est, nec mea illi familiæ officia
unquam defuere. Ab illo vero tempore vix alloqui eum contigit, semel fortasse in
casu cuiusdam ægri. Sed ista valeant, et quæcunque alia, ex quibus amicitia
nostra turbari possit, ipse hoc accepto documento, ei, ut potero, studebo, nec
dubito quin et tu alienior non sis futurus. Si controversi quicquam inter nos contigerit, me facile silentem habebis, a te etiam
atque etiam peto, ut absque iniuriosis vocibus id fiat; his non inquiritur sed
supprimitur veritas. Illa inter nos regula sanciatur, ut nudis solum rationibus
agatur, extra rem nihil proferatur. Sic disserere integra amicitia licebit.
Cupivit doctissimus vir D. Legatus, ut secum te
inviserem, non patiuntur id amici. Si venissem, gratusne fuissem hospes? Interea
his literis revisere te volui, et amicitiam nostram redintegrare. Tu amicum, qui
veram profitetur amicitiam, pristino more et amore complectere. Accepistis natum
nobis esse infantem Principem exoptatissimum, R. matrem vultu et corporis lineamentis refert, redivivum diceres
beatæ memoriæ illustriss. FRIDERICUM
Regem, in lucem cœpit contendere hora tertia post Μ. N. diei 19. Februarij, in
eam venit media ad quartam. Arx Sterlini eiusdem cum hac nostra urbe fere
latitudinis est, vix uno aut altero scrupulo in Boream declinat, Uraniam, si
placet, consule. Salutat P. T. frater
meus natu maior; ad te hoc tempore scripsisset, nisi Serenissimæ
Reginæ negotia impedijssent;
putat sibi maiores gratias esse redditas, quam illud epigramma meritum est;
gratum tamen ipsi fuit, sua tibi accepta esse, et paratus est, quibus poterit
officijs, tibi gratificari.

Vale præstantissime D. et clementissimus Deus tibi
vitam et vires suppeditet, ut illi admirando operi, quod præ manibus habes,
optatum finem possis imponere.

Datæ Edinburgi 22. Aprilis.

Tui observantiss.
IOAN.
CRAIGIUS,
Doctor Medicus.

Salutem precor doctissimis et præstantissimis viris Domm. D. D. CRAIGIJS.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the man most pre-eminent in nobility of birth and in every excellence of learning and virtue, Master Tycho Brahe of the island of Hven, councillor to the Most Illustrious King of Denmark, his lord and friend to be honoured.

Now that the memory of your letters concerning me is nearly extinguished, most noble Master Tycho — a memory that in matters of this kind is wont to be quite fleeting — and having formed of your kindness the opinion that with you too the offences have cooled, it seems a return must be made to friendship. This at the present time the most pre-eminent man Master Junius, who is now acting as envoy to you, has diligently brought about; for as he greatly favours us both, so he by no means wishes the union of minds that there was between us to be dissolved — nor did I hope that it would be so far loosened. Had I suspected that it could come about through any disputation of mine, I should either have imposed silence upon myself, or — since reason did not allow otherwise — you would have had a friend who dissented but did not dispute, since this our friendship seems not to bear. As regards my writing, I do not wish to mount a defence of it; I am content only to declare this much: that the writer had neither the will nor the intent of provoking you even in the least. I would gladly have kept silent, and so my own affairs counselled me, for I have scarcely ever handled mathematics with any profit; but you did not suffer me to be silent, and I could not assent unless I had wished to play the flatterer; had I pronounced an opinion without an argument, you would not have been satisfied; you asked that you be dealt with candidly and sincerely, [and] you promised that my service would be welcome to you. There occurred in your Apologia things that could have stirred anyone's gall — such as being charged with inexperience, ignorance, a judgement enslaved, and the like, without cause, without any offence given. And so I began to set your wish before my own affairs, and it pleased me to gratify you with that examination, such as it was. With friendship guiding me, on behalf of the truth and of the dignity of your Urania I brought forward certain things, which I had believed would be the more acceptable the more they made for these [Goddesses], because you cultivate these Goddesses with such zeal and industry that no one in this our age does so equally, and no one in earlier ages more; so that I never suspected you would be offended by my defence, especially if I carried it out without insult — from which indeed I guarded myself as from fire, for I judged that a contention of this kind neither befitted a philosopher nor was in any way suited to our friendship. Certain things, to be sure, the subject-matter itself had wrung from me, which, being aimed only at the inquiry, were not to be snatched up into offence. If I have distorted, pulled apart, ill-adapted your [statements] and the rest — which now both escape me and, even if they stuck [in mind], are not to be rehearsed — only point out the passages. What I did unknowingly I shall change, and not unwillingly. I did indeed aim at brevity: the things adduced by you copiously and in many places I drew together, lest prolixity breed tedium; with your sense scrupulously preserved, I reduced [them] to the bare appearance of the argument; where I had to dissent, I showed it partly in earnest, partly even in jest — and that after the example of the most learned and the best men in a like business — so that I could in no way have believed you would be offended thereby, especially since nothing was done injuriously, nor was so intended in any case. No one, too, has ever admired your [achievements] more than I, and a just esteem of them has never allowed my love of you to be lessened — so far am I from having wished anything detracted from them, that rather I have always, wherever I was permitted, borne your tireless and scarcely imitable zeal toward heaven up to heaven; for if we dissent in one matter, and that one of no great weight, it does not follow that [we do so] likewise in the rest — but where you proceed from demonstration, you have me subscribing to your views among the very first. And in that matter, the things which have hitherto been brought forward by me I submit to your judgement, I commit also to your discretion: with the truth kept safe — which I know you wish kept safe — change, strike out, cancel what you please. I dealt with you privately, and my writings were not to be carried to others, who either set little store by them or had little understanding of them; had it been established to me in a single word that you took my writings amiss — which were written for your sake — for that same sake they would also have been abolished. You ask of me the offices of a grateful mind; nor have any others ever proceeded from me, and these out of love rather than out of debt — unless they can be owed under the common title, since immense things have been furnished by you toward the common increase of the Republic of Letters, under which title I shall suffer myself too to be bound. As regards the mention made of me before the King and to Master the Chancellor, in that too I acknowledge and embrace your goodwill, but I neither asked it nor was there need: I myself and all my [house], though we do not attend the court, have always found the Most Serene King most clement — whom may God, Best and Greatest, preserve. Master the Chancellor, before there was any acquaintance between you, greatly favoured my affairs, and there is some tie of blood, nor have my services to that family ever been wanting. But since that time it has scarcely befallen me to speak with him — once perhaps, in the case of a certain sick man. But let these matters rest, and whatever others there may be from which our friendship could be disturbed; I myself, having received this lesson, will attend to it as I shall be able, and I do not doubt that you too will not be the more estranged. If anything contentious should arise between us, you will easily have me silent; [and] I beg of you again and again that it be done without injurious words: by these the truth is not investigated but suppressed. Let this rule be sanctioned between us, that we proceed by bare reasons alone, and that nothing be brought forward beside the matter [in hand]. Thus it will be permitted to dispute with friendship whole. The most learned man Master the Envoy wished that I should visit you together with him; my friends do not allow it. Had I come, should I have been a welcome guest? Meanwhile by this letter I have wished to revisit you, and to make our friendship whole again. Do you embrace, in your former manner and love, a friend who professes a true friendship. You [pl.] have received word that a most longed-for infant Prince has been born to us; he recalls his royal mother in countenance and in the lineaments of his body — you would call him King Frederick, of illustrious blessed memory, brought to life again. He began to strive into the light at the third hour after Μ. N. [midnight] on the 19th day of February; into it he came at half past three. The Castle of Stirling is of nearly the same latitude as this our city, scarcely by one scruple or two does it incline to the North — consult Urania, if you please. My elder brother greets you most warmly; he would have written to you at this time, had the affairs of the Most Serene Queen not hindered him; he thinks greater thanks have been rendered to him than that epigram deserved; yet it was pleasing to him that his [verses] were acceptable to you, and he is ready, by whatever offices he can, to gratify you.

Farewell, most pre-eminent Master, and may God most clement supply you with life and strength, that upon that admirable work which you have in hand you may be able to impose the wished-for end.
Given at Edinburgh, 22 April.
Most observant of you,
John Craig,
Doctor of Medicine. I wish greeting to the most learned and most excellent men, the Lord Doctors Craig.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Ioan. Craigius, Doctor Medicus = John Craig (Joannes Craig, d. 1620), Scottish physician and mathematician, trained at Frankfurt(-Oder) and physician at Edinburgh, later physician to James VI/I. He is the fourth of Tycho's four acrimonious antagonists (with Ursus, Wittich, and Rothmann). This letter (Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII, 362.2–364.18) is Craig's complete capitulation in the cometary dispute that had run since Craig's first reply to Tycho's 1588 De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis (letter of May 1589), through Craig's combative Capnuraniae restinctio of 1592 (his refutation of comets being raised into the aether) to this conciliatory peace overture; a parallel letter from his brother on his behalf followed the same year (TBOO VII, 364.20–365.16).
  • Hwaenae insulae … Regis Daniae consiliario — "of the island of Hven … councillor to the King of Denmark": the address gives Tycho's seat (Uraniborg on Hven) and his royal office under Frederik II / Christian IV.
  • Dominus Iunius … legatum ad vos agit / D. Legatus — "Master Junius … is acting as envoy to you" / "Master the Envoy": Peter Young (latinised Iunius), the Scots ambassador (royal tutor and diplomat) then on embassy to Denmark; Tycho had earlier commended Craig to him (letters of 1590 and 1593). Craig's wish to accompany the envoy on a visit to Hven was blocked by his friends.
  • Apologia tua — Tycho's Apologia: the polemical reply (within the cometary controversy) in which, Craig complains, he had been charged with "inexperience, ignorance, a judgement enslaved." Translated as the work-title Apologia; italicised on first use.
  • scriptum nostrum — "my writing": Craig's own contribution to the dispute (his Capnuraniae restinctio and attendant critiques); rendered neutrally as "writing" since Craig declines to defend or re-title it here.
  • Uraniae … has Deas … Uraniam consule — "your Urania … these Goddesses … consult Urania": Urania is the Muse of astronomy, here a figure for Tycho's astronomical work (and for astronomy/astrology generally). The closing Uraniam, si placet, consule ("consult Urania, if you please") is a playful invitation to Tycho to cast or check the new prince's nativity from the data given; "Urania" kept, per the edition's Uraniae convention.
  • ex apodicto agis — "where you proceed from demonstration": apodicto (from Greek ἀπόδειξις, apodictic/demonstrative proof) — Craig concedes Tycho's conclusions wherever they rest on demonstration, dissenting only on the one disputed (and, he says, minor) point. Latinised Greek technical term; kept and glossed.
  • Reipub. literariae — "the Republic of Letters" (Respublica literaria), the early-modern commonwealth of scholars; expanded from the abbreviation.
  • The King and the Chancellor. apud Regem … ad D. Cancellarium — the King is James VI of Scotland (later James I of England), styled Serenissimus Rex; the Chancellor is John Maitland of Thirlestane, Lord Chancellor of Scotland, to whom Tycho had also written about Craig (1591–92) and with whom Craig claims a tie of blood (aliqua sangvinis ratio). Serenissimae Reginae = Anne of Denmark, James's queen (and sister of Christian IV).
  • frater meus natu maior — "my elder brother": Thomas Craig (of Riccarton, c. 1538–1608), the jurist and Neo-Latin poet, author of the epigram (verses) sent to Tycho that this passage acknowledges; he sent his own letter to Tycho the same year.
  • The royal birth. infantem Principem exoptatissimum … FRIDERICUM Regem … diei 19. Februarij — the "most longed-for infant Prince" is Henry Frederick, Prince of Wales (b. 19 February 1593/94 O.S. at Stirling Castle, d. 1612), James VI's heir, said here to resemble his late grandfather King Frederik II of Denmark ("Frederick … brought to life again"), the dedicatee's former patron. Accepistis is second-person plural ("you [pl.] have received word") — Craig addresses the news to Tycho and his circle. Date note: the birth-date 19 February is given inside the letter; the letter's own dateline is 22 April. Both are Old Style (Scotland and Denmark kept the Julian calendar); reproduced as written, not converted. (The civil year in Scotland began 25 March, so 19 February 1594 in modern reckoning fell in the legal year 1593 — not stated in the source and not interpolated here.)
  • Birth-time crux — hora tertia post Μ. N. … media ad quartam. The Greek abbreviation Μ. N. is read as midnight — most plausibly Μεσονύκτιον (Greek "midnight") or a Greek-lettered abbreviation of Latin Media Nox — giving "began to strive into the light at the third hour after midnight … came [fully] into it at half past three [a.m.]." Media ad quartam = "the middle toward the fourth [hour]" = 3:30. The expansion of Μ. N. is editorial: the Greek glyphs are kept verbatim in the Source block, the bracketed gloss [midnight] marks the reading, and the precise resolution is flagged, not asserted — this is the named crux. (Craig gives the time precisely so that Tycho can cast the prince's geniture, hence the immediately following latitude of Stirling and the "consult Urania" invitation.)
  • Arx Sterlini … latitudinis … uno aut altero scrupulo … in Boream — "the Castle of Stirling … of [nearly the same] latitude … by one scruple or two … to the North": scrupulus here = a minute of arc (1/60 of a degree); Craig tells Tycho that Stirling lies almost on Edinburgh's parallel, scarcely a minute or two further north — geodetic data supplied for the horoscope.
  • Dateline. Datae Edinburgi 22. Aprilis = "Given at Edinburgh, 22 April" (O.S.); the year is not written in the source dateline but is securely 1594 from the edition (TBOO VII) and from the contemporaneous royal birth (19 Feb. 1593/94), hence bracketed in the header.
  • Subscription. Tui observantiss. = "Most observant of you" (a courtesy superlative parallel to the E. Tuae studiosiss. of the formula list,); Doctor Medicus = "Doctor of Medicine."
  • A crux, not a clean continuation. The recovered line is a standard epistolary salutation formula ('Salutem precor...' — 'I wish greeting to...') addressed, in the dative plural, to 'the Lord Doctors Craig' (Domini Doctores Craigii) — i.e. both brothers collectively, John and Thomas Craig. A letter signed by John Craig alone closing by greeting 'the Doctors Craig' in the plural, apparently including himself, does not read as a natural authorial sign-off.
  • Likely explanation, left unresolved. This has the shape of an opening salutation, not a closing one — plausibly the superscription of an adjoining letter (perhaps from Tycho, or a third party, to both Craig brothers jointly) that has been run on to the end of John Craig's letter in the source transcription. Flagged here rather than resolved; per instructions, no web search was made to chase it further.
