The original

NOBILITATE GENERIS, VIRTUTE ET DOCTRINA PRÆSTANTI AC MATHEMATUM ILLUSTRATORI EXIMIO D. TYCHONI BRAHE DANO, INSULÆ HUÆNÆDANICI HELLESPONTI PRÆSIDI, DOMINO ET AMICO OBSERVANDO.
S.

ACCEPI literas tuas, Vir Nobilissime et amice observande, quæ fuerunt et gratissimæ et exoptatissimæ. Sed cum ex eis intellexissem meas tibi non fuisse redditas, vehementer conturbatus sum, cœpique quiritari de iniqua sorte, quod ea utrique nostrum invidit illam iucunditatem, quam percepturi eramus, tu quidem legendo mea, ego sciendo, mea tibi reddita esse. Fuisset etiam liberatus sinistra suspicione apud te WITTICHIUS noster, quod tuas mihi non reddidisset, qui certe non solum primo quoque tempore fidelissime tuas mihi per certos homines reddidit, verum etiam literis suis mihi persuadere annisus est, ut me transferrem isthuc, invitabatque ut comes esse vellem sui ad te reditus. Addebat ad persuadendum te ex certa scientia et voluntate Regis ea ad me perscribere ac daturum fuisse ipsummet Regem literas invitatorias ad me, si prius ipsi per te de mea certa voluntate constitisset, quam nunc solum per tuas literas explorare voluit. Hæc vero etsi in tuis ad me non ita diserte perscripta et expilata essent, tamen persuasiones illas ab optimo et propensissimo eius erga utrumque nostrum animo proficisci accipiebam. Tuas quoque rationes, quod ab eodem animo proficiscerentur, admittebam omniaque illa interpretabar, ut verum amicum decuit. Quare carere debes hac in parte omni sinistra suspicione de WITTICHIO nostro. Præstitit enim officium boni viri et amici fidelissimi. Me vero respondisse ad tuas mense Februario anni præteriti, testis esse potest D. CHYTRÆUS. Ad ipsum enim meas ad te misi, et ut eas ad te perferendas curaret, rogavi. Respondit mense Aprili anni superioris se meas accepisse et ad te primo quoque tempore et commoditate missurum esse. Intercidisse igitur meas inter Rostochium et Hafniam oportuit, si alicui perferendæ datæ sunt. Hæc tu ipse, si ita lubet, ex ipsomet D. CHYTRÆO cognoscere poteris. Quod ad invitationem attinet, ut me transferam isthuc, ardua et difficilis est quæstio. Ac primum quæso te, mi TYCHO, expende ipsemet accuratius et verte omnes rationes, quam mihi ea difficilia sint factu. Sum ingravescente ætate; ago enim annum 56. Sum pater multorum liberorum. Commigrare igitur in alienas terras et quasi spontaneum exilium eligere, ubi et mores gentis et lingua peregrina est, profecto difficile, durum et miseriâ plenum est. Addo neque honorificum mihi et meis futurum esse absque honesta et decenti vocatione in alienas terras migrare. Quid enim aliud vestrates dicturos aut saltem suspicaturos esse cogitare possum, quando me non vocatum venisse ad vos conspicerent, quam me hinc vel eiectum vel egestate compulsum alienas quæsivisse sedes? Quid nostrates? Levem et inconstantem parumque prudentem, quod me unius hominis amica invitatione tam facile permoveri passus sim, incerta pro certis elegerim, verba honoris pro amplissimis rebus acceperim. Dicent etiam haud dubie temerarium et inconsideratum, et qui conditionis et existimationis suæ nullam scirem habere rationem, quod qui olim Cæsaris Medicus fuerim, honeste et decenter accitus; qui ante quinquennium a Rege Sueciæ, deinde ab ordinibus Stiriæ expetitus, cui etiam, si voluissem aut vellem, locus fuerit, et esse possit adhuc apud Regem Poloniæ, denique qui huius Regni nobilis civis sum, nunc sponte et temere extra patriam ultro sedes quæram et servitium expetam. Ac ut tandem finiam, migrare ad vos honorifice non vocatus, non propositis mihi certis conditionibus, plane non videtur consultum, non meæ ætatis est, non conditionis, non dignitatis. Excurrere vero ad te, ut præclara illa tua studia Chymica et Mathematica meis oculis conspicerem, teque præsentem complecterer, et in tuo amore totus conquiescerem, percuperem sane, nisi in eo quoque non paucæ essent difficultates et quidem graves: iter longum et difficile, sumptus non exigui, denique excursio illa nimis magna esse videretur. Sed si aliqua commoditas veniendi isthuc et redeundi se offerret, prætermittere eam nulla ratione vellem. Nunc quoque impedior cura uxoris meæ, quæ partui vicina est. Vides, mi TYCHO, meas iustas et honestas rationes, quibus impediar et detinear, ut neque tuæ benevolentiæ, quam ego summam et optimam esse erga me agnosco, neque meo desiderio satisfacere queam. Itaque pro tuo isto erga me optimo et benignissimo animo, cura et solicitudine mei, quod mihi optima cupias, omniaque officia amoris et benevolentiæ sponte deferas, tibi maximas, quas verbis exprimere licet, ago et habeo gratias, vicissimque tibi persuasissimum esse cupio, me ita amore tui incensum esse, ut si liceret tecum esse, reliquum meæ vitæ iucundissimum putarem, tuaque causa nihil non facturum esse.

Hæc fere in prioribus meis ad te scripseram, quæ quoniam alicubi perijsse intelligo, denuo repetere hisce oportuit. Venio ad alteram Epistolæ tuæ partem. Gratulor tibi vehementer istud otium Mathematicum, in quo te versari intelligo. Macte virtute, mi TYCHO, et in hoc cursu, ut cœpisti, studium illud Astronomicum illustrare perge. Ephemeridem Solarem huius anni ex tuis observationibus exstructam accepi, et pro ea maximas tibi ago gratias. Mihi tantæ magnitudinis instrumentum nullum est, ut singula scrupula capere possit, neque in meis ædibus ea mihi est commoditas, ut etiamsi tale quid habeam, collocare queam. Quadrante hactenus meo usus sum in meis stellarum et Cometarum observationibus. In eo dena et quina habentur scrupula. Radium satis magnum habeo, de quo hauddubie WITTICHIUS noster tibi significavit. Ad Solem igitur nullæ mihi fuerunt observationes.Placet quod in Sole habeas rationem parallaxeos, quam huc usque omnes neglexerunt. WITTICHIUS noster eam quoque minime prætermittendam esse censuit. In Apogeo Solis video te tribus circiter gradibus anteriorem esse Copernico. Sed variatio Apogei etiam plurium graduum non mutat notabiliter motum Solis, in cuius Apogei constitutione laborarunt summi Artifices. De Cometarum parallaxibus quæ perscripsisti, ea quoque plurimum grata et iucunda fuerunt. In parallaxi Cometæ anni 77 meum lapsum iam ante agnovi et memet correxi. Nam non ita instructus fui instrumentis idoneis, quam olim Viennæ in observatione novi sideris. Defuerunt mihi etiam multæ aliæ commoditates. Ac tu optime admones ex meo ipsius scripto longe minorem parallaxin fieri oportuisse, quemadmodum huius erroris in Epistola ad MYLIUM et in scripto de proximo Cometa Gœrlitij impresso mentionem feci. Quod culminaverit cum Aquila, non ita a me scriptum est, quasi id a me fuisset observatum (nam culminatio illa Sole supra Horizontem exstante fuerat) sed quod saltem crassiori quadam indagine, collata cum alijs observationibus, de parallaxi aliquo modo iudicium fieri posse existimaverim. Scripsit autem alius quidam ineptus et meus plagiarius, culminasse Aquilam cum Cometa die 6 Decemb. h. 5. Ceterum quæ ego in mea Dialexi olim cap. 9 de invenienda parallaxi ex culminatione Phænomeni cum aliqua stella fixa scripseram, ea iamdudum correxi, ac caput 7,9 et 1O plane mutavi. Illustravi etiam aliquot problemata DIGGSEI et alia adieci tum mea, tum a WITTICHIO accepta. Et certe hæc doctrina parallaxium mediocriter per nos illustrata est, de quo tamen tuum et aliorum intelligentium erit iudicium. Librarius solum nunc nos moratur, qui nostra illa imprimere vetat. Et ut admirandi illius novi sideris Historia ad posteros transmitteretur, non tantum nostra illa de parallaxibus adiungentur meæ Dialexi, sed omnium aliorum scripta nostræ sententiæ consentientia. Titulus libri erit talis, ut hic in charta descriptum vides. Vellem tuas quoque observationes Cometarum prodire, fuitque hæc causa, cur eas in meo proximi Cometæ scripto desideraverim. Nam quod æthereus fuerit uterque Cometa, id iam ante ex tuis ad me intellexeram, ideoque iam ante, ipso adhuc lucente Cometa, ad multos perscripseram. Proxima quadragesima fui Vratislaviæ apud meam filiam et commoratus sum illic ultra mensem. Mea de parallaxibus cum WITTICHIO contuli, qui mecum communicavit vestras observationes proximi Cometæ, quas habuistis diebus XI, XII, XIII et XVII Octob. Ex his ego experiri volui, in deductione calculi aberraverim, an momenta observationum non recte abs te aut a WITTICHIO consignata sint. Nam ex vestris illis datis ne verisimile quidem in parallaxibus omnibus illis diebus quicquam invenire potui. In altitudinibus Cometæ inter nos alias satis recte convenit. Relinquitur igitur, ut momenta temporum minus recte assignata fuerint. Et certi temporis consideratio hac in parte valde est difficilis. Subduc quæso tuum calculum et omnia accuratius considera meque admone. Ego ipse quoque, modo aliquid otij nactus fuero, denuo repetam calculum et vestra examinabo. Nam eorum in meo opere facere cupio mentionem, ut nostra sententia de Cometis in animis eruditorum eo magis confirmetur. Quod si post discessum isthinc WITTICHIJ aliquot dierum alias quoque habes illius Cometæ observationes, eas mecum communicato.

B. V.
Pragæ ex ædibus nostris die 1. Maij Anno 1582.
T. ex animo
THADDÆUS HAGECIUS
ab Hayck.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To Master Tycho Brahe the Dane, eminent in nobility of birth, in virtue, and in learning, and an outstanding adorner of the mathematical sciences, governor of the island of Hven in the Danish Hellespont, his lord and friend to be honoured — greeting.

I have received your letter, most noble Sir and friend to be honoured, and it was both most welcome and most longed-for. But when I gathered from it that mine had not been delivered to you, I was greatly distressed, and began to lament my unjust fortune, in that it had begrudged both of us the pleasure we were to have had — you in reading what I wrote, I in knowing that my letter had been delivered to you. Our friend Wittich, too, would have been freed from any sinister suspicion in your eyes of not having delivered your letter to me — he who assuredly not only delivered yours to me most faithfully at the first opportunity, by trustworthy men, but also strove by his own letters to persuade me to remove myself thither, and invited me to be willing to be a companion of his return to you. He added, by way of persuasion, that you were writing this to me from sure knowledge and at the King's wish, and that the King himself would have given letters of invitation to me, had it first been established for him through you concerning my settled willingness — which he now wished only to ascertain through your letter. And although these things had not been set out and unpacked so explicitly in your letter to me, nevertheless I took those persuasions to proceed from his most excellent disposition, most kindly inclined toward us both. Your own reasons too I admitted, as proceeding from the same disposition, and I interpreted all those things as became a true friend. Wherefore on this point you ought to be free of any sinister suspicion regarding our Wittich; for he discharged the office of a good man and a most faithful friend. That I did indeed answer your letter in February of last year, Master Chytraeus can be a witness. For I sent my letter to you by his hand, and asked him to see to its being carried on to you. He replied in April of last year that he had received my letter and would send it on to you at the first opportunity and convenience. It must therefore be that my letter went astray between Rostock and Copenhagen, if it was indeed given to someone to carry on. These things you yourself, if you so please, will be able to learn from Master Chytraeus himself. As regards the invitation, that I should remove myself thither — it is a hard and difficult question. And first I beg you, my Tycho, weigh it yourself more carefully and turn over all the considerations of how difficult these things are for me to do. I am of advancing years, for I am in my 56th year. I am the father of many children. To migrate, then, into foreign lands and as it were to choose a voluntary exile, where both the customs of the people and the language are strange — that is assuredly difficult, harsh, and full of misery. I add that it would be neither honourable for me and mine to migrate into foreign lands without an honourable and fitting summons. For what else can I think your people would say, or at least suspect, when they saw that I had come to you uninvited, than that I had either been cast out from here or driven by want to seek a dwelling abroad? And what would my own people say? — That [I am] fickle and inconstant and little prudent, in that I let myself be so easily moved by one man's friendly invitation, that I chose uncertainties for certainties, that I took words of honour for the most ample rewards. They will say too, without doubt, that [I am] rash and inconsiderate, and one who knew to take no account of his own station and reputation — that I, who was once the Emperor's physician, honourably and fittingly summoned; who five years ago was sought after by the King of Sweden, then by the Estates of Styria; for whom too, had I wished or were I to wish it, there was a place, and could yet be one, with the King of Poland; and finally I, who am a noble citizen of this Kingdom — that I should now, of my own accord and rashly, go out beyond my homeland unbidden to seek a dwelling and beg for service. And to make an end at last: to migrate to you, not honourably summoned, with no fixed conditions set before me, plainly does not seem advisable; it is not suited to my age, nor to my station, nor to my dignity. But to make an excursion to you — that I might behold with my own eyes those splendid Chymical and Mathematical studies of yours, and embrace you in person, and rest wholly in your love — that I should indeed greatly desire, were there not in this too difficulties, and grave ones: a long and difficult journey, no slight expense, and finally that excursion would seem too great an undertaking. But if some convenient opportunity of coming thither and returning should present itself, I would by no means wish to let it pass. Now too I am hindered by concern for my wife, who is near her delivery. You see, my Tycho, my just and honourable reasons, by which I am hindered and held back, so that I can satisfy neither your kindness — which I acknowledge to be the greatest and best toward me — nor my own longing. And so, in return for that most excellent and most kindly disposition of yours toward me, your care and solicitude for me, in that you wish me the best and unprompted offer me every office of love and goodwill, I give and hold the greatest thanks that words can express; and in turn I wish you to be most fully persuaded that I am so kindled with love of you that, were I permitted to be with you, I would deem the rest of my life most delightful, and that there is nothing I would not do for your sake.

These things, more or less, I had written in my earlier letter to you, which, since I understand it perished somewhere, it has been needful to repeat anew herewith. I come to the other part of your letter. I congratulate you heartily on that Mathematical leisure in which I understand you to be engaged. Go on and prosper in virtue, my Tycho, and in this course, as you have begun, press on to illustrate that Astronomical study. I have received the Solar Ephemeris of this year, constructed from your observations, and for it I give you the greatest thanks. I have no instrument of such size that it can take in the single minutes [of arc], nor in my house is there the convenience that, even if I had some such thing, I could set it up. Hitherto I have used my quadrant in my observations of the stars and comets. On it are reckoned tens and fives of minutes. I have a fairly large cross-staff (radius astronomicus), of which our Wittich has doubtless informed you. To the Sun, therefore, I have had no observations. I am pleased that for the Sun you take account of parallax, which up to now everyone has neglected. Our Wittich too judged that it should by no means be passed over. In the apogee of the Sun I see that you are about three degrees in advance of Copernicus. But a variation of the apogee, even of several degrees, does not notably change the motion of the Sun, on the establishing of whose apogee the greatest craftsmen have laboured. What you have written about the parallaxes of the comets was also most welcome and delightful. As to the parallax of the comet of [15]77, I already acknowledged my slip before this and corrected myself. For I was not so furnished with suitable instruments as I once was at Vienna in observing the new star. Many other conveniences too were lacking to me. And you rightly point out that, from my own writing, a far smaller parallax ought to have resulted — just as I made mention of this error in the Letter to Mylius and in the tract on the recent comet printed at Görlitz. That [the comet] culminated together with Aquila was not so written by me as though it had been observed by me (for that culmination occurred while the Sun stood above the horizon), but [I wrote it] because I reckoned that at least by some rougher line of inquiry, compared with other observations, some judgment about the parallax could in some way be made. But a certain inept fellow, and my plagiarist, wrote that Aquila culminated together with the comet on 6 December, at the 5th hour. As for the rest — what I once wrote in my Dialexis, chapter 9, on finding parallax from the culmination of a phenomenon together with some fixed star — that I corrected long ago, and I have wholly changed chapters 7, 9, and 10. I have also elucidated several of Digges's problems, and added others, both my own and ones received from Wittich. And indeed this doctrine of parallaxes has been moderately well elucidated by us — though on that the judgment will rest with you and with others who understand. The printer alone now delays us, who forbids those things of ours to be printed. And so that the History of that admirable new star may be transmitted to posterity, there will be joined to my Dialexis not only those writings of ours on parallaxes, but the writings of all others that agree with our view. The title of the book will be such as you here see written out on the [enclosed] sheet. I should wish your observations of the comets too to come out, and this was the reason why I missed them in my tract on the recent comet. For that each comet was ethereal, that I had already understood beforehand from your letters to me, and therefore had already, while the comet was still shining, written to many. This past Lent I was at Wrocław with my daughter, and I stayed there above a month. I compared my [findings] on parallaxes with Wittich, who shared with me your observations of the recent comet, which you took on the 11th, 12th, 13th, and 17th of October. From these I wished to test whether I had gone astray in working out the calculation, or whether the moments of the observations had not been correctly recorded by you or by Wittich. For from those data of yours I could not find anything even plausible in the parallaxes on all those days. In the altitudes of the comet we otherwise agree well enough between ourselves. It remains, therefore, that the moments of time were less correctly assigned. And the consideration of exact time is in this respect very difficult. Work out your calculation, I beg, and consider everything more carefully, and advise me. I too, as soon as I have got some leisure, will repeat the calculation anew and examine your [observations]. For I desire to make mention of them in my work, so that our view about comets may be the more confirmed in the minds of the learned. But if, after Wittich's departure from there, you have other observations of that comet for some days, share them with me.

Fare you well.
At Prague, from our house, the 1st day of May, the year 1582.
Yours from the heart,
Thaddaeus Hagecius
ab Hayck.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Thaddaeus Hagecius ab Hayck = Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku (1525–1600), physician, astronomer, and astrologer; personal physician to Emperor Maximilian II and then Rudolf II at the Prague court. He and Tycho had met in 1575 at Rudolf's coronation as King of the Romans; he was Tycho's chief Bohemian correspondent. It was Hagecius who in July 1580 sent Paul Wittich to Hven with a letter of recommendation. The letter answers an invitation (conveyed via Wittich, allegedly with royal — i.e. Imperial — backing) to settle near Tycho, which Hagecius here declines.
  • INSULAe HUAeNAe DANICI HELLESPONTI PRAeSIDI — "governor of the island of Hven in the Danish Hellespont": a learned flourish for Tycho as lord of Hven in the Øresund (cast as the "Danish Hellespont," the strait); praeses rendered "governor." The capitalised salutation reproduces the manuscript's display capitals; D. = Dominus ("Master/Lord,").
  • WITTICHIUS noster = Paul Wittich (c. 1546–1586), the Silesian mathematician from Wrocław who spent some months on Hven in 1580 and later carried Tychonic methods to Kassel. "Our Wittich" (noster) marks him as the friend common to both. The whole opening turns on a lost-letter misunderstanding: Tycho suspected Wittich of not delivering a letter; Hagecius exonerates him.
  • Regis … literas invitatorias — "the King('s) … letters of invitation." The "King" (Rex) is Rudolf II (Holy Roman Emperor, also styled King of Bohemia / of the Romans); the proposal was that Tycho settle, or be invited, under Imperial auspices. Rendered "King" literally, as the source has Rex (not Caesar / Imperator) here; that Hagecius styles himself Caesaris Medicus below ("the Emperor's physician") confirms the same sovereign is meant.
  • D. CHYTRAeUS = David Chytraeus (1530–1600), the Rostock theologian and professor, here the intended forwarding intermediary for Hagecius's earlier (lost) letter, which therefore "went astray between Rostock and Copenhagen" (inter Rostochium et Hafniam; Rostock → Copenhagen,).
  • Hagecius's refusal — career résumé. olim Caesaris Medicus = "once the Emperor's physician"; a Rege Sueciae … ab ordinibus Stiriae expetitus = sought "by the King of Sweden" (John III of Sweden) and "by the Estates of Styria"; apud Regem Poloniae = a place possible "with the King of Poland" (Stephen Báthory); huius Regni nobilis civis = "a noble citizen of this Kingdom" (Bohemia). He pleads age (ago annum 56 — "in my 56th year"), many children, and his wife "near her delivery" (partui vicina).
  • Astronomy — solar theory. Ephemeridem Solarem … ex tuis observationibus exstructam = "the Solar Ephemeris … constructed from your observations." rationem parallaxeos … in Sole = Tycho's reckoning of solar parallax "which up to now everyone has neglected." In Apogeo Solis … tribus circiter gradibus anteriorem … Copernico = Tycho's value for the apogee of the Sun ("apse,") is "about three degrees in advance of Copernicus." Glossary renderings (parallax, apogee, motion) used throughout.
  • Instruments. Quadrante … meo = "my quadrant," reading "tens and fives of minutes" (dena et quina … scrupula) — i.e. graduated only to 5′; scrupulum (arc-minute) rendered "minute [of arc]." Radium satis magnum = "a fairly large cross-staff (radius astronomicus),". singula scrupula capere = an instrument fine enough "to take in the single minutes."
  • The comet-parallax controversy (the technical core). Cometae anni 77 = the great comet of 1577. Hagecius concedes his earlier lapsus: his published parallax ( c. 5°, which would place the comet sub-lunar) was too large; he had already corrected himself, having lacked good instruments — "not so furnished … as once at Vienna in observing the new star" (novum sidus = the nova of 1572, the subject of his Dialexis). Epistola ad MYLIUM = his published Letter to Mylius; scripto … Goerlitij impresso = his comet tract printed at Görlitz (Goerlitium). culminaverit cum Aquila = the comet's culmination "together with [the constellation/star] Aquila" — Hagecius stresses this was a computed/inferred datum (the culmination fell while the Sun was up, so it was not directly observed), a "rougher line of inquiry" (crassiori … indagine). alius quidam ineptus et meus plagiarius = "a certain inept fellow, and my plagiarist," who misreported the culmination on 6 December at the 5th hour — left unnamed in the source and so here.
  • Dialexi … cap. 9 … caput 7,9 et 1O — his Dialexis (1574, on the 1572 nova); he has revised chapter 9 (and recast chapters 7, 9, 10) on deriving parallax from a phenomenon's culmination with a fixed star. 1O is printed with a capital-O for the zero/numeral; read "10." problemata DIGGSEI = problems of Thomas Digges (Diggseus, c. 1546–1595), the English mathematician, from his Alae seu scalae mathematicae (1573) on the 1572 nova.
  • Librarius … imprimere vetat — "the printer (librarius) … forbids those things of ours to be printed": publication of his enlarged collection (his Dialexis + others' parallax writings agreeing with his view, prefaced by a Historia of the new star) is being held up by the bookseller-printer. Titulus … ut hic in charta descriptum vides = the proposed title was written out on a sheet enclosed with the letter (not preserved in the text).
  • The 1582 comet collation. proximi Cometae = "the recent comet" (the comet of 1580, the most recent before this letter). aethereus … uterque Cometa = "each comet was ethereal" (super-lunary) — Hagecius had already accepted this from Tycho's letters. Proxima quadragesima … Vratislaviae = "this past Lent … at Wrocław" (Vratislavia), with his daughter. There Wittich shared Tycho's observations of the comet on 11, 12, 13, and 17 October. Collating them, Hagecius could find "nothing even plausible" in the parallaxes; since the altitudes agreed, he infers the recorded times (momenta temporum) were imprecisely assigned — "the consideration of exact time is in this respect very difficult." He asks Tycho to recompute and to send any further observations made after Wittich left Hven.
  • Subscription. B. V. = Bene vale, "Fare you well". T. ex animo = "Yours from the heart" (T. = Tuus). ab Hayck = the family name z Hájku, kept as written.
  • Calendar / place. Pragae … die 1. Maij Anno 1582 = "at Prague … the 1st day of May 1582." Denmark and Bohemia were both still on the Julian calendar in May 1582 (the Gregorian reform took effect only that October), so the date is O.S. with no Julian–Gregorian gap at issue. The header date matches the dateline; the edition label ("1582. 1. maj. Prag") agrees — no discrepancy.

Collated the full Latin the transcribed original against the printed text sentence by sentence: salutation heading, every proper name (Wittichius/Wittich, Chytraeus, Mylius, Digges/Diggsei, Vienna, Görlitz/Goerlitium, Wrocław/Vratislavia, Rostock, Copenhagen/Hafnia, Sweden/Suecia, Styria/Stiria, Poland/Polonia, Aquila), every number and date (age 56, five years/quinquennium, three degrees apogee, comet of [15]77, 6 December hour 5, Dialexis chapters 7/9/10, the four October observation dates XI/XII/XIII/XVII, 1 May 1582 dateline), and the opening and closing lines all match Dreyer exactly (allowing for long-s/ligature typography only). Then checked the published English against the Latin clause by clause: no invented facts, no dropped sentences, no flipped pronouns/antecedents, no hardened hedges, all bracketed English supplements are clearly marked. One trivial artifact noted but not counted as a defect: our stored the transcribed original renders 'caput 7,9 et 1O' with a capital letter O in place of the digit 0 (Dreyer prints '10'); this is a typographic glyph slip with no semantic effect and the English translation correctly reads 'chapters 7, 9, and 10', so it does not rise to a reportable text error.
