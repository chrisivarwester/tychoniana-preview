The letter of Your Highness, Most Illustrious Prince, written to me about the spring of the year last past — in which you deigned to reply to mine — I received through the same servant of my household whom I had despatched to Your Highness; and for that clement benevolence of Your Highness toward me I humbly give thanks. For at two seasons of the year only is the more convenient occasion of writing hence toward Kassel granted — namely when our countrymen set out for the two Frankfurt fairs. Wherefore, since I had received Your Highness's letter a little after the last spring fairs, necessity compelled me to defer my answer to the autumn fairs that next followed. But when, about the time at which the merchants from here were minded to betake themselves thither for the past fairs held at Frankfurt this last autumn, I had busily arranged with a certain bookseller that a little before his departure he should meet me on this island and carry off with him the letter to be delivered to Your Highness's secretary at Kassel — which indeed he had promised, and had fully resolved to perform — it happened beyond expectation that, in those very days on which he was to come hither, the Illustrious Prince Ulrich, Duke of Mecklenburg, together with the Most Illustrious Princess the Lady Elizabeth his consort (who afterward, on the very return journey toward Mecklenburg, before she had crossed the sea, died to the great grief of all), and with his brother's son Duke Sigismund — our Most Serene Queen herself escorting them, together with a great retinue of noblemen — deigned to visit me here at Uraniborg: partly to behold at first hand the astronomical engines with which they knew this house to be filled, partly that the renowned Duke Ulrich might inspect more closely my pyronomic labours (on which I expend no less effort than on the astronomical) and the mechanical apparatus that serves them. And if this is so (as I am wholly persuaded), it will follow that the elevation of the pole comes out smaller by one scruple, and that of the equator greater by as much — so that it is in truth at Kassel 38 degrees 41 minutes. If, then, the altitudes transmitted to me by Your Highness be applied to this, so that from each a single scruple be taken away on account of the plumb-line's error, they will agree quite exactly with my declinations of the stars. Wherefore I rejoiced greatly that, by this single scruple corrected in the fault of the plumb-line, another scruple far greater — clinging to my mind on account of this difference (though not so great a one) that intervened between our observations — has been taken away. For I judge that all celestial observations are to be instituted to an exact rule, so far as they fall within the human senses — so much so that if anything be wanting in them even by the least amount, I count it for null. But that Your Highness may institute a further comparison between your observations and mine, both as regards the intervals and as regards the meridian altitudes, or the declinations derived from them, I send Your Highness the distances of some stars — both others, and especially the more notable ones that lie along the zodiac — faithfully explored with my trigonic sextants (for so it has pleased to call them); and besides, certain meridian altitudes investigated with diverse quadrants, agreeing nevertheless among themselves — and that both about the end of the year [15]84 and the running [15]85, and also about the close of the lately-past [15]86 and the very beginning of this current [15]87 — that Your Highness may the more readily compare your altitudes with mine at the due times. Since indeed I in no way doubt that in these very years, after the instruments were again renewed, Your Highness has noted a more exact measurement of the altitudes of the stars. For Your Highness very well knows that the declinations of the stars, and on that account their altitudes too, vary somewhat from year to year — and that especially in those stars which are nearer to the equinoctial places. But the elevation of the equator to be employed in our altitudes of the stars comes to 34 degrees 5⅓ [minutes], because by frequent experiments I have found that the pole here at Uraniborg, in the place of my observation — which is the middle of the island of Hven, situated in that famous Danish Sound — is raised by 55 degrees 54⅔ scruples; which I have many times verified, both from the pole-star itself and from the other circumpolar non-setting stars, and from the stars that, by reason of a slope not too perceptible, exclude refraction. The longitudes and latitudes of the stars communicated to me by Your Highness I likewise compared, both among themselves and with mine, and found that that assignment, made mechanically from a globe, though not greatly disagreeing, yet does not everywhere correspond to the distances and altitudes scrupulously noted — though in some a very slight difference is found, as Your Highness can perceive from several comparisons which I have noted in the appended sheet. Nor indeed does it seem to me so very wonderful that longitudes and latitudes sought out mechanically on a globe from the distances and altitudes of the stars should not agree to the exact reckoning in the very scruples. Since I too have a great brass globe, perfectly round, which contains six feet in diameter, on which the ecliptic and the equator are finely divided into single scruples by transversal points, after the manner usual with us in the other instruments; nevertheless I scarcely promise myself everywhere a fixed certainty within one or one-and-a-half scruples in the places of the stars to be investigated by this globe from sure observations — so much do the things that are worked out mechanically, albeit with the greatest diligence, come to be set after geometrical and arithmetical exactitude. But in the very longitudes of the stars transmitted to me by Your Highness — although they be weighed by a more exact balance, geometrically and through the numbers of the sines — I observe that for the most part they are reckoned five or six scruples, or thereabouts, further from the equinox than [in] our observations. In the latitudes, however, there is found hardly a greater difference than what could be caused either from the longitudes assumed a little otherwise, or from a different determination of the obliquity of the ecliptic, but chiefly from the declinations, which differ from ours by two minutes through the fault of your plumb-line. To be sure, this difference, even of the longitudes, much less of the latitudes, is of no very great moment, if we compare the notations of certain ancients and moderns. And I cannot sufficiently wonder that Your Highness has come so close with your instruments to the places of the stars — especially since I can gather that Your Highness, in Aldebaran, or whatever other [star] you used for a foundation, detected its right ascension from its transit across the meridian, the time found by a clock and compared with the Sun. Which method always seemed to me less safe. For although I too have four such clocks of diverse size and exact structure, which indicate not only the single first scruples [minutes] but also the seconds, yet in so subtle a business I have judged it vain to place trust in them, since 4 seconds at least — which in many hours easily slip away — bring in an error of one first scruple in longitude. Add that those clocks, even though most diligently wrought from little brass wheels, are sensibly liable to the change of the air. I say nothing of the weight not being equal when it hangs higher or lower, by reason of the altered length of the cord by which it is attached, which little by little adds something to the weight — which, though very slight, yet brings in, in the seconds, a deviation not to be neglected. Wherefore, since I had perceived that this business does not succeed safely enough by this method, and was not ignorant that no slight difficulty underlies it — that even the true distance of one fixed star from the equinox might be investigated, which once known, the rest [might] either by their distances from one another, or by the equatorial differences, or even by the Hipparchan zodiacal armillae which we have in use (worked out, however, by a surer compendium than by the ancients), the rest (I say), as many as one pleased, might easily be established — and since I judged that the method which the ancients, Hipparchus above all and Ptolemy, used, and in a later age Nicolaus Copernicus, by the Moon (the partner of day and night, applied by instruments both to the Sun and to the stars), is not without its difficulties and is less exempt from error: for although we grant that the place of the Sun, with exquisite diligence applied, is, as to longitude, knowable within one or another scruple, and its interval to the Moon, and the Moon's to the stars, can be given sufficiently exactly — yet the lunar parallaxes, which are not so precisely known, in either observation bring in some variation from the truth. Add that this manner of observing is not conveniently done unless the Sun be low about the horizon, where its apparent place is other than its true place which appeared at the meridian — and that not merely by reason of parallax, but much more on account of refraction, of which I more fully indicated my opinion in my earlier letter to Your Highness; and I understand from Your Highness's reply that you too have detected, both in the Sun and in the other planets and fixed stars, a sensible refraction in the lower situation — on which account I much congratulate you over our mutual observations on both sides. For neither the ancients nor the more recent men, so far as I know, have noted this in the sightings of the stars, or duly guarded against it. This way, I say, having been laid out by our predecessors — since it was not permitted to walk safely enough along it for the aforesaid and several other reasons — I judged that I must enter upon a more convenient and safer path, which should lead to the exact longitudes of the stars; and I found none fitter and more exact than that which, from the opportunity offered us, was presented in the year 1582, when we beheld the star of Venus, being the Evening Star, clearly appearing by day. For then we observed Venus at her exact distance from the Sun, from 24 February right up to the middle of April, on more occasions than can easily be believed, in the daytime most diligently — and that not only in the Sun's lower situation, but many times when it was either at the meridian, or at least removed beyond it by one, or one-and-a-half, hours, and ♀ Venus stood very high along with it — so much so that her very transit across the meridian, with the Sun still much elevated, was many times detected by us, both as to altitude and as to distance from the Sun. Wherefore, since the place of the Sun was established sufficiently exactly both from the meridian altitudes and through the restoration of its motion made by us to the rule from the preceding years, then from the declination of Venus and of the Sun — the exquisite and mutually-agreeing distance of both being given through the sextant (so that the Sun illuminated one sight with its whole magnitude, while through the other Venus was seen on both sides through the little slits parallel to the upper cylinder) — the place of the star Venus was surely disclosed. Which place, at night-time, as soon as some one of the fixed stars near the zodiac was offered to view, applied by like reasoning through the interval taken of it from this [Venus] — account being first had of the proper motion meanwhile transacted — disclosed the place of that same star; and this was often done in diverse stars as well, since the Sun, Venus, and the fixed stars observed were almost everywhere at so great an altitude above the horizon that they excluded all refraction. And if there was sometimes any very slight one, it was guarded against from other observations made about this matter. By this infallible method, then, I attained most happily, as I wished, to an exact knowledge of the longitude of the stars at that time — manifold experiment recurring to the same result, and the distances of those same stars from one another confirming the same. I also everywhere employed the parallaxes both of the Sun and of Venus, in so far as they were varied through the intervals of the hours, that the reckoning might stand the more exact. Nay, I did not hesitate that same year to investigate the parallaxes of Venus herself, that it might more surely be clear whether she revolved about the Sun, or below it. Which thing I also dared to try in the star of Mars at the end of the previous [year] and about the beginnings of the same year, when it was seen acronychal and high in ♋ Cancer, a northern latitude too lifting it higher — that I might search out whether it produced a greater diversity of aspect [parallax] than the Sun; which would be necessary, if the Copernican hypothesis consisted in the truth of the matter. For by it Mars in opposition (☍) is rendered nearer to the Earth by almost a third part than the Sun himself. Whence too it then necessarily induces a greater diversity of parallax; and consequently the orbs of the revolutions of Mars are by no means constituted wholly above the solar orb, as the Ptolemaic hypotheses hold. And therefore either the Earth must be turned by an annual motion, by which all the epicycles of the planets are excused [done away with]; or another disposition of the celestial revolutions, than has been practised hitherto, must be sought out. But these things, now said outside my purpose, will another time, God willing, be set forth more clearly and copiously. Since therefore (to return to the matter) by the aforesaid, in no way doubtful, reckoning I had come, from the Sun through Venus in the year indicated, to a knowledge of the longitude of several of the more notable fixed stars about the zodiac (their latitude easily thrusting itself in along with it); and since from certain other observations, taken in the year [15]85 about the autumnal equinox — at which time the star of Venus, being then the Morning Star, was likewise seen by day, and that at almost a like distance from the Sun and the Earth, and likewise at a like altitude above the horizon as in the year [15]82 (which greatly furthered the certainty of the business) — I had proved all things over again the more exactly in reverse; hence I have worked to restore, as to length and breadth, the places of the remaining fixed stars too, both within and without the zodiac, and at the same time to investigate through these the appearances of all the planets, no opportunity of clear weather being hitherto let slip. Now I find in very many stars the very thing which Your Highness writes me — that they have not only longitudes other than the ancients' traditions exhibit (even with the more correct precession of the equinox added), but also latitudes much otherwise, in some less, in some indeed more — whereas it is held for ascertained, beyond all doubt, that these necessarily remain unaltered in every age from the most ancient astronomers, because the motion of the eighth sphere takes place about the poles of the ecliptic. Whence Your Highness, rightly led into wonder at that variation, clemently asks also my judgement on this matter — namely whether I deem it consonant that there is in the several fixed stars some peculiar motion of their own, by which they change their latitude too in diverse ways. On which matter I shall now set forth my opinion to Your Highness in brief. The places of all the fixed stars, from the first origin of the world, in each and all, as regards one another, I judge to remain wholly unaltered, far beyond all doubt. And this is easily proved from this: that all the fixed stars which Ptolemy enumerates in the first chapter of the seventh book — [stated] by Abrachis or Hipparchus, some 260 years before him, to have stood in one straight line — these same [stars] also in his own age remained in the same situation as regards one another. And since these same, and also those which Ptolemy afterward adds (that he might leave to posterity a handle for experimenting), are observed even in this age — 1400 years having elapsed since him — to retain exactly the very same position which the same Ptolemy enumerates (which could by no means be the case if one star changed its longitude or latitude, with respect to its own sphere, otherwise than another), it will be easy to reason that all the fixed stars subsist in the same situation, as regards themselves, in every age, without any diverse and disorderly change of latitude or longitude. But as to the fact that certain fixed stars, even among the more notable, are found which obtain a latitude much other than Hipparchus and Ptolemy noted — it is likely that this was committed partly through their less-exact observation, partly through the negligence of so many copyists who perhaps were ignorant of the subtlety of the business. For the printing art was scarcely devised before 150 years ago, and previously, over so great an interval of time, by copying so often, many things could be assigned faultily, especially in the characters of numbers — since even in this very printing art, unless diligent correction be applied, an easy slip is admitted, especially in this matter. There is added this too, that by certain proofs it is confirmed from the heavens, in very many fixed stars (especially those about the zodiac), through that change of the inclination of the ecliptic to the equator which has been made from the times of Timocharis, Hipparchus, and Ptolemy down to now, that the latitudes of all the fixed stars have likewise been successively varied, in proportion as their longitudes draw nearer or further from the tropical places. Which indeed seems wonderful, since it is otherwise surely established that the motion of the eighth sphere takes place about the poles of the ecliptic, [and] that the latitudes of the stars are not diminished otherwise than by this minute difference of the obliquity of the Zodiac-bearer [the signifer/ecliptic]. But since I understand from the letters of Your Highness's mathematician Rothmann that he is engaged in the same opinion with me in this part, I have written to him more fully about this business, and have confirmed [him] in the opinion conceived, through more certain observations of very many stars, the intervening doubts being removed. Hence Your Highness will understand some occasion of variation in the latitudes of certain stars. But if any greater one coincides, this must needs come about, as I said before, either from the ancients' less-certain notation, or from the copyists' slip. For the rest, that Your Highness may more clearly know in what situation the places of the fixed stars have been investigated by me, in the aforesaid manner, I have described the longitudes and latitudes of certain principal ones, especially those along the zodiac, which I have reduced to the beginning of this passing year, just as these have been detected by our more exact observations, that Your Highness may the more readily compare those places with your own. I have not judged it worth the trouble to add the assignment of more stars, since even from fewer — nay, from a single one rightly determined — all the rest easily become known to anyone who has convenient and sure instruments to hand. But the interval of motion from the year [15]82 (in which I obtained, as I indicated above, the fittest observations in these matters) I have borrowed neither from the Copernican change of the equinoxes (which he derived from Spica of ♍ Virgo observed, into the first star of ♈ Aries, retaining the Ptolemaic interval of longitude and without consulting the heaven), nor from the Alphonsine reckoning, but I have completed this business from the very observation of Copernicus made on the said Spica of ♍ Virgo in the year 1525, and compared with our more recent one. For since Copernicus asserts that in that year the meridian altitude of Spica was 27 degrees 0 minutes, and the pole of Frauenburg is elevated 54 degrees 22¼ minutes — as, three years ago, I had a trial made by my own exquisite instrument, through a certain student sent thither by me with the instrument [organum] — the declination of Spica was then 8 degrees 37¾ minutes; and therefore, her latitude being assumed at 1 degree 59½ minutes, such as we found (smaller only by half a scruple than Copernicus's position, which alters this business not at all, since I presuppose the same in both cases), the longitude of Spica for that time comes out at 17 degrees 14 minutes of ♎ Libra — although Copernicus himself sets it 7 scruples further on, and that doubtless by the fault of an elevation of the pole less exquisitely known, which it is credible that he investigated through the Sun, from the greatest and least altitude, by a reckoning fallacious on account of refraction in the lower situation. And since at this time Spica declines, by our observations, 8 degrees 57 minutes precisely, and, the former latitude being assumed, her longitude falls at 18 degrees 4 minutes of ♎ Libra, the eighth sphere has, from Copernicus's observation down to now, over an interval of 61 years, advanced 50 minutes. Wherefore in 73 years (provided not fewer) it accomplishes one degree in our age — whereas, nevertheless, the Copernican anticipation of the equinoxes now demands an almost slowest advance of the fixed stars, such that they accomplish a single degree in nearly a hundred years. Hence, then, it appears how much one is to adhere to the Copernican findings about the precession of the equinoxes, whereby the motion of the eighth sphere is excused (to say nothing now of the Sun and the rest) — however subtly devised by that incomparable astronomer. But the exact knowledge of the apparent motion of the eighth sphere I judge scarcely thoroughly searchable by any mortal absolutely. Since [the sphere] has as yet completed no whole revolution, and the little portions of the circuit handed down to us from Timocharis through Hipparchus, Ptolemy, Albategnius [al-Battānī], and Copernicus do not suffice — nor, if they did furnish it, do they stand scrupulously enough [consistent]. Since therefore by experience, in the aforesaid manner, the anticipation of the equinoxes about these times has become known, it was easy to transfer the proportional motion from the year [15]82 into this or any other [years] of our age. For it is not consonant that any sensible inequality in this part should occur in the lifetime of one man. But that these places of the stars still agree, my observations, made in the past year and in this very [year] from the beginning of January, sufficiently declare. As to the fact that in Your Highness's longitudes of the stars — [computed] geometrically from the intervals and declinations reduced to numbers — for the most part five or six scruples, or thereabouts, are in excess, this will easily be excused, if it be established that Your Highness then, in delimiting these, used a place of the Sun [computed] when the Sun was about the vernal equinox, in the ascending part of the ecliptic. For there, by reason of that single scruple which was caused by the plumb-line's fault, the declination of the Sun was rendered higher by two scruples than it ought to have been, and therefore its place [was] further on by five [scruples] in longitude. Which necessarily redounded also onto the fixed stars adapted to the Sun's place — although about the autumnal equinox, in the descending part of the ecliptic, this matter holds in the contrary way. But that Your Highness may see some specimen of our observations in the other wandering stars too, I add the true places of all, such as have been detected by my instruments by accurate remark within a single revolution of the primum mobile, three times in this very month of January from the beginning of the new year. Which, though it otherwise rather rarely happens, has nevertheless been done by me several times in earlier years, especially in the preceding year in October, when ☿ Mercury appeared in the east for many days. The Sun's motion, sought out from the situation of Venus through the fixed stars — since at this time of year it can less surely be had through the declinations, refraction also hindering — I have verified. For in the daytime Venus is, even before noon in these days, or at noon itself and before, seen clearly enough through the purer serenity [of the sky]. But the impediments of refraction and of the parallax of the Sun and of Venus, explored beforehand at a like altitude, I have removed. Likewise I did in Mercury too, since it was observed very low, scarcely 5 degrees high. In the rest there was no need of this delimitation, because they were taken higher up from the heavens. But I have added, for the same times of our observation, the Alphonsine and Copernican places of the planets, that Your Highness might at a single glance the more easily perceive the discrepancy of each calculation from the heaven itself — although they often go astray much more than now. But I ask Your Highness, with what prayers I can, that you be willing to communicate these our observations to no one, and to take care that they do not become public — especially as regards the longitudes and latitudes of the fixed stars, which on that account too I had noted separately, on a sheet of their own. For Your Highness knows full well how much time, labour, diligence, and expense is required before one may attain an exact knowledge of this matter. And indeed to no other in all Germany, or elsewhere, of whatever condition he might be, would I communicate these my findings; and I have suppressed them hitherto, although solicited by many on this score. For most men, since they will themselves perform nothing excellent in these matters, do not blush to adorn themselves with the findings and labours of others, and to vend them as their own, not without grave injury to the author. But I doubt not that Your Highness will reserve these for yourself alone — just as I too make no one a sharer of the observations communicated to me by Your Highness. But in all this astronomical treatment there would be much more, on which I would most gladly treat more copiously with Your Highness — but neither the paper nor the convenience now permits it. I have besides accurate and manifold observations of many years, not only in the fixed stars, but also in all the wandering ones, as often as they shone visible to us, which I preserve in the place of a glorious possession (for they have been procured by no few vigils, and by no small labour and diligence). And I am working to compare these with the findings of others, and at length to bring them forth for the public use, that the whole science of the stars may exactly satisfy the celestial appearances. And I have given it in charge to this man, that he should set forth to Your Highness, or to your mathematician, in my name, more things than it was convenient now to write. These things I have now written to Your Highness the more at length, partly because I know that Your Highness takes intense delight in these astronomical matters, partly that I might recompense the delay hitherto admitted in replying by the very copiousness in writing back. But lest I seem to exceed measure in both, and burden Your Highness with too great prolixity, I shall now make an end, and shall indicate the rest — if anything here be wanting — to Your Highness's mathematician by letter. This one thing at least, in the place of a colophon, I humbly and earnestly beseech of Your Highness: that with all your sinews you not cease to strive, more and more hereafter, to vindicate this divine science of the stars from the faults with which it teems, and to render it more cultivated — which indeed you have hitherto, of your own accord, done excellently. For in this way Your Highness will preserve, among all posterity that shall venerate so sublime and divine a science, a most welcome and most lasting memory of yourself — which will be the more perpetual and excellent because it is procured not from earthly things, liable to perishing, which the common crowd looks up to. Your Highness knows that this study is not for men of slenderer fortune, nor can it be cultivated by them in the due manner, even if they should most greatly desire it and understand it. And since there is none besides Your Highness, among all the princely men of Europe, whom God has adorned with this heroic genius, so as to understand so arduous a science more deeply, and to know how to vindicate it from errors and to restore it by every means — it surely becomes Your Highness, before other princely men, to bear an especial care of this science (most worthy of a princely and of a prince's knowledge), and to strive with all effort at its restoration and its preservation to posterity. On this account too let Your Highness hold your mathematician Christoph Rothmann commended before others, because I recognise by many tokens that he bestows in these exercises a most skilful and faithful labour, and is a most eager investigator of the exact truth. But I suggest these things to Your Highness superfluously, since of your own accord you are of a mind most inclined to the enlarging of astronomical matters. In which purpose may God, the author of this admirable and most beautiful celestial machine, confirm Your Highness as long as possible, with a happy issue of all the goods of mind and body. May Your Highness fare well, and not disdain to confer further with me about this very ethereal and sublime business, and to revisit me with your letters. Given at Uraniborg, the 18th day of January, in the year [15]87. Most devoted to Your Highness, Tycho Brahe.
