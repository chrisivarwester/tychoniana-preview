The original

TYCHO BRAHE AD IOSEPHUM SCALIGERUM.

S. P.

DIU multumque in votis habui, Illustris Domine IOSEPHE SCALIGER, cum adhuc in Dania Patria mea morarer, ut tecum per litteras amicitiam contraherem, præsertim cum a plerisque isthinc ad nos venientibus tuum in me, licet de facie tibi ignotum, candorem et benevolentiam aliquoties perceperim, sed semper aliquid intervenit obstaculi, quominus id efficere commodum fuerit. Nunc autem, cum hunc Nobilem et doctum adolescentem FRANCISCUM TEGNAGLIUM mihi domesticum et meæ e Dania migrationis comitem in Bataviam ablegarem, non intermittendum duxi, quin aliquid saltem ad te litterarum darem, utut iam essem occupatissimus. Ad quod me etiam non minimum invitavit excellentissimum et laboriosissimum illud opus tuum redintegratum de Temporum Emendatione, quod his ipsis diebus primum accepi, atque quantum per negotia licuit, perlustravi. Nec possum satis mirari exactam tuam in eo concinnando diligentiam, pari iudicio atque eruditione coniunctam. Debet tibi sane vel pro hoc solo labore (ut cæteros illos præclarissimos a te exantlatos præteream) omnis posteritas immensas gratias, quod temporum epochas et rationes tam sedulo et subtiliter extricare volueris. Nec curandum si qui (uti fit) inveniantur ingrati Zoili, quales et hactenus nonnullos habuisti, qui e contradictionibus famam venari satagunt, sed ex alto despiciendum. Memini, me cum ad quendam eorum mihi Heidelberga scribentem litteras responsi loco darem, de te ipsum (siquidem is occasionem præbuerat) discrete admonuisse, ut te tuaque suspiceret potius quam vellicaret, atque a talibus contradictionibus abstineret, vel ad minimum ut modestius et maiori verecundia rem ageret. Quid hoc pacto apud ipsum profecerim, ignoro. Nam postea inter nos altum silentium.

Reperi in hoc novo tuo volumine, dum illud evolverem, te alicubi circa finem facere mentionem mearum observationum, quas circa utrumque Æquinoctium in Dania habui, easque cum Hipparcheis conferre, ut anni Alphonsini a te nunc assumpti magnitudinem probares. Ago autem tibi officiose gratias, quod tam honorifice et candide mei et mearum inventionum isthic memineris, et cuperem, ut hæc ipsa uberius adhuc expenderes, conferendo nimirum HIPPARCHI omnes observationes, sed prius limitatas, ut sibi invicem, quoad fieri poterit, correspondeant, aut seligendo eas quæ exactiores videbuntur; hac tamen adhibita cautione, ut is annus Iulianus retro numeratus eandem cum nostro, qui adhibetur, habeat respectu bissexti rationem, ita ut vel ambo sint bissextiles vel pariter hinc removeantur. Ideoque nostra Tabella utriusque Æquinoctij, quam e primo capite Progymnasmatum Astronomicorum assumpsisti, eos annos continue exhibet, qui ab uno in alterum bissextilem includuntur. Pluribus enim opus non erat, licet multos præterea in promptu habeam pariter sibi invicem correspondentes. Optandum sane foret, ut veteres parem in his præstitissent accurationem, quod tamen ab ijs factum non esse, propriæ eorum annotationes, ut tu quoque non iniuria arguis, produnt; utique magis indubiam Anni Cœlestis rationem, quoad minima momenta, nunc obtineremus, præsertim si a pluribus Artificibus per aliquot interlapsa secula res nunquam hians aut claudicans comprobata esset. Quin et utrobique medium Æquinoctium capiendum, ut intricatio ea, quæ ex Apogæi et Eccentricitatis mutatione provenit, præcaveatur. Conducit etiam ALBATEGNIJ Aratensis intermedias Observationes in consilium adhibere. Quibus omnibus rite expensis et adhibita in singulis cautela, quod parallaxin ☉ inter observandum Æquinoctia forte neglexerint, atque his etiam Æquatoris inclinationem non satis scrupulose applicârint, dummodo ex altitudine ☉ meridiana in utroque solstitio Poli sublimitatem rimati sunt, existimârim de Alphonsina Anni quantitate nonnihil adhuc decessurum, ita ut vix 49 minuta ultra integras horas explere possit, nedum ea in 6 H. excederet. Si itaque omnes quas habes observationes Hipparcheas tam in æquinoctio vernali quam autumnali in Tabella discrete expositas (annotando ubique qualis iste annus fuerit respectu bissexti Iuliani) mecum communicaveris, dabo operam, ut hæc cum nostris ulterius collata exactum quid non tantum anno Civili, sed et Astronomico præfiniendo suppeditent. Et si plures in promptu tibi sunt vetustissimæ Æquinoctiorum Observationes, diligentiam aliquam redolentes, eas etiam pari modo impertiaris, quæso. Tum quoque de Epocha antiquissima illa Iudaica, quando Æquinoctium die 21. Aprilis sex horis a Meridie extitisse creditur, si quid indubitati habes, unde id probari queat, et ad quem meridianum referendum sit, atque quomodo hic annus ad bissextum sese habuerit, quantumque ab hisce temporibus citra omnem ambiguitatem distiterit, expone et plenius de his omnibus, quæ a te collecta, mecum per litteras agere non degravare. Erit id mihi longe gratissimum. Quicquid vicissim mea opella præstari queat, quod tibi acceptum sit, habebis me semper ex voto. Fieri autem potest, ut nonnulla horum in hoc libro tuo elucidaveris, quæ adhuc explorata non habeo. Nam mihi accurate eum relegere plurimæ occupationes nondum permiserunt. Porro referet dictus adolescens quædam ad te meo nomine secreto, de quibus tuum iudicium et consilium cognoscere averem. Is etiam plura, cum mearum rerum sit conscius, de ijs aperiet. Quare ut ipsum benigne audias, tibique commendatum habeas, etiam atque etiam rogo.

Vale vir tam doctrina quam genere revera illustris.
Dabantur subito Wandesburgo ex Arce Ranzoviana, quæ est prope Hamburgum, die 14. Martij Anni 1598.
Illustris tuæ Magnis. studiosiss.
TYCHO BRAHE,
manu propria.

Tycho Brahe to Joseph Scaliger.

Heartiest greeting.

Long and earnestly, Illustrious Master Joseph Scaliger, did I wish — while I still abode in Denmark, my homeland — to enter into friendship with you by letter, especially since from very many who came to us from those parts I had several times perceived your goodwill and kindly favour towards me, unknown to you though I was by face; but always some obstacle intervened, so that there was no convenient opportunity to bring it about. Now, however, since I was dispatching this noble and learned young man Frans Tengnagel — a member of my household and the companion of my removal from Denmark — into Holland, I thought I ought not to let the chance slip without at least sending you some lines, occupied as I now am to the utmost. To this I was no little invited, too, by that most excellent and most laborious work of yours, now reissued, On the Emendation of Times [de Temporum Emendatione], which I received for the first time these very days, and have, so far as my affairs allowed, looked through. Nor can I sufficiently marvel at your exact diligence in composing it, joined with equal judgement and erudition. For this labour alone (to pass over those other most distinguished ones you have toiled through) all posterity surely owes you immense thanks, in that you were willing to disentangle the epochs and reckonings of times so painstakingly and so subtly. Nor need one be troubled if some ungrateful Zoili are found (as happens), such as you have had not a few hitherto, who busy themselves hunting fame out of contradictions; rather, they are to be looked down upon from on high. I recall that, when I was sending a letter by way of reply to a certain one of them who was writing to me from Heidelberg, I discreetly admonished him about you yourself (since indeed he had given the occasion) — that he should look up to you and your works rather than carp at them, and abstain from such contradictions, or at the very least conduct the matter with more modesty and greater deference. What I accomplished with him in this way, I do not know. For afterwards there was deep silence between us.

I found, in this new volume of yours, as I was unrolling it, that somewhere near the end you make mention of my observations, which I made about both equinoxes in Denmark, and that you compare them with those of Hipparchus, in order to test the magnitude of the Alphonsine year now assumed by you. I render you dutiful thanks that you remember me and my discoveries there so honourably and candidly; and I could wish that you would weigh these very matters yet more fully — by comparing, namely, all the observations of Hipparchus, but first delimited, so that they may correspond to one another so far as can be, or by selecting those which shall seem the more exact; with this caution, however, applied: that the Julian year, reckoned backward, should have the same relation, with respect to the bissextile [leap day], as ours which is in use — so that either both are bissextile or both are alike removed from it [the leap day]. And therefore our Table of both equinoxes, which you took from the first chapter of the Astronomical Exercises [Progymnasmata Astronomica], exhibits continuously those years which are comprised from one bissextile to the next. For more were not needed, though I have besides many ready to hand likewise corresponding to one another. It were assuredly to be wished that the ancients had shown a like accuracy in these matters; that they did not, however, their own annotations betray, as you too not without justice argue — assuredly we should now obtain a more indubitable reckoning of the celestial year, down to the least moments, especially if, by several practitioners through several intervening centuries, the matter had been verified never gaping nor limping. Moreover, on both sides the mean equinox is to be taken, so that the entanglement which arises from the change of the apogee and of the eccentricity may be guarded against. It is profitable also to bring al-Battānī of al-Raqqa's [Albategnius Aratensis'] intermediate observations into the deliberation. All these things having been duly weighed, and caution applied in each — [namely] that they perhaps neglected the parallax of the Sun [☉] while observing the equinoxes, and that to these too they did not apply the inclination of the equator scrupulously enough, provided they searched out the elevation of the pole from the meridian altitude of the Sun [☉] at each solstice — I should reckon that something will yet have to be deducted from the Alphonsine quantity of the year, so that it can scarcely fill out 49 minutes beyond the whole hours, far less exceed those by 6 hours. If, therefore, you communicate to me all the observations of Hipparchus that you have, both at the vernal and the autumnal equinox, set out distinctly in a Table (noting everywhere what sort of year that one was with respect to the Julian bissextile), I shall take pains that these, further collated with ours, may furnish something exact for fixing in advance not only the Civil but also the Astronomical year. And if you have ready to hand several most ancient observations of the equinoxes that savour of some diligence, impart those too in like manner, I beg. Then also, concerning that most ancient Jewish epoch, when the equinox is believed to have occurred on the 21st day of April, six hours from the meridian [i.e. past noon] — if you have anything indubitable whereby it can be proved, and to which meridian it is to be referred, and how this year stood with respect to the bissextile, and how far it was distant from these times without any ambiguity — set it forth; and do not think it a burden to treat with me by letter more fully of all these things gathered by you. That will be by far most welcome to me. Whatever in return my poor effort can render that may be acceptable to you, you shall always have me at your wish. It may be, however, that you have elucidated some of these matters in this book of yours which I have not yet investigated. For my very many occupations have not yet allowed me to read it through carefully. Further, the said young man will report certain things to you in my name in confidence, on which I should be eager to learn your judgement and counsel. He too will disclose more about them, since he is privy to my affairs. Wherefore I ask again and again that you give him kindly hearing and hold him commended to you.

Farewell, a man truly illustrious as much by learning as by birth.
Given in haste at Wandsbek, from the Rantzau Castle, which is near Hamburg, on the 14th day of March of the year 1598.
Most devoted to Your Illustrious Magnificence,
TYCHO BRAHE,
in his own hand.

Apparatus

  • Sender. Tycho Brahe, writing from exile: having left Denmark in 1597, he had wintered (autumn 1597 – spring 1598) at Wandsbek, the castle of Heinrich Rantzau near Hamburg, where he printed the Astronomiae instauratae mechanica and the star-catalogue. The subscription manu propria ("in his own hand") attests autograph signature of the (clerk-copied) letter.
  • Recipient. Iosephus Scaliger = Joseph Justus Scaliger (1540–1609), the foremost chronological scholar of the age, then professor at Leiden. His Opus novum de emendatione temporum founded modern technical chronology.
  • opus … redintegratum de Temporum Emendatione — Scaliger's Opus novum de emendatione temporum; redintegratum ("reissued / restored anew") signals the second, revised edition (Frankfurt 1598; first edition Paris 1583), which Tycho says he had just received. Tycho owned a copy of the 1583 edition (now Prague UL, Ex Bibliotheca Tychoniana no. 41).
  • Francis(cus) Tegnaglius = Frans Gansneb genaamd Tengnagel (1576–1622), Dutch-born nobleman, Tycho's assistant ("of my household") and travelling companion in the move from Denmark; later married Tycho's daughter Elisabeth. Here sent as Tycho's confidential envoy in Bataviam — to Holland / the Low Countries (Batavia, the humanist name for the Dutch provinces), i.e. toward Leiden.
  • Zoili — "Zoili" (from Zoilus, the proverbial carping critic of Homer): captious detractors; Tycho's standing term for hostile critics, kept as the classical plural with note.
  • The Heidelberg critic. quendam eorum … Heidelberga scribentem — an unnamed correspondent who had attacked Scaliger; Tycho recalls counselling him to defer to Scaliger. The identity is not stated in the letter and is left unidentified here (not silently supplied).
  • Chronology / astronomy of the year-length.
  • anni Alphonsini … magnitudinem — the Alphonsine value of the tropical year (365 d 5 h 49 m 16 s in the Alfonsine Tables), which Scaliger had adopted; Tycho argues from his own equinox observations that it is still slightly too long.
  • vix 49 minuta ultra integras horas … nedum … in 6 H. excederet — Tycho's estimate that the excess over the 365 whole days is barely 49 minutes beyond the [5] whole hours, and certainly does not reach a full 6 hours (i.e. the Julian 365¼-day value is too long). "[5]" is not in the text; "integras horas" ("the whole hours") is rendered literally and the implied figure flagged, not inserted.
  • Progymnasmatum Astronomicorum — Tycho's Astronomiae instauratae progymnasmata (the "Preliminary Exercises for the Restored Astronomy," pr. 1602/1610); its first chapter carried his equinox table, which Scaliger had used.
  • respectu bissexti — "with respect to the bissextile," i.e. whether a backward-reckoned Julian year is or is not a leap year; the caution is that compared years must be in the same leap-cycle phase. bissextus/bissextilis rendered "bissextile [leap day]/leap year."
  • medium Aequinoctium — the mean equinox (as opposed to apparent), to be used on both sides so as to avoid the intricatio arising from the secular change of the apogee (apogaeum) and eccentricity (eccentricitas) — glossary.
  • Albategnij Aratensis = al-Battānī of al-Raqqa (Latin Albategnius; Aratensis = of al-Raqqa, ancient Aracta/Callinicum, c. 858–929), whose 9th–10th-c. equinox observations Tycho recommends as the key intermediate data between antiquity and his own.
  • parallaxin ☉ … Aequatoris inclinationem … Poli sublimitatem … altitudine ☉ meridiana … solstitio — solar parallax; the inclination of the equator (obliquity); the elevation of the pole (latitude); the meridian altitude of the Sun at each solstice — all glossary. The Sun-glyph ☉ is kept in the Source block and named "the Sun" in translation by this chapter's convention
  • The Jewish epoch crux. de Epocha antiquissima illa Iudaica, quando Aequinoctium die 21. Aprilis sex horis a Meridie extitisse creditur — Tycho asks Scaliger about a very ancient Jewish chronological epoch at which the (vernal) equinox was reputedly on 21 April, six hours past noon. The clause sex horis a Meridie is rendered "six hours from the meridian," glossed "past noon"; this is a chronological datum Scaliger treated, queried by Tycho, not a date in the letter's own reckoning.
  • Calendar of the dateline. die 14. Martij Anni 1598 — 14 March 1598, Old Style, consistent with the edition label ("1598. 14. marts. Wandsburg"); Tycho, writing as a Dane in Lutheran territory, used the Julian calendar (Denmark and Hamburg/Holstein had not adopted the Gregorian reform). No conversion is made. The recipient Scaliger, in Leiden, was in a New-Style jurisdiction — a style-gap Tycho does not here remark on, but which is the very subject of the 1600 sequel letter (where Tycho cites Scaliger's claim that the Dionysian Christian epoch is two years short). Recorded, not reconciled.
  • Wandesburgo ex Arce Ranzoviana … prope Hamburgum = Wandsbek, from the Rantzau Castle, near Hamburg (the residence of Heinrich Rantzau, Stattholder of Holstein and Tycho's host).
  • Formulae. S. P. = salutem plurimam → "Heartiest greeting"; Vale → "Farewell"; Illustris tuae Magnis[itudini] studiosiss[imus] → "Most devoted to Your Illustrious Magnificence"; manu propria → "in his own hand."
