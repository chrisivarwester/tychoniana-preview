The original

CLARISSIMO ORNATISSIMOQUE VIRO, MAGISTRO BARTHOLOMÆO SCULTETO, GORLICENSI, MATHEMATICO EXIMIO, AMICO VETERI PLURIMUM COLENDO.
S.

GRATUM mihi fuit, charissime SCULTETE, vir doctissime, quod nostri, et veteris amicitiae, Lipsiae olim inter nos initae, memoriam conserves. Id enim ex libellis, mihi a te dono missis (qui pergrati erunt) liquido perspexi. Pro qua tua benevolentia gratias tibi habeo maximas; daboque operam, ut intelligas, me tui etiam memoriam, quamvis multorum annorum locorumque intervallo disiuncti simus, ex animo minime obliterasse. Legi, et diligenter perspexi scriptum tuum de Cometa anni 77. magna diligentia a te elaboratum; sed ut ingenue et amice de ijs, quae sentio, te moneam, videris partim impossibilibus, partim datis minus exquisite observatis parallaxeos mensuram superstruere. Nam neque Calendis Ianuarij Cometa cum illis duabus stellis, quas assumis, supra Horizontem in eodem circulo Verticali esse poterat, neque stellarum intercapedo rite sumpta est, cum sit illa re vera plus ¾ unius gradus minor tua assignatione; ut de altitudinibus nihil dicam. Nec enim fieri poterat, ut spacio 12 minutorum temporis mutaretur parallaxis uno gradu 7 minutis, etiamsi adhuc longe propior Terrae statueretur Cometa; et ipsemet vides, ex tota inductione absurdum sequi. Nam quod capite octavo longitudinem Cometae 4 gradibus cum ½ fere discrepantem a priore positione calculus admittat, per angulorum angustiam minime excusare poterat. Sed existimo te destitutum exquisitis instrumentis et medijs, quibus observationes perficeres; alias demonstrationem tam accuratam et ingeniosam firmiori fundamento superstruxisses, reique veritatem propius attigisses. Ego sane varijs et exquisitis instrumentis, quae in promptu habeo, e solido metallo, tanta magnitudine ad amussim elaborata, ut singulorum minutorum sint capacia, eius Cometae apparentias observavi, et parallaxes multis modis geometrice demonstratas in numeros redegi. At nusquam invenire potui, Cometam illum parallaxin admittere tantam, quae unius gradus partem tertiam attingeret, ut suo loco sufficienter demonstrabimus. Ultimo vero Cometae apparentiae die, eodem usus observationis et demonstrationum adminiculo, sensibilem esse parallaxin certissimis rationibus deprehendi. Hinc omnibus huius rei amatoribus quam primum per otium licuerit, copiosiorem et sufficientem descriptionem, certis observationibus et demonstrationibus innixam, Deo volente proponam. Instrumenta, quibus in observationibus coelestibus utor, sunt eiusmodi, ut nihil in his, quod ad perfectionem faciat, desiderare possis. Quadrantes e solido metallo fabricatos, Azimutha scrupulose unâ indicantes, tantae magnitudinis habeo, ut singula minuta, quaedam etiam cuiuslibet minuti quartam partem discernant. Armillas HYPARCHI et PTOLOMÆI metallicas, singulorum minutorum capaces, quae novis quibusdam compendijs ad amussim elaboratae sunt, habeo. Sextantem Astronomicum, pro capiendis stellarum distantijs, exquisitius et facilius quam per Radium, quo unâ tamen utor, mira tractandi commoditate pridem parari feci. Globum apprime rotundum, sex pedes in diametro exuperantem, construi curavi, qui singulorum pene minutorum capax, stellarum loca, e coelo per veras distantias desumpta, continebit, et in numeros facili compendio resolvet. Torquetum Armillare magnum e metallo solido, tum etiam regulas HYPARCHI, Azimutha unâ cum altitudinibus monstrantes, longitudine sufficienti nunc confici curo, pluraque alia, Astronomicis exercitijs inservientia, organa, magna et exquisita, partim perfecta habeo, partim indies perfici elaboro. Aedificium etiam, quod Uraniae nomine insignivi, in medio huius Insulae his Astronomiae exercitijs et Spagyricis laboribus apprime opportunum, nec minus secundum architectonicae artis decoram symmetriam extructum, magno sumptu nunc tandem absolvi. Impendi autem tum in hoc, tum in instrumentorum constructionem, intra quinquennium proxime elapsum, plus * ib millibus Ioachimicorum. Serio enim, et pro rei dignitate Astronomica hîc tractanda, commoditatesque aptas iudicavi constituendas, minime ignorans, non esse cuiusque, sumtus tanto negotio rite instruendo sustinere, et eos, qui possint, id in alijs rebus ludicris potius praestare, quam ut tantae scientiae facultates impendant. Quam cupide optarem, ut hic mihi simul adesses! Videres sane ea, quae te non mediocriter oblectarent. Sed quia id fieri vix potest (uxorem enim, uti audio, duxisti) et Reipublicae insuper das operam, effice, ut per literas saltem crebrius confabulemur. Invenies me in conservanda vel per has amicitia minime negligentem. Mitto tibi fasciculum literarum, et te obnixe rogatum volo, ut per certum hominem ad Dominum D. THADEUM HAGECIUM Pragam illas transferri procurare velis. Dedi PAULO WITICHIO a nobis discedenti quasdam ipsi etiam mittendas, quas tamen minus fideliter procuravit, et multis alijs minus sincere mecum egisse (cum tamen benevole fuerit exceptus) deprehendi. Cum enim mea, quae nancisci poterat, clam abstulisset, observandique sidera nostris instrumentis compendia et rationes perspexisset (quibus tamen tractandis minime idoneus erat, nam ne unam quidem observationem aut Cometae aut alicuius stellae, vel Radio saltem capere illi possibile erat, nedum ut Cometae parallaxes deprehenderet, prout DUDITIO frustra persuasit) tandem sub praetextu, quod avunculus eius mortuus esset, haereditatem sibi deberi professus, hinc domum reversus est, et, quo mirabar maxime, finxit se intra paucas hebdomadas reversurum, cum tamen ego eius opera minime indigerem, eoque potius carerem, quam ut multo labore mihi comparata clam a me subduceret, sibique alibi arrogaret, quod, uti in Cometa fecit, sic etiam in Solaris cursus restitutione, et octavae Sphaerae emendatione, quam a nobis nactus est, facturum non dubito. Sed reddidit is me prudentiorem, effecitque, ne cuivis posthac mea committam. At, quia Solaris cursus a nobis restituti mentio facta est, mitto tibi anni proxime futuri Ephemerim Solarem, ex nostra motuum ratione, quam annis aliquot praeteritis, praesertim 78, de coelo instrumentis et Geometricis demonstrationibus in numeros redegi. Hanc si cum ipso coelo, beneficio alicuius instrumenti, contuleris, invenies eam motui Solis, quantum sensus discernere possunt, apprime congruere, a quo tamen uterque calculus Astronomicus hactenus usurpatus longe interdum aberrat, ecclipsibus etiam id ipsum non obscure ostendentibus. Tu itaque per instrumenta nostros motus examina, ita tamen, ut Poli altitudo in ipso scrupulo nota sit, et parallaxeos Solaris habeatur ratio, instrumentique tanta fuerit magnitudo, ut singula minuta commode discernat. Sed, ne videar epistolae metas transcendere, tecum amplius colloqui ipsa charta nos prohibet. Tuum erit per occasionem mihi rescribere. Si quae praeterea fuerint edita, quae a me expeti posse iudicaveris, ut DUDITIJ de Cometis et Astrologia libellum, et si quae sunt alia huiusmodi, peto ut ea ad me transmittere velis. Ego, ubi per otium (ut spero) hybernum, mea, quae proposui de Cometis, absolvero, tecum ea, et si quae habuero alia, tibi grata, lubens communicabo, daboque operam, ut me perpetuo tui memorem experiaris.

Vale, mi SCULTETE, et mei memoriam, uti facis, conserva.
Datae Uranienburgi in Insula nostra Huaena 12. Octobr. An. 1581.
TYCHO BRAHE, Danus.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the most distinguished and accomplished man, Master Bartholomäus Scultetus of Görlitz, an outstanding mathematician, an old friend much to be honoured.
Greeting.

It was welcome to me, dearest Scultetus, most learned man, that you keep alive the memory of our old friendship, formed between us long ago at Leipzig. For I clearly perceived this from the little books sent to me by you as a gift (which will be most welcome). For this kindness of yours I give you the greatest thanks; and I shall take care that you understand that I too, although we are sundered by an interval of many years and places, have by no means erased the memory of you from my heart. I have read, and diligently examined, your treatise on the comet of [the year] '77, worked out by you with great diligence; but — to advise you frankly and in friendship of what I think — you seem to build the measure of your parallax partly upon impossibilities, partly upon data less than exactly observed. For neither could the comet, on the Kalends of January [1 January], have been above the horizon on the same vertical circle as those two stars which you assume, nor is the interval between the stars rightly taken, since it is in truth more than ¾ of one degree less than your assignment — to say nothing of the altitudes. For it could not happen that in the space of 12 minutes of time the parallax should change by one degree and 7 minutes, even were the comet placed far closer still to the Earth; and you yourself see that an absurdity follows from the whole induction. For as to the fact that in the eighth chapter your calculation admits a longitude of the comet differing by nearly 4½ degrees from the earlier position, this could by no means be excused by the narrowness of the angles. But I judge that you were lacking the exact instruments and means by which you might perfect your observations; otherwise you would have built a demonstration so accurate and ingenious upon a firmer foundation, and would have come nearer to the truth of the matter. I, for my part, with the various and exact instruments which I have ready to hand — [made] of solid metal, of so great a size, [and] wrought to the line so that they are capable of [reading] single minutes — observed the appearances of that comet, and reduced to numbers the parallaxes demonstrated geometrically in many ways. But nowhere could I find that that comet admitted a parallax so great as to reach a third part of one degree, as I shall sufficiently demonstrate in its place. But on the last day of the comet's appearance, using the same aid of observation and of demonstrations, I detected by most certain reasonings that the parallax was perceptible. Hence, as soon as leisure shall allow, I shall — God willing — set forth for all lovers of this matter a fuller and sufficient account, resting upon sure observations and demonstrations. The instruments which I use in celestial observations are of such a kind that you could desire nothing in them that would conduce to perfection. I have quadrants fashioned of solid metal, indicating the azimuths together [with the altitudes] scrupulously, of so great a size that they distinguish single minutes, and some even a quarter part of any minute. I have metal armillae of Hipparchus and Ptolemy, capable of [reading] single minutes, which have been wrought to the line by certain new contrivances. I had an astronomical sextant prepared some while ago, for taking the distances of the stars more exactly and more easily than by the cross-staff (which nevertheless I use as well), with a remarkable convenience of handling. I had a globe constructed, exceedingly round, exceeding six feet in diameter, which — capable of [reading] almost single minutes — will contain the places of the stars, taken from the heavens by their true distances, and will resolve them into numbers by an easy method. I am now having made a great armillary torquetum of solid metal, and also the rules of Hipparchus, showing the azimuths together with the altitudes, of sufficient length; and many other instruments serving for astronomical exercises, great and exact, I have partly finished, partly am labouring to have finished day by day. The building too, which I have marked out with the name of Urania, in the middle of this island — exceedingly suited to these exercises of astronomy and to the spagyric labours, and built no less according to the comely symmetry of the architectural art — I have now at last completed at great expense. And I have spent, both on this and on the construction of the instruments, within the five-year period most lately elapsed, more than [* ib] thousand thaler. For I judged that, in earnest and according to the dignity of the astronomical matter to be handled here, suitable conveniences must be established, being by no means ignorant that it is not [in the power] of everyone to bear the expenses of duly equipping so great an undertaking, and that those who can [bear them] would rather lay them out on other frivolous things than expend their means on so great a science. How eagerly I could wish that you were here with me at the same time! You would assuredly see things that would delight you not a little. But because that can scarcely come about (for you have taken a wife, as I hear) and you moreover give your service to the commonwealth, bring it about that we may at least converse more often by letter. You will find me by no means negligent in keeping up our friendship even by these [letters]. I send you a packet of letters, and I earnestly wish to ask you to be willing to see to it that they be conveyed by a trusty man to Master Doctor Thaddaeus Hagecius at Prague. To Paul Wittich, on his departing from us, I gave certain [letters] to be sent by him as well, which however he saw to less than faithfully; and I have found that he dealt with me less than sincerely in many other matters too (although he had been kindly received). For when he had secretly carried off my [materials] which he could lay hold of, and had inspected the methods and procedures of observing the heavenly bodies with our instruments (for handling which, however, he was by no means fit, for it was not possible for him to take even a single observation, of a comet or of any star, even with the cross-staff at least, far less to detect the parallaxes of a comet, as he falsely persuaded Dudith) — at last, under the pretext that his uncle had died, professing that an inheritance was owed him, he returned home from here, and — at which I marvelled most — pretended that he would return within a few weeks; whereas I had no need at all of his service, and would rather be without it than have him secretly withdraw from me things gained by me with much labour, and arrogate them to himself elsewhere — which, as he did in the matter of the comet, so too in the restoration of the solar course, and in the emendation of the eighth sphere which he obtained from us, I doubt not he will do. But he has made me more prudent, and has brought it about that I shall not hereafter entrust my [work] to anyone whatever. But, since mention has been made of the solar course restored by us, I send you a solar ephemeris for the year next to come, [drawn] from our theory of the motions, which in some years past — especially '78 — I reduced to numbers from the heavens by instruments and geometrical demonstrations. If you compare this with the heaven itself, by the help of some instrument, you will find it to agree exceedingly well with the motion of the Sun, as far as the senses can discern — from which, however, both kinds of astronomical calculation hitherto in use sometimes stray far, the eclipses too showing this very thing not obscurely. You therefore examine our motions by instruments, yet in such a way that the altitude of the pole be known to the very scruple [minute], and that account be taken of the solar parallax, and that the instrument be of so great a size that it conveniently distinguish single minutes. But, lest I seem to overstep the bounds of a letter, the paper itself forbids me to converse with you further. It will be for you to write back to me when occasion offers. If, besides, there have been anything published which you shall judge could be sought by me — such as Dudith's little book on comets and astrology, and if there are any others of this kind — I ask that you be willing to transmit them to me. I, when I shall have finished — at the winter leisure (as I hope) — the things I have proposed concerning comets, will gladly share them with you, and any others I may have that are agreeable to you, and will take care that you find me ever mindful of you.

Farewell, my Scultetus, and keep up the memory of me, as you do.
Given at Uraniborg, on our island of Hven, 12 October, the year 1581.
Tycho Brahe, the Dane.

Apparatus

  • Sender / recipient. Tycho Brahe writing from his newly-completed Uraniborg. The addressee, Bartholomaeus Scultetus, Gorlicensis = Bartholomäus Scultetus (Bartholomäus Schultz, 1540–1614), mathematician, cartographer and astronomer of Görlitz (Lusatia), later its mayor; he and Tycho had known one another from their Leipzig student years (1562–65), as the letter recalls. The source is one of four Tycho-to-Scultetus letters printed in the Singularia historico-literaria Lusatica (27th collection, 1743), noted by Dreyer (Tycho Brahe, p. 133, app. p. 177ff).
  • The comet of '77. Cometa anni 77 = the great comet of 1577, the central object of Tycho's cometary work (and of Scultetus's own tract, which Tycho here critiques). Calendis Ianuarij = "on the Kalends of January," i.e. 1 January [1578, O.S.] — the Roman-calendar idiom kept and glossed. Tycho's critique is technical: the comet and Scultetus's two reference stars could not have shared one vertical circle (azimuth circle) at that moment; the stellar interval is overstated by > ¾°; and a parallax change of 1°7′ in 12 minutes of time is impossible. He reports his own finding that the comet's parallax never reached ⅓° — i.e. it lay far beyond the Moon (the supralunary-comet thesis).
  • Technical terms (editorial glossary–5.2): parallaxis → parallax; altitudo → altitude; circulus Verticalis → vertical circle; longitudo → (celestial) longitude; azimuth kept; organa → instruments; quadrantes → quadrants; armillae → armillae; sextans Astronomicus → astronomical sextant; Radius → cross-staff (radius astronomicus); globus → globe; Torquetum Armillare → armillary torquetum; regulae → rules (parallactic rules). ad amussim ("to the line/by rule") rendered "wrought to the line." Spagyricis laboribus = spagyric (alchemical) labours — the laboratory work at Uraniborg; the period term is kept.
  • Armillas Hyparchi et Ptolomaei / regulas Hyparchi — "the armillae of Hipparchus and Ptolemy" and "the rules of Hipparchus": Tycho names his instrument types by their ancient prototypes (Ptolemaic armillary rings; the Hipparchan/Ptolemaic regulae). Names retained in the Greco-Latin form used.
  • Aedificium … quod Uraniae nomine insignivi — "the building which I have marked out with the name of Urania": Uraniborg ("Urania's castle"), named for the muse of astronomy, declared "now at last completed."
  • The expenditure crux. plus * ib millibus Ioachimicorum — flagged crux. Ioachimici = Joachimsthaler / thalers (silver coins of Joachimsthal), rendered "thaler." The numeral preceding millibus is corrupt in the printed source: it appears as an editorial mark (*) followed by ib, not a legible figure. The sense is secure ("more than [N] thousand thaler [spent] within the last five years on Uraniborg and the instruments"), but the quantity itself is not recoverable from this text and is left bracketed [ ib] rather than silently emended to any specific number. (Tycho elsewhere gives figures on the order of a "tonne of gold"; that comparandum is not* imported here.)
  • Thadeus Hagecius = Tadeáš Hájek (Thaddaeus Hagecius) of Prague, imperial physician and astronomer, Tycho's principal Bohemian correspondent; Tycho asks Scultetus to forward an enclosed packet to him.
  • Paulus Witichius = Paul Wittich (c. 1546–1586) of Breslau (Wratislaviensis), the mathematician who had stayed with Tycho on Hven in 1580. Tycho's bitter charge — that Wittich secretly carried off Tycho's methods (instrument designs, the prosthaphaeretic/observational procedures, the work on the solar course [Solaris cursus restitutio] and the emendation of the eighth sphere [the fixed-star catalogue / precession]) and would "arrogate them to himself elsewhere" — is preserved at full force, including the parenthetical claim that Wittich was incompetent at the instruments ("could not take even a single observation … even with the cross-staff"). prout Duditio frustra persuasit = "as he vainly persuaded Dudith." The hedges and Tycho's own qualifiers (non dubito "I doubt not"; cum tamen benevole fuerit exceptus "although he had been kindly received") are kept unsharpened.
  • Duditius = Andreas Dudith (András Dudith, 1533–1589), the humanist who had published on the comet of 1577 (his De cometarum significatione); Tycho both reports Wittich's failed attempt to persuade Dudith of cometary parallax and asks Scultetus to procure "Dudith's little book on comets and astrology" for him.
  • Solaris cursus restitutio → "the restoration of the solar course"; octavae Sphaerae emendatio → "the emendation of the eighth sphere" (the sphere of the fixed stars) — both rendered per the restitutio/instauratio glossary entry. Ephemeris Solaris → "solar ephemeris," derived from Tycho's solar theory worked out "especially [in] '78."
  • in ipso scrupulo — scrupulus here = the minute of arc ("known to the very scruple"); the gloss "[minute]" is bracketed.
  • Calendar / place. Datae Uranienburgi in Insula nostra Huaena 12. Octobr. An. 1581 = "Given at Uraniborg, on our island of Hven, 12 October 1581" (O.S.; Denmark kept the Julian calendar). The dateline (12 October) agrees with the edition label (12 October 1581); no discrepancy. Datae ("Given") kept.
  • Greek / symbols. No Greek and no astronomical/zodiac glyphs occur in this letter. The only non-alphabetic mark is the corrupt numeral sign (*) in the expenditure clause, treated above.
  • Orthography. Source-printed forms retained in the Source block (ijs, medijs, Genesi-style -ij-, intercapedo, ecclipsibus, Uranienburgi, Huaena, PTOLOMAeI, BARTHOLOMAeO); the ae-ligature spellings (-MAeO, -MAeI) reproduced as printed by this chapter's convention(2).
  • Correction. English clause corrected from 'as he vainly persuaded Dudith' to 'as he falsely persuaded Dudith' — Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII, pp.62-63, confirms the Latin 'frustra persuasit' as printed (perfect tense = successful persuasion, but groundless); the prior English wording reversed the sense to imply a failed attempt. The Latin text itself needed no correction.

Latin 'frustra persuasit' most naturally means Wittich DID succeed in persuading Dudith, but groundlessly/falsely (frustra = without real basis); the English 'vainly persuaded' reads to a modern eye as 'tried but failed to persuade', which reverses whether Dudith was actually taken in by Wittich's false claim to have detected the comet's parallax. At: “as he vainly persuaded Dudith”.

Repaired. Verified the single mistranslation against the Dreyer page images (TBOO VII pp.62-63, where the phrase 'prout DUDITIO fruftra perfuafit' spans the page break). Nothing else in the letter was altered.
