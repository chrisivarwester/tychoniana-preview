Dating begun 30 November 1592 [N.S.]

Editorial note. The longest letter in the whole corpus and the last of the Tycho–Rothmann correspondence (Rothmann, ill since his 1590 visit to Hven, never replied; the exchange ends here). After a condolence on Rothmann's illness and an account of why the comet-works remain unpublished, the bulk is a sustained, point-by-point demolition of John Craig (the unnamed "Scot") and his anti-Tychonic Capnuraniae restinctio — defending the comets' aethereal nature, the 1572 nova, and, notably, John Dee (Nucleus parallaticus) and Thomas Digges (Alae), with embedded quotations from Jacob Monau, Craig himself, Hájek, and Curtius. It closes with the Mars/Venus-eccentricity argument against Copernicus and the 1000-star catalogue. Printed-edition text (clean), v/u orthography retained. Declared interventions: the digitised text layer-intruded apparatus "A" sigla on some 40 words removed as non-textual (as in the letters to Scaliger of 23 Aug 1598, to Peucer of 1590 and 13 Sept 1588, to the Landgrave of 16 Aug 1588, to Herwart of 16 Nov 1599, from Rothmann of 13 Oct 1588 and 20 Jan 1587, and to Hájek of 3 Aug 1590). Greek (ἀυτὸς ἔφα, πολυρρητορείαν [src πολυροτορέιαν], κάπνἐγκέφαλος, λόγῳ) and the glyphs (♂ ♀ ♊ ♋ ☉ ☽) are reproduced as transcribed; the long quotations from Craig (and from Monau/Hájek/Curtius), italic in the print, are kept inline and identified in the Apparatus. Ianuarij Anni 1595" (Old Style); but the letter embeds its own composition note — "Hodie, qui est dies ultimus Novembris Anni 1592 novo stylo" (30 Nov 1592 N.S., when Tycho received Rothmann's 28 Sept letter and Craig's screed via the Magdeburg courier) — so it was begun late 1592 and only completed/sent in Jan 1595. CLARISSIMO ET PRÆSTANTISSIMO VIRO D. CHRISTOPHORO ROTHMANNO, VARIA ERVDITIONE INPRIMISQVE RERUM MATHEMATICARUM INSIGNI PERITIA DECORATO, AMICO SUO PLURIMUM DILECTO. S. D.

ACCEPI tandem â te literas, eruditissime et amantissime Rothmanne, diu multumque antea desideratas: cum, quod collationes nostrae in Rebus Astronomicis interea praeter solitum feriarentur; tum quod de te tuâque valetudine, et vbinam locorum haereres (siquidem ad Principem tuum tanto tempore reversus non esses) certi aliquid cognoscere avêrem. Intelligo autem ex his ipsis, te prae nimiâ invaletudine ad ipsum Cassellas ante obitum eius non revenisse, retardante morbo, ex salium Tartareorum resolutionibus proveniente, et te toto hoc tempore, pene absque intermissione infestante. De quâ valetudinis sinistrâ affectione tibi plurimum condoleo; quodque in hac viridi adhuc aetate tam saevis morborum tormentis obnoxius sis. At cum te nonnihil iam respirasse indices, est, quod tibi congratuler; utque haec valetudinis recuperatio quotidie incrementum sumat, constansque permaneat, exopto. Si mea Medicamenta ex officinâ nostra Pyronomica prodeuntia, nec parvo labore aut sumptu praeparata, tibi in his tantis malis (modo, uti fit, aliquando recurrant) praesto esse poterint, ea lubens offero, et lubentius impertiar: quemadmodum et praesenti tibi meam in hisce operam non denegavi. Quod avtem eâ postmodum usus non es, tuâ ipsius culpâ factum est, qui me de adversâ tuâ valetudine, nec certiorem reddidisti; nec quidpiam eiuscemodi (licet discedenti ultro oblatum) â me postea per literas expetijsti. Sive igitur ipsemet huc reversus fueris, uti quodammodo in te recepisti, sive per certum et idoneum hominem hâc de re commonefeceris, invenies me ad praestandum id officij minime difficilem.

Adhortaris in hisce Literis, ut Cometographica nostra Opera in publicum prodire sinam, et cur tam diu apud me delitescant, miraris; praesertim cum illa pro maiori parte diu absoluta, toties, tamque diu me evulgaturum promiserim: Eruditis etiam viris non paucis eorum copiam sibi fieri desiderantibus. Sed et non parum emolumenti ad Mathematica studia ornanda, atque ab imperitorum et calumniatorum obtrectationibus vindicanda hinc proventurum asseveras. Facit id quidem, mî ROTHMANNE, magnâ ex parte animi tui ingenuitas, et erga nos, ac communia nobis studia Amor ille sublimis, atque peculiaris favor: attamen et Veritatem hisce non modicam subesse, (si absque gloriatione et invidiâ id dicere liceret) non eo infidas. Nam quid et quantum Mathematicis exercitijs rite administratis praestari queat, necdum communiter in Scholis philosophantibus, nedum reliquo vulgo â Musis alieno, satis innotuit. Et certe Cometarum exacta dimensio, atque quo motu cieantur, quantumque â nobis absint, ingeniosa Demonstratio, ea quae tot saeculis a Graeco illo Aristotele citra ratam Apodixin prolata sunt, et in omnibus Academijs pro certis atque indubitatis assertionibus proclamata, ita infringunt, ut, quanto ignorantiae chao Philosophiae cultores hâc in parte involuti fuerint, nunc tandem Divinae Matheseos Ope, apud veritatis amantes elucescat. Quod et in multis alijs abstrusis, et hactenus ignotis, vel pro compertis falso creditis rebus pariter eiusdem incomparabilis Scientiae adminiculis fieri posse, nullum est dubium: ut de pluribus corollarijs, quae hinc ad omnes alias Disciplinas plenius excolendas derivantur, nihil dicam. Quod autem Opus nostrum de Cometis nondum publici juris fieri permiserim, multae, et graves me detinuerunt rationes. Praeterquam enim, quod in perennium illorum, et Mundo coaevorum Siderum tam Observationibus, quam eorundem in normam Caelesti harmoniae competentem deductionibus plurimum indefesso studio aliquot iam continuis Annis sudârim; (quae res non parum, uti scis, et temporis et laboris requirit) ut ob id alijs istis serio vacare minus fuerit commodum; Multa etiam praeterea citra expectationem nostram, et omnem amolitionis providentiam, longo iam tempore inciderunt, quae me otio frui Philosophico, et Vraniae sacris, prout vellem, litare non permiserunt. Adeo invida est Mundi huius immundi turbulentia, contra purioris veritatis, etiam in Philosophicis et visibilibus Caeli aspectabilis rebus; nedum ut in Divinis, et invisibilibus Mundi illius Archetypi, et rationi humanae impervestigabilibus, si quid ab ignorantiae tenebris in apertam lucem perducatur, acquiescere possit. Accessit et hoc, quod nostri circa Cometarum, praeteritis aliquot Annis conspectorum apparentias accurate disquirendas, Labores cuiusvis aestimatione maiores, plus temporis, operarum, et sumptuum requisierint, quam ingrati nonnulli homines, adeoque ex ipsis, qui Disciplinarum, et quidem Mathematicarum amorem, scientiamque haud minimam praetendunt, agnoscere potuerint. Inter quos unus praesertim est, qui non saltem nullas mihi agere gratias pro tot vigilatis noctibus, tot erogatis impensis, totque exantlatis in Demonstrationibus et Numeris, ut veritas intemerata erui posset, Laboribus, aequum censuerit; sed et oblocutionibus, perversionibus, certitudinis patefactae impugnationibus; imô cavillis et scommatis nos impetere non erubuerit. Vt ob id Excellentissimum illum et prudentissimum virum IACOBVM MONAVIVM, honorandae memoriae (Nam, uti audio, ante triennium fatis concessit) non immerito ad eximium Mathematicum BARTHOLOMÆVM SCVLTETVM, cum librum nostrum de Cometâ Anni 77. eo accomodante nactus, inspexisset, in hunc modum scripsisse recorder, prout etiam in Sculteti tunc ad me datis literis annotatum reperio: O uos felices animas, quibus in domos superas scandere cura fuit, et est hodie quoque. Miratus sum magis quam intellexi tantam diligentiam D. Tychonis, quanta animaduertitur in hoc illius Opere. Fac illum horteris, ut reliquas etiam promat Operis illius partes. Etiamsi hoc saeculum nostrum non sit dignum istis tot et tantis bonis, quae agnoscit, eâ quâ par erat gratitudine. Wratislauiae die 17. Iunij stili ueteris Anno 1588. Haec optimus ille vir, pro eo, quo excelluit, iudicij acumine, ac in Rebus, tâm Philosophicis, quam Politicis prudentiâ, et protulit, et quasi praesagire visus est.

Is vero, qui prae caeteris, nostrum in Re Cometica studium elevare, et oppugnare attentavit, Scotus quidam natione fuit, Medicinae Galenicae Doctor, et Aristoteleae Philosophiae supra modum addictus. Quem hîc nominare nolo, eius honori parcens. Vult quidem is genere Nobilis haberi, uti de se ipso nonnullibi insinuat: sed tamen dicto Philosopho Ethnico nimis serviliter, potiore sui parte, hoc est, genio et ingenio, in hoc praesertim de Cometis negotio, mancipatus. Cum ille ex intercessione cuiusdam sui conterranei me hîc invisentis librum nostrum secundum de Aetherei Mundi phaenomênis, qui de Cometâ illo ingenti Anni 77. affatim tractat, statim, atque Typis absolutus erat, accepisset, scriptis ad me literis, assertiones nostras isthic comprehensas, praesertim, quod Cometam illum in Aethereâ Mundi Regione, et nequaquam Elementari (contra quam docuit Aristoteles) cursum suum absolvisse ostenderim, in dubium vocare, et quantum in ipso erat, labefactare conatus est. Existimabam certe ab initio, hominem amice et candide, veritatis penitius manifestandae gratiâ id ipsum agere. Quod et multi alij, non ineruditi viri mecum opinati sunt: Inter quos erat Magnificus ille IACOBVS CVRTIVS, Imperij Procancellarius, qui, missis ad me literis, eos, qui nostrae hac in parte assertioni contradicerent, id serio non agere; sed saltem, ut plura his similia expiscarentur, bonâ intentione moliri existimabat. Et erat is sane non saltem in Politicis, sed in Mathematicis quoque Rebus vir perspicaci ingenio, et acri simul iudicio praeditus: ut de Clarissimo illo Doctore THADDÆO HAGGECCIO, et alijs idem sentientibus nihil addam. Ex quo itaque animadverti esse, qui de assertionibus nostris in praedicto libro propositis haesitationem movêre praesumerent, adeo, ut ijs contradicere non dubitarent: Eos quidem, qui in Aristoteleâ schola educati, ex ipsius saltem authoritate, et dudum receptâ opinione, Cometas necessario in Aëre generari, inferebant, nullâ tamen ratione Geometrica vel saltem huius praetextu, rem ita se habêre probantes, refutatione indignos censui: Cum ab authoritate humanâ diducta argumentatio omnium sit infirmissima, et ob id postremo loco merito reponenda. At Scoto huic; cum non solum Aristotelëam authoritatem (licet et hanc plus iusto extulerit) multum contra nos venditârit; sed etiam Geometricum nescio quid, pro suâ opinione stabiliendâ praetexuerit, ipsi soli modeste obviandum censui; partim, ut eius obiectiones, quae speciem quandam veri prae se ferebant, et incautos facile in devia abripere poterant, diluerem, partim ut nonnulla in eodem secundo Tomo contenta plenius et dilucidius explicarem, atque de Re Cometicâ generaliore quodam modo nonnulla uterius consignarem: sperans, me ipsi et alijs veritatis hâc in parte scrutatoribus, rem haud ingratam sic praestiturum. Quare conscriptâ inter alias occupationes extemporaneâ quadam Apologia, eam in Scotiam ad Antagonistam nostrum actutum transmisi, eandemque cum quibusdam alijs in Germaniâ Eruditis Viris, qui has res intelligerent, communicavi, ut eorum censuras pariter exquirerem. In quâ certe Apologia, me modestiae limites uspiam transgressum, aut quid citra rem protulisse, mihi ipsi non sum conscius. An vero aliquo affectu (uti nonnunquam fit humanitus) hîc aberrem, dijudicent alij, qui verum â falso sine omni praeiudicio discernere, et nôrunt, et volunt. At quomodo Philosophus iste Aristotelicus nostram hanc candidam, et ipsi (uti sperabam) acceptam futuram intentionem, exceperit, dici vix potest. Postquam enim usque in tertium Annum hanc amicam Apologiam secum retinuisset, nihilque responsi tanto tempore perscripsisset, eum vel nostris dilutionibus acquievisse, et in has transivisse partes, existimabam; vel, quod contradictiones non facile ulterius occurrerent, continuisse. Tandem vero, rupto illo alto silentio, scriptum quoddam Antilogeticum, paulo virulentiore et mordaciore (uti videtur) animo, quam prius per literas egerat, in nos vibravit; in quo scommatis, dicterijs, cavillis, distractionibus et perversionibus multifariam ludit et illudit, ut veritatem â nobis assertam eludat: Nec tam Scotice, quam scoptice agit. Cum multis enim ê Scotiâ oriundis, nos hîc subinde invisentibus, tam Doctrinâ quam Genere claris mihi res fuit, in quibus eximium candorem, pari humanitate coniunctum animadverti; quibus etiam ingenij et iudicij acumen singulare non defuit. At, qui tam ingrato et averso animo, tamque petutanti linguâ, me meaque incesseret, hactenus sensi neminem. Quin et quidam ex ipsius proprijs conterraneis, hoc eius inverecundum factum aperte detestati sunt; quodque sic ipsorum Gentem, exterorum et saniorum hominum iudicijs obnoxiam redderet, ex quo solus talia attentare, reique ita demonstratae, ut omnibus approbaretur, etiam ijs, qui antea contrarium sentiebant, et in publicum promulgârant, nihilominus arroganti ausu contradicere non erubuerit, conquesti sunt. Et sane, si id quod verum est, pro me ipso dicere liceat, non immerito hoc protulerunt. Nam cum alij in Mathematicis non minus excellentes viri, quam iste Antagonista (cui in hisce disciplinis scientiam non derogo, modo eâ recte uti vellet) qui Cometam illum sublunarem fuisse, et Parallaxin quinque circiter Graduum admisisse, scriptis suis in publicum editis iam antea pronunciassent, accepto hoc nostro libro, et re melius perspectâ, non saltem, se aberrasse, ingenue fassi sint; sed et, quod certiora monstrârim, mihi non modice gratias egerint: Hic solus, licet ipsemet nihil in eodem Cometa, quod alicuius momenti foret, expertus esset, tamen nostras laboriosas vigilias et summam circa haec diligentiam extenuare, et quantum in ipso erat, irrita reddere nitebatur: adeo, ut aliorum quoque data et pronunciata, quae ipsimet authores tandem, re melius intellectâ aversati sunt, et retractârunt, in manus resumserit, et ijs, cum ipsemet nulla habuerit, contra nos usus, vel potius abusus fuerit.

Vt autem aliquâ ex parte cognoscas, optime ROTHMANNE, quantâ is non saltem me; sed et ipsam veritatem, quam penitius pro virili inquirere sategimus, iniuriâ afficere, quamque scoptice mea incessere sibi arrogârit, percurram breviter Invectivae eius (sic enim potius quam moderata aliqua Apologia nominari meretur) praecipuos quosdam locos: solidiorem et specialem eius replicationem, una cum conuenienti refutatione alicui ex nostris Amicis, aut etiam Discipulis harum rerum gnaris, suo tempore exequendam commendans. Titulum fecit huic stomachoso et insolenti scripto: Capnuraniae restinctio, seu Cometarum in Aethera sublimationis refutatio. Cum tamen ego nusquam, Cometas, licet reverâ Aetherei sint, ex fumositatibus constare asseruerim; sed potius e caelesti materiâ: prout istum liber noster secundus, quem impugnat, admonêre potuit; qui sic habet pag. 254. Ego materiam omnium Cometarum prorsus coelestem esse iudico, siquidem etiam omnes in ipso caelo generentur. At de fumis, quos ille vendit, nihil. Tu quidem mî ROTHMANNE, aliquando fuisti in eâ sententiâ, quod fumositates Terrestres Caelum (siquidem illud tuâ et IOHANNIS PENAe Galli opinione planê aërium esset) subintrare possent, et materiam isthîc Cometis praebêre: Quod tamen ego improbavi, et tute ex viâ lacteâ aliter postea edoctus, id ipsum revocasti: uti ex Epistolis inter nos communicatis videre licet. Subiungit deinde in eadem inscriptione, quod ea, quae pro asserendo Caelesti Cometarum situ, et Apologiâ meâ adfero, ab ipso infirmentur omnia: ubi sibi ipsi non constare in hoc ipso frontispicio videtur; dum nunc me pro Aethereis phaenomênis, prius pro caelestibus fumis Cometas habere insimulat: Et non decimam partem eorum, quae libro secundo, atque in Apologiâ, pro asserendo Cometarum Aethereo situ adduximus, vel supremis labris attigit; nedum, ut ea omnia (uti nimis arroganter iactitat) infirmârit. Imô ea ipsa, quae carpit, ne conquassat quidem, nedum, ut labefactet vel destruat. Deinde in ipso scripto statim meorum Instrumentorum observationes prae veterum diutino consensu elevat: quod, quam recte faciat, tute (qui nostra spectasti, et eorum usum hancque tractationem penitius intelligis) dijudicare potes. Postea totus ferme, vel scommaticus est, vel contra nos iniuriosus, nostra distrahens aut pervertens, neque, uti decuit, recte accommodans. Et, ut paucis dicam, ea vel omnimode non intelligit, vel intelligere non vult. Ait, se ab initio non putasse, me serio sic de Cometis sensisse; sed Paradoxa disserendi caussâ struxisse: Cum tamen liber noster secundus satis ostendat, me non iocose in his ludere, tot eâ de caussâ exantlatis et Obseruationum et Demonstrationum, tum quoque Numerorum laboribus, quos facilius est carpere, quam imitari. Ait etiam, se nequaquam ita mecum egisse, ut de veritate sententiae suae quicquam dubitaret: Forte, quia extra aliorum hominum sortem positus, ne dubitare quidem, nedum errare potuerit; utpote in Aristotelem credens, qui apud istos est, ἀυτὸς ἔφα. Dicit quoque, me alios ignaros et erroribus mancipatos criminari; et addit scoptice: Bona verba, quaeso! Cum ego tam rigide nuspiam locutus sim; sed saltem eos, qui Aristoteli nimium cum quodam praeiudicio mancipati sunt, modeste notarim, et errores eorundem ostenderim. Desiderat quoque in nobis Mathematicas Demonstrationes; et nonnullibi postea; me solum affirmare quidlibet, et citra necessariam et Geometricam inductionem, mihi et meis Instrumentis fidem adhiberi velle, impudenter insimulare audet. Quam enim falsa haec sint, tu, et alij eximij Mathematici, qui nostra legerunt, et Demonstrationum invictarum non parvam copiam perspexerunt, testari potestis. Rem insuper hanc de Cometis, tam levem esse censet, ut multis non opus habeat disquisitionibus. Scilicet, quicquid olim ebuccinavit Aristoteles, nulli amplius difficultati obnoxium est. Me veritatem nondum perspicere, et cum detrimento Astronomiae hîc errare, audacter magis, quam vere iudicat, vel potius praeiudicat. Quod te rectius intelligere haud ignoro. Vocat, quae in Apologia nostra scripsimus, πολυρρητορείαν quandam, ad quam respondêre non vult. Atqui sub hoc praetextu multa sicco pede praeterit, quae convellere non sustinet. Obijcit, quod caudatas Caelo Stellas, mira monstra, intruserim: Cum tamen Antiquitas, rotunda saltem isthîc esse corpora docuerit. At ego ipsum Cometae corpus per se rotundum esse, nec caudam adhaerentem de ipsius substantiâ; sed per accidens, Opticâ ratione fieri asseveravi. Vt et haec monstrosa potius sit calumnia. Mox, quod Caelestes circuitus, et Numeros ex Hypothesi diductos Cometae illi attribuam, quodque id imitatione MÆSTHLINI et CORNELII GEMMAe faciam, inverecunde exprobrat. Cum potius noster in his conatus, apud candida, et discreta ingenia gratiam merêri debuisset. Et tantum abest, ut ego â MÆSTHLINO vel GEMMA illa mea extruxerim; ut potius in illorum scripta validius, quam hic ZOILVS facit; sed tamen candide, et bonâ intentione animadverterim. Et testari posset, si adhuc viveret WITICHIVS, quod nostras de Cometâ illo conceptiones hîc conscripseramus, antequâm MÆSTHLINI, vel CORNELII huius libelli mihi satis innotuissent: Adeo, ut cum WITICHIVS antea, Cometas omnino sublunares esse, et MÆSTHLINVM, atque alios in Germaniâ, qui contrarium asserverunt, hallucinatos fuisse, credidisset, ex meis solummodo Observationibus, et Demonstrationibus convictus, sententiam mutârit: Qvod et in Germaniam rediens, apud multos fassus est; quorum aliqui, et quidam Scotus, huius conterraneus, mihi id ipsum retulerunt.

Postea, quod Arcum circuli maximi Cometae motui attribuerim, et quia illum in omni situ perpetuo observârit, inde probârim, sublunarem non fuisse, prolixe cavillari, et varie vertere, atque invertere; imô etiam Dialectice transformare, forte ut Organicum se probet, non intermittit. Vim tamen Demonstrationis vel non perspicit, vel suis sophismatis obscurat. Vtque exemplum det, quomodo Arcus circuli maximi sub Lunâ non minus quam in altissimo Aethere designari queat; Tale ludicrum et futile ê suo versuto cerebello promit: Edinburgo recta Vranoburgum proficiscamur, toto cursu uerticibus nostris in octauo orbe maximi Circuli tramitem tenebimus: et sic maris superficiem sulcantes Vranoburgum ueniemus, in Vranô non erimus. Egregia scilicet similitudo, quae non uno vel altero, sed omnibus quatuor pedibus claudicat! quam cachinnis excipiendam, vel potius exsibilandam, quam seriâ aliquâ responsione dignandam, tute et alij harum Rerum gnari sufficienter intelligitis: cum etiam per se intentioni nostrae nihil officiat. Postea speciosa quadam et fucatâ Demonstratione ostendere nititur, posse Cometas, seu, (uti scoptice loquitur) Pseudoplanetas meos, Circulum describere maximum, et nihilominus in Aëre longe infra Lunam versari; ita ut Parallaxin quinque Graduum admittant. Idque potissimum ex motu eius proprio, qui Parallaxin exhaurit, inferre laborat. At frustra hîc laborat Pseudophilosophus iste, etiamsi millies in Aristotelem iurasset. Nos enim motum proprium Cometae ab ea variatione, quam Parallaxis in diversis Altitudinibus illi insinuare poterat, tali solertiâ discrevimus, ut neutrum alteri obstaculo esse potuerit, quo minus hinc veritatis inquisitio rite procederet: quantum quidem opus esset, inque sensus caderet. Nec tantus erat Cometae Anni 77. motus proprius, in Ortum et non nihil Aquilonem nitens, ut Parallaxin eam, quae vel in concavo Lunaris Sphaerae contingeret, absorbere potuerit; nedum si ea quinque fuisset Graduum; quando per Parallaxin motus Accidentarius, ê superficie saltem Terrae apparens, intra duas vel tres horas tantus evaderet, ut Cometae motum proprium anticiparet, eumque retrogradum videri faceret; quod tamen nunquam contigit: Prout haec liquido â nobis demonstrata sunt. Quae omnia sicco pede transit, nec devia, in quae delabitur, animadvertere vult hic subtilis Aristotelis discipulus. Sed haec alibi forte ex ipsius palliatâ Demonstratione, vel alia plenius refutabuntur. To the most distinguished and most excellent man, Master Christoph Rothmann, adorned with varied erudition and especially with distinguished skill in Mathematical matters, his much-beloved friend. Sends greeting.

I have received at last your letter, most erudite and most loving Rothmann, long and much before desired — both because our conferences in Astronomical Matters had meanwhile, against custom, been on holiday, and because I longed to learn something certain about you and your health, and where you were lodging (since you had not, for so long a time, returned to your Prince). But I understand from these very [letters] that you, by reason of excessive ill-health, did not return to Kassel itself before [the Landgrave's] death, [your] illness retarding [you] — [an illness] arising from the dissolutions of Tartareous salts [gout/stone], and infesting you this whole time almost without intermission. For which sinister affection of health I greatly condole with you, and that in this still-green age you are subject to such savage torments of diseases. But since you indicate that you have now somewhat breathed again, there is [reason] for me to congratulate you; and I wish that this recovery of health may daily take increase and remain constant. If my Medicines, coming forth from our Pyronomic workshop [chymical laboratory] and prepared with no small labour or expense, can be ready for you in these great evils (if, as happens, they sometime recur), I gladly offer them, and more gladly will impart [them] — just as I did not deny you my service in these [matters] when you were present. But that you did not afterward use it was done by your own fault, you who neither informed me of your adverse health, nor sought anything of the kind from me afterward by letter (though offered unasked at your departure). Whether, therefore, you yourself return hither (as you in some way undertook), or remind [me] of this matter through a sure and suitable man, you will find me by no means difficult to perform this service.

You exhort in this letter that I let our Cometographic Works come forth into public, and wonder why they lie hidden with me so long — especially since, for the greater part long completed, I have so often and so long promised I would publish them; and [since] not a few erudite men too desire a supply of them to be made for them. But you assert that no small profit will come hence for adorning the Mathematical studies, and vindicating [them] from the disparagements of the unskilled and the calumniators. This is done indeed, my Rothmann, in great part by the ingenuousness of your mind, and that sublime Love toward us and our common studies, and [your] peculiar favour; yet that no little Truth too underlies these (if it might be said without boasting and envy), you do not therein distrust. For what, and how much, can be accomplished by Mathematical exercises rightly administered, has not yet become sufficiently known, even to those philosophizing commonly in the Schools, much less to the rest of the crowd alien from the Muses. And certainly the exact measurement of Comets, and the ingenious Demonstration by what motion they are stirred and how far they are distant from us, so break those [things] which for so many centuries were brought forward by that Greek Aristotle without a settled demonstration [Apodixis], and proclaimed in all the Academies as certain and indubitable assertions, that into how great a chaos of ignorance the cultivators of Philosophy have been involved in this part, now at last, by the Aid of Divine Mathesis, becomes clear among the lovers of truth. And that the same can be done, by the supports of the same incomparable Science, in many other abstruse and hitherto unknown matters (or falsely believed for ascertained), there is no doubt — to say nothing of the several corollaries which are derived hence for more fully cultivating all the other Disciplines. But that I have not yet permitted our Work on Comets to be made public, many and grave reasons have detained me. For besides that I have sweated very much, with unwearied study, for some continuous years now, both on the Observations of those everlasting Stars coeval with the World, and on the reductions of the same to a norm conformable to the Celestial harmony (which matter, as you know, requires not a little both of time and labour), so that on that account it was less convenient to be at leisure seriously for those other [things] — many [things] besides, beyond our expectation and all foresight of removal, have for a long time now fallen out, which have not permitted me to enjoy Philosophic leisure and to devote myself to the rites of Urania as I would wish. So envious is the turbulence of this unclean World against the purer truth, even in Philosophical and visible matters of the perceptible Heaven — much less can it acquiesce, if anything be brought from the shadows of ignorance into open light, in the Divine and invisible [things] of that Archetypal World, unsearchable to human reason. There was added this too, that our Labours in accurately investigating the appearances of the Comets seen in some past years required more time, work, and expense, beyond anyone's estimation, than certain ungrateful men — and even of those who pretend a love of the Disciplines (and indeed of the Mathematical), and no small science — could acknowledge. Among whom there is one especially, who not only judged it fair to give me no thanks for so many wakeful nights, so many expended outlays, and so many Labours drained in Demonstrations and Numbers, that the inviolate truth might be drawn out, but did not blush to assail us with detractions, perversions, [and] impugnations of the disclosed certainty — nay, with cavils and jeers [scommata]. So that I recall that that most excellent and most prudent man Jacob Monau, of honourable memory (for, as I hear, three years ago he yielded to the fates), not undeservedly wrote thus to the eminent Mathematician Bartholomäus Scultetus, when, [the latter] accommodating [it], he had obtained and inspected our book on the Comet of [15]77 — as I find noted too in Scultetus's letter then given to me: "O you happy souls, whose care it was, and is today too, to climb into the upper houses! I admired more than I understood so great a diligence of Master Tycho as is noticed in this Work of his. See that you exhort him to bring forth the remaining parts too of that Work — even if this our age be not worthy of these so many and so great goods, [acknowledging them] with the gratitude that was fitting. Breslau, 17 June Old Style, in the year 1588." These [things] that excellent man, by the acumen of judgment in which he excelled, and [his] prudence in matters both Philosophical and Political, both brought forward and seemed as it were to presage.

But he who, before the rest, attempted to belittle and assail our study in the Cometary Matter, was a certain Scot by nation, a Doctor of Galenic Medicine, and beyond measure addicted to the Aristotelian Philosophy. Whom I am unwilling to name here, sparing his honour. He wishes indeed to be held noble by birth, as he insinuates of himself in some places; but yet [he is] too servilely enslaved to the said heathen Philosopher in his better part — that is, in genius and talent — especially in this business of Comets. When, by the intercession of a certain countryman of his visiting me here, he had received our second book on the Phenomena of the Aethereal World (which treats amply of that vast Comet of [15]77), as soon as it was finished by the Types, he tried, by a letter written to me, to call into doubt our assertions there comprised — especially that I showed that that Comet completed its course in the Aethereal Region of the World, and by no means the Elementary (contrary to what Aristotle taught) — and, as far as in him lay, to shake [them]. I certainly thought at the beginning that the man did this in friendly and candid fashion, for the sake of more deeply manifesting the truth. Which many other not-unlearned men too supposed with me — among whom was that Magnificent Jacob Curtius, Vice-Chancellor of the Empire, who, by a letter sent me, thought that those who contradicted our assertion in this part did not do it seriously, but only contrived [it], with good intention, that they might fish out more [things] of the kind. And he was indeed a man endowed with perspicacious talent and at the same time keen judgment, not only in Political but in Mathematical Matters too — to add nothing of that most distinguished Doctor Thaddaeus Hagecius and others thinking the same. Since, therefore, I noticed there were [those] who presumed to move a hesitation about our assertions proposed in the said book, so much so that they did not hesitate to contradict them: those indeed who, educated in the Aristotelian school, inferred — from his authority alone and the long-received opinion — that Comets are necessarily generated in the Air, [yet] proving by no Geometrical reasoning (or even under the pretext of one) that the matter stands so, I judged unworthy of refutation — since argumentation drawn from human authority is the weakest of all, and on that account deservedly to be placed last. But to this Scot, since he not only vended much against us the Aristotelian authority (though he extolled even this more than is just), but also pretended some Geometrical [reasoning], I-know-not-what, for establishing his opinion, I judged that he alone should be modestly met — partly that I might dissolve his objections, which bore a certain appearance of the true, and could easily snatch the incautious into by-ways; partly that I might more fully and lucidly explain certain [things] contained in the same second Tome, and set down further certain [things] about the Cometary Matter in a more general way: hoping that I should thus perform a thing not unwelcome to him and to other investigators of truth in this part. Wherefore, a certain extemporaneous Apology having been written among other occupations, I forthwith transmitted it into Scotland to our Antagonist, and communicated the same with certain other Erudite Men in Germany who understood these matters, that I might equally seek out their censures. In which Apology, certainly, I am not conscious to myself of having anywhere transgressed the limits of modesty, or brought forward anything beside the matter. But whether by some affect (as sometimes happens humanly) I here err, let others judge, who both know and wish to discern the true from the false without all prejudice. But how that Aristotelian Philosopher received this our candid intention (which would be acceptable to him, as I hoped) can scarcely be said. For after he had kept this friendly Apology with himself even into the third year, and written nothing of reply in so long a time, I thought either that he had acquiesced in our dissolutions and passed over to these parts, or that he held back because contradictions did not easily occur further. But at last, that deep silence being broken, he hurled at us a certain Antilogetic writing, with a somewhat more virulent and biting (as it seems) mind than he had before dealt by letter — in which he plays and mocks in manifold ways with jeers, taunts, cavils, distractions, and perversions, to elude the truth asserted by us: and acts not so much Scottishly as scoffingly [nec tam Scotice quam scoptice]. For I have had to do with many sprung from Scotland, visiting us here from time to time, distinguished both in Learning and in Birth, in whom I noticed exceptional candour, joined with equal humanity, [and] to whom singular acumen of talent and judgment too was not lacking. But [one] who would assail me and mine with so ungrateful and averse a mind, and so petulant a tongue, I have hitherto found none. Nay, certain of his own countrymen openly detested this immodest deed of his, and complained that he thus rendered their Nation liable to the judgments of foreign and saner men — since he alone did not blush to attempt such things, and, with arrogant daring, to contradict a matter so demonstrated that it was approved by all, even by those who before thought the contrary and had published [it]. And surely, if I may say what is true on my own behalf, not undeservedly did they bring this forth. For whereas other men no less excellent in Mathematics than this Antagonist (to whom in these disciplines I do not deny knowledge, if only he would use it rightly) — who had before pronounced, in their writings published, that that Comet was sublunary and had admitted a Parallax of about five degrees — having received this our book, and the matter being better perceived, not only frankly confessed that they had erred, but gave me no slight thanks because I had shown surer [things]: this one alone, although he himself had found out nothing in that Comet that would be of any moment, nonetheless strove to extenuate our laborious vigils and supreme diligence about these, and to render [them] void as far as in him lay — so much so that he took up again into his hands the data and pronouncements of others too (which the authors themselves at last, the matter better understood, repudiated and retracted), and used — or rather abused — them against us, since he himself had none.

But that you may in some part learn, excellent Rothmann, with how great injury he arrogated to himself the [right] to afflict not only me, but the very truth which we strove to investigate more deeply to the best of our power, and how scoffingly [he arrogated to himself] to assail my [things], I will briefly run through certain chief passages of his Invective (for so it deserves to be named, rather than any moderate Apology) — commending a more solid and special reply, together with a fitting refutation, to be carried out in its time by one of our Friends, or even Disciples skilled in these matters. He made for this peevish and insolent writing the title: Capnuraniae restinctio, or the Refutation of the Sublimation of Comets into the Aether [the "quenching of smoke-heaven"]. Whereas I nowhere asserted that Comets — though really Aethereal — consist of smokiness [fumositates], but rather of celestial matter, as our second book (which he attacks) could have advised him, [it] which has thus on p. 254: "I judge the matter of all Comets to be wholly celestial, since they are all generated in the heaven itself." But of the smokes which he vends, nothing. You indeed, my Rothmann, were once of the opinion that terrestrial smokinesses could enter the Heaven (if indeed it were, by your and Jean Pena the Frenchman's opinion, plainly aereal) and furnish matter there for Comets — which, however, I disapproved, and you, afterward taught otherwise from the Milky Way, retracted that very [thing], as may be seen from the Letters communicated between us. He then subjoins, in the same inscription [title], that those [things] which I bring forward for asserting the Celestial position of Comets, and [in] my Apology, are all weakened by him — where he seems not to be consistent with himself in this very front[ispiece], while now he charges me with holding Comets for Aethereal phenomena, before for celestial smokes: and he has not touched, even with the topmost lips, a tenth part of those [things] which we adduced in the second book and in the Apology for asserting the Aethereal position of Comets — much less weakened them all (as he too arrogantly boasts). Nay, those very [things] which he carps at, he does not even shake, much less overthrow or destroy. Then in the writing itself he at once belittles the observations of my Instruments before the long consensus of the ancients — which, how rightly he does, you (who beheld ours, and understand more deeply their use and this handling) can judge. Afterward he is almost wholly either scoffing, or injurious against us, distracting or perverting our [things], and not, as was fitting, rightly accommodating [them]. And, to say in few [words], he either does not at all understand them, or does not wish to understand. He says that he did not at first think I thought thus seriously about Comets, but had constructed Paradoxes for the sake of disputing — whereas our second book sufficiently shows that I do not jest in these, with so many Labours of both Observations and Demonstrations, and Numbers too, drained for that cause, which it is easier to carp at than to imitate. He says too that he by no means dealt with me so as to doubt anything of the truth of his own opinion — perhaps because, placed outside the lot of other men, he could not even doubt, much less err, believing in Aristotle (who among those [men] is "he himself said it" [ἀυτὸς ἔφα]). He says too that I accuse others as ignorant and enslaved to errors; and adds scoffingly: "Fair words, I pray!" — whereas I nowhere spoke so rigidly, but only modestly marked those who are too much enslaved to Aristotle with a certain prejudice, and showed their errors. He desires too Mathematical Demonstrations in us; and somewhere afterward dares impudently to charge that I alone affirm whatever [I please], and wish credit to be given to me and my Instruments without the necessary and Geometrical induction. How false these [things] are, you and other eminent Mathematicians, who have read ours and surveyed no small abundance of unconquered Demonstrations, can testify. He judges, besides, this matter of Comets to be so light that it needs not many disquisitions — that is, whatever Aristotle once trumpeted is subject to no further difficulty. That I do not yet perceive the truth, and here err to the detriment of Astronomy, he judges (or rather prejudges) more boldly than truly — which I am not ignorant that you understand more rightly. He calls what we wrote in our Apology a certain "much-rhetoricizing" [πολυρρητορεία], to which he is unwilling to reply. But under this pretext he passes many [things] with dry foot which he cannot tear down. He objects that I have intruded tailed Stars, strange monsters, into the Heaven — whereas Antiquity taught that there are at least round bodies there. But I asserted that the Comet's body itself is per se round, and that the adhering tail is not of its substance, but comes about per accidens, by an Optical reasoning — so that this too is rather a monstrous calumny. Soon he immodestly reproaches [me] that I attribute Celestial circuits, and Numbers deduced from a Hypothesis, to that Comet, and that I do this in imitation of Maestlin and Cornelius Gemma — whereas rather our endeavour in these ought to have merited favour among candid and discerning talents. And so far is it [from the truth] that I built those [things] of mine from Maestlin or Gemma, that I rather animadverted on their writings more strongly than this Zoilus does — but yet candidly and with good intention. And Wittich could testify, if he were still alive, that we had written our conceptions about that Comet here before Maestlin's or Cornelius's booklets had become sufficiently known to me — so much so that, whereas Wittich before had believed Comets to be wholly sublunary, and Maestlin and others in Germany who asserted the contrary to have hallucinated, convinced by my Observations and Demonstrations alone, he changed his opinion: which too, returning into Germany, he confessed before many, some of whom — and a certain Scot, this man's countryman — reported that very [thing] to me.

Afterward, that I attributed an Arc of a great circle to the Comet's motion, and, because [the Comet] observed it perpetually in every position, proved thence that it was not sublunary, he does not cease prolixly to cavil, and variously to turn and invert — nay, even to transform [it] Dialectically, perhaps to prove himself an Organic [logician]. Yet the force of the Demonstration he either does not perceive, or obscures by his sophisms. And, to give an example how an Arc of a great circle can be designated below the Moon no less than in the highest Aether, he brings forth from his crafty little brain this ludicrous and futile [example]: "Let us set out straight from Edinburgh to Uraniborg; in the whole course we shall keep, with our vertices, the track of a great Circle in the eighth orb: and so, furrowing the surface of the sea, we shall come to Uraniborg — [yet] we shall not be in Urania." An excellent likeness, forsooth, which limps not on one or another, but on all four feet! — which is to be received with guffaws, or rather to be hissed off, [rather] than dignified with any serious reply, as you and others skilled in these Matters sufficiently understand, since it of itself opposes our intention in nothing. Afterward, by a certain specious and counterfeit Demonstration, he strives to show that Comets — or (as he scoffingly speaks) my Pseudoplanets — can describe a great Circle and nonetheless dwell in the Air far below the Moon, so that they admit a Parallax of five degrees. And this he labours to infer chiefly from its proper motion, which exhausts the Parallax. But here that Pseudophilosopher labours in vain, even had he sworn a thousand times by Aristotle. For we discriminated the Comet's proper motion from that variation which Parallax could insinuate to it at different Altitudes with such skill that neither could be an obstacle to the other, so that the inquiry of truth might rightly proceed hence — as much, indeed, as was needful and fell under the senses. Nor was the proper motion of the Comet of [15]77 (striving toward the East and somewhat the North) so great that it could absorb that Parallax which would occur even in the concave of the Lunar Sphere — much less had it been of five degrees; since by Parallax the Accidental motion, appearing from the surface of the Earth alone, would within two or three hours come out so great as to anticipate the Comet's proper motion and make it seem retrograde — which, however, never happened, as these [things] have been plainly demonstrated by us. All which he passes with dry foot, nor does this subtle disciple of Aristotle wish to notice the by-ways into which he slips. But these will perhaps be more fully refuted elsewhere, from his cloaked Demonstration, or otherwise. And although it be true that those phenomena which are so near us that the semidiameter of the Earth, compared to that distance, comes out sensible, are involved in a threefold motion — namely the universal, the proper, and that which comes about per accidens from Parallax — yet so to separate these, that one does not hinder the other but rather opens [it], is [the mark] of a circumspect Mathematician; nor, with peculiar diligence applied, is it so impossible as this Aristotelian tries in vain to persuade the incautious. For we accomplished that sufficiently, both in the said Comet and in the remaining following [ones] — let him chatter against [it] as much as he will. Let you and others judge these [things] more deeply, weighing [them] without prejudice. Nay, he even doubts whether it can be done in the Moon, if it be presupposed that its true motion is unknown, so that the Parallax may nonetheless be unravelled; and he provokes me to accomplish it. But I judge that this stupidity is not even to be answered, since some one of my recruits [Tyrones], who is accustomed to handle Celestial [matters] not only in books but in the Heaven itself by experience, would resolve this knot not difficultly — which too has very often been done. What will he say of a still fourth divagation of the apparent place, unknown to him, which is suggested optically from the refractions — and that not only in Comets and the Moon, but in all other Celestial bodies, when they especially incline toward the Horizon, and that sensibly more and more below the twentieth degree of Altitude, until at the very Horizon it comes out greatest — as these are sufficiently perceived by you, my Rothmann, from the Landgravian Instruments, [but] which never once came into the mind of this Aristotelian? And although this divarication of refractions lies open to sure knowledge far more difficultly than the other three commixtions of motions and their separations from one another, yet long experimentation, variously sought and confirmed, has taught us both to have this explored and to discern how much it adds to or takes from the rest — so far is it [from the truth] that we cannot conveniently unravel those other variations, which make less trouble.

Perhaps he deems those [things] which seem impossible to himself and those like him — who exercise Astronomy only on papers and in vain speculations — by no means accessible to others too, who handle it solidly and truly, and deduce [it] from the Heaven by settled experience (as is fitting). Nay, even in those argumentations which he afterward weaves to these, he does not consider that the Comet of [15]77 observed exactly the same track of a great Circle at different Altitudes — which could by no means come about if it had been subject to a very sensible Parallax, or lower than the Moon. Nor is there a just place for contradiction, even if all the Aristotelian Capnocometists [smoke-comet men] should burst. Soon he proceeds to play with jeers in his manner, and says that I establish Comets to be Logical animals, nay, that I discourse of them Logically; and he is not affected with shame to summon hither, Ironically, the author of the Predicables [Porphyry], lest he say nothing. If he had been instructed by plausible conjectures from that truer Philosophy of the Platonists rather than of the Stagirite (who acted the apostate from it), he would surely have perceived both what I had said, and that I had said [things] not so dissonant as he thinks, more rightly, and would perhaps have given me thanks in place of sneers. But, that he may propose demonstratively — or (which is truer) impose — by what reasoning [Comets] can designate an Arc of a great Circle even while dwelling in the sublunary Air, and that he may persuade [us] this comes about from the force of matter, he turns himself into various forms after the manner of Prometheus [Proteus], and intricates this business more than he unravels [it]. And since other laws of motions do not suffice, he calls to aid unsearchable and fictitious Helices [spirals], and feigns and refeigns whatever here, to force the Comets to go such ways as Nature did not attribute to them, nor did any experience find out, in anything, to come about from the Heaven. Nay, neither does such a circumrotation — observing fixed modes and bounds by an ordinary law, so that it can persevere for a long time — belong to fiery and inflamed matter, such as his teacher Aristotle wishes [it] to be. If it were thus permitted to feign at pleasure any motions whatever for Comets (so that some phantastic assumption, most alien from truth, might be saved), surely we shall teach that the birds in the air fly otherwise than the Natural and voluntary impulse bears; and we shall prescribe to them laws why they flit thus and not otherwise, and feign composite, bent, and reflected, ascending and descending Helices, inclined whithersoever it please: and yet the birds will fly away whither they will, and elude our speculation. But these absurdities, which deceive the unskilled, will be unfolded further elsewhere. Furthermore, to prove himself still more a Dialectician, he knits and looses my [things] Sophistically, as his futile rather than subtle quibbling persuades, and ill accommodates my inductions. And, lest he seem unlike himself, he receives our labour in Triangles resolved by Numbers — that not slight or contemptible [labour] — with a certain presumptuous jeer, saying that "his stomach has long since nauseated at this inanity." O dainty stomach, to be cured with Hellebore, that all the insipid matter of nausea may hence be removed, and his smoke-brain [κάπνἐγκέφαλος] cured at the same time! Nor does he leave uncensured [the fact] that I plainly demonstrated, from Observations through its whole duration, that the Comet by its course retained perpetually the same inclination to the Ecliptic — tacitly objecting certain few Minutes, which nowhere exceeded a twelfth part of a Degree — whereas the ancient Astronomers were content to designate the Stars coeval with the World within a sixth part of a Degree. And certainly, in the variation of that Angle of which we spoke, a difference of three or four Minutes is neither of itself sensible enough nor introduces anything of moment. He laughs too at Maestlin (whom by contempt he calls my Parson [Parochus]) — though I have never seen him, nor contracted any acquaintance with him, not even by letter — on account of his Animadversions made by a thread [the cross-thread sight], than which, however, this Galenic Doctor has brought none better; and he sneers at his corollary assumption in a hypothesis — which, although we do not defend, yet so licentiously to carp at others' ingenious inventions, and to teach nothing that is better, is not [the mark] of a moderate and prudent talent. Nor does he blush to call into doubt our Observations [made] by infallible Instruments (as you, who beheld them and experienced their consonant certainty, will easily testify). Nay, he insolently boasts of this hardened audacity, and reproaches [us] with these words: "Here you will be astounded that your Observations are called into doubt, which you wish to be infallible and to be held for the most certain principles." But I am not astounded for this, that he is so stupid and immodest as to presume to pass judgment on a matter unknown [to him], and to shrink from them like an Owl from the Sun's brightness. He has many others like himself on this Mundane stage, who, dim-sighted toward the light of truth and clamouring against it, give credit rather to lies. But that he may bring something whereby to render my diligence in making Observations suspect, that which I had advised in the Apology and elsewhere — that the most exact moments of time cannot be sufficiently established from the Altitudes of the Sun or stars — he cavils at so insipidly and sportively that it is shameful to report. And here he shows how subtle and diligent a scrutator of the Stars he is, since he cannot even understand others' circumspect Animadversions in these, nor recognize the inner chambers of truth in them — much less receive [them] with grateful mind. He ignorantly croaks that the places of the stars restored by me are uncertain, unless others have thought the same — depending, in his manner, on the authority of others. He insipidly laughs too that the Sun's course was restored by me in a certain new Reformation of the World, rendering me, out of his Philosophical humanity, thanks for the restored course of the Sun, on which the other motions of the Stars depend. He blurts too, from excessive ignorance, that Refraction, and the slowness of the change of Altitude about the Meridian, and its disparity in others (as to the equal circuit of the Equator), are nothing. And, that he may render suspect and doubtful my method (subject to no ambiguity) by which I taught that Times are investigated more exactly and surely through a Mechanical Equator, analogous to the Celestial, than from the Altitudes of stars, hear, I pray, with what rash words and how great unskillfulness he prates about these: "But, I pray, which do you follow? You offer us a Mechanical Equator, whether revolvable or fixed, Analogous to the Equator in its position, and resting on stone or iron columns. These [things] said, you ask whether I doubt that you can find the smallest particles of Time precisely. I am not wont to play Oedipus: why do you suppress the rest? Why do you not bring forward the whole method, which you vaunt as most certain, that it may be known what of truth it has? Your fixed Equator — how will it exhibit the number of the revolution of the Equator, by which time is defined, unless it be moved from its Tetrapod [four-footed stand]? The movable [one] — by what mover will it be turned around Analogously to the same? If you give a Logicon [a rational mover], how will it understand that it moves to the Celestial analogy? If an Alogon [irrational one], whether it act by weight or by another force, I will easily demonstrate in it an anomaly discrepant from the Celestial analogy." Thus that good man brings forth a ridiculous censure about my Instruments and observing-methods, unperceived by him: so that, as Maro [Virgil] said of Sinon, "From one learn all" (namely the Greeks), so too from these [things] brought forward you may easily estimate of what kind and worth the rest are. He could surely have learned beforehand, from his countrymen frequently visiting me here, whether my Instruments were Logical (i.e. endowed with reason) or not, or whether they were otherwise so elaborated Mechanically that they satisfy our reasoning conveniently and without obstacles — lest he thus seem to rave without reason, and in the very word [λόγῳ] betray himself to be irrational [alogus].

Next, because in taking Distances I am content with clocks showing Minutes (yet so that they be rectified from time to time, if they have contracted any tiny fault, by the Equatorial distances of the Stars or Sun from the Meridian), he cavils that I reject the times taken from others' clocks, and yet myself flee hither; nay, he objects that I bring forth the little portions of Time adverbially, by no means Mathematically, because, in recounting the distances of the Comet of [15]77 from certain fixed [stars], I do not everywhere set down the very moments of the hours, but sometimes say "about that time," or "a little after," and the like. O subtle wrangler, and empty scrutator of the moments of Time! Inspect, my Rothmann, the first chapter of that book on the Comet transmitted to you, and you will see that I noted there the differences of the hours in [their] particles precisely enough, as much as was needful in investigating the Distances — so much so that, if I had applied greater scrupulousness, I could rather have moved a scruple in the prudent. Would not that subtlety be rather a certain futility, and a superfluous affectation? For when the intervals of the Comet from the Stars were diligently taken, that had to be attempted twice or thrice, that they might agree in the very Minute — which indeed is scarcely conveniently accomplished within a sixth or eighth part of an hour. It sufficed, therefore, to assign to the intermediate Observation a fraction of an hour as near as possible — yet so that nowhere is a divagation of a sixth of one hour committed. For the Comet's motion was not sensibly altered within a sixth portion of an hour, so that on that account [to assign] those moments, as to the distances, by a subtler search than was accomplished by us, would have been plainly idle. So that here this contradictor seems to seek a knot in a bulrush [a difficulty where there is none], which he often does elsewhere too. But where I inquire the Parallaxes of the Comet from the Azimuths and Altitudes, there I did not fail to note not only the first minutes but a fraction of them too, in seconds — because in this place such precision is necessarily needful, but by no means in the distances. And [the fact] that I reprehended in others that they used clocks erroneously and in too broad a manner — that was done about this very practice [of parallax], not as to the designation of distances — which, if they had noted rightly even within a quarter of one hour, I should certainly have condoned them, though the indication were general. See, therefore, how candidly and sincerely that Aristotelian deals with me, while he draws my [things] elsewhere and into diverse [senses], and ill accommodates [them], and seeks subtlety where there is no need, but spurns [it] where indicated and found in the fitting place: which is the manner of those who, with preoccupied mind, try to elude the truth and hold [it] captive. So too, [the fact] that in the examination of the Comet's places by Triangles a discrepancy of 2 or 3 Minutes sometimes falls, he argues that I did not rightly designate its track — with arguteness, forsooth, of vanity: he who ought rather to have admired so near a coincidence than too rigidly to exaggerate that which scarcely falls under the senses, nor is of any weight among candid and intelligent appraisers. Besides, he infers that the Comet's Parallax should have been investigated not from Distances, but only from Altitudes and Azimuths — of which, however, we accomplished both. But, even with him unaware and dim-sighted, the method by Distances, duly applied, is surer than that which is taken from Altitudes and Azimuths, in which a scarcely sensible error comes out very fertile [of consequences] — as these are sufficiently ascertained by you, who handled them, and who have explored how easily one errs in Altitudes and Azimuths, but not so in distances. But this Paper Astronomer [Chartaceus Astronomus] speaks of these and the like almost everywhere as a blind man of colours — and yet so greatly pleases himself and his. And at last he concludes that my Observations are not to be trusted. But even by this single negative he could have answered everything, by his hardened immodesty, and so would not be judged worthy of refutation — since against one who denies the principles there is no disputing. For the Observations are here held for principles, which anyone may rashly deny — but that it is not so is not as quickly proved by anyone. What if we should reply that this is a most putrid calumny and an immodest presumption? Which of us is to be believed? — I, who in the most exact denotations of so many other Stars, and indeed of a thousand fixed [stars], have, with greater precision than [by] any of the ancients or recent [astronomers] (let the boast be absent), accomplished very many observations now for some continuous years, and demonstrated that they exactly correspond to the truth (the whole Heaven still speaking and bearing testimony for me) — or he, who never learned to handle such [things] with the accuracy that is needful, nor has hitherto brought forth any specimen of it? Nay, even if he most greatly wished, that (as he persuades himself) would by no means be obvious; and as war is sweet to the inexperienced, so too the observation of the Stars seems easy to the unexercised. But he, from a certain levity, arrogates to himself an empty censure, to be valued at a straw, about ours never seen or understood.

Then he calumniates that I receive Wittich, enjoying blessed rest, indecently and undeservedly. But I deprived that most learned [man] in Mathematics, Wittich, neither living nor dead, of his honours; but the lapses by which he sometimes [erred] in these studies [when] executed from the Heaven (since he was not accustomed to them), I sometimes modestly indicated, for the sake of disclosing the truth. Which I did to him too, often, when present here; nor was that ungrateful to him, as [it is] to this unwarlike champion of his. Where Wittich was worthy of praise — namely in Geometrical [matters], and certain compendia of Triangles — I praised [him], nor ever undeservedly detracted anything from him. But what he chatters too boldly, not only about this [man] but about that supreme man Andreas Dudith [Dudithius], I am unwilling to rejoin. Dudith's own Letter, prefixed to Thaddaeus's [Hájek's] booklet on the Comet of [15]90, sufficiently shows how falsely those [things] are brought forward. Soon he rages, by his rash license, against that excellent man Michael Maestlin; and what I truly advised about the Observation by a thread used by him (when he had no other Instruments at hand), and also what I indicated about the doubts that commonly creep into Instruments, he, perverting and distracting [it] into another sense, in his manner gnaws with biting tongue. And at last he dignifies Maestlin and others — who led the truth, long buried in more-than-Cimmerian darkness in this Cometary business, into open light — with this elogium: "Certainly I know not what they do not deserve, who befoul the sciences" — perhaps judging, by his choleric indignation, that they ought to be brought before Judges and publicly animadverted on, because they do not trust Aristotle. Thereafter, after he had extenuated my Instruments and their use, showing that he dares to utter anything without shame, he on the contrary boasts of his own former skill in handling Instruments (such as he once had in Germany), which had proper sights, but not, [like] mine, slitted [rimosa] — superbly and scoffingly (as is his wont) sneering. But why does he not have such [instruments] at hand in Scotland too? Let him produce even one true and useful Observation which he ever accomplished, whether in Germany or Scotland, on any fixed or wandering star, or the Sun or Moon. I scarcely believe [it]. And yet he presumes, in his sloth, to belittle others' unwearied and exquisite labours. He laughs, indeed, at the slits of my sights, parallel to the Cylinder placed at the Centre, and receives [them] with a slitted jeer — but does not see their necessary and most convenient use, which you, my Rothmann, know more rightly; since you always made the greatest [account] of [the thing] first seen here from that same Wittich (whom this Scot so greatly extols) and once communicated to your Prince — nay, you competently restored the other little slit too, neglected by him too securely. Nay, Wittich himself, when he had learned this compendium here from us, said joyfully that he had now at last seen and experienced what had long been sought in vain by Dudith and by himself, and that he had not come into Denmark in vain, even on this account; nay, that he would not exchange this knowledge for the gain of a hundred crowns. But let this Aristotelian snarl and insult with jeers as much as he will — the matter is of itself manifest; which, that he might understand more deeply, he makes himself unworthy. Afterward he adds — or rather objects — certain [things] about Scultetus: that I said [things] dissonant from myself, while sometimes I approve his inventions, sometimes disapprove [them]. But this is not to differ from oneself — to praise what is praiseworthy, and to disapprove, in its place, what fits less. For both can happen to the same author; wherefore both [may] be admitted in the same. Lastly he brings forth, inelegantly, certain [things] about the Landgravian observations collated with ours and others'; nor does he heed how little consonant with truth he speaks. And he closes all these, in place of a Colophon, with this lofty and itching speech: "No diverticulum [by-path] was to be left on so high a track, by which you could deviate from the truth of your Urania. Return into grace with her, and into the right way." Let that dear Priest of Urania, then, intercede for me, that I may return into grace with her — since he presumes himself more rightly known and accepted to her, as one who for so many years has sacrificed to her more conveniently and more devotedly than we perhaps have done.

Afterward, [the fact] that I asserted that the Comet, [moving] not with a tumultuary and disordered motion but with a certain regular [one] gradually ceasing, displayed the Nature by no means of an Elementary but rather of a Celestial Phenomenon — he, because it at last vanished, tries to infer that it was necessarily Elementary. But this is what, among other [things], we chiefly intend to show — that the Heaven sometimes receives new bodies, subject to alteration — which has hitherto not been believed, on account of Aristotle's authority. But since unconquered experience gainsays [it], let such authority depart — [authority] founded only on plausible and conjectural, not Apodictic, reasonings, and therefore less consonant with truth. And when I had adduced a certain similitude from Metals, that I might somewhat explain, from the Nature of these Terrestrial things, what I wished to hint about the celestial — not that I built any sure proof thence (since similitudes do not sustain that very [thing]) — he, on the contrary, attacks it with many words, and those Sophistical and alien from candour. And, lest he think with us, against the faith given to Aristotle, he sins, departing from him (who affirms certain [things] similar to us about the generation of Metals), and denying that all metals consist of one and the same prima materia — so much so that he even contends that Mercury is not in gold. And he frivolously boasts many [things] about these — perhaps to display himself, by his arrogant talent, as not inexperienced in Chymistry and the Nature of Metals; in which, however, he is equally as little versed as in Astronomical [matters]. For of both he vends words for things. But these will perhaps be more specially refuted elsewhere. But lest that New Star by Cassiopeia stand in the way of him and his fellow-Aristotelians — so that they may not persistently assert, even against experience itself and against Geometrical Demonstrations, that the Heaven does not admit new bodies subject to dissolution — he strives to call into doubt that very Star, and all the truth about its appearances, by such cunning subterfuges, that he thus sufficiently shows how little he is a lover of truth, and how he does nothing else than overthrow this by specious evasions: which ought to have been alien from an upright and sincere man. But it is shameful to report what he here, with the greatest immodesty, does not fear to spew out against the indubitable experiments of many excellent Mathematicians, labouring to abolish all certainty from the variety of opinions. By which argumentation, surely, almost nothing certain will any longer remain, since there is scarcely anything about which men's opinions do not vary. But a distinction must be made between those who discourse according to experience drawn from sure reasonings and Demonstrations, and those who do not: and in these [matters] opinions are not to be numbered, but weighed. Nay, he does not spare even his half-countrymen, that he may make plain the love with which he follows certain Erudite men in England. He falsely reproaches John Dee, a most excellent Mathematician and Philosopher (whom you knew, when he was honourably received by the Landgrave), that in his Nucleus Parallaticus he takes his beginning within the bosom of a half-degree. He scoffingly receives, in those same words, the Wings [Alae] of Thomas Digges — [which], like a falcon, attain immobile Phenomena by a remote flight. From all which Dirges he at last concludes thus, so as to say (or rather feign) everything in one word: "That marvellous Phenomenon so eluded all the Mathematicians of that time that, unless they recognize and recook [it], they cannot then say that they have demonstrated what you say." And since he seems not even thus to have satisfied himself, he at last flees, as to a sacred anchor, to a certain adage of his Teacher: "One swallow does not make spring" — so that he may impose on us [the notion] that the force of proof lurks even in Aristotelian taunts. Let it be, indeed, that one swallow does not make spring, and that such [sayings] have place in similar [cases]: but one star once seen in the highest Heaven, which afterward ceased to be, necessarily proves that the Heaven sometimes admits new bodies subject to perishing — so much so that Aristotle himself, if surviving, had he noticed that new Star to have shone for us from the highest Aether, and at last, after a year or a little more, to have ceased to be beheld, would not have dared even to mutter against so evident an experience, but rather would have totally corrected hence his manner of philosophizing about the Nature of the Celestials — which many other eminent Aristotelians, taught hence, afterward did; this Scot almost alone still wallowing and persevering in the mire of an error once snatched up and confirmed; who reckons it sport to despise even the authority and experience of that ancient Hipparchus, while he says thus: "Nor is a Plinian narration the Demonstration of another" — as if either Pliny said false [things] about Hipparchus, or [Hipparchus] could not discern, among the old Stars (almost all of which he measured with his Instruments), some new one born in the Heaven in his times. Let these insolences depart. But because in our first Tome of the Astronomical Progymnasmata we have treated of this star amply and professedly, and most plainly demonstrated that it stood in the highest Aether for over a year and at last disappeared, so that there is no place for evasion — and at the same time abundantly disquired others' opinions for and against — so that thence it appears more than sufficiently how absurd and insipid, and most alien from truth itself, are the [things] which this Aristotelian here heaps up about that star; and thence, in the rest too, how truthful he is, one may know, as the Lion from the claw [ex ungue Leonem] — therefore I do not judge it worthwhile to refute these at greater length, and to demonstrate their insufficiency.

Nay, while he afterward raves variously about Comets, and seems to attack you too, while he says Ironically that they are even carried above Saturn; and [while] he addresses me — because I said we are taught, from a Nature of the Celestials other than Aristotle thought, that the Heaven is not eternal or a partaker of Divinity (as he wished), but also sometime subject to dissolution, [and] that the Creator admonishes us in these ascititious bodies of the Heaven, which perish, as it were by a certain example — this our Scourge [Mastix] addresses [me] no less scoffingly, saying that I capture great consolation hence, namely that I so establish [it]; and soon, gnawing in his manner, he further adds, not abstaining even from that too-vulgar [syllogism]: "The staff stands in the corner; therefore the Pontiff is at Rome." He calls too, on this occasion, the Comets (which it pleased me to call certain Pseudoplanets by Philosophical liberty) my Pseudoprophets; and he superfluously adduces many other [things] for Aristotle — that the World is by its own Nature eternal and incorruptible, but will have an end only by the will of God. About which I will add nothing, since they make little for the matter in question, but are rather certain pompous ostentations of unexplained disputations. Then, returning to the Comets and their consumption, he feigns many [things] too boldly, which neither belonged to the Comet of [15]77 (of which chiefly [we] treat) nor were anywhere attributed to it by us, but plainly the contrary [was] shown. And because I said that that Comet was slower in its proper motion than the Moon, and therefore stood higher — [a thing] not lacking probability, though I wove in no necessity here — he scarcely finds enough sneers with which to assail [it]. He says nothing prevents [the Comet] from flying off in this way to the throne of Jupiter, like another Icaromenippus; nay, he summons too Lucian's fables of the broken Bridge of Asses — so as to betray that he too greatly esteems Lucian's illusions, and savours his nature. And because I had indicated in passing, as it were, that the Comet directed its journey from the Tropic of Cancer to the Tropic of Capricorn, and contained itself within these bounds (by which the Sun's orbit too is enclosed), and therefore insinuated something hidden, tacitly analogous to the Celestials — he, sneering, mocks thus: "Whether, by its [own] intelligence, fleeing the cold of the former (namely Capricorn), it wished to proceed to the hearth of the latter (namely Cancer)." But let these jeers be — which argue nothing but the author's vanity and levity. Afterward, presuming to tear down my Mathematical reasonings, he enumerates indeed by what method I investigated the Comet's Parallax, that he may display himself as somehow understanding ours; but he weakens these there by no contrary reasons, except that he calls [it] a leaden norm by his affected authority, and objects, by a vain scruple, that the difference of longitude between Kassel and this place is doubtful in some minutes, and rejects the Landgravian Observations — not all, but certain selected ones used by me — and says that others, which we neglected, would by his arbitration have been truer; and heaps up things like these, little proving, nor of themselves wholly true, and making nothing for the matter. If from any Landgravian Observations (which I, in the second book, frankly noted as they had been communicated to me) he could have proved that that Comet was sublime [sublunary], there is no doubt that he would have done that very [thing], as far as in him lay. But because no such [observation] was obvious, he, at pleasure, involves certain and settled [things] in doubts, and strives to obscure [them]. I selected the Landgravian observations not by arbitration, so as to neglect the surer ones, but preferred to use those which were most distant in time, so that the differences of Parallaxes, which are thus increased, might the more readily shine forth thence.

Nay, he inveighs licentiously against you too, most learned Rothmann, because you asserted that the Comet of 1585 was situated in the higher Heaven. But that you may know how he does it, behold his words: "But I come to your artifice. For not only you, but he too — that Christoph Rothmann, the Landgravian Mathematician, so greatly commended by you for [his] science of demonstrating and skill of observing — uses the same Method, by your report, while he pursues the Comet of the eighty-fifth year above Saturn, into the space nearest below the fixed [stars]; with marvellous sagacity, certainly. And he too will be held ignorant of Mathematics who doubts that you have demonstrated that your Comet completed its course in the Aether. This censure you so value that you oppose [it] to the immature opinions of contradictors. Your [judgment], most distinguished Master Tycho, I am, if anyone, about to commend for friendship, and now I would [wish to]: but I am not wont to flatter a Friend against the truth. I should wish the Observations, if not the demonstrations, of that Artificer communicated to me, by which he asserted this Paradox; and whether his skill or science reaches so far, you yourself would be the judge. If, for the defence of your Urania, you wish the truth manifested, communicate the Observations," etc. To these reply yourself for yourself, since you do not need my help; nor is it difficult, since the truth stands on our side, not to be moved by six hundred illusions of this kind. I neither wished nor wish to communicate to him your Observations, and the Demonstrations resting on these — since he strives only not to use but to abuse them, by perverting, distracting, and cavilling (as he does with mine). And to what end would it be needful to impart such [things] to him, since he presumes to pass a censure even on those not seen, much less understood — as becomes good (forsooth) judges? O insipid arrogance!

But afterward, returning to himself, and as it were having got some secret counsel from the Delphic Tripod by which to impugn my Mathematical reasonings about the Comet's Parallaxes, and by the same labour to attack you too (since I had signified that you, by a certain similar induction to that which we too used, had found that the Comet of 1585 had no Parallax at all), he is not ashamed to say that the motion which we call diurnal is only apparent, and by no means true; and therefore that all its subsumed parts too are similarly apparent. But here that perspicacious Critic of others' labours — who, however, knows not how to use the due spectacles — does not attend [to the fact] that the place of the Comet, which is observed at some certain Altitude, although it be apparent and subject to Parallax (if it have any), is nonetheless, the same universal motion being revolved, until it returns again to the same position and the same height above the horizon, then indeed observed apparently. But since the Parallax on both sides is entirely the same, as much as falls under the senses, therefore this too on both sides equally adds to or takes from the true motion. Since, therefore, what is equal is on both sides added or taken away, what remains will necessarily be judged equal to the former, even by the very first Geometrical axioms. From which it follows that, as much as the Comet, observed twice at the same Altitude from the surface of the Earth, appears apparently advanced by one diurnal revolution, just so much has it been truly varied by its [own] motion, which proceeds from the Centre; and therefore the difference of the true and apparent motion, since in such a position they are equated to one another, [means] the apparent does not differ from the true. But in other arcs, which do not have a whole circuit and are taken at different Altitudes, the matter comes out far otherwise. For there the differences of Parallaxes are not equated to one another; but the nearer the phenomenon is to the Earth, the greater the inconvenience [that] falls — from which too the distance from us can be established. But these and the like that acute and arguing Doctor could not see. Nor, if he used several eye-salves [collyria], can he discern [them], on account of the blindness by which he is hindered through the Aristotelian tenets. And therefore he speaks only by authority (which is none), and mixes in here I-know-not-what about the Hypotheses of mean and unequal motions, making plainly nothing for this matter — so that he thus shows that he did not understand ours, much less can judge of them. And therefore he closes this his censure with a certain arrogant prejudice thus: "So that neither you nor Rothmann can here pretend or persuade any demonstration among those skilled in Mathematics. Which I wished sincerely to indicate to you." Here that [phrase] used by him at the beginning would deservedly be subjoined: "Fair words, I pray!" But let him hearken to this too — that nothing is so well said but that it can be depraved by calumny.

Next, variously cutting up our reasonings (by which I amply prove that the Comet introduced a smaller diversity [parallax] than the Moon itself), and abusing them at pleasure, he assails [them], and calls into doubt that which from the foregoing was sufficiently clear to candid Mathematicians and needed no further Demonstration, that he may have [something] to cavil at. But all these will perhaps in their place and time be beaten back upon him — as also the rest subjoined to these in place of a corollary; and what he adds besides against our third proof: and that by the same right as the rest — that is, by a certain blindness of mind and preoccupied judgment, and not without affected presumption — he heaps up here and there craftily and falsely [things] by which he can not difficultly entangle and deceive the incautious. But by those who understand these more deeply, and weigh [them] with a more solid judgment, and that candid and sincere, he will be judged, by saying many [things], to have said nothing. Nay, he afterward instructs me how this third reasoning, which proceeds from diverse places, can be corrected. But since he can so finely instruct others — more rightly perhaps, and longer versed in this Palaestra — and free [them] from errors, why does he not himself set his hand to the Work, and bring forth something surer (if it could be done)? Forsooth, it is easier to say than to do, to speculate than to know, to censure than to imitate. This judicious censor seems to be ignorant that many [things] can be devised speculatively and Theoretically which admit a Geometrical Demonstration too, but are not on that account brought into practice and exercised Mechanically with fruit. So too what he subjoins about the Longitudes of certain places are trifles, proceeding from a malevolent, not a candid, mind. At last, too, against our last proof from Altitudes and Azimuths (Declination being applied at the same time), he sharpens his pen with a license similar to before, and a blind impulse of mind, drawing our [things] into by-ways and using them perversely — so much so that he does not leave uncensured even that diligence which I applied about reducing the Regiomontanus reasoning into use, as far as could be done; and he heaps up many other [things], from mere self-love [philautia] and the lust of contradicting (to which he is accustomed in the Aristotelian and Scotistic schools), which it irks and shames [me] to report or rejoin here singly. Nay, he comes at last to such madness or malevolence that he does not hesitate to charge that I thus profane my name, where I most greatly would wish to consecrate [it]. Which if it were true, surely you, in this very Letter, say in vain that I shall not a little illustrate the Mathematical studies, and consecrate [them] to posterity, if, namely, I publish our Work on Comets. But to refute all these in due manner will be [a matter] of another business; for it would be too prolix here. The time will perhaps come when the more sensible will perceive that this Aristotelian applauded himself too much, and sang the triumph before the victory — however much he be either unable or unwilling to open his eyes to the light of truth. The last clause of this scoffing and arrogant writing — that the last may answer to the first and the middle — runs thus: "These [things] it pleased [me] to write, for the sake of truth and friendship, against your smokinesses." But how consonant with truth [the things] he has brought are, let it be the judgment of you and of others understanding these more rightly. Which too will some time (as I said) appear more fully.

With what friendship he did it — if my censure about those [things] which concern me myself be suspect — let us hear what that excellent [man], endowed with ingenuous candour, Thaddaeus Hagecius wrote to me about this very Capnurania (transmitted both to him and to Lord Curtius, Vice-Chancellor of the Empire, most skilled in Mathematics, that they might inquire into it) — which [things] too were once read aloud to this Antagonist in Scotland, from Thaddaeus's own autograph, not without his unbridled fretting: "Today, which is the last day of November of the year 1592, New Style, when I had received this last [letter] of yours, written 28 September, from the Magdeburg courier, together with that writing — bitter indeed — of that haughty, hardened, biting, and unsound Antagonist of yours, on the next day I gave both your Letter and that writing to Lord Curtius to be read. He first bids you very much farewell [greets you], and [bids] it be signified to you that that writing of the Scot rests on no firm reasons, [and] wishes utterly to abolish the whole doctrine of Parallaxes by certain phantasms of his, [and] is a hostile, not a friendly writing: and so he judges it must altogether be refuted by you. And a little after he subjoins: While I write this, that Scot — called the subtle Doctor — came into my mind: what the Writers report of him, that he was stabbed through with [stylus-]pens [styli pugillares]. I should judge that this Scottish Antagonist of yours — because he desires to overthrow, by his sophisms, the admirable and splendid part of Astronomy concerning Parallaxes — ought much more justly to be subdued and stabbed through by all the students of Astronomy than that [other Scotus]. This you alone, to whom neither faculty nor will is lacking, will be able most happily to accomplish with [your] pen." Thus far Thaddaeus.

He says soon that he wrote those [things] cursorily [carptim], on which he sweated perhaps with great labour over two years. If, therefore, for "cursorily" he had said "by carping" [carpendo], he would surely have brought forth truer [things]. Nay, he calls my conceptions about Comets my smokinesses, not ceasing to vend smokes for things, and impudently to calumniate and blurt jeers — whereas I nowhere (as I protested even at the beginning) judged that Comets consist of any smokinesses; but he, with his Aristotle and his followers, so opines rather than knows. Wherefore let him have his smokes and smokinesses with himself, and not obtrude [them] on others (however vendible they stand for sale with him) who are unwilling to buy such levities. At the end, finally, to excuse cunningly the delays of more than two years in completing this Apologetic writing (for I first received it in the year 1592 about the end of May, whereas our Apology had been transmitted to him in the year 1589), he subscribes that it was completed on the day on which the Sun, in the year [15]91, was obscured to nearly 2½ digits. O bald excuse, pretended even from the Eclipsed Sun itself! And O observer, who could notice in the Sun's eclipse [only] that which no one of the rudest crowd, unless plainly blind, could fail to discern — namely, what part of it was covered! Where [are] the other appearances? Where the exact moments of time? But these do not lie open to Spermologi [gossip-mongers / chatterers]; there is need of doing [the work]. But would that he, with his digits [fingers/eclipse-digits], when he wrote those [things], had striven (as far as in him lay) to obscure the light of truth no more than the Sun's light suffered any loss from those two-and-a-half digits withdrawn from our sight by the Moon's intervention. But let that [man] fare well, with his cunnings and jeers, [and his] infirm infirmations — in reviewing which I have perhaps spent more time than they merit, and than the laws of a familiar Letter permit. It will therefore be yours, my Rothmann, to take this prolixity in good part. But if you desire the whole of that Scot's writing (since it sometimes touches you too), I will see that you obtain it by a convenient opportunity. Then you yourself will perceive more fully that, turning himself into various forms, he cannot storm the truth which he assails, and that he shatters himself against the solid, deluded by his own art. But a competent and full refutation of the same the Lovers of truth will perhaps obtain in its time — if not from me myself, at least from one of mine. For I do not gladly contend with such quarrelsome and arrogant talents, who strive to seek praise for themselves from contradiction alone, nor have the inmost chambers of truth (unsearchable to them) for [their] mark. But I have written about this matter somewhat more copiously to you, most learned Rothmann — both because that invective attacked you too, and because I know you will greatly wonder that there is anywhere any of the Mathematicians who would dare in future to assert that Comets are sublunary, [when] so evident a Demonstration to the contrary has been disclosed. For I remember that you said to me, when you were here, that such [men], if any anywhere came forth, must be held for insipid and stupid, and received rather with laughter than with reply. Let these, then, now be said about those [matters], more than sufficiently.

But one or another [thing] I will add before I conclude this. To your Letter, which you handed me here in person — in which you laboured to build the motion of the Earth, both annual and diurnal, from Copernicus's opinion — I replied [with] some [things] long since, which I think will satisfy you on that account, that, when you were with me, you seemed not to defend that opinion so tooth-and-nail, but gradually to pass over to our parts. But there is, besides, a great argument of the infirmity of the Copernican assertions, that the Eccentricity of Mars by no means stands as they require. For whereas he affirmed that he found the Eccentricity of Mars somewhat smaller than Ptolemy's denotation bears, and hence did not hesitate to build the rest of the Sun and the motion of the Earth, and its variable eccentricity, so much so that he apparently diminished the Eccentricity of Mars: so far is it [from the truth] that the matter stands thus, that the Eccentricity of Mars has been found by us, by least-doubtful experimentations, nearly the same as Ptolemy attributed to it, or rather somewhat greater. By no means, therefore, rendered smaller, does it support the Copernican tenets — which surely moves no little hesitation that his Hypotheses stand less rightly. To say nothing of the Eccentricity of Venus too, which likewise stands far otherwise than Copernicus asserted; its Apogee too by no means remaining in the same place of the eighth sphere where it was in Ptolemy's time — so as now to stick at the seventeenth degree of Gemini, as Copernicus's reasoning bears — whereas it rather holds the beginning of Cancer. About which, otherwise, more amply, God willing. I should wish that those [things] which you set down about the fixed Stars and their Observations, when you were engaged with the Prince Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, of laudable memory, be published — that it may be established for Posterity that our Observations, investigated in diverse places of the Earth and by various method, differ as little as possible from one another, and that it [Posterity] therefore attribute the greater credit to these. And there are others too who eagerly desire to obtain the Landgravian restitutions of the Fixed [stars] accomplished by your labour. Give your effort, therefore, that, as far as in you lies, both the present and the future age may enjoy so great a good. We have restored about a thousand Stars to the very Minute, both as to longitude and latitude, of which the Canonical exposition our First Tome of the Astronomical Progymnasmata, in the second Chapter, exhibits for the greater part. There, in its time, you will see what we have investigated in disposing their places exactly. Meanwhile, and always, my excellent Rothmann, fare happily, and do not further forbear to visit me from time to time by your letters — which has now long been neglected. And again, farewell.

Given at Uraniborg, the 14th day of January of the year 1595. Yours truly, Tycho Brahe.
