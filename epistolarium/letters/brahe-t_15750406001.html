The original

GENEROSISSIMO AC DOCTISSIMO VIRO TYCHONI BRAHE OTTHONIDI.
S. P. D.

IAM agitur annus, TYCHO doctissime, ex quo GULIELMUS LUMMISDAL, cum isthinc domum rediret, librum tuum de Nova Stella reddidit, quod munus multis nominibus mihi fuit gratissimum, vel quod abs te esset, id est, ab homine claritate generis illustri, eximia ingenij felicitate, et literis perinde expolito, qui illam Philosophiae partem, quae est, ut cecinit vester MANILIUS,

Regales animos primum dignata movere,
Proxima tangent es rerum fastigia coelo,
iacentem erexisti, et e vulgi manibus in suam sedem, hoc est, regiam reduxisti. Cumulat etiam rari muneris gratiam tua humanitas, qui e tam excelso et dignitatis et doctrinae culmine nos humiles, penitus penitusque iacentes non solum favens spectas, sed prope manu attollis, et hortaris exemplo, ut et nos velle cupiamus nube vehi, validique humeris insistere Atlantis: illamque etsi falsam, plurimis nationibus tamen pro vera receptam opinionem tuis monumentis, labore et industria refutas, ingenia scilicet hominum sub hac languenti et frigida coeli plaga inertiae a natura damnata esse. Igitur cum omnes populi septentrionales publice, et nos privatim tibi debeamus plurimum, non existimabis, arrogantius a me factum, quod ad hunc usque diem non rescripserim. Procul enim a meae vitae instituto et moribus hoc crimen abesse, omnes norunt, qui me non ignorant. Neque etiam eos clam est, quam proximo biennio morbis assiduis gravissime afflictus fuerim, ac nullam pene horam ad scribendum vacuam habuerim, adeo ut cum alia multa leviora sim coactus intermittere, tamen quinque libros de Sphaera iam dimidiatos emittere, ac spem scribendi carminis penitus abijcere. Quamobrem tibi gratulor, qui istam generis claritatem a maioribus, ingenij felicitatem nactus, aetatem adhuc integram doctrina sic excolueris, ut familiae a natura splendidissimae tua virtute splendorem adieceris. Nos in hac aetatis effetae ignavia certe currenti libenter applaudemus, et si quid officij praeterea exigi a nobis poterit, tibi, quoad potero, gratificabor. Hunc autem GULIELMUM LUMMISDAL, qui has tibi tradet, rogavimus, ut de statu rerum et studiorum tuorum diligenter me certiorem faceret: quem etiam tibi commendamus, ut pro claritate tua ipsum in quacunque re iuves.

Vale.
Sterlini postridie Nonas Aprilis, Anno 1575.
GEORGIUS BUCHANANUS.

Interea autem, dum hæ Buchanani literæ forte sunt in itinere, ego cum quodam
nobili, eius Conterraneo, miseram illi libellum meum de Nova Stella, ea adhuc
durante, editum. Cum vero exemplum nullum earum literarum mihi supersit,
Epigramma solum modo apponam, eidem libro manu scriptum præfixeram .

AD CLARISSIMUM VIRUM ET POETAM EXCELLENTEM GEORGIUM BUCHANANUM
SCOTUM.

Hunc tibi non magnum mittit, Buchanane, libellum

    Ignotus Boreis Tycho remotus aquis:

Sit licet ignotus, tamen is te novit, amatque,

    Carminibus toto notus es orbe tuis.

Nec Boreas animos poterit disiungere nostros,

    Nam te etiam Boreæ frigidus Axis habet

Qualis enim cœlum convolvi Dania spectat,

    Talis et hoc ipsum Scotia vestra videt.

Exigua hæc igitur nostri monumenta laboris

    A Borea in Boream consule missa boni.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the most noble and most learned gentleman Tycho Brahe, son of Otte. He sends heartiest greeting.

A year is now going by, most learned Tycho, since William Lumisden, on his way home from your parts, delivered your book On the New Star — a gift most welcome to me on many counts: both because it came from you, that is, from a man illustrious in the renown of his lineage, of outstanding felicity of intellect, and likewise polished in letters, who has raised up that part of Philosophy which (as your Manilius sang)

first deigned to stir the minds of kings,
[and which,] touching the topmost summits of things, lies next to heaven,
[—that part of Philosophy] which lay prostrate, and have led it back out of the hands of the common crowd into its own seat, that is, into a royal one. Your kindness, too, heaps grace upon a rare gift: you who, from so lofty a peak both of rank and of learning, not only look favourably upon us lowly men, lying utterly and altogether prostrate, but well-nigh raise [us] up with your hand, and exhort [us] by your example, so that we too may long to wish to be borne upon a cloud and to stand upon the shoulders of mighty Atlas; and you refute, by your works, by labour and industry, that opinion — false, yet received as true among very many nations — namely, that the intellects of men under this faint and cold tract of sky are by nature condemned to sluggishness. Therefore, since all the peoples of the north owe you most greatly in public, and we in private, you will not reckon it done too presumptuously by me that to this very day I have not written back. For all who are not unacquainted with me know that this fault is far from the rule and habit of my life. Nor is it hidden from them how grievously I have been afflicted, throughout the last two years, by relentless illnesses, and how I have had scarcely an hour free for writing — so much so that, while I have been compelled to set aside many other lighter [tasks], [I have been forced] nonetheless to let go of my five books On the Sphere, now [only] half-done, and to cast away utterly the hope of writing the poem. Wherefore I congratulate you, who, having got that renown of lineage from your ancestors, [and] felicity of intellect, have so cultivated with learning an age still in its prime that by your own merit you have added splendour to a family by nature most splendid. We, in the sluggishness of this worn-out age of ours, will gladly applaud one [who is] surely running [his course]; and if any further service can be required of us, I shall oblige you so far as I shall be able. This William Lumisden, moreover, who will hand this [letter] to you, we have asked to inform me diligently of the state of your affairs and of your studies; him too we commend to you, that, in keeping with your renown, you may help him in whatever matter [there may be].

Farewell.
At Stirling, the day after the Nones of April [6 April], the year 1575.
George Buchanan.

Meanwhile, however, while this letter of Buchanan's happened to be on its way [to me], I had, through a certain nobleman, his countryman, sent him my little book on the New Star, published while it [the star] was still lasting. But since no copy remains to me of that letter [which accompanied the gift], I shall set down only the epigram which I had prefixed, written in my own hand, to that same book.

To the Most Renowned Man and Excellent Poet, George Buchanan the Scot.

This not-great little book, Buchanan, Tycho sends to you —
unknown, removed by northern waters:
Though he be unknown, yet he knows you, and loves you;
by your songs you are known through the whole world.
Nor will the North Wind be able to part our two spirits,
for the cold Axis of the North holds you too;
For such as Denmark sees the heavens wheeling,
such likewise does your own Scotland see this very thing.
These slight memorials, then, of my labour,
sent from one North to another North, take in good part.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent / sender. Georgius Buchananus = George Buchanan (1506–1582), the Scottish humanist, Latin poet, and historian — at this date tutor to the young James VI and a senior figure at the Scottish court — writing from Stirling. His own De Sphaera, the five-book Latin didactic poem on the cosmos mentioned here, was never completed and was published posthumously (1586). The source cites his collected Opera omnia as edition of record.
  • Recipient. Tychoni Brahe Otthonidi — Tycho Brahe; Otthonidi "son of Otte" is the Greek-style patronymic for Otte Brahe, Tycho's father (cf. Ottonides in the 1571 Wolf letter, the edition's convention).
  • S. P. D. = salutem plurimam dicit, "sends heartiest greeting".
  • Gulielmus Lummisdal = William Lumisden (Lumsden), the Scot who carried Tycho's book to Buchanan a year earlier and now carries this reply back; spelled Lummisdal in the source on both occurrences. He is commended to Tycho in the closing sentence.
  • librum tuum de Nova Stella — Tycho's De nova stella (Copenhagen, 1573), his account of the 1572 supernova in Cassiopeia. The phrase is descriptive ("your book on the new star"), not necessarily the exact title-page wording.
  • The Manilius quotation. ut cecinit vester Manilius — "as your Manilius sang," from the Astronomica (Book I, opening), the work Buchanan treats as Tycho's poetic patrimony (vester, "your [people's]," addressing the astronomer-poet). The two verses concern Philosophia / astronomy: the science that "first deigned to stir the minds of kings" and, "touching the topmost summits of things, lies next to heaven." Textual crux: the source's second line reads "Proxima tangent es rerum fastigia coelo" — a garbled setting of the standard proxima tangentis rerum fastigia caelo (the participle tangentis split across a line-end into tangent | es). The printed form is kept verbatim in the Source block; the translation follows the intelligible reading tangentis ("touching"), construing it with the science that lies proxima … caelo ("next to heaven"). The grammatical hinge (whether tangentis qualifies the science or stands in another case) is the one genuinely soft point and is flagged here, not smoothed.
  • nube vehi, validique humeris insistere Atlantis — "to be borne upon a cloud and to stand upon the shoulders of mighty Atlas": a poetic image of being lifted to the heavens (Atlas the sky-bearer); kept as metaphor.
  • The "northern sluggishness" topos. ingenia … hominum sub hac languenti et frigida coeli plaga inertiae a natura damnata esse — the (classical, Ptolemaic-climatic) commonplace that minds bred under the cold northern sky are doomed to dullness; Buchanan's compliment is that Tycho's work refutes it. Rendered straight, with the hedge etsi falsam … pro vera receptam ("though false, yet received as true") preserved.
  • quinque libros de Sphaera … dimidiatos emittere — Buchanan's own poem De Sphaera in five books, here described as dimidiatos, "[only] half-done." The infinitive emittere is governed by sim coactus ("I have been compelled") and stands in parallel with intermittere ("set aside") and abijcere ("cast away utterly") — a list of works illness forced him to give up, not works he published. emittere is therefore rendered "let go of," not "put out/publish": Buchanan is enumerating renunciations, and the De Sphaera was in fact never finished by him (it was completed and published posthumously, 1586). spem scribendi carminis … abijcere registers the same loss — his fear of never finishing it (illness, morbis assiduis). His epistemic framing — that illness forced (coactus) these renunciations — is kept; no claim is sharpened.
  • istam generis claritatem a maribus … nactus — "having got that renown of lineage from the men of your line." a maribus is specifically "from the males / the male line" (descent through the male line), not the neutral "from your forebears"; the specificity is preserved in the translation and noted here.
  • Calendar / place. Sterlini postridie Nonas Aprilis, Anno 1575 = "at Stirling, the day after the Nones of April, the year 1575." The Nones of April fall on the 5th; postridie Nonas is therefore 6 April (O.S.; Scotland used the Julian calendar). This matches the edition label ("1575. 6. april. Stirling"); no discrepancy. The Roman-calendar idiom is rendered with both idiom and civil date by the edition's convention
  • Orthography. Source spellings ingenij, abijcere, Otthonidi, coelo, vehi and the capitalised proper names are humanist-Latin conventions, left as printed in the Source block.
  • What the recovered text is. This is not part of Buchanan's letter (the sender of record) at all, but Tycho's own explanatory note, in the first person, inserted to account for how the correspondence began: while Buchanan's letter of 6 April 1575 (the published tail) was crossing in transit, Tycho had already sent Buchanan a presentation copy of his De Nova Stella (1573) via an unnamed Scottish nobleman, "his countryman." Since Tycho no longer had a copy of his own covering letter, he preserves only the Latin epigram he had inscribed by hand in the gift book itself.
  • The epigram is Tycho's own autograph dedicatory verse to George Buchanan (1506–1582), the Scottish humanist, poet and historian — at this date tutor to the young James VI and one of the most celebrated Latin poets in Europe. The poem plays on the North/"Boreas" conceit throughout: Tycho, "unknown" in Scotland, sends greetings from one cold northern land (Denmark) to another (Scotland), arguing that the shared northern sky under which both men live unites them despite personal unacquaintance.
  • Confirms the gift-exchange context of the Buchanan correspondence. The published tail of Buchanan's own reply names William Lumisden ("Gulielmus Lummisdal") as the bearer entrusted to bring news of Tycho's affairs back to Buchanan; the recovered note shows this was already the second leg of an exchange that began with Tycho's own unsolicited gift of De Nova Stella, carried by a different, unnamed Scottish nobleman — evidence of Tycho using his 1572 nova publication as an instrument of international humanist self-introduction within a year of its printing.
  • William Lumisden / "Gulielmus Lummisdal" (the courier named in Buchanan's own letter) is not further identifiable in the works consulted at this date; the only Lumisden the works consulted yield is the unrelated eighteenth-century Jacobite antiquary Andrew Lumisden (1720–1801), too late to be relevant here.
  • Correction. Latin corrected from the corrupted 'a maribus' to 'a maioribus' ('from your ancestors'), restoring the word split across the Dreyer page break; English corrected to match, and the bracketed gloss explaining the corrupted reading removed. Per Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII pp.21-22.

Collated against Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia (the edition of record), reading the printed page images. Dreyer TBOO VII pp.21-23 read as page images. The Buchanan letter runs from 'GENEROSISSIMO AC DOCTISSIMO VIRO TYCHONI BRAHE OTTHONIDI' at the bottom of p.21 through the epigram at the bottom of p.22 (p.23 begins an unrelated Pratensis letter and was read only to confirm the boundary; a separate Haintzelius-to-Tycho covering note at the top of p.21 likewise precedes and is not part of this letter). Collated the Manilius quotation, the full prose body, the closing date (postridie Nonas Aprilis = 6 April 1575), Tycho's own editorial note, and the appended epigram word-for-word against the page images. One text error found, as above — a genuine hyphenation-splice corruption in our source Latin, not an digitised text layer artefact, confirmed by re-reading the page-break at 'a ma-/ioribus'.

1 defect found:
- text error (high). Dreyer's page break splits the word as 'ma-' (end of p.21) / 'ioribus' (top of p.22) = 'a maioribus', 'from [one's] ancestors' — the ordinary sense of the clause ('renown of lineage from your ancestors'). The transcribed original drops the 'io', rejoining the hyphenated halves as 'a maribus', 'from the males' — a different and much odder claim. The translator rendered the corrupted reading literally and added a bracketed Latin gloss, but did not catch or flag the underlying corruption against Dreyer. Dreyer prints: qui iftam generis claritatem a ma-ioribus, ingenij felicitatem nactus (TBOO VII pp.21-22, word hyphenated across the page break).

Repaired. Verified against Dreyer TBOO VII pp.21-22 (page images, not the digitised text layer), where the word is unambiguously split 'ma-ioribus' across the page break. This was the one high-severity defect found, now resolved with both Latin and English corrected. No other part of the letter was touched.
