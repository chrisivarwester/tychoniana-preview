IOANNES KEPLER AD TYCHONEM BRAHE.

CUM intellexissem ex MÆSTLINI literis, quas ad me dedit superiori Augusto, respondisse te ad epistolium meum, eiusque responsionis exemplar apud MÆSTLINUM esse, proxima occasione apud illum institi, ut id exemplar ad me mitteret, quando literæ tuæ mihi non redderentur. Sic casu et diligentia tua factum, ut quamvis pereuntibus tuis literis, tamen quod me scire velles, post decimum mensem intellexerim. Hæc initio te certiorem reddidi, quia in illa querela tua, me Christe, quam iustissima! postulas, ut primo quoque tempore meam tibi sententiam aperiam, ne ex hac decimestri mora maior tibi de me suspicio culpæ suboriretur. Quantum igitur attinet meas ad URSUM literas, ex ijs, quæ excerpsisti verbis, pleraque mea agnosco. Ex eo fit, ut quamvis ad aliqua, de quibus dubito, pene cohorrescam, nullum tamen negare possim. In hac itaque parte nullum adhuc URSI crimen est, quod liqueat. Utrum autem integram epistolam publicaverit, maior apud me dubitatio: quæ facile discuteretur, si mihi exemplar typi esset. Nam in ijsdem literis desiderium meum tuorum operum legendorum expressi, eaque in re operam URSI expetij. Quantam autem per Deum immortalem! et quammultiplicem mihi facit iniuriam ferus homo! Ambiebam eius amicitiam missa epistola. Si petitionem meam admisit, fidem eius desidero: sin sprevit, tamen inhumanus est. Sermones amicorum, literæ magis, privatæ esse debent; has ille publicavit, nec me consuluit, nec facturum, nec factum nunciavit, nec exemplar misit. Quibus est curæ æquitas, nec in hostem quidem tale quid audent. Remisisset mihi, quod veteres aiebant, nuncium, aut abolevisset schedam, si non placuit amicus. Quamquam si literis eius ad me altero tandem anno scriptis, si iudicio eius de me, fides haberi potest, et placuit conditio, et amicitiam colere instituerat. Sed hæc levia sunt: illa maior improbitas, iure utique vindicanda, si cui in foro aut spes aut oblectatio: quod id quod γεωμετροῦντι dictum ego volebam, ipse sibi maledicenti, quod scribis, applicat. Acerbissimum omnium, quod quem intelligebat ex meis literis a me maximi fieri, illis ipsis meis literis, quantum potuit, dehonestavit. Sic nimirum ipsi me de laudato inimico suo placuit ulcisci. Sed neque satis mirari possum consilium hominis, dum consilium meum illius hyperbolicæ laudationis ventilo. Quid? Tantine facit ista mea præconia? At cuius hominis? Si vir esset, contemneret, si prudens, non ventilaret. Quærebam obscurus ego laudatorem clarum vixdum nati inventi, mendicabam ab ipso stipem, ecce stipem ipse mendico extorsit. Præceptorem dixi: næ parvam laudi accessionem facit ex adulatione discipuli. Quærat eorum præconia, qui se præceptores ipsius ferunt. Quod si mihi partes iudicis tradit (nec apertius id potuisset, quam sic ostentata mea epistola) sciat iam mihi alios quam discipulo mores competere. Toleratur in discipulo utcunque adulatio, si ex opinione præceptoris et amore profecta ad finem discendi dirigatur: in iudice adulatio flagitium est. Quæ sic scribenda duxi, ut quamvis præstat securitatis causa honoratissimum quemque virum irasci minime, tamen, si non aliter honori tuo consuli posset, haberes arbitrium publicatæ calumniæ publice obviam eundi. Sed missum tandem ipsum faciamus. Nec enim, quam ipse male, sed quam ego bene fecerim, quæritur. Et sunt quidem in illa mea epistola pleraque, quæ defendi, aliqua, quæ excusari, aliqua quæ pessima suspicione liberari debent.

Ordiamur ab eo quod gravissimum est: quod nempe crimini mihi das, contra quam senserim, in os ipsi locutum. Quod dum agis, ita loqueris, ut mihi absolutissimum de alijs iudicium tribuere videaris. Quam ego suspicionem, si a me, quod perquam facile est, removero, magna parte purgationis me defunctum esse putabo. Etenim qui vir sit URSUS, iam non disputare placet, certe quo tempore epistolam illam scripsi, et imbecilliori eram iudicio et non parum ipsi iam diu tribueram. Venit Grætium Doctor quispiam ex Italia reversus Librum eius mihi exhibens, quem triduo concesso raptim percurrerem. In eo salutatos schematismis reperio præcipuos huius temporis Mathematicos, reperio magnificam in præfatione provocationem ad simile quid audendum, ad Astronomiam e fundamentis extruendam, reperio compendia quædam aurea, quæ Tubingæ a MÆSTLINO celebrari memineram, reperio doctrinam sinuum, doctrinam triangulorum, vulgaria quidem, sed nova mihi omnia, ut videat, quo remige famam verberet. Nam pleraque postea in EUCLIDE et REGIOMONTANO reperi, quæ tum ipsi tribuebam. Neque metuas, ut quæ iam dixi, bene cum invento meo stare queant. Dicat ipse pro me MÆSTLINUS, quam tenuibus principijs institerim initio, qui profecto, si tam ieiunum me secundo dimisisset, quam primo dimiserat, nunquam tam exquisitam mihi commendationem (qua tamen, dum vixero, inferior ero) impertivisset. Itaque hac ego specie doctrinæ de URSO in præclaram opinionem erectus, incidi postea in quosdam ex nobilitate Styriaca Mathematum amantes, quibus, quod esset necessaria subinde mora in aula Cæsaris, URSI notitia erat. Illi hominis prædicare ingenium, inventa, hortari me, ut anniterer in eius familiaritatem pervenire, polliceri suam operam. Sed DIOGENEM quempiam mihi describebant aliqui, cui nisi aut lubeat, aut cum improbitate aliqua extorqueri nihil possit. His ego de causis inventa paulo post speculatione mea, cum non esset mihi vicinior Mathematicus, ad quem commodius scriberem, ad scribendum impulsus, formam ipsam partim ad hominis naturam, quæ ferebatur, partim ad eas, quas tum forte fortuito legebam, efficacissimæ brevitatis epistolas dissoluto in pompam animo ex recenti inventionis meæ amore accommodavi securus de hoc eventu. Quomodo si quid mihi excidit paulo maius, quam de ipso senseram, in gratiam laudatorum ipsius et mei ipsius amore, totum id iuvenilis temeritatis est, nec aliter excuso, dummodo sceleris suspicionem aboleam. Ex ijs, quæ dixi, vides primum me bona conscientia Gloriam ipsi tribuisse, qua videbatur regnare in aula Cæsaris et apud Mathematicos, quos in libello suo alloquebatur, illustrissimam autem cur dixerim, causam non reperio, nisi quod Illustres nostri eam prædicant, et Illustrissimus prædicare videtur, dum eum in Aula habet, et quod pomposum ingenium facile muscam conspicatur, ex qua faciat Elephantum. Eodem refero et id, quod, cum constaret de aliquibus, dixi (si modo dixi) ab omnibus doctis ipsum magnifieri. Certe enim MÆSTLINUM et TYCHONEM inter doctos habebam, quos sciebam non admodum præclare de ipso sentire. Quod autem etiam plura in hunc modum declamaturus fuissem, si tempus tulisset, id non memini me polliceri. Imo illa de garrulitate aliena a Mathematicis protestatio non facta est ad excusanda etiam plura huiusmodi, sed potius ad corrigenda priora. Quod si vero mea sunt omnia et singula ista verba, quod Mathematicos huius ævi tantum præcedat unus quantum Phœbeius orbis minuta sidera: me Christe magnam iniuriam multis præclarissimis viris, atque adeo meæ ipsius conscientiæ imprudens feci. Illud verissime dicere possum, nunquam me ullo modo serio, per lusum, publice, privatim, in os, aurem, ex animo volentem et prudentem hoc dixisse, quod REGIOMONTANO, COPERNICO, RHETICO, REINHOLDO, TYCHONI, MÆSTLINO, cæteris unus URSUS præferendus sit. Nunquam sensi, nunquam præsenti animo scripsi, nunquam tam flagitiosam adulationem probavi. Quod si verba mea genuina tale quid sonant, quod nescio, casus in culpa est, meaque festinatio, et quod quid scripserim, non relegi. Poëticum, ut vides, totum id est, et ex Poëta desumptum, et poëtico spiritu dictum: quod non tantum iam nolo, sed et tum, cum scripsi, nolebam, aliorsum rapi volebam, aliam repræsentari, quod antecellat vulgus, vulgus, inquam, Mathematicorum, quod et credebam, et citra præcipuam gloriam fieri potest. Non protestarer tanta asseveratione contra manifesta verba (si modo talia sunt) nisi in ipsa illa epistola documentum maximum extaret meæ innocentiæ, quo de supra. Nam etsi quibus verbis TYCHONIS mentionem fecerim, nescio, fecisse tamen scio: et eiusmodi mentionem quidem, atque ex eiusmodi opinione profectam, quæ merito URSUM pungat, si eum tam feliciter titillant hæc sui ipsius elogia. Sed caute, ut video, posthac scribendum erit. Adeo nunquam non salutare quippiam URSI in aurem insusurrando præcipiunt. Cætera, quæ allegas, levia sunt. Præceptorem dixi, quia ex eius libro aliqua hauseram utilia; Nobilissimum vero, quia in his provincijs Austriacorum nobilitati datur Eruditio et Munus aulicum; Hypotheses vero eius feci, in quibus aliquid mutavit; nam si bene memini, Terram et ipse voluit, et in Martis circulo discrepat, sive, ut dicis, errat. Nam etsi ex MÆSTLINO de hac te inter et ipsum controversia olim inaudiveram, tamen cum et MAGINUS in Italia et RŒSLINUS in Alsatia singuli pro se simile quippiam ex hypothesibus COPERNICI derivassent, existimavi idem et URSO excogitata proclive fuisse. Denique quod finis principio respondet, nihil mirum, nec ulla in voto improbitas, ut valeat sideribus et scientiæ. Id equidem studeat, maledictis omissis et vana ostentatione. Decus autem Germaniæ mihi quilibet est, cui tantum tribuo, quantum illi tribuebam, quia nempe Mathemata verum decus sunt unius Germaniæ. Hæc adeo prolixe, cum ut dolorem meum testarer iusta querela, tum ut appareret, arbitrari me non omnino nihil ad meam existimationem pertinere, quid tu de me sentias.

English translation machine translation, Secure

JOHANNES KEPLER TO TYCHO BRAHE.

When I had understood, from Maestlin's letter (which he sent me last August), that you had replied to my little letter, and that a copy of your reply was with Maestlin — since your letter was not delivered to me — I pressed him at the next occasion to send me that copy. So it came about, by chance and by your diligence, that though your letter perished, yet what you wished me to know I understood after the tenth month. This I made known to you at the outset, because, in that complaint of yours — by Christ, how most just! — you demand that I open my view to you at the first opportunity, lest from this ten-month delay a greater suspicion of fault toward me should arise in you. As far, then, as concerns my letters to Ursus: from the words you excerpted, I recognise most as mine. Hence it is that, although at some, of which I am in doubt, I almost shudder, yet I can deny none. In this part, then, there is as yet no crime of Ursus that is clear. But whether he published the whole letter, [there is] greater doubt with me — which would easily be resolved, had I a printed copy. For in those letters I expressed my desire to read your works, and in that matter sought Ursus's help. But how great — by the immortal God! — and how manifold an injury the savage man does me! I sought his friendship by sending the letter. If he admitted my petition, I miss his good faith; if he spurned it, he is yet inhuman. The conversations of friends — more, their letters — ought to be private; these he published, neither consulted me, nor announced he would do it, nor that he had, nor sent a copy. Those who have a care for fairness do not dare such a thing even against an enemy. He would have sent the message back to me (as the ancients said), or destroyed the sheet, if the friend did not please him — although, if his letters to me, written at last the next year, if his judgement of me, can be trusted, both the condition pleased [him] and he had begun to cultivate friendship. But these are light matters. That [is] the greater wickedness, by all means justly to be avenged (if any has in the court either hope or delight): that what I meant to be said "to one doing geometry" [γεωμετροῦντι], he applies to himself, reviling [you], as you write. Bitterest of all: that the one whom he understood from my letters to be most highly esteemed by me [you], he, by those very letters of mine, dishonoured as much as he could. So, forsooth, it pleased him to avenge himself on me regarding his praised enemy. But neither can I sufficiently marvel at the man's plan, while I ventilate my own plan of that hyperbolic praise. What? Does he value those proclamations of mine so highly? But [praise] of what man? If he were a [true] man, he would despise it; if prudent, he would not ventilate it. I, obscure, sought a famous praiser of my scarcely-born invention; I begged a coin from him; behold, the beggar himself has extorted a coin. I called him "preceptor": truly he makes small addition to his praise from a disciple's adulation. Let him seek the proclamations of those who bear themselves as his preceptors. But if he hands me the part of a judge (and he could not [do it] more openly than by thus displaying my letter), let him know that other manners now befit me than [those of] a disciple. Adulation is tolerated, somehow, in a disciple, if, proceeding from esteem and love of the preceptor, it be directed to the end of learning; in a judge, adulation is a crime. These things I thought should thus be written, so that — although for safety's sake it is best that every most-honoured man be least angry — yet, if your honour could not otherwise be provided for, you might have the choice of going publicly against the published calumny. But let us at last dismiss the man himself. For the question is not how badly he acted, but how well I acted. And there are indeed, in that letter of mine, most things which ought to be defended, some which [ought] to be excused, some which [ought] to be freed from the worst suspicion.

Let us begin from what is gravest: namely, that you charge me with the crime of having spoken to his face against what I felt. In doing which, you so speak as to seem to attribute to me an absolute judgement about others. Which suspicion, if I remove it from myself (which is very easy), I shall think myself discharged of a great part of the purgation. For what kind of man Ursus is, I do not now choose to dispute; certainly, at the time I wrote that letter, I was both of weaker judgement, and had long attributed not a little to him. A certain Doctor, returned from Italy, came to Graz, showing me his book — which, three days being granted, I hastily ran through. In it I find the chief Mathematicians of this time greeted with diagrams; I find in the preface a magnificent provocation to dare something similar, to build Astronomy from its foundations; I find certain golden compendia which I remembered being celebrated at Tübingen by Maestlin; I find the doctrine of sines, the doctrine of triangles — common, indeed, but all new to me — so that he might see by what oar he beats [the way to] fame. For most of what I then attributed to him I afterward found in Euclid and Regiomontanus. And do not fear that what I have just said can [not] stand well with my own invention: let Maestlin himself say for me on how slender principles I stood at the beginning — who indeed, had he dismissed me as meagrely the second time as he had the first, would never have imparted to me so exquisite a commendation (to which, however, while I live, I shall be inferior). And so I, raised by this appearance of learning into a splendid opinion of Ursus, fell in afterward with certain of the Styrian nobility, lovers of Mathematics, who, because of their necessary occasional stay at the Emperor's court, had acquaintance with Ursus. They proclaimed the man's genius and inventions, urged me to strive to enter his familiarity, promised their help. But some described to me a kind of Diogenes, from whom nothing could be got unless he pleased, or [it were] extorted with some improbity. For these causes, a little after my invention by my own speculation — since there was no nearer Mathematician to whom I could more conveniently write — impelled to write, I accommodated the very form [of the letter] partly to the man's reported nature, partly to those letters of most efficacious brevity which I then happened by chance to be reading — my mind dissolved into pomp from the recent love of my invention — secure about this outcome. So that, if anything fell from me a little greater than I felt about him, in favour of his praisers and from love of myself, it is all of youthful temerity, and I excuse it no otherwise — provided I abolish the suspicion of crime.

From what I have said, you see, first, that I attributed to him, with good conscience, the Glory by which he seemed to reign at the Emperor's court and among the Mathematicians whom he addressed in his booklet; but why I said "most illustrious," I find no cause, except that our Illustrious [nobles] proclaim it, and the Most Illustrious [Emperor] seems to proclaim it, while he keeps him at Court — and that a pompous genius easily spots a fly, of which to make an Elephant. To the same I refer that which (when it was established about some) I said — if I said it — that he is magnified by all the learned. For certainly I held Maestlin and Tycho among the learned, whom I knew not to think very splendidly of him. But that I would have declaimed even more in this manner, had time allowed, I do not remember promising. Nay, that protestation about garrulity, alien to Mathematicians, was not made to excuse even more of this kind, but rather to correct the earlier. But if all and each of those words are mine — that one [Ursus] precedes the Mathematicians of this age as much as the Phoebean orb [precedes] the tiny stars — then, by Christ, I imprudently did great injury to many most distinguished men, and even to my own conscience. This I can say most truly: that never, in any way — seriously, in jest, publicly, privately, to the face, to the ear, from the heart, willing and prudent — did I say that one Ursus is to be preferred to Regiomontanus, Copernicus, Rheticus, Reinhold, Tycho, Maestlin, [and] the rest. Never did I feel it, never with present mind did I write it, never did I approve so shameful an adulation. But if my genuine words sound such a thing (which I do not know), the fault is chance's, and my haste, and that I did not re-read what I had written. It is all Poetic, as you see, and taken from a Poet, and said with a poetic spirit — which not only now do I not want, but even then, when I wrote it, I did not want; I wanted it carried elsewhere, another [sense] represented: that he surpasses the vulgar — the vulgar, I say, of Mathematicians — which I both believed, and can happen short of [his having] chief glory. I would not protest with such asseveration against manifest words (if such they are), did there not exist, in that very letter, the greatest document of my innocence, whereof above. For though in what words I made mention of you I do not know, yet I know I did make mention — and such a mention, indeed, and proceeding from such an opinion, as deservedly stings Ursus, if these self-praises so happily tickle him. But cautiously, I see, must I write hereafter — so [much] do they teach [me], by ever whispering something "salutary" into Ursus's ear. The rest you allege are light: I called [him] "preceptor," because from his book I had drawn some useful things; "most noble," because in these Austrian provinces erudition and a court office are given to the nobility; I made [them] his hypotheses, in which he changed something — for, if I remember well, he too wanted the Earth [mobile], and differs in the circle of Mars, or, as you say, errs. For although I had once heard from Maestlin of this controversy between you and him, yet since both Magini in Italy and Röslin in Alsace had, each for himself, derived something similar from the hypotheses of Copernicus, I reckoned that the same had been easy for Ursus too to devise. Finally, that the end answers the beginning, [is] no wonder, nor any wickedness in the wish — that he flourish by the stars and by science. Let him indeed study that, with maledictions and vain ostentation omitted. And "Ornament of Germany" — to me, anyone is [that] to whom I attribute as much as I attributed to him, because Mathematics is the true ornament of Germany alone. These things [I write] so prolixly, both that I might testify my grief by a just complaint, and that it might appear that I reckon it not at all nothing to my reputation, what you think of me.

Apparatus

  • Direction & correspondents. Incoming, Johannes Kepler → Tycho, from Graz, February 1599. Kepler's apologia for his 1595 letter to Ursus — which Ursus had printed in his De astronomicis hypothesibus (1597), using Kepler's extravagant praise as an endorsement against Tycho. Tycho had written Kepler a stinging complaint (which never arrived; Kepler got a copy via Maestlin ten months later); this is the reply.
  • Significance — a keystone of the Tycho–Kepler–Ursus triangle, and a revealing self-portrait. One of the most psychologically important letters in the history of science: the young, obscure Kepler, who had flattered Ursus to win patronage ("one Ursus precedes the mathematicians of this age as much as the sun's orb the tiny stars"; "preceptor," "ornament of Germany"), now must explain himself to the man he most needed to keep — Tycho.
  • Ursus's betrayal. Kepler is outraged that Ursus published a private letter without consent or a copy, applied Kepler's praise to himself while reviling Tycho, and "dishonoured the one [Kepler] esteemed most [Tycho] with Kepler's own words." He invites Tycho, if needed, to go publicly against the published calumny.
  • The self-defence (the heart). Kepler dismantles the charge of "speaking against what he felt": at the time he was "of weaker judgement," dazzled by Ursus's book (read in three days, found "new" things he later traced to Euclid and Regiomontanus), urged on by Styrian nobles who knew Ursus at court, and warned Ursus was "a kind of Diogenes" yielding nothing unless flattered. He confesses the letter's "pomp" was "all Poetic … taken from a Poet, said with poetic spirit," the excess of "youthful temerity" and "love of my [new] invention" — not a sincere ranking. He swears he never truly preferred Ursus to Regiomontanus, Copernicus, Rheticus, Reinhold, Tycho, or Maestlin.
  • The defence of the substance. He explains each phrase: "preceptor" (he learned useful things from the book), "most noble" (Austrian custom gives office to nobility), "most illustrious" (the Emperor keeps Ursus at court); and notes that he "made them Ursus's hypotheses, in which he changed something" — observing, tellingly, that Magini (Italy) and Röslin (Alsace) had each independently derived similar geo-heliocentric schemes from Copernicus, so he thought Ursus could too. The crucial point of innocence: in the very same letter he had also honoured Tycho "from such an opinion as deservedly stings Ursus."
  • The relationship preserved. The closing is the key: Kepler writes "so prolixly" because "I reckon it not at all nothing to my reputation what you think of me" — the anxious deference that, despite the awkwardness, kept the door open to the collaboration sealed in 1600 (cf. brahe-t_16000828001, brahe-t_16001017001).
  • Cross-references. The Tycho–Kepler–Ursus keystone: pair with Kepler's analysis of Ursus's plagiarism (brahe-t_16000911001), the Ursus legal endgame (brahe-t_16000926003), and the employment letters (brahe-t_16000828001, brahe-t_16001017001).
  • Persons named. Johannes Kepler; Tycho; Ursus (Nicolaus Reimers Bär); Michael Maestlin (the intermediary, Kepler's teacher); Regiomontanus, Copernicus, Rheticus, Reinhold, Tycho, Maestlin (the truly great, whom Kepler would not rank below Ursus); Euclid, Regiomontanus (sources of Ursus's "novelties"); Magini, Helisaeus Röslin (independent geo-heliocentric derivers); Diogenes (the character-type for Ursus); Rudolf II (Ursus at court). Greek γεωμετροῦντι ("to one doing geometry") reproduced.
  • Date/place & completeness. Place omitted (Graz); dated February 1599 (identifier 15990200001, label "1599-02"; no day). The source as supplied ends mid-discourse, without valediction, date, or signature — the famous Apologia-letter is longer; the text as transmitted appears partial.
