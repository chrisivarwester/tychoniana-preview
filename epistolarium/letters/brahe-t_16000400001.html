The original

IOANNES KEPLER AD TYCHONEM BRAHE.

SONS manus, quæ nuper ad lædendum celeritate ventos antevertit, quodnam initium faciat ad leniendum, vix invenit. Nam quid ego primum commemorem? An meam intemperantiam? Cuius acerbissima est recordatio. An tua, Nobilissime TYCHO, benefacta? Quæ iam nec enumerari possunt, nec pro dignitate perpendi. Per duos menses gratuito me victu liberaliter aluisti; toti tuæ familiæ meam incolumitatem, mea desideria commendasti: omnia humanitatis officia præstitisti: quæ habuisti charissima, mihi communicasti: nemo me dicto vel facto volens læsit unquam: in summa, non liberis, non uxori, non ipsi tibi plus indulsisti quam mihi. Quæ animo præterea volvebas, de me promovendo, ex ijs, quæ mea causa suscepisti, facile vidi. Quæ omnia, cum ita habere sciverim, fassus fuerim, scripto etiam testata reliquerim: magnâ cum animi consternatione recordor, adeo me a Deo et a Spiritu Sancto meis intemperijs animique morbo fuisse permissum, ut ad tot et tanta beneficia, clausis oculis, pro modestia, trium hebdomadum continuæ morositati erga totam familiam, pro gratiarum actione, iracundiæ præcipiti, pro reverentiâ, audaciæ summæ erga tuam personam, tam illustri stemmate, tam præstanti eruditione, tanta nominis claritate venerabilem, pro amica valedictione, suspiciosissimis imputationibus et acerbissimæ scribendi prurigini tantum indulgere potuerim, nec me respexerim, nec quam crudeliter tuum animum, qui præclare semper de me senseras, hac odiosissimâ ratione sim offensurus, perpenderim. Quæ si hostili animo, si nocendi proposito, suscepissem: equidem vix vel a Deo petere fuissem ausus, uti te mihi præstaret placabilem, iustamque et legitimam vindictam impediret.

Sed quia totum hoc impotentis animi furor et choleræ præstitit exuberantia, et iuvenile vitium, curiositas, cum præcipitantiâ iudicij (abfuit vero diffamandi animus) et quia non experieris, me unquam cum quoquam mortalium ita de te locutum, uti ad te unum ex falsâ imaginatione scripsi (quemadmodum neque in quemquam unquam talia designavi) ex hac qualicunque consolatione post gravissimam pœnitudinem, animum nonnihil recepi: et quia quæ scripsi, coram Deo solo teste, M.T. imputata fuere; primum Deum Opt. Max. veniam exoravi, deinde et pro Christianâ reconciliatione summe fui sollicitus. Quod nisi me afflicta valetudo, immoderatæ affectionis haud dubia stimulatrix, impedivisset, primo quoque tempore Benathkam excurrissem, meque M.T. ad omnem satisfactionem promptum stitissem. Iam quia fors ita tulit, ut prior Μ. T. Pragam veniret, supplex ad Μ. T. venio, per Dei misericordiam orans, uti mihi hanc atrocissimam offensionem Christiano animo remittat. Ego contra, quæ dixi vel scripsi in Μ. T. personam, famam, honorem et doctrinam, vel quocunque alio modo, iniuriosissima (si nulla valeat in meliorem partem interpretatio) uti (quod ex animo doleo) dixi scripsique plurima et plura quam meminerim: omnia et singula revoco, irrita, falsa et probatu impossibilia sponte et ingenue agnosco atque pronuncio. Neque enim ullum facinus patratum, aut ullam partem honestatis a Μ. T. unquam violatam vel vidi vel audivi. Si quid suspicionibus etiam indulsi, id omne, ut Christianum decet, ex animo eijciam, sic ut protestationi Christianæ acquiescens, temeritatem in suspicando libenter Deo καρδιογνώστῃ fatear.

Promitto etiam bona fide, me in posterum, ubicunque versaturus sim, non tantum huiusmodi malesanis actibus, dictis, factis et literis abstenturum, sed neque ullo pacto Μ. T. iniuste et ex proposito offensurum, neque quicquam, quod non deceat, contra Μ. T. moliturum. Oro autem, quia lubrica est vita hominis, ut si M.T. animadvertat me imprudenter in tale quid vergere, me mei ipsius admoneat: experietur enim me morigerum. Quin imo polliceor, daturum me operam, quod quidem nunquam adhuc mentis compos intermisi, ut omni genere officiorum Μ. T. ulterius devinciam, et quantum mihi possibile est, superiorumque meorum patitur authoritas, in omnibus honestis rebus, quasque Μ. T. gratas intellexero, obsequium præstem, et sic ipso facto demonstrem, aliter me in M.T. personam esse et semper fuisse affectum, quam ex illa trium septimanarum intemperata dispositione animi et corporis colligi poterat. Et precor, ut ad id præstandum me Deus adiuvet.

JOHANNES KEPLER TO TYCHO BRAHE.

The guilty hand, which lately in its speed to wound outran the winds, scarcely finds what beginning to make toward soothing. For what shall I first call to mind? My intemperance? — the recollection of which is most bitter. Or your benefactions, most noble Tycho? — which now can neither be enumerated, nor weighed according to their worth. For two months you fed me liberally with free board; you commended my safety, my wishes, to your whole household; you performed all the offices of humanity; what you held most dear, you shared with me; no one ever willingly injured me in word or deed; in sum, you indulged not your children, not your wife, not your own self more than me. What besides you were turning over in your mind, about advancing me, I easily saw from the things you undertook for my sake. All which — since I knew it to be so, confessed it, and even left it attested in writing — I recall with great consternation of mind: that I was so far given over by God and by the Holy Spirit, through my intemperance and my mind's sickness, that to so many and so great benefits, with closed eyes, I could indulge — instead of modesty, three weeks' continuous moroseness toward your whole household; instead of thanksgiving, headlong wrath; instead of reverence, the utmost audacity toward your person (venerable for so illustrious a lineage, so excellent an erudition, so great a renown of name); instead of a friendly farewell, most suspicious imputations and a most bitter itch of writing — nor did I look to myself, nor weigh how cruelly I was about to offend your mind (you who had always thought excellently of me) by this most hateful manner. Which, had I undertaken it with a hostile mind, with a purpose of harming, I should indeed scarcely have dared even to ask of God that he render you placable to me, and hinder a just and lawful vengeance.

But since the whole of this was wrought by the fury of an uncontrolled mind and an exuberance of choler, and a youthful fault — curiosity — with precipitancy of judgement (but the intent to defame was absent); and since you will not find that I ever spoke of you to any mortal as I wrote, to you alone, out of a false imagination (just as I never plotted such things against anyone): from this consolation, such as it is, after the gravest repentance, I have somewhat recovered my spirit. And since what I wrote — God alone being witness — were charged to Your Magnificence: first I begged pardon of God Best and Greatest, then I was supremely solicitous for a Christian reconciliation. And had not my afflicted health (the undoubted stimulant of immoderate emotion) hindered me, I should at the first opportunity have run over to Benátky, and presented myself to Your Magnificence ready for every satisfaction. Now, since chance has so brought it that Your Magnificence came first to Prague, I come a suppliant to Your Magnificence, praying by God's mercy that you would, with a Christian mind, forgive me this most atrocious offence. I, in turn — [as for] what I said or wrote against Your Magnificence's person, fame, honour, and learning, or in any other way most injurious (if no interpretation toward the better part avails), as (which I grieve from my heart) I said and wrote very many things, and more than I remember — all and each I retract, and of my own accord and frankly acknowledge and declare void, false, and impossible to prove. For I have neither seen nor heard any crime committed, or any part of honourableness ever violated, by Your Magnificence. If I also indulged in any suspicions, I will cast all that out of my mind, as becomes a Christian — so that, acquiescing in a Christian protestation, I freely confess my rashness in suspecting to God, the Knower of hearts.

I also promise, in good faith, that henceforth, wherever I shall be, I will not only abstain from such unsound acts, words, deeds, and letters, but neither in any way unjustly and deliberately offend Your Magnificence, nor contrive anything unbecoming against Your Magnificence. But I pray, since the life of man is slippery, that if Your Magnificence notice me imprudently inclining toward any such thing, you would admonish me of it: for you will find me compliant. Nay rather, I promise to give effort — which indeed I never yet, when in my right mind, omitted — to bind Your Magnificence further by every kind of service, and, as far as is possible for me and the authority of my superiors allows, to render obedience in all honourable matters, and such as I shall understand to be welcome to Your Magnificence; and so to demonstrate, by the very deed, that I am, and always have been, otherwise disposed toward Your Magnificence's person than could be gathered from that three weeks' intemperate disposition of mind and body. And I pray that God may help me to perform this.

Apparatus

- Significance — one of the most famous letters in the history of science. This is Johannes Kepler's autograph letter of apology and submission to Tycho Brahe (April 1600), written after the violent first quarrel that nearly ended their association before it began. During Kepler's first weeks at Benátky (early 1600), a dispute over the conditions of his work and access to Tycho's observations erupted into a furious outburst; Kepler left and sent Tycho intemperate, accusatory letters, then — overcome with remorse — wrote this abject retraction. The reconciliation it effected made possible the collaboration on which Kepler's Astronomia nova (and his laws of planetary motion) rests. The MS is Kepler's autograph in the Vienna ÖNB (cod. Vindob. lat. 10703).
- Structure of the apology.
1. The opening figure — "the guilty hand, which lately in its speed to wound outran the winds" — and the antithesis between his "intemperance" and Tycho's "benefactions."
2. The catalogue of Tycho's kindnesses — two months' free board; commending Kepler's safety to the whole household; sharing "what he held most dear"; "you indulged not your children, your wife, your own self more than me" — and his plans "about advancing me."
3. The self-accusation — that, "given over by God and the Holy Spirit through my intemperance and my mind's sickness," he repaid these with "three weeks' continuous moroseness … headlong wrath … the utmost audacity toward your person … most suspicious imputations and a most bitter itch of writing."
4. The mitigation — it was "the fury of an uncontrolled mind, an exuberance of choler, a youthful fault, curiosity, with precipitancy of judgement", "but the intent to defame was absent"; he never spoke ill of Tycho to others, only wrote to Tycho "out of a false imagination."
5. The retraction and submission — he comes "a suppliant," begs a "Christian reconciliation," and "retracts all and each" of his injurious words, "void, false, and impossible to prove," confessing his rashness "to God, the Knower of hearts."
6. The promise of future obedience — to abstain, to be admonished if he errs ("you will find me compliant"), and to bind Tycho "by every kind of service."
- Greek. Deo καρδιογνώστῃ — "to God, the Knower of hearts" (kardiognōstēs, Acts 1:24 / 15:8); kept verbatim, translated.
- Persons & place. Johannes Kepler (sender); Tycho Brahe (M. T. = Magnificentia Tua); Tycho's wife and children (the familia); Benátky (the scene of the quarrel; Kepler now at Prague — "Your Magnificence came first to Prague").
- Calendar / date. The edition and Opera Omnia place it in April 1600 (New Style); the day is not given in the source (Kepler's autograph) and is left unspecified in the header (historically dated to early April 1600). Marked [N.S.].
