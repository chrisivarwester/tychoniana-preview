The original

[TYCHO AD SOPHIAM BRAHE].

KIERRE søster Sophi, Gud allmechtigste werre nu och allthiid hoß dig och beuare dig naadelligen och well fra allt det, som dig skadellig kan werre, och nest megen thacksigelse for allt beuist gode, for huilket du althiid skall finde mig som din hulde broder, der gierne giør huis dig thill aere och gode kan werre. Bekom ieg din skriffuelße met min søn Tyge nogit epther nøtaarsthag, endog du icke da haffde bekommit det lange breff, som ieg skreff dig thill met hannom, for du icke da warst thilstede wdj Danmarck, menß ieg tencker well, att du det siden haffuer bekomit och nochsom forfarit deraff min leylighedt, saa ieg achter icke fornøden att repetere nogit deraff vdigien. Och fornam ieg aff samme din skriffuellse, att du warst wddragen y Lantheholsten och thill Hamborg met din søn Thage, hannom widre paa sin forfremning thill Heidelberg eller Basell att hensende, huilket mig war kiert att høre, att han en gang kunde komme vd och forsøge sig, och haffuer hand schreffuit mig thill nogitt for jull fra Heidelberg och ladet mig wide, att hannom der lidet well och att han achtid sig met skolmestern nu paa foraarid thill Bassel, och ieg skreff hannom beskening och min mening thillbage vdigien, och commenderit hannom die fornembste lerde mend, som ieg der war bekient medt. Han lod mig ocsaa wiide, hor wit du følte hannom och huem der kom thill dig och hort y siiden droge, dog han wiste icke nogen eyglig besked, hor wit det warede, menß du giffuer mig well selff baade det och andit y din skriffuelße thillkiende.

Och som du formelder yblant andit y samme dit breff om hueß ord du och Mercurius haffuer [hafft] met hinanden wdj Landskrone, och han derudjblant skulde haffde sagt nogit om keysern, som dig icke skulde behage, huilket ieg gierne wille wiide huad dett war, thi ieg wed inted, han met sandhedt kunde sige om hanß keys. may., som dig for min skyld kunde ilde behage. Ieg tacker den gode Gudt, som haffuer forsønit mig saa god en from och mild herre vdj hannom, saa att ieg gierne lader mig nøye dermedt. Hanss maytt. beuisser mig icke alleniste allt gott och lader mig intid fatiß, menß occesaa haffuer naadigst aff sig selff lige som en faderlige omhu for mig, huilket hanß may. lod nocksom paakenne y denne forgangen aars store søgdom och skrøffuellighed, som wanckede her vdj landit, thi hanß may. haffde nogle gange bod hid och lod forfare, horledis det haffde sig met mig och mine, och wilde, dersom mig saa behagede, att ieg begaff mig medt mine hen thill Wien y Osterich, hor hanß may. wilde vdj thiide lade forordne mig god verrelße och vnderholdning met megen anden keyserlig øngist och naade, som hanß maytt. aff sin egen fromme och naadigst wille stedze beuiser mig, och lader forskaffue mig huad ieg begerer, och lader yblant medt forskicke mig wildbrad aff hiorte, hinde och wilde swin, och der thillig attskillige leffuendiß ferske fisk vdj kiøckennit. Haffuer och nøligen naadigst depoterit mig en bequem ladegaar her nehr wed slottid, hor ieg kand lade holde fennid och soe saa megit thill aff allehonde slags, som ieg mest kan offuerkomme; er och nogen smocke fiskewan derhos, will och wnde mig en smock haffue och wingaar her wed slotid att haffue nøtte aff och forløste mig y. Saa att hanß may. giør mig aff sin egen naadigst wille mehre thill gode, end ieg selff thortte begierre, endog hanß may. haffuer endnu inted sønderlig set aff min handell, thi mine fornemste jnstrumenter erre endnu thillbage vdj Tydskland, for die icke kunde komme hid op formedelst Elben førend wintern er offuerstanden. Nu haffuer hanß may. paa nø skreffuit thill Magdeburg derudom, saa att ieg wentter dennom met første mulighed. Siden achter hanß may. sig hid thill slottid att besee dennom och min anden handell, bade met distelering och andit, thi hanß may. haffuer en sønderlige løst och behagellighed thill allt saadant. Och thillspurte hanß may. for nogen wger siden sin øffuerste secreterer, h. Baruitio, som er hanß factotum, met huilken han mest gider thallt, om han inted haffde aff mig, som han kunde lade hannom see; da sagde han, att ieg nølig haffde sent hannom en aff mine bøyer, som icke endnu war alldelis ferdig trøckt, och wilde wiide hanß raad, om ieg motte didicere hanß may. den vnderdanigst; daa motte han strax hentte hanom samme bog, som kaldis Astronomiae Instauratae progymnasmata, och keysern haffde den saa hoß sig lenger end y fiorten dage, och leste thit och megit der y, och behagit hannom saare well, och befoll att sige mig, att han enderlig wille haffue, att ieg ingen anden skulde dedicere den end hanß maytt. selff, och skulde sette derudj for alle die anden preueleger en epistel thill hanß maytt. selff, och att ieg skulle lade thengne neden for titellen, att den war begønt vdj Danmarck, menß fuldkommit och vdgaait her paa Benatky y sitt kongerige Behemen. Och lod ocsaa sige mig, huad werß hanß may. willde haffue wdhuggen y marmullsteen offuer die døre paa die thuende portaller, som giørris thill hueß hanß may. laader her bøge for mig, det ene thill hueß der muriß thill itt sønderligt obseruatorium att sette alle mine jnstrumenter ordentlich och paa ree vdj, saa ad itt huert kan haffue sitt vnderskedlig rom att staa vdj, det andit thill itt sønderligt laboratorium, saa att hanß may. kan paa den ene side gange ned aff det huß, som staar ledigt thill hanß maytt. att werre wdj, naar han kommer hid, och gange saa igienom dette alltsammen och besee huad hanß maytt. løster. Och ieg kan occsaa aff dett andit huß, standendiß paa den anden siide, huilket ieg alldeliß haffuer inde, gange ned igienom laboratoriumit och siden paa ree thill alle jnstrumenterne. Och haffuer skriffueren, som stander for regenskaffuit, beregnit, att hueß allerede her thill saadant er giort, skulle haffue kostid icke langt fra thuthussend thaller, saa att dett dog icke nehr hellten er ferdigt for winterenß skyld, som paakom och war meget horer end den pleier att werre her vdj landit, saa man icke endnu well kan mure vde y lufften. Saadant formelder ieg, paa det du kanst wijde, att det seder ingenlunde saa sammen, som Mercurius wille, att du skulde tro, och war enda megit andit att berette derimod, om pennen kunde det tholle och det icke wilde bliffue forlangt. Och det han sagde, att tho aff die, som warre mine beste wenner y keyserenß gaar, die warre affsatt och vdj wnaade, det er lige saa probatum, som det andit, thi keisern haffuer ingen aff sine raad affsatt, er icke heller saa lett thill att giørre saadant, om die end skønt kunde forskylde dett, saa att ieg icke kunde wiide, huem eller huad hand mente dermed, andit end att han kunde sige nogit, som dig kunde fortrøde, och danne det epther sin egen affect. Keiserens och rigens vicecanceller, her Coraducius, som skreff først epther mig aff keyserens befalling, haffuer mesten parten werrit her aff landitt y en legation for keysern thill Rom, och der hand først for nogen vger siden war komen thill keysern wdjgien thill en bø wed naffn Pilsen, 15 mille herfra, hor keiseren haffuer werrit och endnu er siden sist y augusto y dette forgangene aar for søgdom skyld, som vdj Prag och ellers mesten alle wegne haffuer suarligen regierit, och der ieg nogit epther same her Coraducius war ankommen haffde en aff mine folck did thill Pilsen med nogit, som keysern skulde haffue, gick same min absender thill her Coraducium och grøsid hanom paa mine wegne och congratulerid hannom, att han war saa løkelligen och well aff den lange reise kommen hid hiem ygien. Huad han da suarede, kanst du forfare aff denne hoßliggendis fortengnelße, som er vddragen aff itt breff, den samme, som ieg vdskickede, skreff mig thill medlerthid menß han laa der. Och denne h. Coraducius er den fornembste aff keyserns inderste raad, som allt det rømiske riges første bestillinger ligger paa; huad han siger och will haffue frem, giører die andre icke lettelig imodt, ei heller thager keysern nogens andere ord derfor, naar en thingest behoffuer hannom selff well, saa ad Mercurius epther sin angeborne wstadig complex slog feill daa y sin thallenhedt. Det andit, som du skriffuer om den, der skulle haffue bekommit de bøyer du weest, och nu bereter anderledis end thillforn, derpaa giør ieg min gietzing, att dett seder anderlediß sammen, och adt de første ord erre throlliger, de siste kun att komme aff det andit hussis betødelser, store och waffladellig oefftro [?], saa att alle baade hielper, yhor ringe die erre, huilket man for att haffue gedult met och dessimulere, dog kanst du well thill sin thiid laade so himellige forfare formedelst nogen anden, horledes det seder sammen dermedt, thi dersom bøyerne er der, som hand sagde, findis die well der endnu; en aff die høylerte vdj Kiøbenhaffn pleier att forware nøiellen derthill, saa att dette er lettelligen att forfare. Det er mig lige got, hor samme bøyer erre, nar mine auffuendsmen och wuenner kun icke haffuer faait dennom, dog saa ieg helst, att du haffde dennom y din foruaring. Den samme wilde icke werdis thill att suare mig paa mit breff, som ieg skreff hanom thill med min søn, for ieg trode hanom well frem for nogen anden derudj raadit, och erlangit det ocsaa, att herr Baruitius skreff hanom thill epther keyse[r]ns befalling, paa mine weigne, huilket hand dog ellers icke lettelligen giør; hanom wille hand heller inted suare. Endog han sagde thill min søn, att hand wilde sende breffuene, die ieg skulle haffue, vd thil hertzog Vldrigß kanseller docter Bordingius, saa haffuer ieg dog endnu intedt fornumit derthill, menß det maa saa pasere; han haffuer saa mange breffue, som han bekomer aff mig. Der wille icke heller den lille Mons Bertelsen fordemøde sig att suare mig nogit paa mit bref, som ieg fortroeligen haffde skreffuit hannom thill, horudom ieg och nogit formeltte y min siste skriffuelse thill dig; kandske hand torde icke for den forbemelte planetis skyld, huilken formedelst sin wstadighed och wfixitet er icke att bøgge paa, menß tør slage hannom feil, førend hand troer det.

Det du skriffuer om Titan, saa er det mig kiert att høre, att han kunde komme thillrette met hueß hand sig haffuer forretagit, och wille ieg gierne wijde ret besken derom, horledis det sad sammen dermet. Jeg haffuer inted endnu kundid fornummit, att Hans Ernst skulde haffue skreffuet keysern nogit thill derom, och war det icke heller radelligt, att man giorde saadant, førend det war aldelis nochsom proberit, att konsten war wiß, och att der war nogen fordell hoß, thi der haffuer werrit saa mange, som haffuer giffuit saadant forre hoß keysern, och dog inted hafft paa sig, saa hand tror det icke nu letelligen, eyheller kunde hanß inderste raad, som leser hues breffue hannom thillskriffuis, well fordrage, att hand ganger om met saadant, eptherdj der er sked stor bekaastning thill forgeffuis thillforn derudoffuer. Och en ell[er] 2 aff dennom haffuer macht att thage breffuene op, som hannom thillskriffuis, och dersom die indeholder nogit, som dennom icke behager, daa thier die stille dermedt och lader hannom inted faa det att wiide; det sker well y støre handell end dette kan werre. Derfor om saadant war wist, och slet ingen feil derpaa, daa skulde en, som hans may. gad thallt met och trode well, sigen hans may. det muntligen, saa ingen wiste deraff, eller och det skulde skriffuis hans may. thill, indelugt y en sedell vdj itt beseillet breff, saa for die andre inted att wijde, huad sedellen indeholder, om det behaget hannom, menß han lader dennom kun siden faa breffuit att see, om hand will. Saaledis pleier ieg att giøre, nar ieg haffuer nogit att giffue hans may. thillkiende, som enten hans may. selff eller mig [er] macht paa liggendis; thi hans may. will det saa haffue, och der maa ingen thage mit breff op, naar der staar skreffuit vden paa: Ad Caesareas Maiestatis manus proprias. Derfor wiste ieg well god lempe och rod derthill, att hans maytt. skulde fange ret besken derom att wijde, saa ingen skulde merke det, dersom ieg war thillforn nochsom wiß derpaa, att der war slet ingen mangell y sagen, thi ellers thorde ieg slet inted giffue det forre, enten muntligen eller skrifftligen; thi hans may. thror den icke mehre well, som engang falerer hannom, for hand er fix och bestandig y hanß handell. Die wijde well, att ieg vnderthiiden y saa made skriffuer hans may. thill, och hans may. det saa will haffue, derfor motte ieg nochsom høre nu ieg war nøligen vdj Prag, baade hos die høye och lauffue, ligesom ieg skulle haffue skreffuit hans may. thill och raad hannom aff, att hand icke skulde komme thill Prag førend paaske war gangen, thi der war fare hos. Och det kunde icke hielpe, att ieg sagde ney derfor, thi det er y sandhed icke sked, ieg saa selff gierne, att hans may. wilde snart komme did; die mente, att ieg icke wilde besta det, for hand haffde forbød mig att lade andre wijde saadant, och somme mente, att det war ret giørt, att ieg warit hannom ad, hues ieg wiste. Somme, der haffuer profit och gaffn aff, naar hand er thill Prag, fortrød det och tog mig det thill møstøcke, saa att ieg matte lade vndskulde mig thill hoffue, att saadant inted war sked, for die andre raad skyld, der er, som helder wilde werre wdj Prag end ligge der saa lenge thill Pilsen, som keysern er, thi die haffuer icke der saa god gelegenheid. Menß folck skall thalle, for fee haffuer icke lert det.

Det Titan lod skrifftlig thillbøde kongen der y landit kønsten, war icke forsicktellig giort aff hannom, wden hand haffde nogen anden mening dermed; ieg wiste well, att bode han och die andre, som hand mest tror, skulle kun werre spocsk derpaa och haffe vidunder deraff, och Titan ingen suar bekome der paa igien, thi naar personen er suspect och forhadit, da er det suspect och ilde tagit huad den forregiffuer, om det war endskønt ret och sandingen probatum est. Jeg willde, att du sende mig en copi aff samme Titans suplicas for ieg wilde wijde, huad hand derudj haffde erindret kongen. War ieg som hand, da wilde ieg inted bemøde dennom mehre, och saa litt thencke paa dennom, som die paa mig. Ieg haffuer y lang thiid ingen breffue bekommit fra hannom, endog ieg to eller tre gange haffuer skreffuit hanom thill, wed icke heller thillwisse, hor han nu er, menß du skriffuer mig well thill allt, huiß dig er wittelligt om hannom och hans handell, thi ieg lengis effter att wijde dett. Och skriff mig occsaa thill allt, hues du west, som mig kan angaa, och hues der siges vdj landit, bode aff wenner och wuenner, siden die haffuer fa[n]git min leylighed och wilkaar att wijde, anderledis end die kandske thillforn haffde ment. Och lad mig ocsaa wijde, horledis det haffuer sig med voris venner och kønninger; och dersom nogen will skriffue mig nogit thill, da lad denom wijde, att dig nu stedis bod, huilket du ocsaa kanst giffue woris søster Margrette wdj thiide thillkiende, om hun will skriffue mig nogen suar paa mit siste breff, och lad mig wiide, horledis det ganger hindes øngste søner, siiden die er komen thill hoffe, och ocsesaa om voriß moster datter sønner, die vnge Longer, som ieg hører ocsaa att werre vdj gaaren. Gud beuare dennom alle fra det, som skadelligt kand werre. Och forglem ellers inted andit aff hues du west, som ieg wille wiide, som du icke heller pleier att giørre, thi du west well, att ieg well gieder lest lange breffue heller end att leye vdj kort, och eptherdj att ieg ocsaa wed, att du kanst fordrage det, skriffuer ieg dig diß witløfftiger thill, thi herr ganger icke huer dag bod emellom. Dog will ieg icke nu lenger opholde dig, mens nu och allthiid haffue dig Gud allmechtigste befallit, hand beuare dig och dine naadelligen och well fran allt ont; giør well och sig voris morbrøder sønner och døttre met alle andre voris gode wenner mange gode netter paa mine wegne. Claus Bilde Stensøn war her hos mig paa en fieringaars thiid att giøre, siden drog han ind wdj Franckerig, och fuldis mester Nielß Hammer met hanom, som var komen hid y same thid, som kan sprokit och kenner nogen aff folken der vdj landit. Hand loffuede mig att wilde flittelligen tage vare paa Clauß och ramme hans gaffn och beste hor hannom muegligt er, siden haffuer ieg ingen bod bekommit fraa dennom, och haffuer ieg skreffuit hans moder itt breff till derom, huilket du kanst forskaffue hinder. Sig ocsaa jomfru Dorete mange gode netter paa mine vegne.

Iterum et saepius quam felicissime vale.
Daterit paa det keyserlige slott Benatky den 21 dag martij, som er den 11 hoß eder, anno 1600.

[Tycho to Sophie Brahe.]

Dear sister Sophie, may God Almighty be now and always with you, and guard you graciously and well from all that may be harmful to you; and next, with much thanksgiving for all the kindness you have shown — for which you shall always find me your devoted brother, who gladly does whatever may be for your honour and good. I received your letter by my son Tyge somewhat after New Year's Day, though you had not then received the long letter I wrote to you by him, because you were not at that time present in Denmark; but I well believe that you have since received it, and learned sufficiently from it of my situation, so that I think it not needful to repeat any of it again. And I gathered from that same letter of yours that you had travelled into Holstein and to Hamburg with your son Tage, to send him onward, for his advancement, to Heidelberg or Basel — which I was glad to hear, that he might at last get out into the world and try himself; and he wrote to me somewhat before Christmas from Heidelberg, and let me know that he did well there, and that he intended to go with his schoolmaster now in the spring to Basel; and I wrote him back word and my opinion in reply, and commended to him the foremost learned men with whom I was acquainted there. He also let me know how far you accompanied him, and who came to you, and whither you then journeyed — though he knew no exact account of how long it lasted; but you yourself will surely make known to me both that and other things in your letter.

And whereas you mention, among other things in that same letter of yours, the words you and Mercurius had with one another at Landskrona — and that among them he was said to have said something about the Emperor that did not please you, which I should gladly know what it was; for I know nothing that he could truthfully say about His Imperial Majesty that could, for my sake, displease you. I thank the good God, who has provided me so good, pious, and gentle a lord in him, that I gladly content myself therewith. His Majesty not only shows me every kindness and lets me lack nothing, but also has, of his own grace, as it were a fatherly care for me — which His Majesty let be sufficiently felt in this past year's great sickness and pestilence that went about here in the land; for His Majesty several times sent here and had inquiry made how it stood with me and mine, and wished, if it so pleased me, that I should betake myself with my people away to Vienna in Austria, where His Majesty would in good time have good lodging and maintenance arranged for me, with much other imperial favour and grace — as His Majesty, of his own goodness and most gracious will, constantly shows me, and has procured for me whatever I desire, and among other things has game sent to me, of hart, hind, and wild boar, and therewith sundry living fresh fish for the kitchen. He has also lately most graciously assigned me a convenient grange here near the castle, where I may keep cattle and sows, and as much of all kinds as I can manage; there is also some fine fishing-water with it; and he will grant me too a fine garden and vineyard here by the castle, to have profit from and to take my pleasure in. So that His Majesty, of his own most gracious will, does me more good than I myself durst desire — even though His Majesty has as yet seen nothing in particular of my work, for my chief instruments are still back in Germany, since they could not come up here by the Elbe before the winter is past. Now His Majesty has written anew to Magdeburg about it, so that I expect them at the first opportunity. Afterwards His Majesty intends to come here to the castle, to view them and my other work, both with distilling and otherwise, for His Majesty has a special delight and pleasure in all such things. And some weeks ago His Majesty asked his chief secretary, Lord Barvitius — who is his factotum, with whom he most likes to talk — whether he had nothing of mine that he could let him see; then he said that I had lately sent him one of my books, which was not yet quite finished printing, and that I wished his advice whether I might most humbly dedicate it to His Majesty; whereupon he had at once to fetch him that same book, which is called Astronomiae Instauratae Progymnasmata; and the Emperor kept it by him longer than a fortnight, and read often and much in it, and it pleased him exceedingly, and he commanded that I be told that he absolutely wished I should dedicate it to none other than His Majesty himself, and that I should set in it, before all the other privileges, an epistle to His Majesty himself, and that I should have it noted below the title that it was begun in Denmark, but completed and issued here at Benátky in his kingdom of Bohemia. And he also let me be told what verses His Majesty wished to have carved in marble over the doors on the two portals that are being made for the building His Majesty is having built here for me: the one for what is being raised as a special observatory, to set all my instruments in, in order and in a row, so that each may have its own distinct space to stand in; the other for a special laboratory — so that His Majesty may, on the one side, go down from the house that stands ready for His Majesty to lodge in when he comes here, and so pass through all this and view whatever His Majesty pleases. And I too can, from the other house standing on the other side, which I have entirely to myself, go down through the laboratory and then in a row to all the instruments. And the clerk in charge of the accounts has reckoned that what has already been done here toward this must have cost not far from two thousand thalers, so that it is yet not nearly half finished, on account of the winter, which came on and was much harder than it is wont to be here in this land, so that one cannot yet well do masonry out in the open air. I report such things so that you may know that matters by no means stand as Mercurius would have you believe; and there were yet much else to relate against it, if the pen could bear it and it would not grow too long. And as for his saying that two of those who were my best friends at the Emperor's court were dismissed and in disgrace — that is just as well "proven" as the rest; for the Emperor has dismissed none of his councillors, nor is it so easy to do such a thing, even should they deserve it; so that I could not tell whom or what he meant by it, other than that he could say something to vex you, and shape it after his own bent. The Emperor's and the Empire's Vice-Chancellor, Lord Coraducius — who first wrote for me, by the Emperor's command — has for the most part been away from the country on a legation for the Emperor to Rome; and when, some weeks ago, he had first come back to the Emperor, to a town by the name of Pilsen, 15 [Danish] miles from here, where the Emperor has been and still is since the end of August in this past year, on account of the sickness which has raged grievously in Prague and almost everywhere else — and when, somewhat after this same Lord Coraducius had arrived, I had sent one of my people to Pilsen with something the Emperor was to have, this same envoy of mine went to Lord Coraducius and greeted him on my behalf, and congratulated him that he had come home again so happily and well from the long journey. What he then answered, you may learn from this enclosed memorandum, excerpted from a letter that the same man I dispatched wrote to me meanwhile, while he was lying there. And this Lord Coraducius is the foremost of the Emperor's innermost council, upon whom all the chief business of the Roman Empire rests; what he says and wishes to advance, the others do not easily oppose; nor does the Emperor take anyone else's word against it, when a matter closely concerns himself — so that Mercurius, after his inborn inconstant temperament, missed the mark in his talkativeness. The other thing you write, about the one who was to have received the books you know of, and who now reports otherwise than before: on that I make my guess that the matter stands otherwise, and that the first words are the more trustworthy, the later ones springing only from the other house's promptings — great and unceasing self-conceit [reading uncertain] — so that all are helpers in it, however slight they be; which one must have patience with and dissemble. Yet you may well in due time have it secretly inquired into, through someone else, how the matter stands; for if the books are there, as he said, they will surely still be found there — one of the learned men in Copenhagen is wont to keep the key to it, so that this is easy to ascertain. It is all the same to me where those books are, so long as my enviers and enemies have not got them — though I should best like that you had them in your keeping. The same man would not deign to answer me on my letter, which I wrote to him by my son (for I trusted him well, above any other in the council), and I obtained it, too, that Lord Barvitius wrote to him by the Emperor's command, on my behalf — which he otherwise does not easily do; him too he would rather not answer. Although he told my son that he would send the letters I was to have out to Duke Ulrich's chancellor, Doctor Bording, yet I have still heard nothing of it; but let it pass — he has so many letters that he gets from me. Nor would little Mogens Bertelsen deign to answer me anything on my letter, which I had written to him in confidence, concerning which I also mentioned something in my last letter to you; perhaps he durst not, for the sake of the aforesaid planet, which, by reason of its inconstancy and unfixedness, is not to be built upon, but may fail him before he expects it.

What you write about Titan — it is dear to me to hear that he could come to rights with what he has undertaken; and I should gladly know the true account of it, how the matter stood. I have not yet been able to learn that Hans Ernst should have written anything to the Emperor about it; nor would it be advisable that one do such a thing before it were quite sufficiently proven that the art was certain, and that there was some profit in it; for there have been so many who have proposed such things to the Emperor, and yet had nothing in them, so that he does not now easily believe it; nor could his innermost council, who read whatever letters are written to him, well endure that he busy himself with such things, since great expense has been incurred in vain over this before. And one or two of them have authority to open the letters written to him; and if they contain anything that does not please them, they keep silent about it and let him learn nothing of it — that happens even in greater matters than this can be. Therefore, if such a thing were certain, and no fault at all in it, then someone whom His Majesty liked to talk with and trusted well should tell His Majesty it by word of mouth, so that no one knew of it; or else it should be written to His Majesty, enclosed in a note within a sealed letter, so that the others should not know what the note contains, if it pleased him — while he afterwards lets them merely see the [outer] letter, if he will. Thus I am wont to do, when I have something to make known to His Majesty that is of importance either to His Majesty himself or to me; for His Majesty will have it so, and no one may open my letter when there stands written on the outside: To His Imperial Majesty's own hands. Therefore I should well know good means and counsel for His Majesty to come to the true account of it, so that no one should notice it — provided I were beforehand sufficiently sure of it, that there was no defect at all in the matter; for otherwise I durst by no means propose it, either by mouth or in writing, for His Majesty no longer trusts well the man who once fails him, since he is fixed and constant in his dealings. They know well that I sometimes write to His Majesty in this way, and that His Majesty will have it so; therefore I had to hear enough, now that I was lately in Prague, both from high and low, as though I had written to His Majesty and advised him against coming to Prague before Easter was past, because there was danger about. And it could not help that I said no to it, for it has truly not happened: I myself would gladly that His Majesty would soon come thither. They thought I would not admit it, because he had forbidden me to let others know such things; and some thought it rightly done that I should warn him of what I knew. Some, who have profit and gain when he is at Prague, resented it and took it amiss of me, so that I had to excuse myself at court — that no such thing had happened — for the sake of the other councillors, [some of] whom would rather be in Prague than lie so long there at Pilsen where the Emperor is, for they have not such good accommodation there. But people must talk, for cattle have not learned how.

That Titan offered the King in that country the art in writing was not prudently done of him — unless he had some other purpose by it; I knew well that both he and the others whom he most trusts would only be mocking about it and make a jest of it, and that Titan would get no answer to it in return; for when the person is suspect and hated, then whatever he proposes is suspect and ill-received, even though it were right and the truth demonstrated. I should wish that you send me a copy of that same supplication of Titan's, for I should like to know what he had reminded the King of in it. Were I as he, I would trouble them no more, and think as little of them as they of me. I have for a long time received no letters from him, though I have written to him two or three times; nor do I know for certain where he now is; but you will surely write me all that is known to you about him and his affairs, for I long to know it. And write me also all that you know that may concern me, and what is said in the land, both by friends and enemies, now that they have learned my situation and circumstances — otherwise than they perhaps had thought before. And let me also know how it stands with our friends and kindred; and if anyone wishes to write me anything, let them know that a messenger is now available to you — which you may also make known in time to our sister Margrethe, if she will write me any answer to my last letter; and let me know how it goes with her youngest sons, now that they have come to court, and also about our mother's-sister's-daughter's sons, the young Langes, who I hear are also at the court. God guard them all from what may be harmful. And forget nothing else of what you know that I should wish to know — as indeed you are not wont to do — for you know well that I would gladly read long letters rather than be put off with short ones; and since I also know that you can bear it, I write to you the more at length, for here a messenger does not pass between us every day. Yet I will not now detain you longer, but now and always have commended you to God Almighty; may He guard you and yours graciously and well from all evil. Fare well, and bid our maternal uncles' sons and daughters, with all our other good friends, many good nights on my behalf. Claus Bilde, Sten's son, was here with me for about a quarter-year's stay; then he went into France, and Master Niels Hammer accompanied him — who had come here at the same time, who knows the language and is acquainted with some of the people there in that country. He promised me that he would diligently look after Claus and seek his advantage and best wherever possible for him; since then I have had no word from them; and I have written his mother a letter about it, which you can convey to her. Bid Mistress Dorthe, too, many good nights on my behalf.

Again, and oftentimes, fare most happily.
Dated at the imperial castle Benátky, the 21st day of March — which is the 11th with you — the year 1600.

Apparatus

  • Edition of record. Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia vol. VIII (Epistolae). MS witness: A = Basel, Universitätsbibliothek, Autographensammlung Vol. 35, Fol. 42–51, marked Kladde — i.e. Tycho's own draft/concept, not the dispatched fair copy. (The heading [Tycho ad Sophiam Brahe] is the editor's.)
  • Date / place. The dateline is itself a textbook O.S./N.S. statement: "den 21 dag martij, som er den 11 hoß eder" — "the 21st of March, which is the 11th with you," Tycho spelling out the 10-day Julian/Gregorian gap between his Imperial-court (New Style) dating and his sister's Danish (Old Style) reckoning. The catalogue label "1600. 21 marts n.s." agrees. Written from Benátky nad Jizerou (Arx Benatica). This is the Danish family-register parallel to the Latin letter brahe-t_16000324002 (to Niels Krag, 24 March N.S.) — the same week, the same news (plague, the Vienna offer, the instruments stranded in Germany, the Progymnasmata dedication, Coraduz, the Prague-before-Easter rumour), here in the intimate vernacular.
  • Recipient. Sophie Brahe (1559–1643), Tycho's youngest sister — the learned "Urania" of the family (astronomy, medicine, horticulture, genealogy), Tycho's lifelong confidante and sometime observing companion. Widow of Otte Thott of Eriksholm.
  • Persons named.
  • Tyge / "my son" — Tycho Brahe the Younger, the courier.
  • Tage — Tage Ottesen Thott (1580–1658), Sophie's son by Otte Thott, sent via Holstein/Hamburg to study at Heidelberg and Basel; later a leading Scanian magnate.
  • Mercurius — a derisive code-name for an unidentified person who spoke with Sophie at Landskrona and disparaged the Emperor/Tycho's standing; Tycho rebuts his gossip point by point and puns on the planet Mercury — "after his inborn inconstant temperament … the aforesaid planet, which, by reason of its inconstancy and unfixedness, is not to be built upon." Identity not recoverable from the text.
  • the Emperor / His Majesty — Rudolf II (here at Pilsen, sheltering from plague), whose "fatherly care," gifts of game and fish, grant of a grange, garden and vineyard, and reading of the Progymnasmata are recounted with evident warmth.
  • Lord Barvitius — Johann Anton Barvitius, Rudolf II's chief secretary and factotum (cf. brahe-t_16000324002).
  • Lord Coraduz (Coraducius) — Rudolf von Coraduz, Imperial Vice-Chancellor (Reichsvizekanzler), who first wrote summoning Tycho to Bohemia on the Emperor's command; back from a legation to Rome, now at Pilsen; "the foremost of the Emperor's innermost council." (The standing pairing Coraduz/Barvitius recurs across the imperial-period correspondence.)
  • "the one who was to have received the books you know of" — the keeper of Tycho's presentation books left locked in Copenhagen (the key held by "one of the learned men in Copenhagen") — the same book-deposit business as the Holger Rosenkrantz letter brahe-t_15980810001; the man now reports inconsistently. Left unnamed by Tycho.
  • Duke Ulrich's chancellor, Doctor Bording — chancellor of Duke Ulrich III of Mecklenburg (cf. brahe-t_15980816001), an intended forwarding-conduit for Tycho's letters.
  • little Mogens Bertelsen (Mons Bertelsen) — a Danish correspondent who failed to answer Tycho's confidential letter; not further identified.
  • Titan — an alchemical code-name for someone pursuing a "kønst" (the art = transmutation) and offering it in writing, by a supplication, to the King of Denmark. Given the alchemical register and Sophie Brahe's circle, this is most plausibly Erik Lange (Sophie's betrothed, the gold-seeking alchemist who beggared himself in the magnum opus); identification MEDIUM — kept as the code-name in the translation. Hans Ernst (unidentified) is named as a possible intermediary to the Emperor for the same "art."
  • the King — Christian IV of Denmark, to whom "Titan" addressed his supplication; Tycho foresees only mockery, since a "suspect and hated" petitioner gets no hearing "even though it were right and demonstrated."
  • sister Margrethe (Margrette) — another Brahe sister; her "youngest sons" newly at court.
  • "the young Langes" (die vnge Longer) — kinsmen (sons of a maternal cousin) at court; family of the Lange house (the same house as Erik Lange).
  • Claus Bilde, Sten's son (Claus Bilde Stensøn) — young Danish noble who lodged c. 3 months with Tycho, then travelled to France; Master Niels Hammer his tutor-companion.
  • Mistress Dorthe (jomfru Dorete) — a member of Sophie's household/kin, greeted in closing.
  • Bibliographic detail — the Progymnasmata dedication (important). Tycho reports Rudolf II's express commands regarding the Astronomiae Instauratae Progymnasmata (Tycho's great work on the 1572 nova and solar/lunar theory): (1) sole dedication to the Emperor himself, (2) an epistle to His Majesty placed before all the privileges, and (3) a title-note that the work was "begun in Denmark, but completed and issued at Benátky in his kingdom of Bohemia." A primary-source datum on the genesis of the printed Progymnasmata (which was in fact completed and printed posthumously, 1602, by Tycho's heirs at Prague). The "two portals" verses to be carved in marble over the observatory and laboratory doors are the same imperial commission described to Krag in brahe-t_16000324002 (the "Dania quae renuit" epigrams).
  • The confidential-letter protocol (a vivid procedural detail). Tycho describes how he by-passes the screening council: matters of importance go to the Emperor by word of mouth through a trusted intimate, or as a sealed note inside a sealed letter endorsed Ad Caesareae Maiestatis manus proprias ("to His Imperial Majesty's own hands"), which no councillor may open — and notes that one or two councillors otherwise have authority to open and suppress incoming letters. This privilege is the very thing that fuelled the (false) rumour, retailed in Prague "from high and low," that Tycho had astrologically advised the Emperor to stay away from plague-struck Prague until after Easter.
  • Latin tags. probatum (est) — "proven / demonstrated" (twice, used ironically of Mercurius's tales and earnestly of Titan's "art"); Ad Caesareae Maiestatis manus proprias — the letter-endorsement formula; the valediction Iterum et saepius quam felicissime vale — "Again, and oftentimes, fare most happily." Proverb: "folk must talk, for cattle have not learned how" — Tycho's wry dismissal of court gossip.
  • Reading crux. "de siste kun att komme aff det andit hussis betødelser, store och waffladellig oefftro [?]" — the editor marks [?] at oefftro; the clause (roughly "the later words spring only from the other house's promptings — great and unceasing self-conceit/credulity") is textually uncertain. Rendered with a flagged "[reading uncertain]" rather than smoothed.
  • Correction. The Emperor's Vice-Chancellor was rendered “Lord Coraduz” throughout the English (4 occurrences); both the source transcription and Dreyer's print (TBOO XIV pp.179–180) consistently give “Coraducius”. Corrected to preserve cross-reference to this figure under his attested form.
