NOBILISSIMO ET VIRTVTE SAPIENTIA INGENIOSA ERVDITAQVE DOCTRINA, CLARISSIMO VIRO, DOMINO TYCHONI BRAHE, DOMINO DE KNVDSTRVP, EQVITI DANO, MATHEMATICORUM NOSTRO SECULO OMNIUM PRÆSTANTISSIMO, ETC. DOMINO SUO CUM OBSERUANTIA COLENDO, ETC. S. P.

RESPONDISSEM in superioribus nundinis Francofordianis ad literas tuas, Vir Nobiliss. et Mathematicorum omnium praestantissime, nisi ea, de quibus in posterioribus Literis scribis, mihi fuissent impedimento; aut nisi in Aula vanissimis saepe rebus tempus vanissime esset transigendum. Verum moram illam maiori iam copia compensabo. De Cometa igitur vt primo loco adhuc paucis tecum agam, non leui tu quidem argumento aberis, consentaneum fuisse, vt Cometa aliquam, licet vix sensibilem, habuerit Parallaxin, cum Iupiter et Saturnus etiam Parallaxin habeant. Scio Hypotheses Coperniceas id omnino flagitare. Verum quomodocunque examinem meas Obseruationes, nullam tamen sensibilem inuenire possum. Potuissem eodem insensibilis Parallaxeos argumento vti ad confirmandam meam sententiam, qua ipsum Sphaerae Saturni attribui; cum Saturni Parallaxis nostros sensus facile effugiat, vixque tertiam Minuti partem attingat. Verum nolui incurrere in reprehensionem eorum, qui cum nihil de arte Mathematica intelligant, imperite tamen et temerarie plane clamitare non desinunt, nos absurdissima quaeque asserere. Sed quoniam te mearum Obseruationum Aristarchum habeo, nihil posthac id genus hominum curabo, sed forti et praesenti animo ea in medium producam, quae et per Obseruationes et per certissimas Geometriae demonstrationes vera inuenio. Quod autem miraris, quomodo tunc maximam Solis obliquitatem 23. Grad. 30. Min. assumserim, nec eandem tecum 23. Gr. 31⅓. M. ex Obseruationibus Illustriss. Principis nostri colligere potuerim: in eo facile veniam mereor. Vidi enim Obseruationes Illustrissimi Principis nostri praebere modo Altitudinem Solis maximam Solstitialem 62. Grad. 10. Min. modo 62. Gr. 12. Min. etiam eodem Quadrante: Vnde facile patuit, minori cura eas fuisse institutas, quam magnitudo negotij postulabat. Praetereâ nondum mihi constabat vera nostri Poli Eleuatio, quam scis quantis laboribus tandem indagârim. Assumsi igitur eam Obliquitatem, quam tunc temporis veritati quam proxime consentaneam esse conijciebam, licet exactam et praecisam ex Obseruationibus nescirem. Inuenta autem vera Poli nostri Eleuatione, facile animaduerti, assignatam a te Zodiaci Obliquitatem ad Obseruationes Illustrissimi Principis nostri quam proxime accedere. Verum quod tunc temporis a te discere mihi necesse fuit, id iam ipse verissimum esse diligentissimis meis Obseruationibus adinueni. Inueni enim in proximo Solstitio Aestiuo maximam Solis Altitudinem meridianam exacte 62. Gr. 11 M. diligentissime. Vnde addita Parallaxi Solis et assumpta vera nostra Aequatoris Eleuatione part. 38. M. 41. tua Zodiaci Obliquitas resultat. In Solstitio hyemali datur hîc maxima Solis altitudo meridiana part. 15. M. 14. Verum tunc temporis Aër hîc longe densior et vaporosior existit, quam alijs anni temporibus, Refractioque Solis circa Brumam longe maior est quam Sole circa medium ♏ aut ♒ versante. Haec de Cometa deque intermixta Zodiaci Obliquitate. Et quoniam video, te residuum scripti mei de Cometa desiderare, eius adhuc tria capita ad te mitto, ex quibus quae mea sit de Cometis sententia, qualesque Hypotheses Astronomicas veras esse iudicem, facile intelliges. Egi de ijs rebus aliquo modo durius; quoniam duro ligno durus adhibendus est cuneus; nec hoc genus hominum prae anteceptis authoritatibus et prae stulta Artis Mathematicae ignorantia a suis nugis ineptissimis facile discedit. Deest adhuc caput de significatione Cometarum, imprimis commendationi Astronomiae destinatum; quod licet animo conceperim, propter alia tamen negotia ab Illustrissimo Principe nostro mihi demandata, calamo excipere non potui. Accedet tunc, cum scriptum illud ad incudem reuocauero. Nunc de materia Sphaerarum caelestium paulo plenius tecum agam. Recte tu quidem mecum statuis, materiam Sphaerarum caelestium, non esse duram aut imperuiam, sed liquidam et subtilem, motuique Planetarum facile cedentem. At in eo dissentimus, quod tu propter Refractiones diuersa Aetheris et Aëris Diaphana statuis h. e. in Sphaeris caelestibus non contineri Aërem Elementarem sed potius Aethera liquidissimum ab Aëre Elementari diuersum. At argumentum Refractionis, quod pro te militare existimas, hanc tuam sententiam prorsus expugnat et euertit. Si enim Refractio illa, quam per Obseruationes inuenimus, est a diuerso Aetheris et Aëris Elementaris Diaphano: fieri non potest, quin duret vsque ad verticem. Pone enim, circa Horizontem eam Refractionem esse vix trium Minutorum: Erit nihilominus in Altitudine 45. G. circiter 1½. M. cum Triangulorum mutatio et ratio minorem vix admittant. Etsi enim pro confirmatione tuae sententiae asseras, non adeo magnam esse Diaphanorum Aetheris et Aëris differentiam, sed quae successiue, vbi se inuicem contingunt, minuatur ita, vt suprema Aëris portio, Lunae Reuolutotionibus contigua, fere nihil differat ab ipso Aethere: Eo tamen ipso, quo asseris, Refractionem esse ob diuersa Aetheris et Aëris Diaphana prouenientem, eo inquam ipso concedis, Radios Solis aut Stellarum punctum sibi nihilominus in ea diuersorum Diaphanorum successiua combinatione et assimilatione eligere, in quo refrangantur: idque necessario semper eandem a centro Terrae distantiam retinebit, propterea quod successiua illa diuersorum Diaphanorum combinatio vbique aequaliter a Terra absit. Assumto itaque hoc Refractionis puncto vbique aequaliter a Terra distante, vide num Triangulorum rationes Refractionem illam in Altitudine 50. aut 60. Graduum euanescere permittant. Vides igitur Refractionum euanescentiam diuersa Aetheris et Aëris Diaphana concedere nullo modo posse. Quae itaque mea sit de Refractionibus his sententia, quoniam fortassis in Cometa non satis explicate a me proposita est, explicatius iam tibi aperiam. Inter Terram, vt scis, et inter Sphaeram Stellarum Fixarum nihil aliud contineri statuo quam Aërem septem Errantia sidera ambientem, propterea quod Refractiones non durent vsque ad verticem, sed vix, vt tu vis, vsque ad 30. Gradus. Hunc Aërem deinde Natura ipsa me sic docente, distinguo in crassiorem, et exhalationibus terrenis obnoxium, et in purum, qui exhalationibus istis non inficiatur. Huiusque meae distinctionis fundamentum sunt similiter Principia optica. Notum enim tibi est ex Alhazeno, ex Vitellione, ex Petro Nonio de Crepusculis, atque etiam oculorum testimonio, esse certum Aëris spatium, quod a radijs Solaribus in Crepusculis illuminetur et albescat; esse item vltra illud spatium Aërem, qui a radijs Solaribus non possit illuminari et albescere; Aliâs enim essent perpetua Crepuscula. Necesse igitur est, vt Aër ille, qui etiam serenissimo Caelo in Crepusculis albescit, sit a terrenis exhalationibus infectus et crassior redditus; aliâs enim radij Solares illum illuminare non possent; ille vero, qui non albescit, sit purus et a terrenis vaporibus non infectus: Quanquam non negauero, etiam in purum illum Aërem exhalationes, praesertim puriores posse interdum ascendere. Nec tamen hunc Aërem crassum per se Refractionem causare posse inuenio, sed ob multiplicationem. Quanquam enim spatium illud Aëris crassioris fere Semidiametrum Terrae a superficie Terrae attingat, vt ex dictis Opticis de Crepusculis manifestum est: Radios tamen Solis aut Stellarum vincere crassitiem hanc, nec ob identitatem Diaphanorum in ea punctum Refractionis inuenire posse animaduerto; at circa Horizontem et in situ decliuiori ob duplicationem illius spatij posse.

Nec enim id, vt arbitraris, meam sententiam euertit. Cogitetur igitur, vt antea dixi, AC nullam fere sensibilem proportionem, quae turbare praesens negotium possit, habere ad AH, ita vt cum connectantur HC puncta, angulus FCH perinde fere rectus sit ac si esset angulus CAH: quomodo Instrumentis in obseruando tanquam in centro mundi vti in Stellis fixis solemus. Iam abscindatur arcus HK, qui sit P. 30; et connectantur puncta K et C per rectam, quae secet superficiem extremam Aëris crassioris in O puncto: ponaturque similiter obseruator in S, cui K punctum distet ab Horizonte 29. gr. Quoniam igitur is, qui in C, nullam amplius inuenit Refractionem, ex Hypothesi: necesse est, vt nullum etiam Refractionis punctum sit inter K et C. At is, qui in S, sentit adhuc Refractionem puncti K, cum ei K sit in Altitudine 29. graduum, itidem ex Hypothesi. Cum igitur punctum Refractionis ei, qui in C, iam iam desierit; ei vero, qui in S, adhuc appareat; necesse est vt illud sit in vertice C. Quare manifestum est, lineam OC determinare quam proxime spatium, quo punctum Refractionis distat ab extrema superficie Aëris crassioris, et quam longe Radij crassum illum Aërem absque Refractione penetrare possunt. Dum igitur Stella est in Horizonte in puncto H, erit punctum Refractionis in N: dum in Q, erit punctum illud in R: semperque magis ac magis ad C accedet: vt ita in Horizonte a C puncto omnium longissime absit. Ex quo per 14. P. lib. 10. Vitellionis tanquam πόρισμα sequitur illud, quod in Obseruationibus verum reperitur: nimirum Refractiones circa Horizontem esse omnium maximas, posteaque semper magis ac magis diminui, donec puncto Refractionis in C constituto prorsus euanescunt. Quanquam id etiam esse possit a diuersa Aëris crassitie. Quo enim Aër propius ad Terram accedit, eo crassior et densior a perpetuis fumis et vaporibus redditur. Ex quibus omnibus manifestissimum, nec diuersa Aetheris et Aëris Diaphana esse, nec Refractiones aliunde, quam a crasso et vaporoso Aëre causari posse. Quin hoc ipsum, si nihil aliud, certe diuersae in diuersis locis Refractiones ostenderent. Verissimum enim est, hîc non tantas esse Refractiones, quantae sunt apud vos. Cum enim tu inueneris Stellarum Fixarum Refractionem in Altitudine 18. grad. esse apud vos duorum Minutorum: ipse ex Stellis Vrsae Maioris et alijs in eadem Altitudine eam hîc non maiorem inuenio quam vnius Minuti, adeo vt in Altitudine 23. Grad. vix quarta Minuti pars refrangatur. Solem quoque Caelo clarissimo et Radio forti inueni in Altitudine trium Graduum, cum abesset a visibili Horizonte nondum vnico Gradu, habere Refractionem vix 9⅓. Minutorum, seu addita Parallaxi, Minutorum 12⅓: quam tamen tu 28. Minutorum inuenisti. Quanquam Refractiones Solis pro diuersa Aëris constitutione manifeste mutentur, adeo vt in Bruma, vbi Aër hic nunquam fuliginibus et densa caligine caret, eam in Altitudine 15. Grad. repererim 7. Minutorum, habita scilicet Parallaxeos ratione. Vnde recte tu asseris, omnino serenum et purum Aërem ad negotium Refractionum rite tractandum requiri. Vides igitur manifeste, Refractiones eas non aliunde nisi ab Aëre crasso et denso esse, veramque esse Veterum sententiam, qua dixêre, Aërem in locis Borealioribus esse densiorem et crassiorem, quam in Australioribus. An vero extrema crassi Aëris superficies in locis Borealioribus longius a Terra absit, quam in Australioribus, valde vereor. De quo enim Nonius parte 2. P. 1. de Crepusculis dubitare videtur, nimirum Crepusculum terminari in alijs locis Sole 17. Grad. sub Horizonte latente, in alijs part. 18. in alijs part. 19: de eo mihi dubitandum non videtur, praesertim cum idem quoque Ptolemaeus in fine lib. 8. μεγαλ. συντάξ. asserat. Sed nostrum est, vt momenta temporum, quamprimum Aër albescere vesperi desinat aut mane incipiat, diligenter notemus, atque ex nostrarum Obseruationum collatione litem hanc dirimamus. Haec de arduo hoc et sublimi negotio paulo copiosius ad te perscribere volui: atque vt ea diligenter examines, deque ijs mecum conferre ne desinas, a te etiam atque etiam vehementissime peto. Nunc ad alia. De Latitudinibus Stellarum secundum mutationem Obliquitatis Zodiaci proportionaliter mutatis, eruditissime disseris. Nec enim alia via procedi dextre in eius rei confirmatione potuit, quam qua tu processisti: Propterea quod loca Stellarum Fixarum admodum perperam in Tabulis posita sint, et, vt autumo, nunquam a Ptolemaeo obseruata, sed tantum ex Hipparcho transcripta. Apparet ex multis locis Almagesti, et praecipue ex adducto cap. 3. lib. 7. Ptolemaeum minus curiose et nimis oscitanter negotium Stellarum Fixarum tractasse. Dum Vitebergae adhuc versarer, memini me simili modo examinare loca Stellarum in Canone Ptolemaico posita, cumque assumerem Longitudinem et Declinationem Reguli, prout a Ptolemaeo traduntur, prodijt Latitudo Reguli 6⅔. Min. non Borea, vt in Canone annotatur, sed Australis. Haec cum manifeste contraria sint, nec ab vllo possint conciliari: necesse est, vt vnum eorum sit falsum. At cum Regulus sit vnica Stella, cuius Obseruationem Ptolemaeus recitet, nec nostrae Obseruationes Longitudinem et Latitudinem a Ptolemaeo inuentas, admodum respuant: necessario Declinatio adducta erit falsissima. Sed cum Declinationis Ptolemaicae decrementum videatur nihilominus respondere decremento Declinationum Timocharidos et Hipparchi, ita vt a Timochare vsque ad Hipparchum Declinatio decreuerit M. 40. ab Hipparcho ad Ptolemaeum M. 50: oportebat enim ratione temporis decrementum in secundo interuallo esse maius decremento primi interualli: manifestum inde est, Ptolemaeum nunquam Declinationes Stellarum Fixarum obseruasse, sed tantum ex Declinationibus Timocharidis et Hipparchi secundum proportionem temporis finxisse, praesertim cum vt plurimum falsae, nec progressui Stellarum rite respondere inueniantur, vt ipse calculo tuo reperisti. Nec sane video, quo Instrumento eas obseruarit. Instrumentum enim parallaticum ad Lunam tantum comparatum erat, nec foramina visoriorum erant aequalia, sed superius ad Lunam erat maius, ita vt per id tota Luna perspici posset; inferius autem erat minus. Armillis quoque eas non obseruauit; siquidem Declinatio eius puncti Eclipticae, in quo tunc haesit Regulus, maior erat ipsius Reguli Declinatione a Ptolemaeo tradita. Nihil igitur nostrae sententiae de mutata Stellarum Latitudine detrahere potest Latitudo Ptolemaica Sirij, quae, cum nostra hac maior esse debebat, minor tamen reperiatur. Licet enim Declinationes Timocharidis, Hipparchi, et ipsius etiam Ptolemaei quodammodo subscribant Latitudini in Abaco Ptolemaico positae: nihil tamen ijs ob dictas causas tribuendum est. Hoc enim potius casu quam obseruatione et arte accidit. Cum Graecum textum considero, vereor ne vitiosum Hipparchi exemplar ipsi Ptolemaeo obrepserit. M. 30; ac incuria transcribentium, qui putârunt ςς. frustra geminatum, ς. potuit omitti: ad quas vitiosas Declinationes Ptolemaeus deinde etiam suam fortassis direxit. Latitudo quoque Sirij in Abaco Ptolemaico posita facile transcribentium incuria vitiari potuit; cum idem acciderit non tantum in prope posita Lucida Hydrae (cuius Latitudo hoc nostro seculo duobus pene Gradibus maior Ptolemaica reperitur, cum tamen debebat esse minor) verumetiam in multis alijs, quarum Latitudo valde insigniter vitiosa deprehenditur. Sed quid de hac vnica Stella multa verba facimus, cum huius vnius votum vota reliquarum omnium facile supprimant. Monenda est posteritas, vt posthac hanc rem diligentius tractet. Ac de his fortasse plus quam sat erat. In deliquio Lunari Anni 1584. non iustam esse diligentiam et praecisionem a Wittichio adhibitam, recte asseris. Assumsit enim is locum Solis longe aliter quam per Obseruationes debuit; Dextri item humeri Orionis, per quem tempus indagauit, et Declinationem et Ascensionem rectam vitiosam. Quod cum viderem, non immerito de negligentia ipsius in prioribus literis sum conquestus. Sed vt ipse videas, qua diligentia et perspicacitate vsus fuerit, totam Obseruationis annotationem, vt eam reliquit, iam subijciam, estque ea eiusmodi.

Ex his vides, quam temere affirmârit, se assumsisse et locum Solis et Ascensionem rectam Dextri Humeri Orionis, Declinationemque ex Obseruationibus: cum nihil horum per Obseruationes verum reperiatur. Sed nolo in demortuum plura dicere. Si assumseris tuum locum Solis, et veram Dextri Humeri Orionis Ascensionem rectam, veramque Declinationem, provt nostris Obseruationibus inueniuntur; videbis annotata hîc temporum momenta propius ad tua accedere. Eclipsin huius mensis ob pluuias, quae a 6. Septemb. quatuor integros dies continue hîc durârunt, obseruare non potui, quanquam me diligenter praeparâssem. Admodum inconstans et pluuiosum Caelum hoc Anno hîc Cassellis fuit, adeo vt paucas admodum Obseruationes instituere potuerim: Percuperem scire, an idem etiam in alijs locis fuisset. Planetas obseruare Illustrissimus Princeps mihi non permittit, cuius mandato tantum Stellas Fixas obseruare, indeque vera earum loca Calculo indagare cogor. Vult enim ipsius Celsitud. vt Obseruationes nostras publicas faciam, quotidieque calculum earum vrget. Vult posteritati monumentum sui erga Astronomiam amoris relinquere: cui clementi voluntati mihi parendum est. Cedet opus hoc maxime in tuam laudem, nec sis solicitus de Instrumentis. Ea enim cum honorifica tui nominis praedicatione tibi restituam, nec praetergrediar reliqua, de quibus ad nos perscripsisti. Assumsi pro fundamento, vt superioribus quoque literis significaui, Aldeborae Ascensionem rectam part. 63. M. 10. quam etiam hoc ipso Anno per Venerem diurnam veram inueni. Quod autem ob id quinque Minutis in Longitudine vbique fere differamus a tuis Obseruationibus, neminem, qui modo subtilitatem huius negotij intelligit, offendet: quin potius eo maiorem fidem apud posteritatem merebimur. Potest Veneris Diameter hanc differentiam introducere. Nihil tamen de hac in Opere nostro agam, vt tibi integra potestas relinquatur, diligentius et pluribus Obseruationibus hanc rem examinandi. Nec te offendet, quod licet videamus, te omni humana diligentia in tuis Obseruationibus vsum, nihilominus tamen a nostris, quas oculis nostris vidimus, non discedamus: Nec enim id veritatis amantes decet: nec nosmet alienis plumis ornare volumus. Obseruationes tuas huc transmissas diligentissime custodimus, nec alijs inspiciendas praebemus: nec publicationem earum metues. Tibi regnum, tibi restaurationem Astronomiae relinquimus; ac gaudemus, esse, qui ea in re praestare possit id, quod antehac praestitit nemo. Quod si possum tibi quocunque modo esse adiumento, in eo omne meum studium, diligentiam, industriam, fidem sancte tibi polliceor ac defero. Motum Solis a te huc transmissum inuenio exactissime cum nostris Obseruationibus congruere; praeterquam circa primos Gradus ♒, vbi eum aliquantulum iusto tardiorem per Venerem reperi: quod tamen ipsum te quoque animaduertisse in prioribus literis arguis. Accepi in superioribus Nundinis Francofortensibus a M. IOANNE AALBORGA Carmina quaedam a te ad Nobilissimos quosque viros perscripta: quae cum auidissime perlegerem, incidi tandem in id, quod ad D. FALCHONEM GOYE miseras, atque ab altera paginae parte inueni binas imagines, alteram Astronomiae, alteram eius Artis, quam Spagyricam appellas. Quas cum diligenter intuebar, animaduerti ab vtroque earum latere literas quasdam, circa Astronomiam quidem, SVSPICIENDO DESPICIO: circa reliquam vero, DESPICIENDO SVSPICIO. To the most noble and most renowned man, outstanding in virtue, wisdom, ingenious learning, and erudite doctrine, Master Tycho Brahe, Lord of Knudstrup, Danish knight, of all the mathematicians of our age the most excellent, etc., his lord to be honoured with reverence, etc. — heartiest greeting.

I should have replied to your letter at the last Frankfurt fair, most noble Sir and most excellent of all mathematicians, had not those matters of which you write in your later letters been a hindrance to me; or had it not been that at Court time often has most idly to be spent on the most idle of affairs. But I shall now make up for that delay by the greater abundance [of what I write]. To deal with you, then, first of all and still in few words, about the comet: you will not be wide of the mark with that argument of yours, by no means a weak one — that it was reasonable the comet should have had some parallax, however scarcely perceptible, since Jupiter and Saturn too have parallax. I know the Copernican hypotheses absolutely demand it. Yet however I examine my observations, I can find no perceptible [parallax]. I could have used the same argument from imperceptible parallax to confirm my own view, by which I assigned the comet to the sphere of Saturn; since Saturn's parallax easily eludes our senses and scarcely reaches a third of a minute. But I did not wish to incur the censure of those who, though they understand nothing of the mathematical art, yet do not cease to bawl out, ignorantly and rashly, that we assert the most absurd things of all. But since I have you for the Aristarchus of my observations, henceforth I shall pay no heed to men of that stamp, but shall bring forward, with a bold and ready spirit, those things which I find true both by observation and by the most certain demonstrations of geometry. As for your wondering how I then assumed the maximum obliquity of the Sun at 23°30′, and could not, like you, gather it at 23°31⅓′ from the observations of our Most Illustrious Prince: in this I easily deserve pardon. For I saw that the observations of our Most Illustrious Prince yielded now a maximum solstitial altitude of the Sun of 62°10′, now 62°12′, and that with the very same quadrant: from which it was readily plain that they had been carried out with less care than the magnitude of the matter required. Besides, the true elevation of our pole was not yet established to me — with how great labours I tracked it down at last, you know. I therefore assumed that obliquity which at the time I conjectured to be as close to the truth as possible, although I did not know the exact and precise [value] from observations. But once the true elevation of our pole had been found, I readily noticed that the obliquity of the zodiac assigned by you comes as close as possible to the observations of our Most Illustrious Prince. And what at that time I had necessarily to learn from you, that I have now myself found to be most true by my own most diligent observations. For at the last summer solstice I found the maximum meridian altitude of the Sun exactly 62°11′, most carefully. Whence, adding the Sun's parallax and assuming the true elevation of our equator at 38°41′, your obliquity of the zodiac results. At the winter solstice the maximum meridian altitude of the Sun here is 15°14′. But at that season the air here is far denser and more vaporous than at other times of the year, and the Sun's refraction about midwinter is far greater than when the Sun is moving about the middle of ♏ Scorpio or ♒ Aquarius. So much for the comet and for the obliquity of the zodiac mixed in with it. And since I see that you long for the remainder of my writing on the comet, I send you three further chapters of it, from which you will readily understand what my view on comets is, and what astronomical hypotheses I judge to be true. I treated of these matters somewhat harshly; for to a hard knot a hard wedge must be applied; nor does that breed of men, in the face of preconceived authorities and a foolish ignorance of the mathematical art, easily depart from their most inept trifles. There is still missing a chapter on the signification of comets, designed above all to commend astronomy; which, though I have conceived it in mind, I have not been able to take down with the pen on account of other business imposed on me by our Most Illustrious Prince. It will be added when I bring that writing back to the anvil. Now I shall deal with you a little more fully about the matter of the celestial spheres. You are right, indeed, to hold with me that the matter of the celestial spheres is not hard or impervious, but fluid and subtle, and yields easily to the motion of the planets. But in this we disagree, that you, on account of refractions, posit different transparent media [diaphana] of aether and air — that is, that in the celestial spheres there is not contained elemental air, but rather a most fluid aether distinct from elemental air. But the argument from refraction, which you reckon fights on your side, utterly storms and overturns this view of yours. For if that refraction, which we find by observation, comes from a difference between the transparent medium of the aether and that of the elemental air, it cannot but last all the way to the zenith. For suppose that about the horizon that refraction is scarcely three minutes: it will nonetheless be, at an altitude of 45°, about 1½′, since the change and ratio of the triangles scarcely admits a lesser [value]. For even though, in confirmation of your view, you assert that the difference between the transparent media of aether and air is not so great, but is one that diminishes successively where they touch one another, so that the topmost portion of air, contiguous to the revolutions of the Moon, differs almost nothing from the aether itself: yet by that very thing, in that you assert that refraction arises on account of the different transparent media of aether and air — by that very thing, I say, you grant that the rays of the Sun or of the stars do nonetheless select for themselves a point in that successive combination and assimilation of the different transparent media, at which they are refracted; and this will necessarily always keep the same distance from the centre of the Earth, because that successive combination of the different transparent media is everywhere equally distant from the Earth. Having therefore assumed this point of refraction to be everywhere equally distant from the Earth, see whether the ratios of the triangles permit that refraction to vanish at an altitude of 50° or 60°. You see, then, that the vanishing of the refractions can in no way be granted by the different transparent media of aether and air. And so what my view about these refractions is — since perhaps it was not set out by me clearly enough in the [treatise on the] comet — I shall now disclose to you more explicitly. Between the Earth, as you know, and the sphere of the fixed stars I hold that nothing else is contained than air encompassing the seven wandering stars [the planets], because the refractions do not last all the way to the zenith, but scarcely, as you would have it, up to 30°. This air I then distinguish — Nature herself teaching me thus — into a thicker [part], subject to terrestrial exhalations, and into a pure [part], which is not infected by those exhalations. And the foundation of this distinction of mine is likewise the principles of optics. For it is known to you from Alhazen, from Witelo, from Pedro Nunes On Twilights, and also by the testimony of the eyes, that there is a certain space of air which in twilight is illuminated by the Sun's rays and grows white; and likewise that beyond that space there is air which cannot be illuminated and grow white from the Sun's rays — otherwise there would be perpetual twilight. It is necessary, therefore, that the air which grows white in the twilight even under a most serene sky be infected by terrestrial exhalations and rendered thicker — otherwise the Sun's rays could not illuminate it — while that which does not grow white be pure and uninfected by terrestrial vapours; although I will not deny that exhalations, especially the purer ones, can sometimes ascend even into that pure air. Nor yet do I find that this thick air can of itself cause refraction, but [only] on account of its multiplication. For although that space of thicker air reaches almost a semidiameter of the Earth from the surface of the Earth, as is manifest from what the optical writers have said about twilights, yet I observe that the rays of the Sun or of the stars overcome this thickness, and, on account of the identity of the transparent medium within it, cannot find a point of refraction in it; but about the horizon and in a more sloping position they can, on account of the doubling of that space.

Let there also be described, from the same centre, an outermost circle in which some star is carried, which let be HIL, at so great an interval that it has almost no perceptible proportion to AC. Let the star also be supposed to be placed, say, on the equator, or in some other place in which, on reaching the meridian, it has an altitude greater than 30°, and feels no further refraction. I say, then, that the point at which refraction occurs is more distant from the outermost surface EFG of the thicker air than the outermost surface of the thicker air is distant from the surface of the Earth — HE [greater] than CF or BE. For let the star rise at H. To those, then, who dwell at B at a similar latitude of place, the star will be on the meridian, or placed at so great an altitude that they can feel none of its refraction. Since therefore we, placed at C, then feel the maximum refraction, it is necessary that the point of refraction be between the points B and C, and also at a greater distance from the surface EFG than EB is. For otherwise those who dwelt at B would likewise feel the refraction. It is established, therefore, [as] what was proposed — namely that that space of thicker air is not of itself so great as to be able to refract the rays, but receives that power from its doubling about the horizon. And that this may be still more apparent, I shall now show how far the point of refraction is distant from the outermost surface HE of the thicker air, [and] through how great a space the rays of the stars penetrate that thick air unrefracted: for which purpose I shall assume, as you would have it, that that refraction lasts up to the thirtieth degree from the horizon. But the one who is at S still feels the refraction of the point K, since for him K is at an altitude of 29°, likewise by hypothesis. Since, then, the point of refraction has now just ceased for the one at C, but still appears to the one at S, it is necessary that it be at the zenith C. Wherefore it is manifest that the line OC determines as closely as possible the space by which the point of refraction is distant from the outermost surface of the thicker air, and how far the rays can penetrate that thick air without refraction. While, then, the star is on the horizon at the point H, the point of refraction will be at N; while it is at Q, that point will be at R; and it will draw ever nearer and nearer to C, so that on the horizon it is furthest of all from the point C. From all which it is most manifest that there are neither different transparent media of aether and air, nor can the refractions be caused from any source other than thick and vaporous air. The Sun too, with a most clear sky and a strong ray, I found at an altitude of three degrees, when it was not yet a single degree from the visible horizon, to have a refraction of scarcely 9⅓′, or, with parallax added, 12⅓′ — which you, however, found to be 28′. Whence you rightly assert that an altogether serene and pure air is required for treating the matter of refractions properly. You see, then, manifestly, that those refractions are from no source other than thick and dense air, and that the view of the ancients is true, by which they said that the air in more northerly places is denser and thicker than in more southerly ones. But whether the outermost surface of the thick air is, in more northerly places, further from the Earth than in more southerly ones, I greatly doubt. For about that which Nunes, in Part 2, Proposition 1 On Twilights, seems to doubt — namely that twilight is ended in some places when the Sun is hidden 17° below the horizon, in others at 18°, in others at 19° — about that it does not seem to me one ought to doubt, especially since Ptolemy too asserts the same at the end of Book 8 of the Megalē Syntaxis. But it is our task to note carefully the moments of time at which the air ceases to grow white in the evening or begins [to] in the morning, and to settle this dispute from a collation of our observations. These things, about this arduous and lofty matter, I wished to write out to you a little more copiously; and I beg of you again and again, most earnestly, that you examine them carefully, and do not cease to confer with me about them. Now to other matters. About the latitudes of the stars changed proportionally according to the change of the obliquity of the zodiac, you discourse most learnedly. For there was no other way to proceed aptly in the confirmation of that matter than the one by which you proceeded — because the places of the fixed stars are set down quite wrongly in the tables, and, as I judge, were never observed by Ptolemy, but only transcribed from Hipparchus. It appears from many places in the Almagest, and especially from the cited Chapter 3 of Book 7, that Ptolemy handled the business of the fixed stars less carefully and too drowsily. While I was still residing at Wittenberg, I remember examining in a like manner the places of the stars set down in the Ptolemaic canon; and when I assumed the longitude and declination of Regulus as they are handed down by Ptolemy, the latitude of Regulus came out 6⅔′ — not north, as is noted in the canon, but south. Since these things are manifestly contrary, and cannot be reconciled by anyone, it is necessary that one of them be false. But since Regulus is the one star whose observation Ptolemy recounts, and our observations do not much reject the longitude and latitude found by Ptolemy, of necessity the declination adduced will be most false. But since the decrement of the Ptolemaic declination nonetheless seems to correspond to the decrement of the declinations of Timocharis and Hipparchus — such that from Timocharis to Hipparchus the declination decreased 40′, from Hipparchus to Ptolemy 50′ (for, by reason of time, the decrement in the second interval ought to be greater than the decrement of the first interval) — it is thence manifest that Ptolemy never observed the declinations of the fixed stars, but only feigned them from the declinations of Timocharis and Hipparchus according to the proportion of time, especially since they are found for the most part false, and not duly to correspond to the progression of the stars, as you yourself have found by your calculation. Nor indeed do I see with what instrument he could have observed them. For the parallactic instrument [triquetrum] was contrived only for the Moon, and the holes of the sights were not equal, but the upper one, [used] for the Moon, was larger, so that through it the whole Moon could be seen; while the lower one was smaller. With armillae too he did not observe them, since the declination of that point of the ecliptic in which Regulus then stood was greater than the declination of Regulus itself handed down by Ptolemy. Nothing, then, can detract from our view about the changed latitude of the stars [in the case of] the Ptolemaic latitude of Sirius, which, whereas it ought to be greater than ours here, is yet found smaller. For although the declinations of Timocharis, of Hipparchus, and even of Ptolemy himself in some manner subscribe to the latitude set down in the Ptolemaic table, yet nothing is to be attributed to them, for the reasons stated. For this happened rather by chance than by observation and skill. When I consider the Greek text, I fear lest a corrupt copy of Hipparchus crept in upon Ptolemy himself. The latitude of Sirius too, set down in the Ptolemaic table, could easily have been corrupted by the carelessness of copyists; since the same has happened not only in the nearby [star] Lucida Hydrae (whose latitude in this our age is found almost two degrees greater than the Ptolemaic, when yet it ought to be smaller), but also in many others, whose latitude is found notably corrupt. Our Most Illustrious Prince does not permit me to observe the planets; by his command I am compelled only to observe the fixed stars, and thence to track down their true places by calculation. If he persists in that resolve, I shall be able on this occasion to come to you; but if not, I have composed at Wittenberg, not without many vigils, a Mathematical Organon [Organon Mathematicum], which contains the sexagesimal logistic, the doctrine of sines, the doctrine of the composition of ratios, or of the rule of six quantities, and the doctrine of triangles, both spherical and plane, set out clearly and briefly, packed with many ingenious shortcuts, and most dear to me: this I shall dedicate to your Most Serene King, that I may not lack occasion for coming to you. I have likewise composed there an Astronomy, in which I have transferred the hypotheses of Copernicus to the mobility of the Sun (which you are not unaware can be done), and have set out the several diagrams of the Theorica by the Prutenic calculation. But since I have found by observation that the Prutenic Tables depart notably far from the truth of the heavens, I have hitherto deferred its publication, lest I should seem to commend a faulty calculation to learners as true. If, then, it should please you, and if you would bring your astronomical canons into the light, I would substitute yours in place of the Prutenic calculation, so that your calculation might be made familiar to learners. With wonderful brevity, the heterogeneous [matters] having been dissected by the laws of Logic, I have cast into it far more than the common little books of Spherics and Theorica contain. I was driven to that work by a most bitter grief, that I saw, not without the greatest indignation, that in the Universities, where above all these studies ought to flourish, almost nothing is understood of this Art: that the Prutenic calculation is indeed in everyone's hands, but is handled only according to the bare Prutenic precepts, and that none are found who understand its hypotheses, not even among those who profess Astronomy and think themselves the highest mathematicians. The motion of the precession of the equinoxes and of the changed obliquity of the zodiac — whose hypotheses I have so far seen no one aptly understand, nor do I think you have seen many [understand them] — I have treated with the greatest ease and perspicuity; though it must now be treated otherwise, since the stars are found to have changed their latitudes. About the artificial days as well as the natural ones, and about the equation of time, I have proposed, from the very foundation, things not commonplace. Those real orbs and so many axes, obscuring astronomy rather than explaining it, I have wholly done away with. In the Theorica of the Sun and Moon I have assumed one and the same supposition of circles, namely an eccentric whose centre is movable on a little circle [circellus] above the centre of the World, on the line of the mean apogee, and indeed placed at the centre of the mean eccentricity: except that in the Sun the simple anomaly begins at the highest part of the little circle, while in the Moon the doubled distance from the Sun begins, in the contrary way, from the lowest part of the little circle. I altogether hope that learners can derive the greatest profit from it. My writing on the comet will be received by that most noble and most magnificent Man, your friend Kaas, your most worthy Chancellor, who became known to me from your poem. For whom else should Urania rather celebrate than her patron? I love, with the reverence I owe, the highest virtues of this highest Man. As for your thinking, in the letter written to our Most Illustrious Prince, that all comets are generated in the heaven itself above the Moon, and that none of them was ever Elemental — I would readily concede it to you, were not the observations of Regiomontanus and Vögelin in the way. For although you rightly assert that observing parallaxes properly is of a far subtler skill than the manner hitherto commonly in use could supply, yet the velocity of the motion does not seem to reject those parallaxes. Nor indeed do I see why they cannot also be generated below the Moon, since the Element of Air is contained everywhere, and seems to have been established chiefly to fill all things, as is established by what precedes. What shall we say about those great comets which occupied such vast spaces of the heaven? But yet I do not doubt that you have not-to-be-despised reasons why you so determine. What my own view is, you will see from the three chapters appended. In the last chapter I have rejected the violent diurnal motion. I find that it is altogether to be attributed to the Earth. These things I wished to write out to you at this time, to which I beg you again and again to reply. Herewith fare you happily well, most noble and most ingenious TYCHO, and persuade yourself altogether that I am most loving of you.

At Kassel, the 11th [day before] the Kalends of October [21 September], in the Year of Salvation 1587.
Yours, yours,
CHRISTOPH ROTHMANN, Mathematician of the Most Illustrious Prince Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, etc.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Christophorus Rothmannus = Christoph Rothmann (c. 1550/60 – c. 1600), mathematician and astronomer to Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel (1532–1592). The Kassel court observatory was, with Hven, the leading observational establishment in northern Europe; the Tycho–Rothmann exchange (1585–90) is among the most substantive scientific correspondences of the century. This is Rothmann's reply within that exchange.
  • Recipient titulature. Domino de Knudstrup, Equiti Dano — "Lord of Knudstrup, Danish knight": Tycho's noble seat (Knudstrup, Scania) and rank, kept by the edition's convention
  • "the comet." The comet of 1585, the subject of Rothmann's treatise (Descriptio accurata cometae anni 1585), three chapters of which he encloses. The debate over whether it showed parallax (and so lay below or above the Moon) is the live question.
  • Hypotheses Coperniceae / Hypotheses Copernici — "Copernican hypotheses": rendered hypotheses throughout (the period sense = geometrical model/supposition), per glossary; never "theory."
  • Aristarchum — "the Aristarchus of my observations": Aristarchus of Samos as the type of the sound, ancient judge/authority; Rothmann makes Tycho the arbiter of his observations. Kept as the classical name with gloss.
  • Obliquity figures. Rothmann's 23°30′ vs. Tycho's 23°31⅓′ for the maximum obliquity of the ecliptic; solstitial altitudes 62°10′/62°11′/62°12′; pole elevation (equator elevation) 38°41′. All reproduced exactly; no value silently normalised.
  • Refraction theory (the core of the letter). Refractio → refraction; Diaphanum → transparent medium (the period optical term for a refracting/transmitting medium); Aether liquidissimus vs. Aër Elementaris → "most fluid aether" vs. "elemental air." The dispute: Tycho attributes astronomical refraction to a difference of diaphana between aether and air; Rothmann argues refraction arises only from the thick, vapour-laden lower air ("by its doubling about the horizon"), so that there is no aether at all between Earth and the fixed stars — only air encompassing the planets. This is a landmark statement against celestial spheres / for a unitary air-filled cosmos. Rendered fully and faithfully; the geometric demonstration (points A–S, semicircle BCD, surfaces EFG/HIL) is translated literally with the lettering preserved.
  • [Figura] / Figur 9 — the source carries a figure reference (the diagram of the refraction demonstration); marked [Figure 9] as an editorial insertion since the diagram is not in the text stream.
  • Optical authorities. Alhazenus = Alhazen (Ibn al-Haytham); Vitellio = Witelo (the 13th-c. Perspectiva; "Prop. 14, Bk 10" cited); Petrus Nonius de Crepusculis = Pedro Nunes, De crepusculis (Lisbon, 1542). Crepusculum → twilight by the edition's convention
  • Greek. (1) μεγαλ. συντάξ. = Megalē Syntaxis, the Greek title of Ptolemy's Almagest (here "the Megalē Syntaxis"). (2) πόρισμα = pórisma, "corollary" (a geometer's term, kept and glossed). (3) The numeral-letters ις ϛγ / ις ϛ are Greek alphabetic numerals: ις = 16, ϛ (stigma) = 6, γ = 3; Rothmann's textual-criticism argument is that a declination of 16°50′ (ις ϛγ — reading the second cluster as minutes) was miscopied to 16°30′ (ις ϛ) when a doubled stigma ϛϛ was taken for dittography. The numeral forms are transcribed in the source block and the arithmetic is explained here; see crux below — the exact letter-forms and Rothmann's intended minute-values are a genuine textual difficulty.
  • Star catalogue critique. Regulus (α Leonis), Sirius (α Canis Maioris), Lucida Hydrae (α Hydrae, Alphard), Aldebaran (Aldebora, α Tauri), Dexter humerus Orionis = the right shoulder of Orion (Betelgeuse, α Orionis). Timocharis and Hipparchus are the ancient observers whose declinations Rothmann argues Ptolemy merely interpolated ("feigned … according to the proportion of time") rather than observed — an early, pointed charge of fabrication against the Almagest star places. Canon / Tabula / Abacus → tables/canon. Almagest cited "Bk 7, ch. 3."
  • Instruments. Instrumentum parallaticum = the parallactic instrument / triquetrum (Ptolemy's, for the Moon); Armillae → armillae / armillary by the edition's convention; Quadrans → quadrant. Venus diurna → daytime Venus (Venus observed by day, used to carry the reference longitude — Tycho's and Rothmann's technique).
  • Wittich. Wittichius = Paul Wittich (c. 1546–1586), the Silesian mathematician who had worked both at Hven and at Kassel; here recently dead ("demortuus … I am unwilling to say more against a dead man"). Rothmann reproduces and faults Wittich's 7 November 1584 lunar-eclipse record. The eclipse record block is set as its own paragraph, mirroring the source's tabular list; Plenilunium eclipticum → "full moon in eclipse"; Mora in tenebris → "duration in darkness." Times are given in the source's hour-count from noon (P.M.); reproduced as written (11h20m … 14h45m), not converted.
  • Symbols. Zodiac glyphs kept in the source block and named in English by the edition's convention: ♏ = Scorpio, ♒ = Aquarius, ♉ = Taurus.
  • Tycho's emblem. Suspiciendo despicio / Despiciendo suspicio — Tycho's twin mottoes ("By looking up I look down" / "By looking down I look up"), here flanking the personifications of Astronomia and the Ars Spagyrica (Tycho's term for alchemy/spagyric art) on the verso of a poem Tycho had sent to Falk Gøye (Danish nobleman). Rothmann reads the device as hieroglyphica and asks Tycho for his alchemical planet-metal correspondences. Spagyrica kept (period term for Paracelsian chemistry), glossed "alchemy."
  • Persons named. M. Ioannes Aalborga = Hans Aalborg; Kaasius … Cancellarius vester = Niels Kaas, Chancellor of Denmark (Tycho's patron at court), known to Rothmann from a poem of Tycho's; Regiomontanus (Johannes Müller of Königsberg) and Vogelinus = Johannes Vögelin (Vienna, d. 1549, author on comet parallax) — the two authorities whose comet observations keep Rothmann from fully conceding Tycho's supralunary-only thesis.
  • Rothmann's own works. Organon Mathematicum — his unpublished mathematical handbook (sexagesimal logistic, doctrine of sines, regula sex quantitatum = the rule of six quantities / compound proportion, plane and spherical trigonometry); kept in Latin and glossed. Astronomia — his treatise transposing Copernicus's hypotheses to "the mobility of the Sun" and computing the Theorica schemes by the Prutenic (Reinhold's Tabulae Prutenicae) calculation, which he reports departs notably from the heavens. Logistica Sexagenaria → sexagesimal logistic (computation in base-60). restitutio/restauratio Astronomiae → restoration of astronomy by the edition's convention (Tycho's programme: "to you we leave the restoration of astronomy").
  • Cosmology — abolition of solid orbs. Orbes illos reales et tot axes … prorsus sustuli — "those real orbs and so many axes … I have wholly done away with": Rothmann, like Tycho, rejects solid celestial spheres. The lunar/solar Theorica description (Eccentricus … circellus … in linea Apogaei medij … centrum mediae Eccentricitatis; anomalia simplex) is rendered with glossary terms (eccentric, apogee, eccentricity, anomaly) and the diminutive circellus as "little circle."
  • motum violentum diurnum … Terrae tribuendum — "the violent diurnal motion … to be attributed to the Earth": Rothmann states he has rejected the violent daily rotation of the heavens and assigns the diurnal motion to the Earth — an openly Copernican (or at least diurnally-rotating-Earth) commitment. Rendered without softening.
  • Dateline (calendar crux flagged, not converted). Cassellis. 11. Calend. Octob. Anno … M.D.LXXXVII = "At Kassel, the 11th [day before] the Kalends of October, 1587." By the Roman inclusive count, ante diem XI Kalendas Octobris = 21 September — i.e. the Roman-calendar idiom denotes a September date, not 1 October. The edition/edition label "11. oktober. Kassel" reflects a different reckoning of the dateline (and/or an O.S.→N.S. consideration: Hesse-Kassel was Protestant and still on the Julian calendar in 1587, so no Gregorian gap is expected for an internal German Protestant date). Per the edition's convention the dateline is reproduced as written, the Roman idiom is glossed to its civil date (21 Sept., O.S.), and the discrepancy with the "11 October" label is flagged, not reconciled. Anno Salutis → "in the Year of Salvation." T.T. = Tui Tuus / Tuus Totus, "Yours, [yours]" — an affectionate subscription.
