TYCHO BRAHE AD IOHANNEM PRATENSEM.

ACCEPI literas tuas, eruditissime et amantissime PRATENSIS, ex quibus intelligo te negotium nostrum Rostochiense probe curasse, quo nomine tibi gratias ago. Cupis a me scire quid rerum in mundo geratur, quidque ferat foecundus ille Auster, a quo nuper redij. Atque ego pleraque eorum quae istic cognoveram, tibi, quam primum reversus essem, Haffniae retuli, plura etiamnum relaturus, si quae ulterius in mentem venerint, ubi vos denuo invisero, quod intra paucos dies facere decrevi; est enim res magni momenti, quam nulli praeterquam Magnifico Domino Legato Gallico DANZÆO et tibi intimo meo aperire animus est, vestrumque de hac audire consilium. Quoniam vero de abitu meo denuo in Germaniam mens tibi nonnulla praesagit, licet ego idipsum supprimam, dicam tibi quippiam in aurem, nemini nisi soli Domino DANZÆO, quem scis taciturnum et fidum, aperiendum. Recte quidem sive vigilanti sive somnianti tibi tuus dictavit Genius vatidicus, me aliud iter versus Austrum iterum meditari. Quod forte inde subolfecisti, quia nullas ex ijs arcibus, quas Serenissimus Rex noster mihi, cum in reditu illum debitae observantiae causa Sorae submisse salutassem, clementissime et motu proprio obtulit inhabitandas, recipere voluerim, et quod videas me de ijs quae in hac patria geruntur non admodum esse solicitum. Quod insuper Basileam saepenumero in ore isthic apud vos habuerim, eius oportunitatem cupido philosophiae alumno et tranquillitatis Apollineae, Musarumque amatori depredicârim, in qua urbe et tu aliquando vixisti, eamque plurimi facere solitus es: Ex his ut opinor, Genius seu potius ingenium tuum perspicax et providum de meo abitu et istuc eundi intentione nonnulla coniecit et suspicionem tibi movit non prorsus frivolam. Nam ut te sensa animi mei occulta non prorsus celem, sic est ut coniectaris: Constitui enim, si Deo sic visum fuerit, hac instante aestate Germanos revisere, adeoque Basileam usque, quae in limitibus Italiae, Galliae et Germaniae iucunde et commode sita est, excurrere, ibique sedes firmas pro studijs meis Olympicis figere, atque Astronomiae instaurationem, quam diu multumque animo volvo, favente astrorum conditore, moliri. Quin et haec erat prioris meae peregrinationis non minima causa, ut locum inquirerem et rationes invenirem, quomodo meis studijs tutius et plenius vacare possem, ijsque rectius consulerem quam hîc in Dania: displicet enim, ut me saepe conquerentem audivisti, haec conversatio, hi mores, haec quotidiana fabula; ex quo vix aliquem hîc reperiam praeter te, cum quo de rebus arduis et vulgi captum exuperantibus, solidi quid conferam: at quam raro nobis simul esse contingat, tute scis. Cum mei ordinis hominibus, quoniam crebrius mihi agendum est, praesertim Herressuadij, Avunculum, quando apud ipsum dego, convenientibus, aut etiam me ipsum Knudstorpij invisentibus (alias enim quantum possum illorum consortia subterfugio) multum temporis mihi perit, multa alias ingrata audienda et facienda. Nosti enim quam sint nobilium horum mores a meis institutis alieni, quamque ab ipsa philosophia: excipio excipiendos, quos tamen plures esse mallem quam sunt.

Sed audi quid hisce diebus praeter omnem meam expectationem acciderit, idque solus audi, et nemini nisi uni DANZÆO nostro aperi, idque secreto. Cum ultimo die praecedentis septimanae, qui erat undecimus Februarij matutinis horis in lecto expergefactus, adhuc decumberem, et iter hoc de quo modo dixi, in Germaniam tacitus et solicite animo mecum agitarem, simulque qua ratione, consanguineis meis non attendentibus, elabi possem, cogitarem, idque in varias partes volverem atque revolverem; ecce ab improviso nunciatur Regium puerum huc Knudstorpium advenisse, qui tota nocte properasset, ut literas a Rege mihi inscriptas confestim traderet; erant autem adhuc tenebrae, instante saltem crepusculo matutino, et Sol intra duas horas vix oriebatur: iubeo itaque puerum illum ad lectum accersiri, qui erat genere Nobilis atque ex mea consanguinitate, is mihi statim Regis exhibuit literas, atque sibi ab ipso Rege iniunctum esse aiebat, ut absque omni mora me, dies noctesque iter faciendo, quaereret, ubicunque in Scania invenirer, atque illas mihi in manus proprias traderet, statimque reverteretur, quod et factum est. Erant autem scriptae in domo Regis venatoria Ibstrup, quae prope vestram Haffniam intra unum milliare, uti scis, in sylva proxima sita est, idque praecedente die decimo nimirum Februarii. Ego vero literas mox resignans comperio in ijs Regem iubere, ut se confestim accedam, ubicunque in Selandia versaretur, quod et citra omnem moram obsequiose praestiti, adeo ut eodem die, Regi in dicta domo venatoria ante occasum Solis adfuerim. Is tunc me per Nobilem suum cubicularium NICOLAUM PASSBERG in locum quendam solitarium acciri iussit, atque sic, nemine praesente, clementissime allocutus est: «Scis TYCHO, quae et qualia volens tibi obtulerim, cum ultimo Sorae apud me esses, iussique ut brevi ad me rediens, mihi ipsi significares, quidnam in his expeteres, aut ea de re Cancellarium meum NICOLAUM KAASIUM, vel Secretarium IOHANNEM SCHOUGARDIUM, per literas admoneres. At ex quo nihil eiuscemodi interea feceris, cum tamen alij talia avide affectent expectentque, nescio quid animo volvas, aut cur te non mature resolvas. Quidam ex Aulicis meis mihi insinuare visus est, se ab avunculo tuo STENONE BILLE tacite cognovisse te denuo in Germaniam cogitare: Quare te advocare volui, ut ex te ipso cognoscerem, qualis tua sit sententia in ijs quae obtuli, et cur talia non acceptes. Fieri potest, veluti conijcio, quod nulla eiuscemodi Arcium magnarum beneficia Regia obtinere cupias, ne studia tua, quibus delectaris, per causas forenses et alia negotia politica, quae requiruntur in tuendis et administrandis talibus, interturbentur, atque otium ijs vacandi tibi praecludatur. Quare cum nuper in Arce Helsingorensi essem, ubi nunc aedifico, prospiciens ex fenestra quadam Insulam illam parvam Huennam, quae isthic in freto, versus Landskroniam sita est, et a nullo nobili inhabitatur, conspexi, de qua etiam, si recte memini, dictus tuus avunculus STENO BILLE mihi semel dixit, idque antequam in Germaniam ultimo te contulisti, quod eius situs tibi arrideret. Incidit itaque mihi in mentem hanc esse tuis exercitijs tam Astronomicis quam destillatorijs apprime accommodam, siquidem alta sit et solitaria. Nullae quidem aedes ibi sunt, quas inhabitare posses, neque etiam reditus inde suppetunt necessarij. Verum ego tibi de utrisque sufficienter prospiciam. Si itaque hanc Insulam incolere volueris, lubens eam tibi soli concedam. Isthic degere potes quiete et eas exequi artes, quibus afficeris, nemine interpellante. Cumque arcem Helsingorensem splendidius instauravero, veluti nunc incepi, illic subinde commoratus, ad te nonnunquam in eam Insulam transfretabo, atque quid rerum agas in Astronomicis et destillationibus tuis inspiciam, conatusque tuos lubens iuvabo, non quod ego Astronomica intelligam aut sciam quorsum per omnia spectent, vel aliquid utilitatis inde pro mea persona sperem, sed saltem QVIA REX SVM, tuque in hoc meo Regno, ex ea familia, quae mihi semper chara fuit, oriundus, non postremam in his operam navasse diceris: Unde existimo mei officij esse, talia convenienter sustentare et promovere. Quorsum vero opus est, ut denuo in Germaniam concedas, teque isthic peregrinum reddas, cum hîc in patria eadem commode efficere possis quae isthic. Dabimus potius operam, ut eo res deducatur, quod Germani et aliarum Nationum homines, qui talia scire cupiunt, huc accedant, atque ea hîc videant et discant, quae alibi vix reperient. Delibera itaque tecum spacio aliquot dierum, et deinde quid in his tibi faciendum censeas, mihi significa, ubi inaudiveris me in arcem residentiae nostrae Friderigsburgensem reversum esse: Nolo enim ut coacte quidpiam hîc facias, sed liberum tibi relinquo, expensis ijs, quae dixi, statuere id, quod ex re tua iudicaveris. Atque ubi Friderigsburgi mihi adfueris, et quidpiam hac de re conclusum fuerit, tum demum de reditibus necessarijs atque aedificationum sumptibus ita ordinabo et tibi prospiciam, ut indemnis esse queas, atque illic commode peragere ea, quae regno huic et mihi Regi, tum quoque tibi ipsi et tuis esse poterint tam hîc quam alibi honorifica.« In hunc sane modum optimus ille et Patriae ac publici boni, honorisque studiosissimus Rex verba habuit, quibus peractis, ego qua potui et debui reverentia gratias humilime egi, et me, quamvis Insula clementer oblata, ob maris fluctus, quibus undique cingitur, accessus habeat difficiles et saepenumero admodum periculosos, submisse respondi ea de re deliberaturum, atque brevi meam resolutionem Friderigsburgi subiectissime indicaturum: sicque discessi et sequente die, huc domum reversus, ubi, uti et in itinere, de hoc Regio proposito multa tacite pensito; nam mirabile mihi videtur, imo alicuius peculiaris ominis, aut fati instar esse, quod Regius puer eo ipso tempore, quo de abitu in Germaniam solicite mecum deliberarem, matutinis horis tale nuncium adferret; quodque serenissimus et clementissimus Rex noster ex se ipso, me non petente, vel eiuscemodi quid cogitante, talia proponeret; et ut dicam aperte quod sentio, impense me moverunt haec Regis verba, eiusque laudabilis intentio, adeo ut animus meus vacillare et haesitare coeperit, et de priori proposito nonnihil remittere. Vereor enim, ne, si Rege quasi praevidente ea quae mihi novi abitus causas suggesserunt, atque his praeveniente, nihilominus discederem, et forte postea alibi in peregrinis oris mihi quidpiam adversi eveniret, nec omnia ex voto succederent (uti sunt res humanae fluxae et varijs casibus obnoxiae) tum eo magis inde affligerer atque anxius redderer et tanto plus poenitudinis inde caperem, quod nimirum has satis tolerabiles conditiones a laudatissimo Rege mihi singulari clementia benignissime cupienti, oblatas non acceptârim ac in Patria manserim. Nihil tamen certi adhuc constituere possum, nec etiam volo, antequam Domini DANZÆI et tuum audivero consilium. Matri et Avunculo aut etiam Fratri, haec, antequam penitus inter nos deliberatum et conclusum fuerit, in quas partes me dem, non aperiam. Neque enim volo ijs innotescere me denuo in Germaniam proficisci decrevisse. Haec mi PRATENSIS, eo plenius, prout peracta sunt, tibi refero, ut eo expeditius et melius cum DANZÆO nostro deliberare possis, quid mihi faciendum duxeritis, atque vestram sententiam, quamprimum isthuc venero (quod intra biduum vel triduum ad summum futurum spero) ingenue pro amicitia inter nos mutua et syncera aperiatis; neque enim diu cunctandum censeo, antequam Regi Friderigsburgi adsim, atque quomodo me resolverim exponam; quod facere D. V. constitui quamprimum unicum saltem diem Haffniae hac in re vobis tribuero, et quid suaseritis intellexero.

Quando hae literae a te lectae fuerint, et saepedicto Domino DANZÆO nostro ostensa, eas mox disrumpe aut in cineres redige, ne forte per incuriam alicubi repositae in aliorum manus perveniant; sicque secretae nostrae consultationes, ubi quam maxime nollemus, propalentur. Atque de his nunc satis; plura et particulariora, ubi praesens fuero, intelliges. Verum antequam epistolam claudam, unum adhuc restare video discutiendum, quod prioribus intentus, ferme praeterieram, de opinione illa Pliniana, ad quam quidem non tam absolute, prout rei moles exigit, nunc me resolvere datur, quae tamen obiter et tam cito in mentem venire possunt, dicam. PLINIJ ille locus, quem adducis, videtur obscurior, quam quod in continenti eum prorsus enodare concedatur, neque enim, quae voluit dicere, visus est. Imo mea quidem opinione, nisi plura hac de re intellexerit quam scripsit, sibi ipsi non satisfecit, nedum alijs. Non quidem dubium est planetas ad Solem evidentem obtinere relationem, atque respectu eius Stationes et Retrogradationes, atque nonnullas inaequalitates apparentes, tam quoad Longum quam Latum, differentias exhibere, at quod circa Trinum Solis quam proxime stent superiores tres Planetae, radiisque Solis inhibeantur, rectumque agant cursum, et ignea eius vi in sublime levantur (uti de his dictus ratiocinatur PLINIUS) tum quoque ea quae mox de Retrogradationibus subiungit, non video quomodo constare possint. Neque enim Solis radij quasi catenâ aut aliâ quadam combinatione sunt cum Planetis alligati, qua eos hac vel illac impellant, trahant, aut retrahant, nisi vim quandam quasi Magneticam illis hoc modo inesse imaginemur; et ne sic quidem motio erit coelestibus competens utpote circularis et regularis. Certum enim est Planetarum motiones ubique orbiculariter fieri, vel ex orbicularibus componi, ideoque ea quae PLINIUS insinuat, si ad Solem referenda sunt, quod etiam fieri posse non inficior, alia ratione applicanda veniunt, de qua nonnunquam cogitavi, sed nondum satis me resolvi, ut videlicet tollantur Epicycli (quos veteres etiam non assumpsisse ex PLINIO liquet) et nihilominus apparentiae constent. Sed existimo PLINIUM hanc suam speculationem undecunque tandem acceptam et quomodocunque intellectam in praxin et usum deducere nequivisse, ideoque difficultatem quandam hîc subt esse fassum. Simile quid apud VITRUVIUM, qui diu ante PLINIUM fuit, reperitur, sed locus nunc non succurrit. Is vero Architectonice magis quam Astronomice et Geometrice executus videtur, ex quo hanc sententiam PLINIUM hausisse verosimile est, licet is idipsum dissimulans eam sibi propriam et privatam asseruit. Verum de his ubi D. V. convenerimus, et commoditas dabitur talia disquirendi, ulterius philosophabimur. Tu vero, mi charissime PRATENSIS, quam felicissime vale, et Dominum DANZÆUM nostrum ex me officiose saluta; meque intra paucos, uti dixi, dies isthic ambo incolumes expectate.

Dabantur e sede nostra Knudsdorpiana die 14. Februarij A. 1576.
TYCHO BRAHE.

Tycho Brahe to Johannes Pratensis.

I have received your letter, most learned and most loving Pratensis, from which I gather that you duly took care of our Rostock business, for which I thank you. You desire to know from me what is going on in the world, and what that fruitful South Wind brings — from which I have lately returned. And most of what I had learned there I reported to you, as soon as I had returned, at Copenhagen; and I shall report still more, should anything further come to mind, when I visit you again — which I have resolved to do within a few days; for it is a matter of great moment, which I am minded to disclose to no one save the Honourable Lord, the French Ambassador Dançay, and to you, my intimate, and to hear your counsel upon it. But since your mind has some presentiment of my departing into Germany again — even though I myself keep it suppressed — I will say something in your ear, to be disclosed to no one but Lord Dançay alone, whom you know to be discreet and trustworthy. Rightly indeed, whether waking or dreaming, did your prophetic Genius dictate to you that I am once more contemplating another journey toward the South. This you perhaps got wind of from the fact that I was unwilling to accept, for my habitation, any of those castles which our Most Serene King most graciously and of his own accord offered me when, on his return, I had humbly saluted him at Sorø out of the observance due to him; and because you see that I am not much concerned about the things that go on in this homeland. And further, because I had Basel often in my mouth there among you, and extolled its convenience for one who is an eager nursling of philosophy and a lover of Apolline tranquillity and of the Muses — a city in which you too once lived, and which you were wont to value highly: from these things, I suppose, your Genius — or rather your keen and provident wit — conjectured something about my departure and my intention of going thither, and roused in you a suspicion not altogether idle. For — not to conceal from you entirely the hidden feelings of my mind — it is as you conjecture: for I have determined, if it shall so seem good to God, to revisit the Germans this coming summer, and indeed to make an excursion as far as Basel — which is pleasantly and conveniently situated on the borders of Italy, France, and Germany — and there to fix a firm seat for my Olympic studies, and, with the favour of the founder of the stars, to set about that restoration of astronomy which I have long and much turned over in my mind. Indeed this too was no small cause of my earlier travels: to seek out a place and find means whereby I might more safely and more fully devote myself to my studies, and attend to them better than here in Denmark. For — as you have often heard me complain — this society, these manners, this daily round, displease me; and I can scarcely find anyone here besides yourself with whom I might confer anything solid about difficult matters that surpass the common grasp; yet how rarely it falls to us to be together, you yourself know. With men of my own order, since I have rather frequently to deal with them — especially at Herrevad, [meeting] my uncle when I am staying with him, or with those who come to visit me at Knudstrup (for otherwise I avoid their company as much as I can) — much of my time perishes, and many other unwelcome things must be heard and done. For you know how foreign the manners of these noblemen are to my own pursuits, and how far from philosophy itself: I make exception of those who deserve it, whom I could nonetheless wish to be more in number than they are.

But hear what has befallen in these days beyond all my expectation — and hear it alone, and disclose it to no one but our one Dançay, and that in secret. On the last day of the previous week, which was the eleventh of February, in the morning hours, having woken in my bed and still lying there, and turning over within myself, silently and anxiously, this journey into Germany of which I have just spoken — and at the same time considering by what means I might slip away without my kinsmen noticing, and rolling this this way and that — behold, all of a sudden it is announced that a King's page had arrived here at Knudstrup, having hastened all night long to deliver at once a letter addressed to me from the King; and it was still dark, with only the morning twilight coming on, and the Sun barely rising within two hours. So I order that page to be summoned to my bedside — he was a nobleman by birth and of my own kindred — and he straightway showed me the King's letter, and said that it had been enjoined upon him by the King himself to seek me out without any delay, travelling day and night, wherever in Scania I might be found, and to deliver it into my own hands, and then return at once — which was done. The letter had been written at the King's hunting-lodge of Ibstrup, which, as you know, lies near your Copenhagen within one mile, in the nearest wood — and that on the preceding day, the tenth of February, namely. I, then, breaking the seal at once, find in it that the King commands me to come to him immediately, wherever in Zealand he should be — which without any delay I obediently performed, so that on the same day I was present before the King at the said hunting-lodge before sunset. He then ordered me to be called, through his noble chamberlain Niels Parsberg, into a certain solitary place, and thus, with no one present, most graciously addressed me: «You know, Tycho, what things, and of what sort, I willingly offered you when you were last with me at Sorø; and I bade you, returning to me shortly, to signify to me yourself what you desired in these matters, or to advise my Chancellor Niels Kaas, or my Secretary Johan Schougaard, of it by letter. But since you have done nothing of the kind in the meantime — though others eagerly covet and look for such things — I do not know what you are turning over in your mind, or why you do not resolve yourself in good time. One of my courtiers seemed to hint to me that he had quietly learned from your uncle Steen Bille that you are thinking of going to Germany again. For which reason I wished to summon you, that I might learn from your own self what your view is on the things I offered, and why you do not accept them. It may be — as I conjecture — that you do not desire to obtain any royal grants of such great castles, lest your studies, in which you delight, be disturbed by lawsuits and other political affairs which are required in maintaining and administering such places, and the leisure for attending to them be cut off from you. Wherefore, when I was lately at Helsingør Castle, where I am now building, looking out from a certain window I caught sight of that small island Hven, which lies there in the sound, toward Landskrona, and is inhabited by no nobleman — concerning which also, if I rightly remember, your said uncle Steen Bille once told me, and that before you last betook yourself to Germany, that its situation pleased you. So it came into my mind that this island is exceedingly well suited to your exercises, both astronomical and distillatory, seeing that it is high-lying and solitary. There is indeed no dwelling there which you could inhabit, nor do the necessary revenues come from it. But I will provide you sufficiently with both. If, therefore, you should be willing to dwell on this island, I will gladly grant it to you alone. There you can live quietly and pursue those arts by which you are affected, with no one interrupting. And when I shall have more splendidly restored Helsingør Castle — as I have now begun — staying there from time to time, I will now and then cross over to you on that island, and inspect what you are doing in your astronomical matters and your distillations, and gladly assist your endeavours — not that I understand astronomy or know what it all aims at, or hope for any utility from it for my own person, but simply BECAUSE I AM KING, and you, born in this my kingdom, of that family which has always been dear to me, are said to have laboured not least in these things: whence I deem it part of my office to support and promote such things fittingly. But what need is there for you to withdraw into Germany again, and make yourself a stranger there, when here in your homeland you can conveniently accomplish the same things as there? Rather will we take pains that it be brought to this, that Germans and men of other nations who desire to know such things may come hither, and see and learn here what they will scarcely find elsewhere. Deliberate, therefore, with yourself for the space of some days, and then signify to me what you judge ought to be done in these matters, when you have heard that I have returned to the castle of our residence, Frederiksborg: for I do not wish you to do anything here under compulsion, but I leave it free to you, having weighed the things I have said, to determine that which you shall judge to be to your advantage. And when you have presented yourself to me at Frederiksborg, and something has been concluded in this matter, then at last I will so order things concerning the necessary revenues and the costs of building, and provide for you, that you may be without loss, and may conveniently carry out there those things which can be to the honour of this kingdom and of me the King, and also of you yourself and yours, both here and elsewhere.» In just this manner did that best of kings — most zealous for his fatherland and for the public good and honour — frame his words; and these ended, I, with all the reverence I could and ought, most humbly gave thanks, and submissively replied that — although the island was graciously offered — because of the sea's waves, by which it is girt on every side, it has approaches that are difficult and very often quite perilous, I would deliberate upon the matter, and would shortly, most submissively, declare my resolution at Frederiksborg. And so I departed, and on the following day returned home hither, where — as also on the road — I silently ponder much about this royal proposal; for it seems to me wondrous, indeed as though it were some peculiar omen or stroke of fate, that a King's page should at that very time, when I was anxiously deliberating with myself about departing into Germany, in the morning hours bring such a message; and that our Most Serene and Most Gracious King should, of his own accord, without my asking or even thinking of any such thing, propose such things. And — to say openly what I feel — these words of the King, and his praiseworthy intention, moved me exceedingly, so much so that my mind began to waver and hesitate, and to give up somewhat of its former resolve. For I fear lest, if — the King as it were foreseeing the things that suggested to me the causes of a new departure, and forestalling them — I should nonetheless depart, and afterwards perhaps something adverse should befall me elsewhere in foreign parts, and not everything should turn out as wished (as human affairs are fleeting and subject to various chances), then I should be the more afflicted by it and rendered anxious, and take from it so much the more regret — for having not accepted these tolerable enough conditions, offered me with singular clemency by a most praised King most kindly wishing me well, and for having stayed in my homeland. Yet I can determine nothing certain as yet, nor do I wish to, before I have heard the counsel of Lord Dançay and yourself. To my mother and my uncle, or even my brother, I will not disclose — before it has been thoroughly deliberated and concluded among us — which course I shall take. For I do not wish it to become known to them that I have resolved to set out for Germany again. These things, my Pratensis, I report to you the more fully, just as they took place, so that you may the more expeditiously and better deliberate with our Dançay as to what you judge I ought to do, and may frankly disclose your opinion to me — as soon as I shall have come thither (which I hope will be within two days, or three at most) — in keeping with the mutual and sincere friendship between us; for I think there must be no long delay before I present myself to the King at Frederiksborg and explain how I have resolved; which, [God willing,] I have determined to do as soon as I shall have devoted to you at least one single day at Copenhagen in this matter, and learned what you will have advised.

When this letter shall have been read by you, and shown to our oft-named Lord Dançay, tear it up at once or reduce it to ashes, lest perchance, laid by somewhere through carelessness, it come into others' hands, and so our secret consultations be made public where we should least of all wish it. And of these matters now enough; more, and in more particular detail, you will learn when I am present. But before I close the letter, I see there yet remains one thing to be discussed, which — intent on the foregoing — I had almost passed over: concerning that Plinian opinion, upon which, indeed, it is not now granted me to resolve so absolutely as the weight of the matter requires; yet I will say the things that can come to mind in passing and so quickly. That passage of Pliny which you adduce seems more obscure than that it may be unravelled altogether on the spot, for he does not appear [to have made clear] what he meant to say. Nay, in my opinion at least, unless he understood more about this matter than he wrote, he did not satisfy even himself, much less others. There is indeed no doubt that the planets bear an evident relation to the Sun, and with respect to it exhibit stations and retrogradations, and certain apparent inequalities — differences both as to longitude and latitude; but that the three superior planets stand as near as possible about the trine of the Sun, and are held back by the Sun's rays, and run a direct course, and are lifted on high by its fiery force (as the said Pliny reasons about these matters) — and then too the things he adds next about the retrogradations — I do not see how they can hold good. For the Sun's rays are not bound to the planets as if by a chain or some other linkage, by which they should drive, draw, or draw them back this way or that — unless we imagine some force, as it were magnetic, to be in them in this way; and not even so will the motion be one fitting for the heavenly bodies, namely circular and regular. For it is certain that the motions of the planets everywhere come about in a circular fashion, or are compounded of circular ones; and therefore the things Pliny insinuates, if they are to be referred to the Sun (which I do not deny can also be the case), come to be applied in another way — about which I have sometimes thought, but have not yet sufficiently resolved myself — namely, so that the epicycles be removed (which the ancients too did not assume, as is clear from Pliny) and the appearances nonetheless hold good. But I judge that Pliny was unable to bring this speculation of his — from wherever in the end it was received and however it was understood — into practice and use, and therefore confessed that there was some difficulty lurking here. Something similar is found in Vitruvius, who lived long before Pliny, but the passage does not now come to me. He, however, seems to have treated it architecturally rather than astronomically and geometrically; from whom it is likely that Pliny drew this view, although Pliny, dissembling that very thing, claimed it as his own and proper. But of these things, when, [God willing,] we have met and opportunity is given for examining such matters, we will philosophize further. But you, my dearest Pratensis, fare you most happily, and dutifully greet our Lord Dançay from me; and both of you, safe and sound, expect me there within a few days, as I said.

Given from our seat of Knudstrup, the 14th day of February, in the year 1576.
TYCHO BRAHE.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Iohannes Pratensis = Johannes Pratensis (Hans Philipsen, 1543–1576), physician and professor of medicine at Copenhagen, Tycho's closest intellectual friend in these years; he died only a few months after this letter. Per the edition's convention
  • Significance. This is Tycho's own contemporaneous, confidential narrative of the audience at which Frederik II offered him the island of Hven (Hveen) — the foundational document for the founding of Uraniborg. Tycho reports the king's words in oratio recta (direct speech), set off here by guillemets as in the source.
  • negotium Rostochiense — "our Rostock business": an errand Pratensis had transacted on Tycho's behalf at Rostock (unspecified here).
  • foecundus ille Auster — "that fruitful South Wind": a figurative reference to Tycho's recent return from a journey to the south (Germany); kept as the author's metaphor.
  • Magnifico Domino Legato Gallico DANZAeO = Charles de Dançay (Danzaeus, c. 1510–1589), the long-serving French resident ambassador at the Danish court and a humanist confidant of Tycho's. Per the edition's convention register, Magnificus rendered "Honourable."
  • Genius vatidicus / Genius seu potius ingenium — "prophetic Genius … your Genius, or rather your … wit": Tycho plays on Genius (attendant/prophetic spirit) and corrects it to ingenium (native wit); the wordplay is preserved, not flattened.
  • studijs meis Olympicis — "my Olympic studies," i.e. studies of the heavens (the Olympian/celestial); kept as figure.
  • Astronomiae instaurationem — "restoration of astronomy": Tycho's lifelong programme, the instauratio astronomiae; rendered "restoration of astronomy" by the edition's convention
  • favente astrorum conditore — "with the favour of the founder of the stars" (God as creator of the heavens); a pious formula.
  • Places. Haffnia = Copenhagen; Basilea = Basel; Sora = Sorø; Herressuadium = Herrevad (the abbey held by Tycho's maternal uncle Steen Bille, where Tycho had his laboratory); Knudstorpium / Knudsdorpiana sedes = Knudstrup (the Brahe family seat in Scania, Tycho's birthplace); Ibstrup = the royal hunting-lodge of Ibstrup (later Jægersborg), N. of Copenhagen; Arx Helsingorensis = Helsingør (Kronborg) Castle, then being rebuilt by Frederik II; Insula Huenna = the island of Hven (Hveen), in the Sound toward Landskrona (Landskronia); Friderigsburgum = Frederiksborg Castle (Hillerød), the royal residence; Scania / Selandia = Scania / Zealand; Dania = Denmark. Per the edition's convention
  • Persons at court. Nicolaus Passberg = Niels Parsberg, royal chamberlain (cubicularius); Nicolaus Kaasius = Niels Kaas (1535–1594), Chancellor of Denmark; Iohannes Schougardius = Johan Schougaard (Jacob Skougaard / the royal secretary named in chancery); Steno Bille = Steen Bille (Tycho's maternal uncle, abbot of Herrevad). "My mother" = Beate Bille; "my brother" and the unnamed Avunculus of the opening paragraph (the uncle at Herrevad) = Steen Bille.
  • King's self-justification. saltem QVIA REX SVM — "but simply BECAUSE I AM KING": the small-capitals emphasis is in the source and is preserved in the translation as small caps/upper-case; it is the rhetorical hinge of the king's speech and is not editorially added.
  • Astronomy / the Pliny excursus. The closing paragraph debates a passage in Pliny the Elder (Naturalis Historia II, on the planets being checked and accelerated by the Sun's rays near the trine aspect). Glossary renderings used: Stationes et Retrogradationes → "stations and retrogradations"; inaequalitates apparentes → "apparent inequalities"; Longum / Latum → "longitude / latitude"; Epicycli → "epicycles"; Trinum Solis → "the trine of the Sun" (the 120° aspect). vim quandam quasi Magneticam — "some force, as it were magnetic": a notable early gesture by Tycho toward a magnetic celestial force; rendered literally, the hedge ("as it were," quasi) preserved. Vitruvius = Vitruvius (De architectura IX), cited as Pliny's probable source.
  • Textual note. In the Pliny paragraph the printed text reads "difficultatem quandam hîc subt esse fassum"; subt is an obvious misprint for subesse (so that the phrase = difficultatem quandam hîc subesse fassum, "confessed that some difficulty lurks here"). The printed form is retained in the Source block; the translation follows the corrected reading.
  • ACCCEPI (opening word) — printed with three C's; a typographical error for Accepi ("I have received"). Retained as printed in the Source block.
  • Crux: the abbreviation D. V. — D. V. occurs twice: (i) "quod facere D. V. constitui quamprimum unicum saltem diem Haffniae hac in re vobis tribuero"; (ii) "de his ubi D. V. convenerimus". The abbreviation is genuinely ambiguous and is not resolved by the printed text. The most economical reading is Deo Volente ("God willing") — the standard early-modern epistolary formula, and one Tycho uses in clear form earlier in this same letter (si Deo sic visum fuerit); on that reading D. V. is parenthetical and the surrounding words carry the sense (Haffniae = "at Copenhagen", vobis = "to you [both]", convenerimus = "we shall have met"). It could, however, conceivably stand for some other reference (e.g. a place/time formula). The editorial expansion [God willing] is therefore bracketed at both points rather than absorbed silently; D. V. is left unexpanded in the Source block. The earlier draft silently rendered both instances as "at Copenhagen" (the place-sense being supplied independently by Haffniae), which over-determined the abbreviation; that has been corrected.
  • Calendar / dating crux. All dates are Old Style (Julian); Denmark used O.S. until 1700. The dateline reads "die 14. Februarij A. 1576" ("the 14th day of February, 1576"), at Knudstrup — which agrees with the edition label (1576. 14. februar. Knutstorp). The body, however, narrates an internal sequence of distinct dates, reported, not reconciled: (i) the king's summons was written at Ibstrup on die decimo … Februarii (10 February); (ii) Tycho received it and rode to the audience on undecimus Februarij (11 February), described as "the last day of the previous week"; (iii) he wrote this letter on 14 February. These three dates are internally consistent (the audience three days before the letter) and are preserved exactly as written; none is silently converted or "corrected."
  • Correction. Two Latin transcription errors corrected: 'ACCCEPI' -> 'ACCEPI' (tripled-C typo for the letter's opening verb); 'invisere' -> 'invisero' (the 'ubi' clause requires the future-perfect, not the bare infinitive). No Dreyer page image (TBOO VII p.25) was available for this letter, so both corrections rest on internal Latin orthographic/grammatical evidence rather than direct collation against the page image; the English translation required no change in either case.

2 defects found:
- text error (low). Our Latin prints the opening verb with a tripled C (ACCCEPI); Dreyer prints ACCEPI. A corrupted word; sense unaffected and the translation renders it correctly regardless. Dreyer prints: ACCEPI literas tuas, eruditissime et amantissime PRATENSIS (TBOO VII p.25, opening word).
- text error (low). Our Latin has the present infinitive invisere where Dreyer prints the future-perfect invisero, the form the ubi-clause and following quod-clause require; a verb-form corruption. The English rendering happens to be correct either way, since English does not distinguish the two forms in this construction. Dreyer prints: ubi vos denuo invisero, quod intra paucos dies facere decrevi (TBOO VII p.25, l. c. 11).

Repaired. No Dreyer page images were available for this letter, so neither defect could be verified against the edition of record by direct inspection. Both were nonetheless resolved on internal evidence internal to the Latin itself: 'ACCCEPI' is not a possible Latin spelling (accipio's perfect is invariably 'accepi'), and 'invisere' cannot stand as the finite verb of a 'ubi' clause, which requires 'invisero' given the surrounding future sense. Both fixes are low severity and do not affect the English, which was already correct in each case. Because collation against Dreyer's actual page image was not possible, this letter is graded B rather than A even though both defects are resolved.
