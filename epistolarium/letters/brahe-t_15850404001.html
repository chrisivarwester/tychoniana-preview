The original

HENRICUS BRUCÆUS AD TYCHONEM BRAHE.

LITERAS tuas, amantissime TYCHO, quibus Calendarij a tuo domestico Studioso edendi mentionem fecisti, accepi et iam Typos fusos a VITO miseram unâ cum libris illius Florentini, adiectis schedulis duabus, una VITI, altera aurigae Witebergensis, quibus de precio tibi constabit. Miratus autem sum, te potioribus intentum permittere, ut talia in tua Typographia edantur, quae trita sunt et vulgaria, in quibus si quis aëris mutationes praedicendas suscipiat, paene ridiculum sese toti mundo exponit. Et tot a vero aberrantes praedictiones effecerunt, ut nugarum mendaciorumque eiuscemodi libri habeantur. Nec credo fuisse quemquam, qui huius hyemis levitatem et siccitatem praedixerit, cuius constitutio prope verna habenda est, si frigoris imbecillitatem spectes. Et quam ridiculum sese praebuit quidam Astrologaster, qui in suo Calendario Martio magna frigora et nives minatur, adeo fisus suis praedictionibus, ut si eventus falleret, iam artem nullam crederet. Et ego arbitror, veris et irrefutabilibus rationibus posse demonstrari, incertam plane esse hanc de aëris mutationibus praedictionem, praeter anni tempora, quae ut plurimum suas constitutiones servant. Sed tu forte, mi TYCHO, alias rationes habes, cur haec a tuis Discipulis fieri permittas, etiamsi tu ipse non multum tribuas eiuscemodi praedictionibus, quarum difficultatem, immo paene impossibilitatem ipsemet satis nosti, quod et in familiari nostro colloquio a te audivisse recordor, cum alias etiam Astrologica vaticinia, uti multi alias solent, non pluris facias, quam par est. Credo itaque, te sic indulgere tuis Discipulis, qui prognosticis annuis, more recepto, conficiendis delectantur, ut habeant, quo graviora studia leniant, fortassis etiam aliquid praeter communem formam a te hauserunt. Si sic est, prodeat Calendarium illud in publicum, et discamus inde experiamurque, quae prius nos latuerunt. Scio enim te nil vulgare et tritum moliri, neque levia tuis concedere. Plura non possum, urgente nuntio.

Bene vale, et ut hoc in genere nobis sis Apollo, opto.
Datae in Holsatia ipso die Palmarum 1585.

English translation machine translation, Secure

Heinrich Brucaeus to Tycho Brahe.

I have received your letter, most loving Tycho, in which you made mention of a calendar [almanac] to be published by your resident student; and I had already sent the founts of type [Typos fusos] from Vitus, together with the books of that Florentine, with two notes [schedulae] added — one from Vitus, the other from the Wittenberg carter — by which the price will be clear to you. But I was astonished that you, intent on weightier matters, should permit such things to be published at your press as are hackneyed and commonplace, in which, if anyone undertakes to predict the changes of the weather, he exposes himself to the whole world as well-nigh ridiculous. And so many predictions straying from the truth have brought it about that books of this kind are held to be trifles and lies. Nor do I believe there was anyone who predicted the mildness and dryness of this winter, whose constitution is to be reckoned almost spring-like, if you regard the feebleness of the cold. And how ridiculous did a certain astrologaster make himself, who in his calendar threatens great frosts and snows for March, so trusting in his predictions that, if the event should prove him wrong, he would believe there to be no science [art] at all [in it]. And I, for my part, judge that it can be demonstrated by true and irrefutable arguments that this prediction concerning the changes of the weather is plainly uncertain — apart from the seasons of the year, which for the most part keep their [own] constitutions. But perhaps, my Tycho, you have other reasons why you permit these things to be done by your pupils, even if you yourself do not attach much weight to predictions of this kind, the difficulty — nay, the well-nigh impossibility — of which you yourself know well enough; which I recall having heard from you, too, in our familiar conversation, since you do not — as many others are otherwise wont to do — set more store by astrological prophecies than is fitting. I believe, therefore, that you indulge your pupils thus — who take delight in compiling annual prognostics, after the received custom — so that they may have something with which to relieve their heavier studies; perhaps too they have drawn from you something beyond the common form. If it is so, let that calendar [Calendarium] come forth into public, and let us learn from it and try out the things that were hidden from us before. For I know that you devise nothing common and hackneyed, and grant nothing trifling to your pupils. I can [write] no more, as the messenger presses.

Fare you well; and I pray that in this kind [of thing] you may be an Apollo to us.
Given in Holstein, on Palm Sunday itself, 1585.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Henricus Brucaeus = Heinrich Brucaeus (1531–1593), professor of medicine and mathematics at the University of Rostock, a Lutheran scholar of Flemish origin; Tycho's friend and one of his early academic mentors, with whom he maintained a long technical correspondence. The letter is preserved among those Tycho gathered for the Epistolae (1568–1587).
  • Heading. HENRICUS BRUCAeUS AD TYCHONEM BRAHE — "Heinrich Brucaeus to Tycho Brahe," the editorial/archival rubric of the copy; kept as the salutation line.
  • Subject — the Calendarium / annual prognostic. The letter concerns an almanac (Calendarium; prognostica annua) that one of Tycho's resident students (domesticus Studiosus … Discipuli) was to print at the Uraniborg press (tua Typographia). Brucaeus's whole drift is a friendly remonstrance: weather-prediction almanacs are trita et vulgaria ("hackneyed and commonplace"), beneath Tycho's press; he is surprised Tycho permits them, while granting that Tycho himself sets little store by such forecasting. Tycho's press did issue student prognostications in this period (e.g. those of his famuli), so the reference is to a real publishing activity, not a hypothetical.
  • Persons named in the consignment. VITO / VITI = Vitus (Veit), evidently an agent/founder at or near Wittenberg who supplied type (Typos fusos, "cast/founded types"); aurigae Witebergensis = "the Wittenberg carter/wagoner," the carrier who conveyed the consignment. libris illius Florentini = "the books of that Florentine" — an unidentified Florentine (bookseller or author) whose books accompanied the type; left unidentified rather than guessed. schedulis duabus = "two notes/slips" (here invoices or price-memoranda), una VITI, altera aurigae "one from Vitus, the other from the carter," quibus de precio tibi constabit "by which the price will be clear to you."
  • Astrology / meteorology (technical sense). aëris mutationes praedicere = "to predict the changes of the weather/air" — the standard early-modern term for almanac weather-forecasting (astro-meteorology), not astronomy proper. anni tempora … suas constitutiones servant = "the seasons of the year keep their own constitutions" (constitutio in the medical-meteorological sense of the prevailing temperament of a season); rendered "constitution" consistently. Astrologica vaticinia = "astrological prophecies."
  • Astrologaster — a contemptuous coinage, "petty/quack astrologer" (the -aster suffix = a poor imitation, as grammaticaster, philosophaster); kept as "astrologaster" with the pejorative force flagged rather than smoothed to a neutral "astrologer."
  • Calendario Martio — "in his calendar for March" (i.e. the March entry/forecast of the offending astrologer's almanac), not a calendar of Mars; rendered "in his calendar … for March."
  • artem nullam crederet — ars here = "science / art [of prediction]"; "he would believe there to be no science at all [in it]," i.e. would abandon faith in the whole art if his forecast failed.
  • Crux: Calcearium illud. prodeat Calcearium illud in publicum — literally "let that shoe-leather / cobbler's-stuff (calcearium) come forth into public." Calcearium (from calceus, "shoe") is not a standard word; the most natural reading is a deliberately dismissive/jocular tag for the almanac just described ("that cobblery," i.e. that pieced-together job of work), parallel to nugae "trifles" above. It is not securely established, however, that this is the intended sense; the word may conceal a proper name, a title, or a copyist's corruption in the Vienna copy (Afskrift). The dismissive sense is given in the translation with the Latin retained in brackets, and the reading is flagged here rather than silently normalised. This is the named crux that holds the grade to Possible.
  • ut … nobis sis Apollo — "that you may be an Apollo to us": a closing compliment, Apollo as god of both medicine and the [astral] arts; Brucaeus wishes Tycho to be their authority "in this kind [of thing]" (hoc in genere), i.e. in setting astrology/almanac-making on a sound footing.
  • Epistolary formula. Bene vale = "Fare you well". Plura non possum, urgente nuntio = "I can [write] no more, as the messenger presses" — a stock close signalling haste.
  • Dateline & calendar. Datae in Holsatia ipso die Palmarum 1585 = "Given in Holstein, on Palm Sunday itself, 1585." Brucaeus, of Rostock, wrote while in Holstein (the duchy south of Denmark). Die Palmarum = Palm Sunday, the Sunday before Easter; the dateline gives the moveable feast, not a civil day-of-month. In the Julian (Old Style) calendar that Brucaeus, as a Rostock/Holstein Lutheran, used in 1585, Easter fell on 18 April O.S., placing Palm Sunday on 11 April 1585 O.S. The edition label ("1585. 4. april. Holsten") assigns 4 April, which matches neither the Old-Style nor the New-Style Palm Sunday for 1585 (N.S. Palm Sunday = 14 April); 4 April 1585 O.S. was a Sunday but the fifth Sunday in Lent (Judica/Passion Sunday), not Palm Sunday. The discrepancy is flagged, not reconciled: the header leads with the as-written feast-date ("Palm Sunday 1585 [O.S.]") and appends the catalogued "(catalogued 4 April 1585)"; the basis of the catalogue's "4 April" is not recoverable from the letter text and is left unresolved. No date is silently converted.
  • Correction. Latin: 'Calcearium' corrected to 'Calendarium' throughout its single occurrence, per Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII, p. 92 — the letter's subject is an almanac, not shoe-leather. English: the corresponding clause corrected from 'that shoe-leather' to 'that calendar', and the earlier clause on astrological prophecies corrected to restore the sense that Tycho is the exception to what 'many others' customarily do (overvalue such prophecies), not a fellow example of restraint.

The translation and its checking

Grade Secure · collated against Dreyer's page images, Opera Omnia VII pp. 91–92; defects the audit noted: text error (1), mistranslation (1) (the translator's own grade before the audit was Possible).

Collated against Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia (the edition of record), reading the printed page images. Collated the full letter (the type-founts/Vitus passage, the astrologaster-and-weather-prediction critique, the pupils'-prognostics defense) against Dreyer TBOO VII pp.91-92 as page images. Confirmed a source-text word corruption (Calcearium for Calendarium) that directly produces nonsensical English, and a contrast-flip mistranslation in the 'many others' clause. Both defects change what a scholar would take away from the letter.

2 defects found:
- text error (high). Dreyer clearly prints 'Calendarium' (the calendar/almanac that is the letter's entire subject — set up in the opening line 'Calendarij... edendi mentionem fecisti'). The transcribed original has the nonword 'Calcearium', which the translator then rendered literally as 'shoe-leather', producing a baffling non sequitur in a letter that is otherwise coherently about an almanac. Dreyer prints: prodeat Calendarium illud in publicum.
- mistranslation (medium). The Latin's 'uti multi alias solent' borrows its verb from the main clause and means 'as many others are otherwise accustomed [to value them more than is fitting]' — i.e. Brucaeus is praising Tycho as an exception to a common failing. The English reads as though the 'many others' also show the same restraint, inverting the compliment into a banal generalization. At: “since you also set no more store than is fitting by astrological prophecies, as many otherwise are wont to do”.

Repaired. Verified both defects by direct visual inspection of the Dreyer TBOO VII pp.91-93 page images. 'Calendarium' is unambiguous on p.92 and directly continuous with the letter's opening subject (a student's almanac). The mistranslation defect concerns English phrasing only (the Latin the transcribed original itself was already correct and unchanged); the corrected clause follows the Latin's word order and the elliptical-verb reading given in the audit's own diagnosis, staying close to the source without adding interpretive colour beyond restoring the correct logical direction of the compliment.
