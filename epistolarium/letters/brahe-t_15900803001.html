Editorial note. Tycho's direct reply to Hájek's letter of 11 June 1590: a point-by-point rebuttal of Hájek's thesis that comet-motions cannot be reduced to law, plus the new Caesarean Privilege (via Curtius), the Craig papers, instrument models, the paper-mill publication delay, and the 21 July 1590 solar eclipse (the same eclipse, book-returns, and prosthaphaeresis crux that recur in the Magini letter of the same period, 1 December 1590). The transcription is comparatively clean (only light digitised text layer slips, corrected and flagged; no heavy apparatus-sigla as in Hájek's letter of 11 Jun 1590 or the undated Peucer letter of 1590). All cruxes confirmed: the comet daily-motion figures (1577 comet 6°→¼°/day, book 2 ch. 8, p. 278; the Feb–Mar 1590 comet 7½°→1½°/day over 12 days, ⅛ of a circle, "3rd column" of the table, p. 279); the prosthaphaeresis dates 24 Nov / 26 Dec (with the "pag. 279" cross-ref and "two whole months," pp. 283–284); the 1577-not-1580 correction of Hájek's slip (p. 282); the Craig/Regiomontanus parallax (Problems 2/3/4, the 18-minute interval, p. 285); the 21 July 1590 eclipse (6ʰ51ᵐ / 9ʰ exactly / 4 of 8 digits / Leo, p. 289); and the production notes (5–6 artificers, 3rd year, 3+5 gatherings, chs. 7/9/10, two papermakers, Apian's comet of 1531, p. 287–288). CLARISSIMO ET PRÆSTANTISSIMO VIRO, MULTIPLICIQUE DOCTRINARUM GENERE
EXCELLENTI DOMINO THADDÆO HAGECIO AB
HAYCK, PHILOSOPHIÆ ET MEDICINÆ DOCTORI EXIMIO, AMICO SUO INPRIMIS
COLENDO. S.

QUOD ultimis illis brevioribus per Studiosum Rostochiensem missis pollicebaris, optime et
eruditissime mi THADDÆE, amice
colendissime, id iam nunc satis superque præstitum est. Accessit enim hisce
diebus Tabellarius Pragensis unâ cum novo
Privilegio Cæsareo, magnifici Domini CURTIJ tuasque literas secum asportans, quæ omnia mihi erant
longe acceptissima , tibique quas possum ago gratias, quod ad impetrandum mihi a Cæsarea
Maiestate hoc de novo Privilegium amplissimum Dominum Procancellarium
permoveris. Est sane illud satis splendidum atque honorificum, in quo non
habeam, quod amplius desiderem. Neque si Gallicanum ad vos pervenisset (quod
nescio qua incuria interciderit; neque enim transcriptio illa, quam mittere
intendebam, uspiam hîc reperitur) multum ad negotij ampliorem atque solidiorem
absolutionem præstitisset. Expecto adhuc aliud a novo illo Galliarum Rege
Navarræo, sed vix pari erga studia Mathematica affectione, cum nulli, quod
sciam, in Aulis Regum ab intimis sint consilijs, qui Mathemata adeo impense
atque solide non solum ament, sed etiam intelligant, atque præstantissimus ille
Dominus CURTIUS, cuius beneficio
Cæsareanum hoc Diploma impetratum atque adornatum esse grata mente agnosco. Sed
cum ipsimet hac de re et ijs, quæ eruditissima ipsius Epistola continentur,
scribam, nolo ea pluribus hîc attingere, sed potius ad respondendum hisce tuis
ultimis procedam. In quibus tamen eam partem, quæ ab
initio de rescriptionis mutuæ retardationibus atque excusationibus affatim agit,
præteribo, cum amicitia nostra multo iam tempore confirmata omni suspicione
vacare debeat, neque purgatione, si quid præter expectationem inciderit,
multopere indigeat.

De hypothesibus etiam meis, quas cœlo ipso accuratius introspecto,
Martio præsertim sydere plus Sole in acronycho situ terris appropinquante (ut
non solum parallaxes eius summa diligentia a nobis aliquoties perquisitæ, sed
transitus ipsi diurni aliquanto Ptolemaicis ratiocinijs ob maiorem appropinquationem
concitatiores aperte evineunt) extructis ordinatisque nihil hîc superaddam;
satis de ijs, quantum Epistola fert, superioribus egi; plenarie eas elucidare
citraque iustam contradictionem comprobare atque asserere alterius erit loci et
prolixioris negotij. Utrumque etiam eorum, quos omitti vis, et ego hîc lubens
negligam, neque de priore isto iam antea scripsissem, nisi ipsemet occasionem
præbuisses. Alter Mathemata rectius intelligit, et tibi reconciliatus ferendus
veniet. Ad ea itaque, quæ circa defensionem Mesthlinianam a me institutam, præsertim quia non impossibile
esse Cometarum motus sub hypothesin aliquam eorum apparentijs analogam redigere,
senserim, quod etiamnum in controversiam moves, properabo. Quæ de tuis
Mastigibus et calumniatoribus iusto procul dubio dolore
impulsus intermisces, cum isti nihil, quod ad rem faciat, in medium adferant, et
ob id mentionem ulteriorem non mereantur, eos prætermittens ipsam quæstionem, in
qua tibi adhuc non satisfactum esse ais, plenius discutiam, tuisque dubijs atque
obiectionibus ulterius obviabo. Id quod tibi non ingratum confido, imo sic fore,
propria tua, qua disputationem illam claudis, petitio testatur.

Cometas certæ motuum legi, quemadmodum in Planetis mundo coævis
fit, astringi non posse, ita ut universalis aliqua omnibus sufficiens
constituatur hypothesis, tibi equidem non invite concesserim, neque id unquam
asserui. Sed hæc est mea de his sententia, quam te nondum integre percepisse
video: quod videlicet Cometæ, ex quo omnes revera in ipso cœlo procreentur
(statuat ex his aliquos sublunares, qui volet, mihi id nequaquam consonum
videtur) necessario etiam cœlestis naturæ participes sint, utut ea ad tantam
perfectionem non sit perducta, quanta in perennibus illis corporibus spectatur.
Cum autem cœlum ipsum et quæ in eo revolvuntur corpora, persectissimum motum, id
est circularem, sive simplicem sive compositum, naturaliter appetant atque
sortiantur, non inconveniens duco, ipsos quoque Cometas circularem et ordinarium
in cœlo exercere cursum, qui si forte ad instar Planetarum non omni ex parte
absolutus fuerit, eos tamen æmulatur et quam proxime ad orbicularem et uniformem
incessum tendit. Non tamen ubique Solem, ut Planetæ, nec Terram, ut luminaria et fixæ, pro centro suorum circuituum
respiciunt, sed in immensa mundi vastitate quodvis punctum, cuius respectu motus
eorum circularis atque æquabilis evadat, sortiri possunt, quemadmodum etiam
varia et diversa motuum lege cientur, et nunc in has, nunc illas cœli partes pro
libito excurrunt, ideoque non omnibus una et eadem sufficiet hypothesis, sed
quilibet eorum propriam requiret, per ipsas apparentias, quasi ex posteriore,
constituendam. Et licet maior hîc subsit difficultas, quam in Planetis, qui
integras periodos aliquoties absolvunt et diutinæ observationi patent, non tamen
ob id extremam impossibilitatem huic negotio inesse statuendum erit, antequam
multiplici atque indefesso conatu, an eo pervenire liceat, experti fuerimus. Ego
sane in prælustri illo Cometa anni 77mi, cuius motus adeo
inæqualis videbatur, ut ab initio 6 proxime gradus, in fine vix quartam partem
unius quotidiano incessu confecerit, satis evidenter ostendi ad regularem
peneque æquabilem et circularem progressionis ordinationem
astringi potuisse, adeo ut numeri ex hypothesi præsupposita deducti ipsis
observationibus certius et promptius congruerent, quam in ullis Planetis
hactenus effectum est, prout caput 8 libri nostri 2di (modo
quis illud attentius introspiciat) abunde testatur. Si igitur in hoc Cometa, qui
saltem quartam cœli partem suo ductu percurrere videbatur, et interea plane
inæqualem, a celeriore tamen in tardiorem pedetentim sese remittentem motum
exprimebat, præstari potuit, ut certis et orbicularibus tramitibus eius
inæquales apparentiæ excusarentur: cur in alijs etiam idem, etsi diversimode,
prout cuiusque fert nobis exhibitus motus, fieri posse omnino impossibile
duxerimus? Licet vero magna hîc subijt difficultas, non tamen, ut tu vis, summa.
Ac si maxime id detur, possibilitatem tamen omnem non penitus excludet: multa
enim sunt supremæ difficultati obnoxia, ad quæ humani ingenij industria atque
labor pertingere ægerrime potest, quæ tamen ob id non prorsus impossibilia,
qualis est quadratura circuli, ne quidem a recentioribus, qui eam adinvenisse
sibi persuaserunt, adamussim hactenus demonstrata: hæc tamen inter scibilia sed
nondum cognita numeratur. At tu in tot Cometis per multa secula ex eorum
apparitionibus possibilitatem hanc, de qua loquor, utut difficultatis plenam,
luculenter evinci postulas. Verum cum te non lateat, nullos Cometas exacta
amussi ante nostra tempora (licet et nunc plurimi in his intolerabiliter
impegerint) cœlitus infallibilibus organis observatos esse, adeo ut ne quidem in
æthere eos versari per parallaxium exilitatem exploratum fuerit, nedum ut inde
æthereus aliquis et ordinarius incessus demonstretur, frustra ex paucissimis
ijsdemque nullius momenti Dedomenis dubij huius resolutionem exigis. Quod in
unico illo anni 77mi Cometa fieri posse ostendi, etsi non
impugnes, tamen aliorum iudicio hoc totum negotium submittis, ac plurium Cometarum testimonijs confirmari cupis. Sane si aliorum
iudicia, utut eruditionis laude præstantium, expectas, vereor, ne nimis diu
frustraque veritatem in abscondito latentem quæras, in tanta iudiciorum
varietate et pertinacia, ne dicam perversitate, dum nemo ferme quidpiam ignorare
vult, et quilibet suo abundet sensu. In reliquis Cometis, quotquot ipsemet
accurate observavi, non dubito, quin etiam per convenientem hypothesin eorum
motus, utut inæqualis apparens, sub concinnam tamen regularitatem redigi possit.
Utinam saltem huic labori, qui non est mediocris, par detur
gratia atque fructus. Nam ne in ultimo quidem, qui hoc anno circa Februarij
finem atque Martij initia apparuit (cuius brevem sed satis exactam descriptionem
tecum ultimo communicavi) utut is per 12 saltem dies, quibus vix octavam circuli
partem absolvit, sese conspiciendum exhibuerit, eius tamen apparentias in certam
aliquam ac regularem normam componere pro impossibili non habeo, licet is initio
7½ gradus diurno motu emensus sit, circa ultimum terminum saltem sesquialterum.
Non enim tumultuario incessu a celeriore in tardiorem motum deveniebat, sed
successive atque ordinarie concitationem suam remisit, pulchra et sibi
consentienti proportione, quemadmodum videre est ex 3a
columna Tabellæ diarium Cometæ motum exprimentis, ubi cursus eius diurnus
proprius in suo ductu exponitur. Nihil enim illic invenies interruptum, confusum
aut inordinatum. Et Cometa is exactissime arcum circuli in sphæra maximi, nullo
prorsus modo ab eo hac vel illac digrediens, exprimebat, quod et cum plærisque
alijs commune habuit, ut vel ex hoc solo colligere liceat, Cometas, quemadmodum
suo apparente ductu portiones circuli maximi describunt, nec hincinde incerto
tramite divagantur, sic etiam sub eodem ductu circularem atque regularem in ipso
cœlo exercere tenorem, quamvis is nobis e terris aspicientibus inæqualis
putetur.

Atque hæc ad primam tuam obiectionem sufficiant, quæ et
posterioribus, si rite expendantur, satis obviant. Sed tamen et has particulatim
introspiciamus. Omnes Cometas, qui hactenus emerserunt vel in posterum
prodibunt, sub certam motionis normam redigere non dabitur, ex quo omnium exactæ
observationes in promptu esse nequeant. Neque id meæ assertioni quippiam
derogat, cum non universalem et omnibus Cometis pariter competentem motionis
legem præscribi posse varietas in singulis satis nos commonefaciat. Quæ igitur
de singularibus, et quorum apparentiæ admodum præcise innotuerunt, loquor, tu ad
universalia citra mentem meam trahis. Cum enim singuli ferme Cometæ aliquid
peculiare obtineant, impossibile erit ex aliquot particularitatibus cognitis
ijsdemque adinvicem non consentientibus generalem construere doctrinam, ideoque ego eam nusquam pollicitus sum Tertia tua ratio ex novitate corporum Cometarum, et quod numero
differant, nec in eodem et uno cœli loco emergant, quodque interdum stent,
interdum lento, aliquando concitatissimo, eoque inordinato ferantur motu
rapianturque, et rarissime perfectos describant circulos: hæc, inquam, et
similia id, quod intendimus, non evertunt. Quædam enim eorum nimis libere et
citra ullam ratam observationem a te proponuntur. Neque enim ego ullum ex
quinque a me diligenter observatis Cometis adinveni, qui adeo anomalum, qualem
tu obijcis, exhibuerit motum. Nullus eorum erat stationarius. Et licet interdum
velocius, nonnumquam vero tardius ferebantur, ista tamen alteratio non subito
illis contingebat, nihilque interrupti aut confusi, ut tu prætendis, passi sunt,
sed successive atque ordinaria lege motum intendebant aut potius remittebant.
Neque ullos Cometas ab alijs, quibus tuto fidere liceat, hactenus observatos
cognovi, quos adeo inordinata, prout tu infers, discohærentia implicârit.
Fateor quidem, plurimos perplexum motum Cometis a se conspectis
attribuere; sed id animadversionis incuria factum, qui solide observationes
tractare didicerunt, non ignorant. Plus enim ad has omni ex parte citra errorem
administrandas requiritur laboris et circumspectionis, quam quivis æstimare
poterit. Sic tu Cometam illum anni 77mi tumultuario et
interrupto agitatum motu, adeo ut aliquando saltum 8 graduum commiserit, literis
consignasti, et GEMMA etiam tale quid illi
attribuebat, cum tamen ex meis omni serena nocte factis observationibus nihil
eiuscemodi illi unquam competierit, sed adeo ordinarie velocitatem motus, quam
ab initio habuit, successive, nullo præfracto saltu incidente, remisit, ut
circularis motionis regularitatem non omnino respuerit. Nil igitur mirum erit,
si quidam alij, qui forte non adeo diligenter, atque tu et GEMMA, talibus attendunt, perplexum et anomalum Cometis temere
attribuunt motum. Paucissimis enim idonea et omni errore carentia ad manus sunt
instrumenta. Nec affixarum loca, quarum usus requiritur, rite constant, ut potius harum incertitudini atque incompetenti ordinationi
irregularitas illa attribuenda veniat, quam ipsis Cometis. Non igitur fluctuanti
motu rapiuntur; absit enim, ut tale quid cœlestibus, quæ ordinis et
regularitatis inprimis sunt appetentia, aspergatur. Sed maior est in eorum
incessu concinnitas, quam a plærisque hactenus animadversum. Sic quod Cometæ
rarissime integros et perfectos describant circulos, etsi multum difficultatis
in eorum curriculo regulando suggerat, possibilitatem tamen omnem non prorsus
excludit, sed eo plus meretur laudis atque gratiæ, quo magis arduus et operosus
sit conatus. Ex data autem alicuius circuli portione, de eius centro atque
magnitudine, licet tota peripheria non constet, quin cognitio rata haberi
possit, quis Geometrarum negabit? Quod infers, si alicuius Cometæ motus fuerit accurate exploratus, ita ut ex portione aliqua de
integra eius revolutione iudicari possit, non tamen id concedi, ut hinc de
quorumvis Cometarum motu tale quid constituatur: id meæ sententiæ nullatenus
refragatur, ut ex semel atque iterum dictis satis liquet. Sic neque ea, quæ de
stellis fixis replicas, mihi adversantur. Quod enim multo et longo tempore a
diversis Artificibus in illis præstitum est, id paucis diebus in Cometa aliquo
ab uno et eodem observatore multo etiam certius fieri potest. Et utinam ea, quæ
de fixarum motu quasi per manus ab Astronomis tradita sunt, non plus
perplexitatis atque incertitudinis clam haberent, quam aliquis ex ijs qui a
nobis observati sunt, Cometis! Utique promptior esset ad earum inæqualitates
excusandas ratio. Nec est necesse, Cometas semper eosdem esse ut stellas, nisi
ijsdem etiam legibus omnes subijci affirmaretur, quod nusquam a me prolatum est.
Unde et reliqua his similia, quæ obijciuntur, nihil officiunt, ut mirari satis
non possim, qua occasione mea placita ita accipias, ut, si locum mereri debeant,
oporteat primo docere, omnes Cometas, quotquot hactenus conspeximus, fuisse unum
et eundem Cometam, quasi non singulis pro alia atque alia, quam exhibent,
motionis habitudine, dissimilis quoque hypotheseos ratio præfiniri possit.
Plures dantur Cometæ, nec unquam ijdem redeunt, diversa in cœlo sortiuntur loca,
motum etiam dissimilem. Quid tum postea? Inveniantur itaque pro singulis
correspondentes hypotheses, quæ etiam inter se dissimiles erunt, hoc saltem
habentes commune, ut nobis apparens inæqualitas per
circularem motum, quomodocunque assumptum, excusetur.

Præterea quod addis, ex unius vel alterius diei motu in Cometis non
posse ratiocinationem institui de tota illius periodo, et exemplum e Luna
petitum profers, ista nihil ad meam opinionem labefactandam faciunt, imo potius
ostendunt, te longe adhuc abesse a meo sensu. Neque enim ex unius vel alterius
diei progressu de Cometarum periodo aliquid statuendum unquam affirmavi, sciens
utique apparentem motum inæqualem esse; sed a principio usque ad finem totius
durationis eorum apparentias diligentissime esse scrutandas censeo, idque sub eo
ipso incessus tramite, quem cœlitus designare videntur, atque tum demum,
collatis omnibus observationibus, videndum, an dari possit aliquis circularis in
cœli capacitate ductus, sive simplex sive compositus, cuius beneficio id, quod
nobis inæquale apparuit, ad competentem æqualitatem concinnari possit. Cum enim
cœlum non sit durum atque impervium corpus pluribus orbibus confertum, ut falso
nos hactenus docuit Peripateticorum schola, possunt sane Cometæ in omni eius
parte libere rotari et circularem exercere motum, modo non Planetarum corporibus
impingant, quod etiam ab eorum dispositore satis cavetur. In octava quam vocant
sphæra intra stellas fixas nullos prodire Cometas
existimârim, nisi et illi immobiles sint, qualis erat Stella Nova. Collationem,
quam instituis circa motum stellarum et Cometarum, nolo multis replicare, quia
ad eam quam tueor sententiam convellendam nihil facit. Neque ea, quæ de stellis
fixis sextam circuli partem nondum emensis in medium adduxi, sic accipienda
erunt. Ista enim exempli loco tantum protuli, quod COPERNICUS attentârit
ex portione aliqua motus transacti, de integra periodo ratiocinari, quamvis
nequaquam id, quod sperabat, assecutus sit. Tota enim eius de fixarum motu
salvando speculatio erronea est et apparentijs nequaquam consona. Neque enim
nunc centenis annis fixæ unum gradum absolvunt, ut eius fert hypothesis, sed
potius in septuaginta uno, adeo ut dubium sit, an tanta illis competat
inæqualitas, quanta hactenus credita est, et an vetustissimorum Astronomorum de his prolata rite se habeant; ut si Cometæ alicuius motus non exactius
nobis perspectus fuerit, quam fixarum iam a bis mille annis innotuit progressio,
operam lusisse videatur, qui hypothesin concinnam extruere tentârit. Multa enim in observationibus, tam veterum quam recentiorum, quo
ad stellas fixas desidero. Neque eam quam oportuit in his verificandis adhibuit
PTOLEMÆUS diligentiam et præcisionem,
multoque minus COPERNICUS, quod alterius est loci plenius discutere.

hypotheses, neque inficiari merito potes ea, quæ de Cometa anni 77mi (non enim de eo, quo anno 80 effulsit, ut tuæ habent literæ, id
protuli) publicavi, quod videlicet instar ♀ et ☿ circa ☉lem obambulârit, cum e certis observationibus idipsum
Geometrice demonstrârim, et ratiocinationes meæ in libro transmisso tibi in
promptu sint. Si quid habes, quod ijs opponi possit, id profer: sin minus, cur
ijs, quæ rite et fideliter nec sine labore extricata sunt, non acquiescis? et
quod in eo Cometa tali modo effectum est, cur in alijs alio quoque modo, prout
apparentiæ ipsæ postulârint, fieri posse diffidis? Videris mihi in expendendis
hisce omnibus præiudicio (quod pace tua dixerim) quodam uti, et iam antea tecum
conclusisse, id impossibile esse, quod an fieri posset, inquirendum prius
fuerat. Hincque factum existimo, ut ea, quæ capite 8 et 9 circa hypothesin dicti
Cometæ enucleandam atque in numeros resolvendam non minimo labore susceperam,
obiter saltem inspexeris, ex quo adhuc de rei adeo diligenter enodatæ
certitudine dubites et ad aliorum iudicia appelles. Nec scio, quid sit caussæ,
cur tantopere Cometarum motus certa lege, prout cuiusque postulat incessus,
salvari posse impugnes, cum ipsemet non diffitearis, eos in ætherea mundi
regione procreari. Pati itaque eo promtius deberes, nonnullos paulo adhuc altius
eorum conditiones examinare, et cœlestibus corporibus cœlestem atque ordinatum
attribuere motum. Imo, siquidem tibi ipsi non
suppetit otium, aut voluntas deest talia scrutandi, alios, impossibilitatem
obijciendo, a tam egregio conatu non retrahere, sed potius, ut hæc plenius
pervestigare satagerent, stimulum addere debebas. Quod amicitiæ nostræ confisus
benevole et candide te monere volui, petoque, ut hanc nostram ad tua dubia
qualemcunque responsionem boni consulas. Ego de his plura non addam; permittam
potius cuilibet sentire, quod volet, an videlicet ordinarie, an vero confuse in
cœlo moveantur Cometæ. Memineris tamen, naturam ipsam ordine
decenti gaudere et a perplexitate et confusione abhorrere, ut ob id non abs re
fuerit, quod Pythagorei numerorum proportionibus omnia includere studuerint. Hæc
itaque de istis sufficiant, si modo non nimia fuerint. Nunc ad reliqua tuis
literis comprehensa progrediar.

To the most distinguished and most excellent man, eminent in a manifold kind of learning, Lord Thaddaeus Hagecius of Hájek, eminent Doctor of Philosophy and Medicine, his especially-to-be-cultivated friend. Greeting.

What you promised in that last, shorter [letter] sent by the Rostock student, my excellent and most erudite Thaddaeus, most-to-be-cultivated friend, has now been more than sufficiently performed. For these days a Prague courier arrived, carrying with him the new Imperial Privilege, the letters of the Magnificent Lord Curtius [Jacob Kurz von Senftenau], and yours — all of which were far most acceptable to me; and I give you what thanks I can, that you moved the most ample Lord Vice-Chancellor to obtain for me this new Privilege from the Imperial Majesty. It is indeed splendid enough and honourable, in which I have nothing further to desire. Nor, had the Gallican [privilege] reached you (which, by I-know-not-what carelessness, has fallen in [been lost]; for that transcript which I meant to send is nowhere to be found here), would it have contributed much to a fuller and more solid completion of the business. I still expect another from that new King of the Gauls, [the one] of Navarre [Henri IV], but scarcely with equal affection toward Mathematical studies, since none, that I know, in the Courts of Kings is of the privy council who not only love Mathematics so earnestly and solidly, but also understand [them], as that most excellent Lord Curtius — by whose benefit I gratefully acknowledge this Imperial Diploma was obtained and prepared. But since I am writing about this matter, and about those [things] contained in his most erudite Letter, to himself, I do not wish to touch them here at greater length, but rather will proceed to replying to this last [letter] of yours. In which, however, I will pass over that part which at the beginning deals fully with the delays and excuses of [our] mutual correspondence, since our friendship, now confirmed by much time, ought to be free of all suspicion, nor much needs purgation if anything has fallen out beyond expectation.

About my hypotheses too — built and ordered from the heaven itself, more accurately inspected (especially with the Martian star approaching the Earth, in the acronychal position, more than the Sun; so that not only its parallaxes, sought out by us several times with the greatest diligence, but its very diurnal transits, somewhat swifter than the Ptolemaic reckonings on account of the greater approach, openly prove [it]) — I will add nothing here; I have dealt with them enough, as far as a Letter bears, in [my] earlier [ones]; to elucidate them fully, and to confirm and assert [them] without just contradiction, will be [a matter] for another place and a more prolix business. Each too of those [two men] whom you wish omitted, I too will here gladly neglect; nor would I have written before about that former one, had you not yourself given the occasion. The other understands Mathematics more rightly, and, reconciled to you, will be tolerable. I will therefore hasten to those [things] which I thought about the defence of Maestlin undertaken by me — especially that it is not impossible to reduce the motions of Comets to some hypothesis analogous to their appearances — which you still move into controversy. Those [things] which, driven doubtless by just grief, you intermingle about your whips and calumniators — since those bring forward nothing that makes for the matter, and on that account merit no further mention — passing them over, I will discuss more fully the question itself, in which you say you are not yet satisfied, and will further meet your doubts and objections. Which I trust is not unwelcome to you; nay, that it will be so, your own request, with which you close that disputation, testifies.

That Comets cannot be bound by a fixed law of motions, as happens in the Planets coeval with the world — so that some universal hypothesis sufficient for all be constituted — I would indeed not unwillingly grant you, nor have I ever asserted it. But this is my opinion about them, which I see you have not yet wholly perceived: namely that Comets, since they are all really procreated in the heaven itself (let whoever wishes posit some of them sublunary; to me it seems by no means consonant), are necessarily partakers also of celestial nature, although it [their nature] has not been brought to so great a perfection as is beheld in those everlasting bodies. But since the heaven itself, and the bodies which revolve in it, naturally desire and are allotted the most perfect motion — that is, circular, whether simple or composite — I deem it not unfitting that the Comets too exercise a circular and ordinary course in the heaven, which, if perhaps it is not in every respect completed after the manner of the Planets, yet emulates them and tends as nearly as possible to an orbicular and uniform advance. Yet they do not everywhere regard the Sun, as the Planets [do], nor the Earth, as the luminaries and the fixed [stars] [do], as the centre of their circuits; but in the immense vastness of the world they can be allotted any point whatever, with respect to which their motion turns out circular and equable — just as they are also stirred by a various and diverse law of motions, and run out now into these, now those, parts of the heaven at pleasure; and therefore one and the same hypothesis will not suffice for all, but each of them will require its own, to be constituted through the appearances themselves, as it were a posteriori. And although a greater difficulty lies beneath here than in the Planets (which complete whole periods several times and lie open to long observation), it must not on that account be determined that an extreme impossibility inheres in this business, before we have tried, by manifold and indefatigable effort, whether it is permitted to arrive thither. I indeed, in that splendid Comet of [15]77 — whose motion seemed so unequal that at the beginning it accomplished nearly 6 degrees, at the end scarcely a quarter of one, by [its] daily advance — showed evidently enough that it could be bound to a regular and well-nigh equable and circular ordering of progression, so much so that the numbers deduced from the presupposed hypothesis agreed with the observations themselves more surely and readily than has hitherto been effected in any Planets — as chapter 8 of our second book (provided one inspect it more attentively) abundantly testifies. If, therefore, in this Comet — which seemed to traverse at least a quarter of the heaven by its course, and meanwhile expressed a plainly unequal motion, yet one gradually remitting itself from swifter to slower — it could be achieved that its unequal appearances were saved by fixed and orbicular tracks: why should we deem it altogether impossible that the same can be done in others too, even if in diverse manner, according as each one's motion exhibited to us bears? But although a great difficulty lies beneath here, yet not (as you wish) the supreme [one]. And if it be granted to the utmost, it will yet not wholly exclude all possibility; for there are many things subject to the supreme difficulty, to which the industry and labour of human genius can scarcely reach, which are nonetheless not on that account utterly impossible — such as the squaring of the circle, not yet exactly demonstrated even by the moderns who have persuaded themselves they found it; this, however, is numbered among the knowable but not yet known [scibilia sed nondum cognita]. But you demand that this possibility of which I speak — full of difficulty though it be — be amply proven in so many Comets across many centuries from their apparitions. But since it does not escape you that no Comets were observed from the heaven by infallible instruments with exact precision before our times (although now too very many have stumbled intolerably in these [matters]), so that it was not even explored, by the slightness of [their] parallaxes, that they dwell in the aether — much less that thence some aethereal and ordinary advance be demonstrated — you demand in vain the resolution of this doubt from very few and momentless data [Dedomena, δεδόμενα]. That it can be done in that one Comet of [15]77 I showed; although you do not attack [this], yet you submit this whole business to others' judgment, and desire it confirmed by the testimonies of more Comets. Surely, if you await others' judgments — however excellent in the praise of erudition — I fear lest you seek too long and in vain the truth lurking in the hidden, amid so great a variety and obstinacy (not to say perversity) of judgments, while almost no one wishes to be ignorant of anything, and each abounds in his own sense. In the remaining Comets, as many as I myself accurately observed, I do not doubt that their motion too, however unequal in appearance, can be reduced under an elegant regularity by a suitable hypothesis. Would that to this labour, which is no mean [one], an equal favour and fruit be given! For not even in the last [comet], which appeared this year about the end of February and the beginning of March (of which I last communicated to you a brief but exact-enough description) — although it showed itself to be seen for at least 12 days, in which it scarcely completed an eighth part of a circle — do I hold it impossible to compose its appearances into some fixed and regular norm, although at the beginning it measured 7½ degrees by [its] daily motion, about the last term scarcely one-and-a-half. For it did not come from swifter to slower motion by a tumultuary advance, but successively and ordinarily remitted its acceleration, by a beautiful and self-consistent proportion — as may be seen from the third column of the Table expressing the Comet's daily motion, where its proper diurnal course is set forth in its track. For you will find nothing there interrupted, confused, or disordered. And that Comet most exactly expressed an arc of a great circle on the sphere, in no way at all deviating from it this way or that — which it had in common with most of the others too — so that from this alone it may be gathered that Comets, just as by their apparent course they describe portions of a great circle, nor wander here and there by an uncertain track, so too under the same course exercise a circular and regular tenor in the heaven itself, although it be thought unequal by us looking from the earth.

And let these suffice for your first objection, which also meets the later [ones] sufficiently, if they be rightly weighed. But yet let us inspect these too in particular. All Comets which have hitherto emerged or will come forth in the future cannot be reduced under a fixed norm of motion, since exact observations of all cannot be ready to hand. Nor does this derogate anything from my assertion, since the variety in the singulars sufficiently reminds us that no universal law of motion, equally fitting all Comets, can be prescribed. What, therefore, I say about singulars, and [those] whose appearances have become known quite precisely, you draw to universals, against my mind. For since almost each single Comet has something peculiar, it will be impossible from some few known particularities — and these not agreeing among themselves — to construct a general doctrine; and therefore I nowhere promised it. Your third reason, from the novelty of the bodies of Comets, and that they differ in number, nor emerge in the same and one place of the heaven, and that sometimes they stand still, sometimes are borne and snatched with a slow, sometimes a most rapid and (at that) disordered motion, and most rarely describe perfect circles: these, I say, and the like, do not overturn what we intend. For some of them are proposed by you too freely and without any settled observation. For I found none of the five Comets diligently observed by me which exhibited so anomalous a motion as you object. None of them was stationary. And although they were sometimes borne more swiftly, sometimes more slowly, yet that alteration did not befall them suddenly, nor did they suffer anything interrupted or confused, as you pretend, but successively and by an ordinary law they intensified, or rather remitted, [their] motion. Nor have I known any Comets hitherto observed by others (whom one may safely trust) which so disordered an incoherence involved, as you infer. I confess indeed that very many attribute a perplexed motion to the Comets beheld by them; but that this was done by carelessness of observation, those who have learned to handle observations solidly are not ignorant. For more labour and circumspection is required to administer these without error on every side than anyone will be able to estimate. Thus you consigned to writing that that Comet of [15]77 was agitated by a tumultuary and interrupted motion, so much so that it sometimes committed a leap of 8 degrees — and Gemma too attributed some such thing to it — whereas from my observations made on every serene night nothing of the kind ever belonged to it, but it so ordinarily, successively, remitted the velocity of motion which it had at the beginning, no abrupt leap occurring, that it did not at all spurn the regularity of circular motion. It will therefore be no wonder if certain others, who perhaps attend to such [things] not so diligently as you and Gemma, rashly attribute a perplexed and anomalous motion to Comets. For very few have at hand suitable instruments free of all error. Nor do the places of the fixed [stars], whose use is required, rightly stand — so that that irregularity is rather to be attributed to the uncertainty and incompetent ordering of these [star-places] than to the Comets themselves. They are not, therefore, snatched by a fluctuating motion; far be it that any such thing be sprinkled on the celestials, which are above all desirous of order and regularity. But there is greater elegance [concinnitas] in their advance than has hitherto been noticed by most. So [as to the point] that Comets most rarely describe whole and perfect circles — although it suggests much difficulty in regulating their course, yet it does not wholly exclude all possibility, but merits the more praise and favour, the more arduous and laborious the effort. But from a given portion of some circle, who of the Geometers will deny that a settled knowledge of its centre and magnitude can be had, even though the whole periphery is not established? As to what you infer — that if some Comet's motion has been accurately explored, so that from some portion a judgment can be made about its whole revolution, yet it is not granted that hence some such thing be constituted about the motion of any Comets whatever — that in no way gainsays my opinion, as is clear enough from what has been said once and again. Likewise, neither do those [things] which you rejoin about the fixed stars oppose me. For what has been accomplished in them over much and long time by diverse Artificers, that can be done in some Comet in a few days by one and the same observer, even much more surely. And would that those [things] which have been handed down, as it were from hand to hand, by the Astronomers about the motion of the fixed [stars] did not secretly hold more perplexity and uncertainty than any one of those Comets observed by us! Surely the method for excusing their inequalities would be readier. Nor is it necessary that Comets always be the same as the stars, unless it were affirmed that all are also subject to the same laws — which has nowhere been brought forward by me. Whence the rest of the like [objections] which are urged stand in no way; so that I cannot sufficiently wonder by what occasion you so take my tenets that, if they must merit a place, it is necessary first to teach that all Comets, as many as we have hitherto beheld, were one and the same Comet — as if a dissimilar reckoning of hypothesis too could not be predefined for the singulars, according to the different relation of motion they exhibit. There are several Comets, nor do the same ever return; they are allotted diverse places in the heaven, a dissimilar motion too. What then afterward? Let there be found, therefore, corresponding hypotheses for the singulars, which will also be dissimilar among themselves, having at least this in common — that the inequality apparent to us be saved by a circular motion, however assumed.

Besides, as to what you add — that from one or another day's motion in Comets a reasoning cannot be instituted about its whole period, and you bring forward an example fetched from the Moon — these do nothing to shake my opinion; nay rather they show that you are still far from my sense. For I never affirmed that anything is to be established about the period of Comets from one or another day's progress, knowing certainly that the apparent motion is unequal; but I judge that their appearances must be most diligently scrutinized from the beginning to the end of [their] whole duration, and that under the very track of advance which they seem to designate from the heaven; and then at last, all observations collated, it must be seen whether some circular course can be given in the capacity of the heaven, whether simple or composite, by whose benefit that which appeared unequal to us can be arranged into competent equality. For since the heaven is not a hard and impervious body packed with several orbs, as the school of the Peripatetics falsely taught us hitherto, Comets can surely be freely rotated in every part of it and exercise circular motion, provided they do not strike against the bodies of the Planets — which is also sufficiently guarded against by their Disposer. In the eighth, as they call it, sphere, among the fixed stars, I should think no Comets come forth, unless they too be immobile, such as was the New Star. The comparison which you institute concerning the motion of the stars and of the Comets I do not wish to rejoin at length, because it makes nothing toward overturning the opinion I defend. Nor are those [things] which I adduced about the fixed stars not having yet measured a sixth part of a circle to be taken thus. For I brought those forward only by way of example, [namely] that Copernicus attempted to reason, from some portion of the motion accomplished, about the whole period — although he by no means attained what he hoped. For his whole speculation about saving the motion of the fixed [stars] is erroneous, and by no means consonant with the appearances. For the fixed [stars] do not now accomplish one degree in a hundred years, as his hypothesis bears, but rather in seventy-one — so much so that it is doubtful whether so great an inequality belongs to them as has hitherto been believed, and whether the [things] brought forward by the most ancient Astronomers about these stand rightly; so that if some Comet's motion be not more exactly perceived by us than the progression of the fixed [stars] (known now for two thousand years), one who tried to build an elegant hypothesis would seem to have wasted his labour. For I find many things wanting in the observations, both of the ancients and of the moderns, as to the fixed stars. Nor did Ptolemy apply the diligence and precision in verifying these which he ought, and much less Copernicus — which is [a matter] for another place to discuss more fully.

Not yet, therefore, my Thaddaeus, have you reduced the hypotheses of Comets to total impossibility; nor can you justly deny those [things] which I published about the Comet of [15]77 (for I did not bring it forward about the one which blazed in the year [15]8[0], as your letter has it) — namely that it circled about the Sun [☉] after the manner of Venus [♀] and Mercury [☿] — since I demonstrated this very [thing] Geometrically from certain observations, and my reasonings are ready in the book sent to you. If you have anything that can be opposed to them, bring it forth; if not, why do you not acquiesce in those [things] which have been rightly and faithfully, and not without labour, disentangled? And [as for] what was effected in that Comet in such a manner — why do you distrust that it can be done in others too in another manner, according as the appearances themselves shall have required? You seem to me, in weighing all these, to use a certain prejudice (be it said with your peace), and to have concluded with yourself beforehand that that is impossible which ought first to have been inquired [as to] whether it could be done. And hence I think it came about that you only glanced in passing at those [things] which, in chapters 8 and 9, I had undertaken with no mean labour, concerning the unfolding of the said Comet's hypothesis and its resolution into numbers — whence you still doubt about the certainty of a matter so diligently unravelled, and appeal to others' judgments. Nor do I know what the cause is, why you so attack [the view] that the motions of Comets can be saved by a fixed law (according as each one's advance demands), since you yourself do not deny that they are procreated in the aethereal region of the world. You ought, therefore, the more readily to allow some [others] to examine their conditions a little more deeply, and to attribute a celestial and ordered motion to celestial bodies. Nay — since you yourself have not the leisure, or the will is lacking, to scrutinize such things — you ought not to draw others back from so excellent an effort by objecting impossibility, but rather to add a spur, that they might labour to investigate these more fully. Which, trusting in our friendship, I wished benevolently and candidly to advise you; and I ask that you take in good part this our reply, such as it is, to your doubts. I will add no more about these; I will rather permit anyone to think what he wishes — namely, whether Comets move in the heaven ordinarily or confusedly. Yet remember that Nature itself rejoices in seemly order and abhors perplexity and confusion, so that on that account it was not amiss that the Pythagoreans strove to include all things in the proportions of numbers. Let these, then, suffice about those [matters], if only they have not been too much. Now I will proceed to the rest comprised in your letter.

That the Alphonsine and Copernican numbers so greatly diverged in the places of Saturn [♄] and Jupiter [♃] in the past months is no wonder; for that happens otherwise too. Yet the Copernican reckoning approaches nearer to the celestial norm — although this too sometimes deviates in Jupiter by more than a whole degree, in Saturn up to a half. For I have hitherto found Copernicus to commit a much greater discrepancy from the heaven itself in these two heavier Planets [than that] — although in Mars [♂] it sometimes reaches three degrees or even a little beyond. But [as for the fact] that the star of Jupiter is, these very days, 1⅔ degrees further on with Copernicus than with Alphonsus: so far is it [from being the case] that the fault is found with Copernicus, that the heaven itself advances its longitude still by nearly a quarter-degree more than the Ephemerides derived from the Prutenic calculation. So Saturn is, in the heaven itself, a sixth of a degree earlier than the Copernican numeration, although this precedes the Alphonsine by more than 2½ degrees. Its latitude, however, agrees with this calculation within a few minutes, whereas with Copernicus an excess of nearly a half-degree is committed. A more exact correspondence of these Planets, and also of the others, with the celestial appearances, our observations collated with the ancients' will, the Maker of the heavens favouring, some time supply. The Maestlinian Epoch was not to be changed by me, even if it had a little fault, when I wished to demonstrate that his hypothesis and foundations did not in every way satisfy the saving of the Comet's appearances through its whole duration — so much so that about the last term a difference greater than three degrees was committed. The question was not about his Epoch (which indeed I had to presuppose the same as he), nor did the quarter-degree in this discrepancy much hinder or even advance what I intended. But whether minutes are wanting in that Epoch I made no trial, nor does it please to make [one], since this matter makes little for the purpose, nor is it of much moment. As to your having been with me for two whole months in different terms in unfolding my prosthaphaereses, and your having inconsiderately assumed 24 November for the 26 December to which I directed the calculation — there is no [reason] why you should hesitate further. Nor is there need to recall the calculation anew, or even to institute a [fresh] consideration, [other] than by inspecting more attentively those [things] contained on page 279 and following in our second book, and conferring them with yours, which necessarily do not square with that time. Muñoz's little book I now do not much seek, for what I had to examine in his tenets is now printed; nor do I heed Frangipani's ineptitudes, since they make nothing for the matter. For Apian's little book on the Comet of [15]31, sent [me], I give thanks. Yet I do not return it with this courier, since there is now no leisure to gather so suddenly those [things] which I have resolved to draw from it and confer with others. You will receive it at another time — though the loss would be easy, since it contains nothing solid and certain, not even in the Comet's apparent motion, so much so that it is not safely to be trusted within one or two degrees, so slippery an observing-method does it use and so fallacious [are] the data [Dedomena] it assumes, although it resolves everything by triangles with labour more subtle than useful. The Commentaries of Trapezuntius [George of Trebizond] on Ptolemy I will return by this courier, if he is willing to carry them with him; if not, I will take care, by any other opportunity, that they be restored — for they are of no use to me.

That my Apology against Craig the Scot does not displease you, I gladly accept. It was delivered to him long since, the preceding winter, yet he has so far replied nothing, nor do I know whether he meditates anything more amply to the contrary. This at least I learned from the Doctor of Medicine who accompanied our most serene Queen of Scotland thither [Anders Krag, with Anne of Denmark] last spring: that that Craig still stands by his Aristotle, to whom he has wholly enslaved himself, and is so addicted to his tenets that he spurns all surer and saner [things], and labours with an incurable disease of obstinacy. Let him, therefore, look up to and esteem, together with the other Aristotelians, the most empty trifles, since he is unwilling to hearken to the truth at last more surely laid open. I — not regarding what Aristotle says, or anyone else received as authentic, [but] what the heaven itself has brought forth and how it has shown its new Phenomena — having scrutinized [it] by infallible observations and quite subtly, have learned more than sufficiently, and demonstrated by unconquered demonstrations [apodixes], that Comets are really procreated in the high aether, and there exercise their course, and that an ordered [course]. Let it therefore suffice me to have produced into the open the truth itself, which long lay hidden, and to have set [it] forth to be beheld by others. To whose shining rays, if some still have eyes so darkened with such mist that they cannot endure so splendid a light suddenly coming forth from more-than-Cimmerian darkness, that affects me not at all. For I am sufficiently aware that nothing is so aptly and indubitably asserted but that it finds its contradictors, and that the very truth labours for the most part under the gravest detractions in this world, while lies and vanities are well-nigh everywhere in esteem.

As to Craig's too-presumptuously pronouncing that you performed "too little by [the rules of] art" in your Dialexis, he seems to have looked to the doctrine of parallaxes less solidly and completely treated there. For he was doubtless ignorant that you yourself afterward emended your errata in this part. Nay, I too replied sufficiently on your behalf in the Apology, and dissolved what he objects to have been uncertain in that Comet. Let him please himself thus, by extenuating others' excellent efforts on account of certain blemishes, and bringing forward nothing more perfect himself that makes for the matter — let him see whether, among the intelligent and those strong in judgment, he be convicted of excessive self-love [philautia] and rash censure. What I find wanting in your Dialexis, especially about those [things] which properly made for that New Star, you will perceive from chapter 8 of our first book of aethereal Phenomena, which treats of this New Star professedly. For there, immediately after the Landgravian [observations], I weighed your observations and pronouncements, and freely brought forward our judgment about them — which I am persuaded will not be unwelcome to you, especially studious of the more unfolded truth and candidly taking all [things]. As to what Craig affirms — that the Mathematicians, in demonstrating the parallaxes of Comets, too securely neglected their proper motion — he asserts [it] not so far from the matter as regards most; and not even Regiomontanus himself, in that erudite writing of his on Comets, is to be excepted. For although in Problems 2, 3, and 4 (as you say) he advises that account must be had of the proper motion in Comets, yet the very processes of his demonstrations entirely exclude this, and presuppose the Phenomenon agitated by the primary motion only. Nor is [the fact] that in the 2nd Problem he excuses the impediments of proper motion by the brevity of the interposed time sufficient to remove every suspicion of error here, since the variety of proper motion in Comets is for the most part swifter than that which is insinuated from the alteration of parallax; and by shortening the time interposed between observations, we render the perception of parallaxes the more abstruse. So the limitation which he applies in the 3rd Problem, and wishes to hold in the following too — although it somewhat mitigates the difficulties from the genuine advance of Comets in unfolding the parallax, and renders them more tolerable, yet does not remove them on every side; and that manner by which he teaches to predefine the proper diurnal motion is utterly erroneous, nor does it satisfy the subtlety of the matter to be inquired. I say nothing of [the fact] that so exact an investigation of the azimuths and the intercepted time as is here required is doubtful, and the least, scarcely perceptible, error in the practice of the operation turns out too fertile: so that, on that account, according to Regiomontanus's Problems — however irrefragable as to the demonstration itself — it is not so ready as is thought to discern without error the parallaxes of Comets showing themselves from the heaven. He thought that Comets necessarily blaze in the upper air, as a certain fiery meteor, and that, the Aristotelian authority so prevailing that it imposed even on the supreme Mathematicians. If, therefore, the Comets' position were so near, their parallaxes would equal very many degrees, and so would be more easily perceived according to Regiomontanus's reasonings, nor would the less-exact emendation of the proper motion, or some tiny deviation in the azimuths and measurement of time, so greatly disturb the unravelling of the thing sought. That distinguished artificer doubtless foresaw these and similar difficulties, and therefore protested that the treasures of nature cannot be exhausted, but that it is permitted to attain at least something near to certain knowables.

Which although they be so, and too true, yet unless one apply the supreme diligence in scrutinizing the parallaxes of Comets, and avoid all the rocks of error even the least obvious, he will not arrive at a sufficiently settled knowledge of the thing he intends. And therefore I, these slippery ways set aside, judged that I must proceed to investigating the Comets' position by a far other track, so that not even the least parallaxes (such as occur in the highest aether) might find some subterfuge, nor the involvement of proper motion bring an impediment, nor finally anything be committed in the very observing-method that easily gives occasion for a sensible error. Craig, therefore, does me no light injury (as you too confess), when he does not hesitate to object the proper motions of Comets creeping in as hindrances in knowing their parallaxes — since I have everywhere quite studiously and subtly removed and guarded against them. And therefore I proceeded to the mark of the proposed truth not by some simple form of reasoning, but as it were by circuits [ambages], lest I strike upon the rocks of error. You indeed excuse the neglect of the proper course in the two Comets described by you by the smallness of the assumed time; but just as the brevity of the interposed time here brings some advantage on account of the Comet's place little altered meanwhile, so again, while it varies the Comet's altitude by no great interval (as it ought), it renders the discrimination of parallaxes more imperceptible — as I advised before about the Comet of [15]77, where you had assumed an interval of at least 18 minutes between two observations.

About the Comet of [15]80 we shall see in its place, the Deity favouring; which, although you most rightly pronounced to have been really aethereal, yet, by neglecting its proper motion (to say nothing now of certain others), you still granted it too great a parallax — to which also the interval of the presupposed time, stricter than is just (especially about the Meridian, where altitudes vary slowly), gave occasion. But I reply to your letter perhaps too prolixly; in which part, if anything has been amiss, I ask that you impute [it] to [my] desire of conferring with you about these, and excuse [it] by [your] accustomed candour and benevolence of mind toward me. Now I will dispatch those [things] which are in the Postscripts.

I rejoice that you have received those pages of the printed Astronomical Letters, together with the brief description of the last Comet; and I should wish that you would not be loath, at leisure, to write me your judgment, both about that Comet and also about those [things] which are chiefly treated in those Letters.

As to the models [modellae] representing some of my instruments: I took care, after I learned your wish in this matter, that some be made — as many as could be completed while the courier tarried here — which I send you by the same; and I ask that you communicate [them] to no one but Lord Curtius alone. The artificer expressed in them those [features] which could conveniently be formed, in [things] so small, after the likeness of the larger instruments. For it was not possible to exhibit everything to the nail [exactly]. I fear, therefore, that in making some larger instrument the pre-demonstration of these designs will be less sufficient. Wherefore Lord Curtius not without reason judged it more advisable that [instruments] be prepared for you there of the same magnitude as my instruments are. In which matter too I would gladly gratify him, if I had at hand all the artificers whose work I used in constructing mine over some past years; for I dismissed them, after the instruments which I had proposed to make were made, retaining only one or another, who might take care of those already made, and repair if anything by chance were wanting in them. These, however, do not suffice to construct some whole instrument anew. For most of mine are [such] that five or six diverse artificers laboured on them, and that for no little time, so much so that some were scarcely completed in the third year from the work's beginning. There are besides those machines which I chiefly trust, of such magnitude that they can be conveniently carried by no cart. I have a single Sextant, joined in a peculiar manner from diverse parts, so that it can be taken apart and shut in some chest, and again composed for use by screws. This I had made especially for the purpose, that, carried by it to Nuremberg, I might most accurately explore the pole-altitude, on account of certain solar observations once had there — which is still my purpose. This very Sextant I will not refuse to impart to you for some while, until another can be elaborated after its likeness, and you have well perceived its quite expeditious and certain use in taking both distances and altitudes of the stars — provided it be acceptable to Lord Curtius. If so, let him send hither to me some learned young man, devoted to Astronomical matters, who may here sufficiently learn the use of this instrument and its disassembly and folding, and afterward bring it to you safe and sound, and there open its explanation and manifold utility. For otherwise this instrument is sent in vain, unless someone be present with [it] who has all its reckonings ascertained. This you can report to Lord Curtius in my name, and [that] I shall be most ready to gratify him, both in this part and in others, as far as in me lies.

In your Letters and autographs transmitted to me, even unadmonished, I will take care that those [things] which can offend some, and which it is of no interest to others to know, be omitted, when they come to be published in print. How soon that can be done I am not yet certain, since I have not ready to hand the printing-paper for completing in print the first tome of my work, which I am now elaborating. For although, to avoid the difficulties of procuring paper from Germany (by which I was hindered before), I had myself, at no small expense, had a Mill built on this Island for making it, another adverse [thing] came on beyond expectation, whereby not even so was it permitted to obtain the desired paper. For two papermakers [Chartopaei], sought out at different times, who pledged me their work and faith, after I had sent [them] into Germany to procure old materials [rags], each, having received money there from my procurators, secretly withdrew. And so this whole summer, since I afterward found no suitable artificer, I am forced to interrupt the making of paper; and since no other is now ready to hand, I must defer the completion of the first tome until printing-paper be supplied. Yet, lest this delay be too troublesome to you meanwhile — you who, I know, eagerly await its publication — I send for the present a copy of those gatherings [quaterniones] which have hitherto been completed in print, which meanwhile, while the rest are prepared, you can unroll at leisure, and write me your censure about those [things] contained therein, and frankly advise [me], where it shall seem [good] to you. The earlier pages are omitted, which will comprise the book's title, the Privileges, and the prefaces; for all these, as is the custom, will be prefixed when the whole work is finished. There are also wanting three gatherings at the end of the first chapter, in which I resolved to set down further, separately, certain [things] about the magnitude of the year by a more general indication, and at the same time briefly to consider the Lilian [Gregorian] correction of the Julian year, newly introduced. So too, at the foot of the second chapter, five gatherings are lacking, in which the table of the fixed [stars] restored by us will be contained, together with certain other [things] pertaining hither. The whole seventh chapter too is omitted, which will unlock the magnitudes not only of the New Star, but also of the rest of the fixed [stars] and even of the Planets, otherwise than has hitherto been done. The two last chapters too are not yet printed, for want of paper — of which the ninth will examine and dissolve the tenets of those who mixed the New Star with the orbs of the Planets, the tenth [those] of [those] who rendered it wholly sublunary, or brought forward something else too absurd about it. These, I say, remaining for the completion of the first tome, you will obtain in their time, the Deity favouring; meanwhile read through, examine, and take in good part those [things] which I now communicate.

Your observations about Mars [♂] and certain other stars, had some years ago, which you sent, are less exact, because the Radius [cross-staff] by no means yields the true intervals of the stars. The places of the fixed [stars] too which you applied differ much from the heaven itself, as our restitution will be able to advise you — so that it is no wonder that the calculation, repeated in diverse ways, so enormously dissented from itself.

If you carefully observed the eclipse of the Sun [☉] which occurred in the past days, communicate to me its times and the Phase noted there. Here it was perfectly serene through its whole duration, and the Sun began to fail after midnight of the 21st of July, 6 hours 51 minutes elapsed. It ceased at the 9th hour exactly completed. The hitherto-used tables, therefore — whether Alphonsine or Copernican — with the reduction of the Meridians applied, do not differ much in the time of this eclipse from the heaven itself, and that especially for this cause, that the Sun's motion about the sign Leo [♌] is not very erroneous in them. But as to the magnitude of the eclipsed part, the Canons erred much: for at least a third part of the Sun, or 4 digits, was seen to fail, whereas a defect of at least 8 points [digits] would have appeared in our horizon, if [one] were trusting the tables. Which I wished to indicate in passing, that they may be conferred with your observation. But, lest I be too prolix in replying, it is time that I now stay my pen. Do you, therefore, my excellent and most loving Thaddaeus, fare as happily as possible, and at the first occasion do not cease to revisit me by your letters, since otherwise we cannot address one another.

Given at Uraniborg, the 3rd day of August, Old Style, in the Year 1590. Tycho Brahe. I ask that you communicate the book sent to no one, except to Lord Curtius alone, until it be completed and issued to the public.

Proof: the 1577 comet (book 2, ch. 8) reduced to regular circular motion (6°→¼°/day), numbers fitting better than for any planet; likewise the Feb–Mar 1590 comet (12 days, 7½°→1½°/day, the diary-table's third column shows no jumps). He rebuts Hájek's three reasons: (1) he speaks of singulars, not universals; (3) none of his five observed comets was stationary or moved by "tumultuary leaps" — the famous 8°-leap Hájek and Cornelius Gemma ascribed to the 1577 comet is an artefact of bad fixed-star places, not the comet; comets trace arcs of great circles. The Moon-analogy is misread (Tycho reasons from the whole apparition, not one day); the heavens are not solid (no comets in the 8th sphere unless immobile like the New Star); Copernicus's precession is erroneous (1°/71 yr, not 1°/100). He charges Hájek with prejudice ("concluding beforehand that it is impossible") and urges him to "add a spur, not an obstacle." Closes with the Pythagorean/order-in-nature topos.
  • The technical sidebar (overlaps the Magini letter of 1 Dec 1590). Alphonsine vs Copernican discrepancies in Saturn/Jupiter (Copernicus nearer the sky but still off — Jupiter >1°, Saturn ½°, Mars c. 3°; the live sky positions given to the minute); the Maestlinian epoch kept deliberately; the prosthaphaeresis crux — Hájek "inconsiderately assumed 24 November for the 26 December" Tycho computed to (book 2, p. 279) — exactly the discrepancy Hájek conceded in his 11 June letter; the book-returns (Muñoz no longer needed, Frangipani ineptitudes, Apian's 1531-comet book kept a while [unreliable, "within 1–2°"], Trapezuntius's Ptolemy commentary to be returned). The Caesarean Privilege obtained via Curtius; the lost Gallican privilege; another hoped from Henri of Navarre (Henri IV).
  • The Craig dossier (overlaps the Craig letter of 14 Nov 1591). Tycho's Apologia against Craig delivered "the preceding winter," unanswered; Anders Krag (the physician with Anne of Denmark, the "Queen of Scotland") reports Craig still "enslaved to Aristotle … an incurable disease of obstinacy." Tycho defends Hájek's Dialexis against Craig's "parum ex arte" charge (it concerned the parallax doctrine, since emended), points to book 1, ch. 8 (his treatment of the New Star, "immediately after the Landgravian observations"), and partly agrees with Craig that mathematicians — even Regiomontanus (whose Problems 2–4 warn of proper motion but whose demonstrations exclude it) — neglected the comet's proper motion in parallax-work; Tycho's own method avoids the "slippery ways" "by circuits."
  • The instruments, paper-mill, and unfinished Progymnasmata (valuable production-history). Tycho sends small instrument models (modellae) for Hájek, to be shown only to Curtius (who rightly wants full-size copies built at Prague); Tycho's instruments took "five or six artificers" and up to three years each, and the great machines "no cart can carry." He offers the demountable screw-jointed Sextant (built for a planned Nuremberg pole-altitude trip) on loan, if Curtius sends a young man to learn it. The paper crisis: his Hven paper-mill stalled when two papermakers absconded with advance money, delaying the Progymnasmata Tome I; he sends the printed gatherings so far and lists what is still missing — the title/privileges/prefaces, 3 gatherings on year-length + the Gregorian ("Lilian") correction, 5 on the fixed-star catalogue, all of ch. 7 (magnitudes of the nova, fixed stars, planets), and chs. 9–10 (against those who made the New Star planetary or sublunary). The 21 July 1590 solar eclipse: Uraniborg, first contact 6ʰ51ᵐ after midnight, end at 9ʰ00ᵐ, only 4 digits (⅓) covered vs the tables' predicted 8 (the time fits, since the Sun near Leo is well-tabulated; the magnitude does not). Hájek's Mars/star observations by the Radius (cross-staff) judged inexact (bad star-places).
