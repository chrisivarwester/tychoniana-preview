The original

CLARISSIMO NOBILISSIMOQUE VIRO D. D. TYCHONI BRAHE, MATHEMATICO EXIMIO, DOMINO ET AMICO SUO PLURIMUM COLENDO.
S. P.

LITERAS tuas, amantissime TYCHO, misi Illustriss. Principis nostri Cancellario IACOBO BORDINGO, verum papyrus non est ad me transmissa; si ad me Illustriss. Princeps miserit, dabo operam, ut a coeli iniurijs tuta Haffniam mittatur. Intelligo autem BORDINGUM in Daniam a nostro Principe missum, quae res forte moram inijciet chartae. Egi quoque cum typorum fusore, sed is non vult in Daniam traijcere. Indicavi id mercatori, qui Francofurtum petebat, cui idipsum in mandatis dederas.

Bene fecisti, quod librum tuum de Cometa Anni 77 ad me miseris; legi maxima cum voluptate et admiratione, quae in priore voluminis parte ingenuose a te inventa et magno iudicio accommodata et Geometricis rationibus confirmata in lucem emisisti. Vicisti me hercule meam expectationem. Animadverto enim non solum in observando diligentiam, verum summam solertiam et ingenij acumen ex observatis alia colligendi et maximum iudicium in Geometricarum demonstrationum accommodatione. Hypothesis autem illa tua, quae circa Solem tanquam centrum reliquos Planetas, excepta Luna, constituit moveri, in primis mihi placet. Nam Terram ponit immobilem, centrumque universi tenere, nec motuum aequalitatem a non proprijs centris deducit. Per hanc si in opere tuo Astronomico Phaenomena tuearis, adhibitis Geometricis demonstrationibus et numeris, omne profecto tuleris punctum. Haec certa ad immortalitatem via, macte animo, ingenij tui vim hic explica, neque Herculeis istis laboribus frangi te patiaris, sed tanquam Atlas coelum humero ferens, profer in lucem, quae ego alijque unice amemus et posteritas nobiscum admiretur. Capite secundo emendationes longitudinum et latitudinum stellarum instituis ex declinatione stellae observatae et ascensione recta, ex tempore, quo pervenit ad meridianum, cognita. Verum exactam temporis notitiam dari, quo stella meridianum praeterit, merito quis in dubium vocet; quo dato et Solis loco exacte cognito, caetera nullam habent dubitationem. Et si de hisce constat, expeditam video rationem omnium stellarum, globo secundum suam inter se distantiam insertarum, longitudines latitudinesque depraehendendi. Fuerit enim Aequatoris et Eclipticae, ijs datis, non difficilis inscriptio. Sed animadverto in additione eiusdem capitis te vidisse hunc scrupulum, et ob id exactiores longitudines et latitudines stellarum tradere. Ex Veneris enim stella et lucidiore, quae est supra caput Arietis, certum et definitum locum determinasse, ut libro primo demonstratum scribis. Hinc, datis ab ea distantijs, reliquarum stellarum cum earundem declinatione, infallibili demonstratione, ex triangulorum doctrina petita, longitudines et latitudines stellarum definiuntur, inventa differentia ascensionis rectae, ut in Pegasi stella a te huius libri cap. 2 demonstratum est. Dehinc 3 capite Cometae locum apparentem ex distantias a duabus vel pluribus stellis, quarum loca sunt definita, erudite et laboriose investigasti. Nec minus erudite persecutus es rationes discernendi parallaxes longitudinis et latitudinis ex angulo, quem facit colurus solstitiorum cum meridiano, et alijs, quae ex observatis consequuntur. Sed quae de Lunae latitudine maxima mones, maiorem esse Ptolemaicam 15 scrupulis, miror, cum et eclipsium magnitudo et calculus huic latitudini respondeat, et REGIOMONTANUS, COPERNICUS, WERNERUS alijque insignes Mathematici Ptolemaicam servârint, quos dubium non est diligentes fuisse huius latitudinis observatores. PTOLEMÆI autem observatio me movet, quae habita est Alexandriae, cuius verticem Luna in maxima sua latitudine in Cancro visa est obtinere. In qua observatione neque parallaxis neque refractio, quae forte in Aegypto non habuit locum ob aëris puritatem, difficultatem aliquam in observationibus parere potuerunt.

Porro circulum descripsisse Cometam, quamvis nonnunquam quinque vel paucioribus scrupulis (quod nullius est momenti) a iusta peripheria ipsae observationes dissentiant, et eiusdem circuli cum Ecliptica et Aequatore inclinationem locumque et angulum intersectionis in Sagittario ingenuose invenisti et demonstrasti. Ex hoc motu, quem circularem etiam demonstrasti, et ordine motus, et quod motu Lunae tardior, confirmas aethereum, id est supra Lunam positum esse. Deinde ex distanttijs a stellis fixis in diversa altitudine Cometae supra finitorem observatis certius comprobas, habita ratione motus, supra Lunam positum esse. Non enim est ea distantiarum a stellis fixis differentia, quae foret, si sublunaris esset Cometa, ut ex parallaxibus in concavo orbis Lunae consideratis colligis. Quae omnia a te solerter et erudite nobis tradita sunt et apodictice ostensa. Videris autem non uno in loco asserere, omnes Cometas in aetherea regione positos fuisse. Memini vero, LYCOSTHENEM in eo, quem de Ostentis reliquit libro, sic enim (ut arbitror) opus inscribit, prodidisse quosdam Cometas conflagrantes incendium in subiecta Terra excitasse. Sed quid movet, ut materiam eius aetheream in aetherea regione constituas? Si enim aër diffusus per omnes mundi partes syderum est vector, quid aliam materiam quaerimus? Sin vero aetherea regio alterius sit materiae quam aër, undique tamen pervia, quid vetat scientiam motus in Cometis introducendi, hoc est, talem vim movendi a Deo inditam ex materia sublunari esse genitam, quae sursum in aethera vi syderum attracta sit, talem naturam motus consequi, donec plane extinguntur? Et certe ipsorum Cometarum interitus huic sententiae potius astipulatur, et quod veteres nonnulli, referente PLINIO et ACHYLLE STATIO, sydera humore vesci sunt arbitrati. Quod ob id commemoro, ut intelligamus tenue et lucidum, sive aëreum sive igneum coeleste sit corpus, aut quinta quaedam essentia, ut voluit ARISTOTELES, hasce exhalationes suscipere posse, et eum, qui scientiam motus, ut tuo utar verbo, indidit, eundem et hac sublunari materia, si non naturaliter et vi syderum coacta sit, effingere potuisse. Porro Cometas nunquam eosdem apparere, et interire, hos moveri, illos esse immobiles, et qui moventur, alios in antecedentia signorum, alios in consequentia, alios in septentrionem ferri, nonnullos ordinem quendam in motu servare, quosdam vero sine ordine vagari, rursus plerosque in septentrione conspici, ut PLINIUS refert, vagae inconstantisque materiae naturam potius referentes quam coelestis. Quod autem scribis, si recte tuam sententiam assequor: Cometam circa Solem moveri et posse sextante circuli a Sole distare, id non videtur tuae hypothesi consentaneum. Nam cum semidiameter sextantem circuli subtendat, videretur Cometa aliquando Terram contingere, si sui circuli revolutionem perficeret.

Quae in secunda libri parte a te pertractantur, in qua aliorum observationes et sententias excutis, praeter animadversiones in MÆSTLINUM non legi, quas obiter percurri. Sic enim, doctissime amantissimeque TYCHO, sentio, in ijs, quae Geometrica demonstratione recte sunt confirmata, non opus esse contrariarum sententiarum refutatione, nisi aliquae forent eius generis, quae subtili paralogismo possent lectori non imperito imponere. Et certe rudes ego eiusdem Cometae feceram observationes, ex quibus secutus REGIOMONTANI modum iam collegeram in aetherea regione positum fuisse, ut multis indicaveram, et ipsi quoque WITTICHIO, qui paulo post ad te se conferebat. Sed quid de re nunquam recurrente, imo intereunte, tantum laboris, obiecit mihi quidam, sumimus? ut et motum eius demonstremus circularem et eiusdem a Zodiaco latitudinem et loca eiusdem secundum latitudinem et longitudinem definiamus, aliaque ingenuose demonstremus, cum suffecerit ostendisse, in aetherea regione consistere; id enim controversum. Individuorum enim, quae generationi et corruptioni obnoxia sunt, non esse scientiam, nec secus, quam si halonis, qui circa Lunam aut aliam stellam conspicitur, magnitudinem et distantiam a Terra definiat; extingui enim cum ipso metheoro eius generis scripta. Quae Francofurti ad Oderam a M. CAMERARIO edita sunt de eodem Cometa, nondum vidi, si consequar, mittam.

Bene vale, mi TYCHO, et me mutuo ama.
Rostochij 20. Septembris Anno Domini 1588.
HENRICUS BRUCÆUS,
plane tuus.

To the most renowned and most noble man, Master Tycho Brahe, distinguished mathematician, his lord and friend most worthy to be honoured.
Heartiest greeting.

Your letter, most beloved Tycho, I sent on to Jacob Bording, chancellor of our Most Illustrious Prince; but the paper [packet] has not been forwarded to me. If the Most Illustrious Prince should send it to me, I shall take pains that it be sent to Copenhagen secured against the injuries of the weather. But I understand that Bording has been sent into Denmark by our Prince — a circumstance that will perhaps cause delay to the document. I have also dealt with the type-founder, but he is unwilling to cross over into Denmark. I made this known to the merchant who was making for Frankfurt, the very man to whom you had given this same charge.

You did well to send me your book on the comet of the year [15]77; I read with the greatest pleasure and admiration the things that, in the earlier part of the volume, you ingeniously discovered, fitted together with great judgement, and confirmed by geometrical reasoning, and so brought into the light. By Hercules, you have surpassed my expectation. For I observe not only diligence in observing, but the highest skill and acuteness of mind in gathering further things from the things observed, and the greatest judgement in the fitting-together of the geometrical demonstrations. That hypothesis of yours, moreover, which makes the remaining planets, the Moon excepted, move about the Sun as their centre, pleases me especially. For it sets the Earth motionless and holding the centre of the universe, and it does not derive the uniformity of the motions from centres not their own. If by this [hypothesis] you maintain the phenomena in your astronomical work, with geometrical demonstrations and numbers applied, you will assuredly carry every point. This is the sure road to immortality: bravo! — here unfold the force of your genius, and do not let yourself be broken by those Herculean labours, but, like Atlas bearing the heavens on his shoulder, bring forth into the light the things that I and others may love beyond measure and that posterity may admire along with us. In the second chapter you set up corrections of the longitudes and latitudes of the stars from the declination of the observed star and the right ascension, [the latter] known from the time at which it reaches the meridian. But that exact knowledge can be had of the time at which a star passes the meridian, one might rightly call into doubt; this once given, and the place of the Sun exactly known, the rest admits of no doubt. And if these things are established, I see a ready method of ascertaining the longitudes and latitudes of all the stars set upon a globe according to their distance from one another. For, these things being given, the inscribing of the Equator and the Ecliptic [upon it] would not be difficult. But I observe that in the addendum to the same chapter you have seen this difficulty, and on that account deliver more exact longitudes and latitudes of the stars. For [you write] that from the star of Venus and from the brighter star which is above the head of Aries you have determined a fixed and definite place, as you write was demonstrated in the first book. Hence, the distances from it being given, together with the declination of those same [stars], the longitudes and latitudes of the remaining stars are defined by an infallible demonstration drawn from the doctrine of triangles, once the difference of right ascension has been found, as you have demonstrated in the case of the star of Pegasus in chapter 2 of this book. Thereafter, in the third chapter, you have learnedly and laboriously investigated the apparent place of the comet from its distances from two or more stars whose places are defined. No less learnedly have you pursued the methods of distinguishing the parallaxes of longitude and latitude from the angle which the colure of the solstices makes with the meridian, and from other things that follow from what has been observed. But as to what you advise concerning the Moon's greatest latitude — that the Ptolemaic [value] is greater by 15 minutes — I marvel at it, since both the magnitude of eclipses and the calculation answer to this [Ptolemaic] latitude, and Regiomontanus, Copernicus, Werner, and other distinguished mathematicians have kept the Ptolemaic [value] — men whom there is no doubt to have been diligent observers of this latitude. But Ptolemy's observation moves me, which was made at Alexandria, whose zenith the Moon, at its greatest latitude, was seen to occupy in Cancer. In which observation neither parallax nor refraction — which perhaps had no place in Egypt on account of the purity of the air — could have given rise to any difficulty in the observations.

Further, that the comet described a circle — although now and then the observations themselves diverge by five minutes or fewer (which is of no moment) from a true circumference — and the inclination of that same circle with the Ecliptic and the Equator, and its place and angle of intersection in Sagittarius, you have ingeniously found and demonstrated. From this motion, which you have also demonstrated to be circular, and from the order of the motion, and from the fact that it is slower than the motion of the Moon, you confirm that it is ethereal, that is, placed above the Moon. Then, from the distances from the fixed stars observed at the comet's differing altitudes above the horizon, you prove more surely — account being taken of the motion — that it was placed above the Moon. For the difference of distances from the fixed stars is not such as it would be if the comet were sublunary, as you gather from the parallaxes considered in the hollow of the Moon's orb. All of which has been delivered to us by you skilfully and learnedly, and shown apodictically. You seem, however, in more than one place to assert that all comets have been placed in the ethereal region. But I remember that Lycosthenes, in the book he left on Portents — for thus, I think, he entitles the work — reported that certain comets, blazing, kindled a conflagration on the Earth beneath. But what moves you to place its ethereal matter in the ethereal region? For if the air, diffused through all parts of the world, is the bearer of the stars, why do we seek other matter? But if the ethereal region is of a matter other than air — yet everywhere passable — what forbids [us to suppose] that the principle of motion to be introduced into comets — that is, such a power of being moved imparted by God — has been begotten from sublunary matter, which, drawn upward into the ether by the force of the stars, takes on such a nature of motion until [these comets] are wholly extinguished? And certainly the very perishing of comets rather agrees with this opinion, and [so does] the fact that some of the ancients — as Pliny and Achilles Statius report — judged that the stars feed on moisture. This I mention for the reason that we may understand that the thin and shining heavenly body — whether airy or fiery, or some fifth essence, as Aristotle held — is able to take up these exhalations; and that he who imparted the principle of motion (to use your own word) could also have fashioned [it] from this sublunary matter, if it was not constrained naturally and by the force of the stars. Further, that comets never appear the same and [then] perish; that some are moved, others are motionless; and that of those which are moved, some are carried into the preceding [signs], others into the following, others to the north; that some keep a certain order in their motion, but others wander without order; and again that most are seen in the north, as Pliny reports — [all this] rather reflects the nature of wandering and inconstant matter than of celestial [matter]. But as to what you write — if I rightly grasp your meaning — that the comet is moved about the Sun and can be distant from the Sun by a sextant of a circle [60°], that does not seem consistent with your hypothesis. For since the semidiameter subtends a sextant of a circle, the comet would seem at some time to touch the Earth, if it completed the revolution of its circle.

The things treated by you in the second part of the book — in which you sift the observations and opinions of others — I have not read, except the strictures upon Maestlin, which I ran through in passing. For thus, most learned and most beloved Tycho, do I think: in those matters which have been rightly confirmed by geometrical demonstration, there is no need of a refutation of contrary opinions, unless there be some of that kind which by a subtle fallacy might impose upon a reader who is not unskilled. And indeed I had made rough observations of the same comet, from which, following the method of Regiomontanus, I had already gathered that it was placed in the ethereal region, as I had made known to many, and to Wittich himself too, who shortly afterward betook himself to you. But "why," a certain man objected to me, "do we take so much trouble over a thing that never recurs, nay, that is perishing — to demonstrate that its motion is circular, and to define its latitude from the Zodiac and its places according to latitude and longitude, and to demonstrate other things ingeniously — when it would have sufficed to show that it stands in the ethereal region? For that [alone] is in dispute. For of individual things which are subject to generation and corruption there is no science; no otherwise than if one were to define the magnitude of a halo, which is seen about the Moon or some other star, and its distance from the Earth — for writings of that kind are extinguished along with the meteor itself." What was published at Frankfurt an der Oder by Master Camerarius on the same comet I have not yet seen; if I obtain it, I shall send it.

Fare you well, my Tycho, and love me in return.
At Rostock, 20 September, in the year of the Lord 1588.
Heinrich Brucaeus,
wholly yours.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Henricus Brucaeus = Heinrich Brucaeus (Hendrik van den Broeck, c. 1530/31–1593), Flemish-born professor of medicine and mathematics at the University of Rostock, a senior figure of Tycho's Rostock student years and a lifelong scientific friend. The letter is his considered scholarly response to Tycho's De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis (the "book on the comet of the year [15]77"), the second volume of the Astronomiae instauratae progymnasmata programme, freshly off the Uraniborg press in 1588.
  • librum tuum de Cometa Anni 77 — Tycho's De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis liber secundus (Uraniborg, 1588), on the comet of 1577. Brucaeus reviews its first part chapter by chapter (the stellar-coordinate method, ch. 2; the comet's apparent place, ch. 3; the parallax analysis) and the second part (the critique of rival authors), which he had only skimmed.
  • The world-system. Hypothesis … quae circa Solem tanquam centrum reliquos Planetas, excepta Luna, constituit moveri … Terram ponit immobilem, centrumque universi tenere — Brucaeus's notably early, approving paraphrase of the Tychonic (geo-heliocentric) hypothesis: the planets (Moon excepted) circle the Sun, while the Earth stands still at the centre of the universe. Rendered literally; hypothesis kept.
  • Persons named. Iacobus Bordingus = Jacob Bording, chancellor to the Duke of Mecklenburg (whose father had been physician to King Christian III of Denmark — Dreyer, Tycho Brahe); here the intermediary for forwarding Tycho's papers, then absent on a mission to Denmark. Regiomontanus = Johannes Müller of Königsberg (1436–1476), to whose comet-method Brucaeus says he himself adhered. Copernicus; Wernerus = Johann Werner of Nuremberg (1468–1522). Maestlinus = Michael Maestlin (1550–1631), Tübingen astronomer, target of Tycho's strictures in the book's second part. Wittichius = Paul Wittich (c. 1546–1586), the Breslau mathematician who passed from Rostock to Hven. Lycosthenes = Conrad Lycosthenes (Wolffhart, 1518–1561), author of the Prodigiorum ac ostentorum chronicon (the "book on Portents"). M. Camerario … Francosurti ad Oderam = Master [Elias] Camerarius at Frankfurt an der Oder, who published on the comet of 1577 (Norlind, Tycho Brahe, names Elias Camerarius of Frankfurt a.d. Oder among the comet's commentators). Plinius = Pliny the Elder (Naturalis Historia II, on comets). Achilles Statius = Achilles Tatius, the ancient commentator on Aratus's Phaenomena (the "Isagoge"), regularly cited in this Latinised form. Aristoteles = Aristotle (the doctrine of the quinta essentia, the fifth element/quintessence).
  • Technical terms (editorial glossary–5.2). longitudo / latitudo (stellarum) → (celestial) longitude / latitude; declinatio → declination; ascensio recta → right ascension; meridianus → meridian; parallaxis → parallax; refractio → refraction; Ecliptica / Aequator → Ecliptic / Equator; colurus solstitiorum → colure of the solstices; altitudo … supra finitorem → altitude above the horizon (finitor, the "bounder," = the horizon); orbis Lunae → the Moon's orb; sublunaris / aethereus → sublunary / ethereal; globus → globe; semidiameter → semidiameter (here = the radius of the comet's supposed circle). scrupulum / scrupulis in the astronomical passages = minute(s) [of arc] (the standard early-modern term for arc-minutes), rendered "minutes"; the same word in vidisse hunc scrupulum carries its ordinary sense "difficulty / scruple" and is so rendered — the pun is noted here rather than forced in the English.
  • sextante circuli — "a sextant of a circle," i.e. one-sixth of a circle = 60°; glossed in brackets in the translation. Brucaeus's geometrical objection: if the comet can stand 60° from the Sun while circling it, and "the semidiameter subtends a sextant" (i.e. the circle's radius equals the chord of 60°, which it does), the comet's circle would be so large that the comet would at some point graze the Earth.
  • Lunae latitudo maxima … maiorem esse Ptolemaicam 15 scrupulis — Tycho had argued that the Moon's greatest latitude differs from Ptolemy's value; Brucaeus reads this as Tycho making the Ptolemaic value greater by 15 [arc-]minutes and resists it, citing the agreement of eclipse magnitudes and of Regiomontanus, Copernicus and Werner with the Ptolemaic figure, and Ptolemy's own Alexandria observation of the Moon at the zenith in Cancer. The numeric claim is reproduced exactly as written, not adjudicated.
  • scientiam motus … indidit / scientia motus … introducendi — "the principle of motion … imparted / to be introduced" (lit. "knowledge/science of motion"); Brucaeus twice flags this as Tycho's own term (ut tuo utar verbo, "to use your own word"), so the phrasing is kept close to the Latin rather than modernised.
  • halo / metheoro — halo[n] = a halo about the Moon or a star; meteorum = a meteor in the Aristotelian sense (an atmospheric/sublunary appearance), the category the objector assigns to comets. Kept as period terms.
  • Individuorum … non esse scientiam — the Aristotelian maxim "of individuals [particulars] there is no science" (scientia being of universals), here voiced by the unnamed objector against Tycho's laborious treatment of a unique, perishing object.
  • Textual crux — in antecedentia lignorum. The Source block preserves the printed reading lignorum ("of woods/timbers"), which is meaningless here. The required word is signorum: in antecedentia / in consequentia signorum is the fixed astronomical idiom for motion "into the preceding / the following [signs of the zodiac]" (i.e. retrograde vs. direct in zodiacal order); the same Brucaeus passage as paraphrased in Tycho's printed reply reads in antecedentia signorum (Tycho Brahes Breve; cf. Opera Omnia IV). The translation therefore reads "into the preceding [signs]"; the printed lignorum is retained above by this chapter's convention and explained here.
  • Greek / symbols. No Greek script and no astronomical glyphs occur in this letter; the planet and zodiac names (Venus, Aries, Pegasus, Cancer, Sagittarius) are written out in the Latin and carried over directly.
  • Orthography. Standard humanist-Latin print conventions: ij for double-i (iniurijs, proprijs, distantijs, alijs), ae-ligature (PTOLEMAeI, BRUCAeUS, MAeSTLINUM), Francosurti = Francofurti (long-s/f confusion in print), typorum fusore = "type-founder," finitorem (horizon). distanttijs (with doubled t) and extinguntur (for extinguuntur) are printer's forms, left as printed.
  • Calendar / place. Rostochij 20. Septembris Anno Domini 1588 = "at Rostock, 20 September 1588" (O.S. — Lutheran Mecklenburg, like Denmark, kept the Julian calendar in 1588). The dateline agrees with the catalogue label "1588. 20. september. Rostock"; no discrepancy. No Roman-calendar idiom is used.
  • Subscription. S. P. = Salutem plurimam → "Heartiest greeting"; Bene vale … et me mutuo ama → "Fare you well … and love me in return"; plane tuus → "wholly yours."
  • Correction. Collated against Dreyer, TBOO VII pp.141-144: 'Intelligo' (not 'Intellige,' p.141) restores Brucaeus's first-person report rather than an imperative addressed to Tycho, with a corresponding change to the English mood; 'signorum' (not 'lignorum,' p.143), 'Francofurti' (not 'Francosurti,' p.144), and 'adhibitis' (not 'adbibitis,' p.142) correct three further single-letter corruptions in the source Latin with no effect on the existing translation.

- text error (medium). Our source Latin corrupts 'signorum' (of the zodiac signs) into the nonsensical 'lignorum' (of woods/timbers). The translator silently repaired the sense with a bracketed '[signs]' rather than flagging the underlying source corruption. Dreyer prints: hos moveri, illos esse immobiles, et qui moventur, alios in antecedentia signorum, alios in consequentia (TBOO VII p.143).
- text error (low). The place-name 'Francofurti' (Frankfurt) is corrupted to 'Francosurti' in our source text (f→s); the translator rendered the correct city regardless. Dreyer prints: Quae Francofurti ad Oderam a M. CAMERARIO edita sunt de eodem Cometa, nondum vidi (TBOO VII p.144).
- text error (low). Single-letter corruption (b for h) in our source text ('adbibitis' for 'adhibitis'); no effect on meaning and the translation is unaffected. Dreyer prints: adhibitis Geometricis demonstrationibus et numeris (TBOO VII p.142).

Repaired. All four text errors were verified directly against the Dreyer page images for TBOO VII pp.141-144 (the letter's pp.141-145 extract, pages 1-4 sufficed since all four cited passages fall within them). Each corruption and its Dreyer reading were visually confirmed letter-for-letter. Only the first defect required a corresponding English change (mood restored from imperative to first-person indicative); the other three were orthographic corruptions with no effect on sense or translation. No crux remains.
