The original

TYCHO BRAHE AD NICOLAUM CRAGIUM.

S. P.

REDDIDIT mihi filius TYCHO, statim post huius Anni initia huc ad me domum reversus, literas tuas, Excellentissime et Amantissime Domine Doctor NICOLAE KRAGIE, quae mihi gratissimae erant, utut circa initia expostulationem meam, ob diutinam literarum intermissionem, gravius quam forte decuit, ferre videaris, et Amici in iocando libertatem nonnihil perstringere, quasi veterem tuum TYCHONEM, licet sic amicos nonnunquam alloqui soleat, candide tamen de ijs sentire et constantem esse non noveris. Ago autem tibi gratias, quod meos emissarios tam benevole exceperis atque tractâris: noluissem tamen, ut meâ de caussâ eos sumptus faceres: si quid vicissim in tui gratiam a me praestari poterit, inveniar huius etiam unâ cum coeteris beneficij memor.

De statu rerum mearum, cum ex ijs, quae ad teipsum, tum etiam ROSÆKRANTZIUM nostrum perscripsi, et insuper filij relatione luculenter iam antea cognovisti, nolo hîc quidpiam repetere, quodque conditiones a Sa Ca Mte Clementissime mihi oblatae et deputatae vobis ita placuerint, ut te et tuam ELISAM non mediocri affecerint gaudio, gratum habeo. Crescit (sit Deo gratia) indies erga me Caesaris benignissimus favor, tum quoque eorum, qui ei sunt ab intimis atque hîc authoritate praevalent, licet apud quosdam morosulos non absit invidia, virtutis, uti fit, umbra. Caesar per semestre Pragae non fuit, sed in civitate quadam munita, quae 10 milliaribus inde removetur, Pilsena dicta, cum praecipuis suis consiliarijs interea moratus est, idque ob luem Epidemicam, quae totam Bohemiam et vicina loca graviter praecedente Anno usque in solstitium hybernum infestavit, quâ etiam de caussâ ego unâ cum meis in non modico fui discrimine, peste et dyssenteriâ tam in vicina urbe quam toto territorio Benatico atrociter saeviente. Quo nomine Sa Caesarea Matas proprio motu, Clementissimo animo, imo quasi Paterno quodam affectu erga me et meos non parum fuit sollicita, adeo ut aliquoties huc mitteret expiscatum, quomodo valeremus, et sponte obtulit copiam alio transeundi et pericula declinandi, si sic mihi placuisset, nominatim vero Viennam Austriae indigitavit, siquidem ea urbs contagionis expers erat, et commoditates oportunas, tam quoad habitationem, quam coetera necessaria se isthic per suos mihi ordinaturam, modo eo proficisci luberet, Clementissime obtulit. At ego, actis humillime gratijs, me potius hîc manere et omnia Divinae provisioni committere atque meis exercitijs vacare velle respondi. Quod etiam ipsius Mati non displicuit. Et antidota, quae intellexerat me contra istos morbos efficacia in promptu habere, unâ cum praeparationis ratione expetijt, quae et subiectissime communicavi. Secessi vero tandem, formidini mearum muliercularum (uti fert sexus iste) indulgens, in aliam quandam Arcem Caesaream 3 milliaribus hinc distantem (siquidem mihi liberum erat, quamcunque in viciniâ vellem, inhabitare) ubi, interea dum mei in Daniâ absunt, per aliquot hebdomadas non incommode mansi. Ago autem Deo opt. max. gratias, quod me et meos omnes, toto eo periculoso tempore, salvos et incolumes servârit. Spes est Caesam Matem statim a Paschatis festo Pragam redituram, prout plurimi avide desiderant. Cum nuper ibi fuissem, rescivi a multis, quod et pariter in itinere a Capitaneo Brandeisensi meo vicino cognoveram, non saltem cives et mercatores, sed et praecipuos quosque mihi tribuere, quod Caesar tam diu Pragâ absit, tanquam is hac in re ex meo penderet consilio, quodque accessum ante finem Martij dissuaserim, nec patuit excusationi locus, licet id serio inficiatus sim: Saaledis er mig nu her kommen for ordt. Hinc talia suspicantur, quod nonnunquam ipsius Mati de quibusdam, ipsâ sic cupiente, scribere soleam, nec liceat consiliarijs, uti alias mos est, literas meas resignare.

Instrumenta mea, quas in Dania reliqui, avecta esse, et unâ cum coeteris nunc in fundo Teuthonico conservari, Caesarea Matas libenter intellexit, eidemque gratum fuit, quod nullas hac in re isthic moverint difficultates, alias Regi vestro hoc nomine scriptura. Dedit autem literas tam ad Hamburgenses quam Magdeburgenses, ut quaecunque apud eos reposita habeo, unâ cum reliquâ meâ supellectile suis sumptibus in gratiam ipsius Matis huc per Albim advehi faciant, cumque in prioribus literis subterfugium quoddam astute invenissent Magdaeburgenes, denuo alteras misit, quae priores dilucidius exponerent, cumque ijs acceptis ad me rescriberent, ut eos hac in re apud Caesaream Matem excusatos haberem, siquidem durities obsidionis, quam olim passi sunt, eos adhuc ita premeret, ut absque suo incommodo ijs sumptibus ferendis non essent pares: quod cum a Domino BARVITIO supremo Secretario intellexisset Caesar, admodum aegre tulit, consiliarij vero eius in κακιννος resoluti sunt, quod obsidio ante 50 Annos peracta adhuc tantopere ijs incommodaret, ut exiguas istas impensas sustinere nequirent; excepitque sic quidam ex Caesaris intimis, venturum forte tempus, quo ipsius Matis operâ indigeant, tunc tergiversationum diverticula adhuc plausibiliora, quam illi effinxerunt, adinveniri posse. Iussit vero mox Caesar ad capitulum Magdaeburgense literas, hac de caussâ, scribi, quas ex suo ore nunc paratas e Cancellaria adferri procuro, mox ad Reverendum illud capitulum dirigendas, quod sponte suum erga Caesaream Matem hac in parte obsequium subiectissime obtulit, postquam rescivisset Senatores id detrectare; est enim (uti fieri solet) nonnulla inter eos aemulatio. Spero itaque me tam instrumenta quam coeteras res meas primo quoque tempore huc recepturum, nam de Hamburgensibus nihil addubito, quin voluntati Caesae Matis promptius sint obtemperaturi; interea aedificia pro Instrumentis ordine et sigillatim disponendis ante hyemem inchoata, et illius frigore superveniente, intermissa, complere satagimus.

Epigrammata duo, quae iuxta calcem sunt eorum quae mitto carminum, Caesar, ut duabus praecipuis valvis, ex dorico et Ionico opere factis, superius marmori insculperentur, mandavit. Cumque ego in primo, ut ab initio legeretur Quae Boreas renuo, et in altero suo loco Arctois pro Danis, quo Patria mea non nominaretur, Caesar iussit, ut expresse scriberetur Dania quae renuit, et in altero Epigrammate Danis pro Arctois. Sed haec non nisi secretioribus Amicis revelanda sunt.

Parant varijs in locis plures venâ Poëticâ pollentes eiuscemodi carmina, in mei gratiam, inter quos est IOSEPHUS SCALIGER, qui subinde mihi scripsit, et MELISSUS, Poëtarum nostri temporis antesignanus : et quia memini me aliquando tibi pollicitum, eorum quae accepero, ubi in aliquam copiam excrevissent, communicationem, haec nunc impertior, donec plura nactus fuero, tibi soli reservanda, et non nisi OLIGERO nostro et alias integris atque sincere sentientibus monstranda.

De ijs quae mihi in Daniâ evenerunt, non est quod existimes, me quidpiam scripturum aut publicaturum, nisi extrema sic urserit necessitas, atque vestrates occasionem praebuerint, utinam alij aeque silere vellent; sunt enim nonnulli, qui meâ sorte plus afficiantur atque aegrius hanc mutationem ferant (ob Astronomiae praesertim, quam affectant, instaurationem) quam ego, qui omnia infracto animo perfero; nec mihi adversi quid sic evenisse autumo; sic neque Polemica scripta unquam êditurus sum, utilioribus et magis serijs intentus, ut non opus fuisset eâ quam adducis admonitione, mihi tamen non ingratâ. Obstrepent veritati et calumnientur, nobisque illudant, aliorumque scurrilibus convitijs applaudeant, quicunque volent: Stans tegor in solido etc. Si tamen alij sponte sua pro veritate asserenda in mei honorem quidpiam scripserint, incivile et inhumanum foret idipsum averso animo improbari. Quod vero tu in gratiam istius Scotuli veritatem tibi, si cui alias, satis perspectam dissimulare et vinclis constringere malis, ferendum duco, neque enim invito libenter quidpiam extorserim, et perinde erit, sive id quod res est aperte professus fueris, sive non; id tamen, etiam te dubitante, certo constat, eum in publica quadam et impressa intimatione, valvis Collegij Helmstadij affixa, cuius exemplum penes me est, sibi meam arrogasse inventionem, praeter ea, quae privatim effutijt; quod quo iure fecerit, nemo melius quam tu (si velles) qui adfuisti, cum ipsi eam Hypothesin aperirem, testari poterit. Sed missum facio ista, tibi, uti video, morosa.

De MULÆO vestro quae subiungis, te metuere, ne mora illa longior, quam apud vos traxit, ab Italicâ profectione per hyemem iam praeteritam eum sit remoratura: id equidem sic est; neque enim aliqua concedebatur occasio, quâ commode eo, postquam huc redijt, proficisceretur: sed tamen, appropinquante iam vere, modum adinveni, quâ id nihilominus fieri posse sperem; commendavi enim ipsum diligenter Serenissimo Electori Coloniensi, qui mihi Clementissimâ propensione favet; per eius Celsitudinem ipsum promotum iri, ut tam Italiam quam Gallias, prout in votis habet, perlustrare commode queat, mihi persuadeo. Constituit autem prius ad suos in Daniam ire, ut isthic sibi acquirat viaticum; et res suas ad iter istud disponit. Quin et ego pariter ut tu sentio ex re ipsius non fore, ut de me nihil dicam, ipsum diutius hîc iuveniles suos Annos consumere, quibus potius peregrinationibus et studijs vacandum, neque enim eae, quas ego tracto, disciplinae ipsi usui sunt, uti etiam earum minus capax.

TENGNAGLIUS meus sub Brumam huc ad me ex Italia, ubi intra annum linguam probe didicit, moribus et favoribus auctus, reversus est; ante octiduum in Patriam suam ivit, Parentes isthic salutaturus; postea, volente Deo, huc rediens, in Aula Caesaris honesto loco aliquamdiu futurus.

Quae de honoribus Politicis mihi ex Aulâ Caesarea indicatis, cum adhuc Wittebergae essem, mones, ut prius in possessionem feudi veniam, recte quidem mones, nam et ego in ea sum sententia, sed non plane intelligis, quid innuere voluerim, neque enim hoc est, de quo tecum ante abitum meum in Dania locutus sum, sed quiddam insuper aliud, de quo prius non cogitavi, prout suo tempore [D.] V. percipies.

Apud TYPOTIUM de eo, quod scis, te iam antea sufficienter purgavi, et ubi convenerimus, id etiamnum ulterius faciam. Est is mei amantissimus, et ubicunque occasio datur, de me et med familiâ honorifice sentit et loquitur. Studia vero nostra a meipso probanda, cum eorum non sit gnarus, asserit.

Non dubito, quin multa in Legatione tam Polonicâ quam Anglicanâ, a Doctis hinc inde audieris, haud dissimilia ijs, quae MICHAELIDES narravit, licet is Wordingburgi apud Arcis Praefectum calumnias effutire, nec tamen impune, non erubuerit, prout forte a TYCHONE meo percepisti; ideoque eo promptius carmina, ob quae ista fingebat, tecum communico, ut dispicias, nihil in ijs reperiri in Regem, de quo semper honorifice loqui, eumque excusare soleo, aspere et indecenter prolatum. Narratum mihi est a quibusdam Danis, cum Wittebergae essem, eos, cum per Bohemiam aliquando peregrinarentur, a Parocho quodam Pontificiae doctrinae admonitos fuisse, ne se Danos esse profiterentur, sed potius ex aliqua Germaniae parte oriundos dicerent, partim quod Rex in coronatione Pontificis Romani habitum ludicre imitatus fuisset, partim quod me sic amisissent. Quid IVARUS FRIIS per Ungariam et Austriam, praesente Regis fratre (qui tamen, quis esset, ignorabatur) meâ de caussâ, audire et decorare necesse habuit, ipsemet, cum tibi sit familiaris, aliquando forsitan narrabit; dum ea Dresdae me invisens inter alia suggerere voluit, datâ operâ orationem alio declinavi, nolens certis de caussis ista mihi referri, qui usque ad nauseam similia saepius inaudivi. Viderint illi, qui in culpa sunt. Deus in Patria nostra animorum coniunctionem largiatur et stabiliat, dissidia et bellorum semina auferat. Quid Legati vestri in Suecia expedierint, a te cognoscere cupio, tum quoque quid praeterea apud vos et in toto illo Boreali tractu geratur.

Nova, quae hinc tibi significem, pauca in promptu habeo. De recuperata Transylvania te iam antea intellexisse opinor. Aiunt autem BAIUODAM, qui eam fortiter pugnando pro Caesare recepisse putabatur, sibi feudi loco reservare constituere, ipso etiam Caesare invito, quamobrem Legati eius nuper Pilsnae fuerunt, qui cum se impatienter et pervicaciter gessissent, missus est qui eos apud BAIUODAM accusaret, increpante Caesare admodum severe senem quendam eorum primarium. Turcam maiorem quam unquam antea parare contra Ungariam exercitum, rumor est. Deus Armipotens furorem et vires eius retundat: Caesar noster vicissim se equitibus et militibus, quotquot nancisci poterit, illi obviaturus, munit. Imperij Principes, maiori ex parte, suppetias detrectant, Hispanos potius quam Turcas formidantes. Deus author concordiae et pacis nos protegat et a malis liberet.

Doleo vicem consiliariorum Sueticorum, si graviter in eos saevierit CAROLUS; sibi ipsi minus prudenter consuluerit, quâ etiam de re, ut in ultimis tibi indicavi, TYPOTIUS, me consentiente, ipsum per literas admonuit, exemplum ERICI fratris proponens.

MOSCUS apud Caesarem et Archiducem MAXIMILIANUM nonnulla tacite molitur, quae si successerint, Rex Poloniae maluisset utique se avito regno non excessisse. Quod meâ etiam opinione vix recuperabit unquam. Non minor est virtus, quam quaerere, parta tueri.

Plura iam non addo, neque enim publicas tumultuationes, quibus hîc fluctuans terrenus Orbis iamdudum assuevit, multum moror; nihil novi sub Sole, eadem fere semper luditur fabula, sub alijs atque alijs Personis. Si paulo fusius quam forte velles, tibi hac Epistola scripsisse videar, scias id consulto admissum, ut et tu copiose rescribas. Tantum abest ut prolixitas, quam in tuis ultimo excusas, mihi displicuerit. Rariores nunc conceduntur internuncij, plenius itaque per literas colloquemur, siquidem oretenus non detur. Nam quod tu, ut aliquoties ad vos excurram, optas, ita facile, ac lubenter vellem, fieri nequit. Constitueram quidem ad comitia huius Anni, si qua apud vos celebrarentur, proficisci: at quia de ijs incertus sum, nec Caesar hoc primo Anno veniam in Daniam eundi mihi concedere volet, id in alterum Annum, si Deo ita visum fuerit, differendum erit. Quomodo vero id meo cum bono, uti insinuas, futurum sit, a te apertius intelligere aveo.

Vale quam optime, salutatâ tuâ coniuge a meâ et filiabus peramanter.
Dabantur ex Arce Caesarea Benatica Die 24 Martij stylo renovato Anni 1600.

Tycho Brahe to Niels Krag.

Heartiest greeting.

My son Tycho, returning home to me here just after the beginning of this year, delivered your letter, most excellent and most loving Lord Doctor Niels Krag — a letter most welcome to me, even though at the outset you seem to take my reproach (over the long lapse in our correspondence) more heavily than was perhaps fitting, and to chide somewhat a friend's freedom in jesting, as though you did not know that your old Tycho, though he sometimes addresses his friends so, nonetheless thinks candidly of them and is constant. I thank you, moreover, for receiving and entertaining my emissaries so kindly; yet I should not have wished you to incur those expenses on my account. If anything can in turn be rendered by me for your benefit, you will find me mindful of this kindness too, along with the rest.

Of the state of my affairs — since you already learned it plainly both from what I wrote to you and to our Rosenkrantz, and besides from my son's report — I will here repeat nothing; and that the terms most graciously offered and assigned to me by His Sacred Imperial Majesty so pleased you, that they affected you and your Elisa with no small joy, I take kindly. The Emperor's most benign favour toward me grows daily (thanks be to God), as does that of those who are of his inner circle and prevail here in authority — though with certain peevish folk there is no lack of envy, the shadow (as it goes) of virtue. The Emperor was not at Prague for half a year, but stayed meanwhile, with his chief councillors, in a certain fortified town ten miles distant thence, called Pilsen — and that on account of the epidemic pestilence which in the past year grievously afflicted all Bohemia and the neighbouring places, right up to the winter solstice; for which cause I too, together with my people, was in no small peril, plague and dysentery raging cruelly both in the neighbouring town and throughout the whole territory of Benátky. On this score His Sacred Imperial Majesty was, of his own accord, with a most clement spirit — nay, with a kind of fatherly affection toward me and mine — not a little concerned, so much so that he several times sent here to ascertain how we fared; and of his own motion he offered the chance of moving elsewhere and avoiding the dangers, if it should so please me — naming, in particular, Vienna in Austria, since that city was free of contagion, and most graciously offered that he would have suitable conveniences arranged for me there through his officers, both as to lodging and the other necessaries, if only I were willing to go thither. But I, having rendered most humble thanks, replied that I would rather remain here, commit everything to Divine providence, and be free for my own pursuits. Which did not displease His Majesty either. And he requested the antidotes which he had learned I had ready and effective against those diseases, together with the manner of their preparation — which I most submissively imparted. I did at length withdraw, however — indulging the fear of my womenfolk (as that sex is wont) — into a certain other Imperial castle three miles distant from here (since I was free to dwell in whatever place in the vicinity I wished), where, while my people are away in Denmark, I stayed for some weeks not inconveniently. And I give thanks to God most good and great that He has kept me and all my people safe and unharmed throughout that whole dangerous time. There is hope that His Imperial Majesty will return to Prague immediately after the feast of Easter, as very many eagerly desire. When I was lately there, I learned from many — what I had likewise gathered on the road from the Captain of Brandeis, my neighbour — that not only the citizens and merchants but also the foremost men attribute it to me that the Emperor is so long absent from Prague, as though he depended in this matter on my counsel, and that I had advised against his coming before the end of March; nor was there room for excuse, though I seriously denied it. Such is the talk that has now arisen about me here. They suspect such things because I am sometimes accustomed to write to His Majesty about certain matters, at his own wish, and the councillors are not allowed — as is otherwise the custom — to open my letters.

That my instruments, which I left in Denmark, have been carried away and are now kept, together with the rest, on German soil, His Imperial Majesty was glad to learn; and it pleased him that they raised no difficulties about this there — otherwise he would have written to your King on this account. He gave letters both to the people of Hamburg and to those of Magdeburg, that whatever I have deposited with them, together with the rest of my furnishings, they should at their own expense, as a favour to His Majesty, have conveyed here by the Elbe; and since in the earlier letters the Magdeburgers had cunningly found a certain subterfuge, he sent further letters which set out the former more plainly; and when, on receiving these, they wrote back to me asking that I have them excused in this matter before His Imperial Majesty — since the hardship of the siege they once suffered still so pressed them that, without inconvenience to themselves, they were not equal to bearing those expenses — when the Emperor learned this from Lord Barvitius, his chief Secretary, he took it very ill, and his councillors broke into laughter [κακιννος], that a siege carried out fifty years ago should still so inconvenience them that they could not sustain those trifling expenses; and one of the Emperor's intimates remarked thus, that a time might perhaps come when they would need His Majesty's good offices, and then more plausible by-ways of evasion than these they had contrived could be found. The Emperor soon ordered that letters be written, for this cause, to the cathedral chapter of Magdeburg — which letters, now prepared from his own mouth, I am having brought from the Chancery, to be directed presently to that Reverend chapter, which has of its own accord most submissively offered its compliance toward His Imperial Majesty in this matter, after learning that the city Senators were declining it; for (as commonly happens) there is some rivalry between them. I hope, therefore, to receive here both the instruments and my other things at the first opportunity; for of the Hamburgers I have no doubt that they will more readily obey His Imperial Majesty's will. Meanwhile we are striving to complete the buildings — begun before winter, for arranging the instruments in order and singly, and broken off when its cold came on — for setting up the instruments.

Two epigrams, which are near the foot of the verses I send, the Emperor has ordered to be carved above, on the marble, on the two chief portals, made of Doric and Ionic work. And whereas I had wished that in the first it be read from the beginning "which I, the North, refuse," and in the second, in its own place, "the Northern lands" in place of "the Danes," so that my fatherland should not be named, the Emperor ordered that it be written expressly "Denmark, which refused," and in the second epigram "the Danes" in place of "the Northern lands." But these things are to be revealed to none but the more confidential friends.

In various places several men strong in the poetic vein are preparing verses of this kind in my honour — among them Joseph Scaliger, who has written to me from time to time, and Melissus, the standard-bearer of the poets of our age. And because I remember that I once promised you a sharing of what I should receive, once they had grown to some quantity, I now impart these — to be reserved for you alone until I have got more, and shown to none but our Holger and others of upright and sincere sentiment.

Of the things that befell me in Denmark, you have no reason to suppose that I shall write or publish anything, unless the utmost necessity so press, and your countrymen give occasion — would that others were equally willing to keep silent; for there are some who are more moved by my lot, and bear this change more grievously (especially on account of the restoration of astronomy, which they cherish), than I, who endure everything with an unbroken spirit; nor do I reckon that anything adverse has thus befallen me. Thus neither shall I ever publish polemical writings, being bent on more useful and more serious matters — so that there was no need of that admonition you bring forward, though it was not unwelcome to me. Let them clamour against the truth and slander, and mock us, and applaud the scurrilous abuse of others, whosoever will: I stand sheltered on solid ground, and so forth. Yet if others, of their own accord, in defence of the truth, write something in my honour, it would be uncivil and inhuman to disapprove it with an averse mind. But that you, for the sake of that little Scot, prefer to dissemble the truth — which is sufficiently clear to you, if to anyone — and to bind it in chains, I think must be borne; for I would not gladly wring anything from an unwilling man, and it will be all the same whether you openly profess what the case is or not. Yet this much is certain, even though you doubt it: that he, in a certain public and printed notice affixed to the doors of the College at Helmstedt — of which I have a copy — arrogated to himself my invention, besides the things he blabbed in private. By what right he did this, none can better testify than you (if you would), who were present when I disclosed that Hypothesis to him. But I let these matters drop, troublesome to you, as I see.

As to what you add about your Mule — that you fear that the long delay he has incurred among you will hold him back, the winter being now past, from his journey to Italy: indeed it is so; for no opportunity was granted by which he might conveniently set out thither after he returned here. Yet, spring now approaching, I have found a way by which I hope it may nonetheless come about; for I have diligently commended him to the Most Serene Elector of Cologne, who favours me with a most clement inclination, and I persuade myself that he will be advanced through His Highness, so that he may conveniently traverse both Italy and France, as he desires. He has resolved, however, first to go to his people in Denmark, to acquire travelling-money there, and is arranging his affairs for that journey. Nay, I think, just as you do, that it is not to his advantage — to say nothing of mine — to consume his youthful years here any longer, which ought rather to be devoted to travels and studies; for the disciplines I practise are of no use to him, as he is also less capable of them.

My Tengnagel returned to me here about the winter solstice from Italy, where within a year he learned the language well, increased in manners and in favour; eight days ago he went to his own homeland to greet his parents there, afterwards (God willing) returning here, to be for some while in an honourable post at the Emperor's court.

As to the political honours indicated to me from the Imperial court while I was still at Wittenberg, you advise that I first come into possession of the fief — you advise rightly, indeed, for I too am of that opinion; but you do not quite understand what I meant to hint at, for it is not the thing of which I spoke with you before my departure in Denmark, but something further besides, which I had not thought of before, as you will perceive in due time, Your [Lordship].

With Typotius I have already sufficiently cleared you of that which you know; and when we meet, I shall do so yet further. He is most loving toward me, and wherever occasion is given, thinks and speaks honourably of me and my family. As for our studies, he asserts that they are to be judged by me myself, since he is not versed in them.

I do not doubt that you have heard, from learned men here and there, many things in connection with both the Polish and the English embassy, not unlike those which Michaelides related — though he was not ashamed to blab calumnies at Vordingborg before the Governor of the castle, yet not with impunity, as you perhaps gathered from my Tycho; and therefore I share with you the more readily the verses on account of which he was fabricating those tales, that you may discern that nothing is found in them uttered harshly or improperly against the King — of whom I am always accustomed to speak honourably and to excuse. It was told me by certain Danes, while I was at Wittenberg, that when they were once travelling through Bohemia, they were warned by a certain priest of the Pontifical doctrine not to profess themselves Danes, but rather to say they were sprung from some part of Germany — partly because the King had mockingly imitated the garb of the Roman Pontiff at [his] coronation, partly because they had thus lost me. What Iver Friis, in Hungary and Austria, in the presence of the King's brother (who, however — who he was — was not known), had to hear and to gloss on my account, he himself, since he is on familiar terms with you, will perhaps relate some day; while at Dresden, visiting me, he wished to suggest those things among others, I purposely turned the discourse elsewhere, unwilling for certain reasons to have such things reported to me, having too often heard the like to the point of nausea. Let those who are at fault see to it. May God grant and establish a union of hearts in our fatherland, and take away discords and the seeds of wars. What your envoys have accomplished in Sweden, I desire to learn from you, and likewise what else is going on with you and in all that Northern tract.

News that I might signify to you from here, I have little ready to hand. Of the recovery of Transylvania I think you have learned already. But they say that the Voivode, who was thought to have recovered it by valiant fighting on the Emperor's behalf, is resolving to reserve it for himself as a fief, even against the Emperor's own will; on which account his envoys were lately at Pilsen, who, having conducted themselves impatiently and obstinately, [the Emperor] sent someone to accuse them before the Voivode, the Emperor rebuking very severely a certain elderly chief among them. There is a rumour that the Turk is preparing a greater army than ever before against Hungary. May God Almighty in arms blunt his fury and his forces; our Emperor in turn fortifies himself to meet him with as many horse and foot as he can obtain. The Princes of the Empire, for the greater part, refuse aid, dreading the Spaniards rather than the Turks. May God, the author of concord and peace, protect us and free us from evils.

I grieve at the lot of the Swedish councillors, if Charles has raged grievously against them; he will have counselled himself less prudently — on which matter, too, as I indicated to you in my last, Typotius, with my consent, admonished him by letter, setting before him the example of his brother Eric.

The Muscovite is quietly contriving certain things with the Emperor and with Archduke Maximilian, which, if they succeed, the King of Poland would certainly have preferred not to have left his ancestral kingdom; which, in my opinion too, he will scarcely ever recover. It is no less a virtue to keep what is won than to seek it.

I add no more now, for I do not much linger over the public tumults to which this wavering earthly globe has long been accustomed; there is nothing new under the Sun, almost always the same play is acted, under one set of characters and another. If I seem to have written to you somewhat more fully in this letter than perhaps you would wish, know that it was done of set purpose, so that you too may write back copiously. So far am I from being displeased by the prolixity which you excuse in your last. Messengers are now more rarely granted; we shall therefore converse more fully by letter, since it is not given to do so by word of mouth. For as to your wish that I should run over to you now and then — easily and gladly as I should wish it, it cannot be done. I had indeed resolved to set out for this year's Diet, if any were held among you; but since I am uncertain of it, and the Emperor will not be willing to grant me leave to go to Denmark this first year, it must be deferred to another year, if God so see fit. But how it will be to my good, as you intimate, I long to understand more openly from you.

Fare you most well; your wife being most lovingly greeted by mine and by my daughters.
Given from the Imperial Castle of Benátky, the 24th day of March, by the renewed style, the year 1600.

[Postscript:] POSTSCRIPT. Since my sister — that Urania, the little Earth-dweller [Terricula] — in her last letter insinuated some things about your countryman Mercurius (you know whom she marks), namely that he had spoken at Landskrona somewhat dishonourably of His Imperial Majesty, and afterwards had added some things too about the chief Councillors, all of which tended to this, that he might render her doubtful and diffident about the success of my affairs — as though I were to find no one here who would advance me and mine, and as though those from whom I most hoped for some help were in ill favour with the Emperor and of no authority: although she, perceiving well enough whither these things tended, dissolved, as far as she then could, his less candid utterances, and threw back what came up (clever little thing that she is), and although it matters little what he or his like judge — for we know with what spirit they proceed: nevertheless, that you may, as occasion offers, instruct that Urania and further confirm her that the matter (thanks be to God) stands far otherwise, I send you a copy of the letter which my Tengnagel lately, while he was at the Court at Pilsen, wrote to me, before he departed thence to his homeland, that you may understand from it of what kind is the zeal and favour toward me, not only of the Magnificent Lord Barvitius — who is at the Emperor's side almost every hour, being his chief Secretary — but also of the Illustrious and most Ample Lord Corraduz, Vice-Chancellor of the Empire, who deservedly holds the foremost authority at Court. I have added also a letter of Lord Doctor Jessenius — who was my host at Wittenberg in the Philippic house, and who about the same time was conducting a certain case at Pilsen — to almost the same effect, but written a little more fully about Lord Corraduz, and it is in Jessenius's own hand. You will be able to set out the contents of both Letters to my sister, when you meet, that she may have material with which to pin down and beat back that unstable and shifty Mercurius, if perhaps he should at any time suggest the like again, or blab it among others in his sharp-tongued way. Nonetheless I too have sent, in my letter to the same Urania, certain things in German, written to me a little before from Pilsen by a member of my household, which make mention, among other things, of the singular goodwill and candid affection of Lord Corraduz toward me; about which she in turn will be able to report to you. There are also other Illustrious men of exceptional dignity at the Imperial Court who wish me well in my affairs — to say nothing of His Sacred Imperial Majesty himself, who is constant in his purpose, nor heeds the sinister judgments of others (if any there be) so lightly as may be the case elsewhere. I shall add nothing now. I give thanks to God most good and great for this my lot, and pray that He may deign in future to attend me with His protection and blessing. Again farewell. Given as above in the letter.

Apparatus

  • Edition of record. Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia vol. VIII (Epistolae). MS witness: A = Basel, Universitätsbibliothek, G I 23.
  • Date / place. Dateline Die 24 Martij stylo renovato Anni 1600 — explicitly New Style (stylo renovato = Gregorian), matching the catalogue label "1600. 24. marts n.s." Written from Benátky nad Jizerou (Arx Benatica), the imperial castle north-east of Prague where Rudolf II installed Tycho on his arrival in Bohemia. Note the calendar self-consciousness: Tycho, a Dane normally writing Old Style, here dates by the Gregorian style of his new Imperial-Catholic context — the very 10-day gap discussed elsewhere in the correspondence (cf. brahe-t_16000126002).
  • Recipient. Niels Krag (Nicolaus Cragius, c. 1550–1602) — Danish humanist, royal historiographer and diplomat, professor at Copenhagen, then a royal councillor; one of Tycho's most trusted Danish correspondents. His wife is named "Elisa."
  • Persons & powers named.
  • His Sacred Imperial Majesty / the Emperor / Caesar — Rudolf II, Holy Roman Emperor, Tycho's new patron.
  • filius Tycho — Tycho Brahe the Younger, the elder son, just back from Denmark.
  • Rosenkrantz (Roseakrantzius) / Holger (Oliger) — Holger Rosenkrantz of Rosenholm (cf. brahe-t_15980810001), to whom Tycho writes in parallel and who is to share the documents.
  • Lord Barvitius, chief Secretary — Johann Anton Barvitius, Rudolf II's leading secretary, Tycho's conduit to the Emperor.
  • Joseph Scaliger — Josephus Justus Scaliger, the foremost philologist of the age, then at Leiden; he corresponded with Tycho (cf. the Scaliger precession exchange, brahe-t_15980709001 etc.).
  • Melissus — Paul Schede Melissus (1539–1602), German neo-Latin poet, "standard-bearer of the poets of our age."
  • "that little Scot" (Scotulus) — Duncan Liddel (1561–1613), the Scottish mathematician at Helmstedt accused of claiming Tycho's geo-heliocentric world-system as his own. This letter is a primary document of the plagiarism dispute: Tycho states Liddel "arrogated my invention" in a printed notice affixed to the doors of the College at Helmstedt, of which Tycho holds a copy — and that Krag himself was present when Tycho first disclosed the Hypothesis to Liddel, so Krag could testify. Directly continuous with the Duncan-Liddel dossier (brahe-t_15990926001) and Krag's testimony-origin letter (brahe-t_15991204001); Tycho here gently presses the reluctant Krag (who would rather "dissemble the truth and bind it in chains" out of regard for Liddel).
  • Mule (Mulaeus) — Krag's young associate, whom Tycho commends to the Elector of Cologne for a study-journey to Italy and France (cf. the Mule/Boekel business in brahe-t_15980810001).
  • Elector of Cologne — Ernst of Bavaria, Archbishop-Elector, Tycho's well-wisher.
  • Tengnagel (Tengnaglius) — Frans Gansneb Tengnagel, Tycho's assistant (later son-in-law), back from a year in Italy, bound for an honourable court post.
  • Typotius — Jacob Typotius (1540–1601), Flemish humanist and imperial historiographer at Prague, Tycho's friend and advocate.
  • Michaelides — a Dane (patronymic "son of Michael," not securely identified) who spread calumnies against Tycho before the Governor of Vordingborg castle (Wordingburgum).
  • Iver Friis (Ivarus Friis) — Danish noble; the King's brother travelling incognito through Hungary/Austria is most plausibly Duke Ulrik of Denmark (Christian IV's brother).
  • the King — Christian IV of Denmark, of whom Tycho insists he always speaks honourably; the report that Christian IV "mockingly imitated the garb of the Roman Pontiff at his coronation" refers to anti-papal pageantry associated with the 1596 coronation.
  • the Voivode (Baiuoda) — Michael the Brave (Mihai Viteazul) of Wallachia, who had seized Transylvania (autumn 1599) nominally for the Emperor but sought to hold it as his own fief; his envoys at Pilsen.
  • the Turk — Sultan Mehmed III, the Ottoman threat to Hungary (the Long Turkish War).
  • Charles (Carolus) / his brother Eric — Duke Charles of Södermanland (the future Karl IX of Sweden), regent, "raging against the Swedish councillors" (the aftermath of his victory over Sigismund — the executions would culminate in the 1600 Linköping bloodbath); Eric = Erik XIV, Charles's deposed and imprisoned brother, cited as a cautionary example.
  • the Muscovite (Moscus) — the Tsar of Muscovy (Boris Godunov), intriguing with the Emperor and Archduke Maximilian III against the King of Poland = Sigismund III Vasa, who had "left his ancestral kingdom" (Sweden) and would "scarcely ever recover" it.
  • Places. Prague; Pilsen (Pilsena, Plzeň), the fortified town c. 10 (German) miles from Prague where Rudolf II sheltered from the plague; Benátky (Benatica); the second imperial castle c. 3 miles off to which Tycho withdrew — Brandýs nad Labem (its Capitaneus Brandeisensis, "Captain of Brandeis," is named as Tycho's neighbour and informant); Vienna (offered as a plague-refuge); Hamburg and Magdeburg (custodians of Tycho's shipped goods); the Elbe (Albis) as the transport route; Wittenberg (Witteberga, Tycho's station before Bohemia); Dresden; Helmstedt (Liddel's university).
  • The instrument-transport. A valuable logistical record: Tycho's instruments and household goods, left in Denmark and then deposited at Hamburg and Magdeburg, are to be shipped up the Elbe to Bohemia at imperial expense, by Rudolf II's letters. Magdeburg's evasion — pleading impoverishment from "the siege fifty years ago" (the 1550–51 siege by Maurice of Saxony) — is mocked by the imperial councillors; the Emperor redirects the request to the cathedral chapter, which complies out of rivalry with the city Senate. Tycho is simultaneously completing the purpose-built instrument buildings at Benátky (begun before winter, halted by cold).
  • The marble epigrams (a striking detail). Rudolf II ordered two of Tycho's epigrams carved in marble above the two main portals of the castle (one of Doric, one of Ionic order). Where Tycho, out of patriotic delicacy, had written "which I, the North, refuse" (using Arctoi, "the Northern lands," in place of Dani, "the Danes") so as not to name his fatherland, the Emperor insisted on the explicit "Dania quae renuit" ("Denmark, which refused [him]") and on "the Danes" for "the Northerners" in the second — a deliberate imperial sharpening of the reproach against Denmark, which Tycho marks as a secret for confidential friends only. (The self-monument theme links to the Baldi/Magini self-monument thread, brahe-t_16000304001 / brahe-t_16010124001.)
  • Greek interpolation. consiliarij vero eius in κακιννος resoluti sunt — "his councillors broke into [derisive] laughter." The Greek word as transcribed (κακιννος) is corrupt (most likely a garbling of καγχασμόν / κιχλισμόν, "cackling/giggling laughter," vel sim.); reproduced as printed by this chapter's convention, with the sense ("burst out laughing / into mockery") secure from the Latin frame.
  • Latin tags & allusions. "Stans tegor in solido" — "I stand sheltered on solid ground" (a Stoic emblematic motto of constancy; Tycho's etc. shows he quotes a known tag). "nihil novi sub Sole" — Ecclesiastes 1:9. "Non minor est virtus, quam quaerere, parta tueri" — Ovid, Ars Amatoria 2.13. "virtutis … umbra [invidia]" — the commonplace that envy is the shadow of virtue.
  • §1 Dreyer collation. The POSTSCRIPTA (the "Mercurius"-gossip parallel: Urania = sister Sophie; the unnamed Landskrona slanderer; enclosing the Tengnagel + Jessenius letters from Pilsen as counter-evidence), restored from Dreyer VIII, pp. 277–278. The slanderer's name is a contracted/symbol form, garbled to "[...]rio/[...]rium"; left as [...], not invented.
  • Correction. Three defects restored against Dreyer, Opera Omnia VIII: (1) p.274, the dropped clause ‘et in altero suo’, which shows the two epigrams originally carried different wording (‘Quae Boreas renuo’ in the first, ‘Arctois pro Danis’ in the second) before the Emperor's edit — English corrected to match; (2) pp.277–278, a private cipher — a planetary glyph standing for the name ‘Mercurius’, the same figure named openly in Tycho's companion letter to Sophie Brahe of 21 March 1600 over the same Landskrona incident — restored in place of an editorial ellipsis, at both occurrences; (3) p.278, ‘effutierit’ (blab/blurt out) restored for the corrupted ‘effuderit’ (pour out), matching the verb's earlier use in this same letter.

The translation and its checking

Grade Probable · collated against Dreyer's page images, Opera Omnia VIII pp. 271–278; defects the audit noted: text error (6).

Collated against Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia (the edition of record), reading the printed page images. Read the full 9-page Dreyer extract (TBOO VIII pp.271-279) as page images plus p.9, confirming the letter itself runs pp.272-278 and p.279 belongs to the next letter (to Ioestelius). Collated the opening and closing lines, the dateline (24 March 1600, Benátky), and every proper name (Rosenkrantz, Elisa, Barvitius, Coraducius/Corraduz, Jessenius, Scaliger, Melissus, Oligerus/Holger, Michaelides, Iver Friis, Charles/Erik, Landskrona, Mulaeus, Tengnagel, the Helmstedt 'Scotulus') and number (10 milliaribus, 3 milliaribus, 50 Annos) against the page images. Found one high-severity text error (a dropped clause in the epigram passage that collapses a two-epigram distinction into one), one medium-severity text error (the postscript's private cipher glyph rendered as unexplained ellipsis rather than the legible sign Dreyer prints), and one low-confidence text error (a probable verb substitution near the letter's end). No fabrication, sentence-level omission, or mistranslation was found beyond what these text-level discrepancies induce downstream.

3 defects found:
- text error (high). The transcribed original silently drops the clause 'et in altero suo' ('and in the other, in its own place'), which in Dreyer marks that Tycho's own original wording differed between the TWO epigrams (Quae Boreas renuo in the first, Arctois pro Danis in the second). Without it the sentence reads as a single compressed substitution, and the English follows the defective source, describing only one epigram's original wording instead of two before the Emperor's edit. Dreyer prints: Cumque ego in primo, ut ab initio legeretur Quae Boreas renuo, ET IN ALTERO SUO loco Arctois pro Danis, quo Patria mea non nominaretur, Caesar iussit... (TBOO VIII p.274, lines c. 29-33).
- text error (medium). Tycho used a planetary glyph as a private cipher for an unnamed person's identity. Dreyer's typeset edition reproduces that glyph legibly and consistently twice; our transcribed original (and the translation that follows it) instead substitutes an unexplained ellipsis, dropping a real, transcribable character rather than describing it. Dreyer prints: prints a specific planetary/astrological glyph (reads as the sign for Venus, ♀) directly before 'rio'/'rium', consistently in both occurrences, TBOO VIII pp.277 and 278 — not a gap or illegible mark.
- text error (low). If Dreyer indeed prints 'effutierit', the sense is 'blab/blurt out' rather than 'pour out'; the English 'pour it out' matches our (possibly corrupted) the transcribed original rather than Dreyer. Flagged at low confidence given the visual similarity of the two verb forms in this typeface. Dreyer prints: reads, with moderate confidence, 'effutierit' (to blab/blurt out) rather than 'effuderit' (to pour out) — TBOO VIII p.278; the same verb stem 'effutire' appears earlier in this letter ('calumnias effutire').

Repaired. Collated against Dreyer TBOO VIII pp.271-279 in full (page images, read visually across three passes of at most 4 pages, never the long-s the digitised text layer). Defect 1 (the dropped 'et in altero suo') is unambiguous on p.274; both Latin and English were corrected. Defect 2 (the cipher glyph) is confirmed as a real, non-ellipsis character at both pp.277 and 278; its identification as the Mercury rather than Venus symbol rests on two independent pieces of internal evidence — the -rio/-rium oblique endings fit only Mercurius's declension, and the same cipher figure is named openly as 'Mercurius' in the companion letter to Sophie Brahe three days earlier, describing the identical Landskrona incident and the identical charge of inconstancy — rather than on a fully certain visual reading of the glyph's exact shape at this image resolution, which remains the one residual crux. Defect 3 ('effutierit' for 'effuderit') is clearly legible on p.278 and consistent with the verb's other use in the same letter. All three defects resolved.
