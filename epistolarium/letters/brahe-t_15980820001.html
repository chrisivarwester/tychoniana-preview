The original

EXCELLENTISSIMO ET PRÆSTANTISSIMO VIRO DOMINO NICOLAO CRAGIO I. V. DOCTORI, HISTORICO REGIO ET ACADEMIAe HAFFNIENSIS PROFESSORI EXIMIO, AMICO SUO EX INTIMIS UNI ET SINCERE DILECTO.

S. P.

ANTE octiduum primum huc reversus est MULÆUS, literasque tuas, Praestantissime et amantissime Vir, attulit, multo serius quam sperâram. Detentus quidem ab ESCHILLO et ACCIO, at non, ut causas morae interea per literas significaret prohibitus. Quod tamen serio ei in mandatis dederam. Nec tanta illi secreta aut mysteria scribenda erant, quod interfuerit, in quorumcumque tandem manus literae eae venirent. Et quod omnium maxime displicet, praecipuo negocio, nempe pecuniario, infecto reversus est. Quod tamen non tam illi quam dictis duobus imputo, et ACCIO potius quam ESCHILLO. Nam hic ministrum suum Flensburgum misit pro duobus millibus Ioachimicis meâ causâ isthic obtinendis, sed frustra. Ille solutionem summae debitae tergiversatur, et dilationem petit ob debita fratris defuncti et nescio quas alias impensas; cum tamen sit in bona re, et Cancellario nunc affine gaudeat, quem diutina avaritia et tenacitas satis nummosum reddidit. Huor kand da AXEL staa til at trenge? At ego totius summae restitutionem denuo urgeo. Nolo enim quidpiam mearum pecuniarum amplius in Dania haerere, praesertim in istâ invisâ affinitate. Ego quidem non incircumspecte hactenus eidem ACCIO scripsi. Nam quamvis non obscura mihi cum eo intercesserit familiaritas praeter sangvinis rationem, quae hac tempestate etiam inter ipsos fratres in aquam degeneravit (ideoque eam minus reputo) tamen nos satis novimus nos, et plus esse in verbis quam factis compertum habemus. Si is serio rem egisset tacentibus fratribus meis caeterisque consangvineis, et Regno et Regi et Parenti servatus fuisset is, de quo scribis, forte honor. Ille enim ferme solus, quae et quanta in istâ Insulâ confeceram, rectius novit, partim ob habitationis suae vicinitatem, dum Landscroniae Praeses erat, partim quod nonnunquam me invisere solitus, eaque inspicere, et de ijs me referentem audire. Attamen ad literas mihi obsignatas, quibus unâ subscripserat, non minus quam caeteri obticuit, magno equidem omnium illorum honore, et ad posteritatem historijs commendando. O prisca fides! ô obsoleta veterum Danorum constantia! sed fit nunc perinde, prout Germania nostra vestrae nuper scripsit Daniae:

Curat se quilibet unum,
non alios, metuitque sibi, si causa probetur
alterius, sit iusta licet.

Sed utinam haec de solo ACCIO, quod ad plurimos alios, qui ipso mihi coniunctiores esse debebant, redundat, dicere liceret. Det var en gott, om der var ingen vaerre end hand. Si non multum boni praestat, attamen non ita nocet, ut caeteri. Och det er en stor naade i denne tiid. Et interdum etiam consolari videtur, meliorisque fortunae spem facere, quam tamen in vestrâ Daniâ nec exspecto nec expeto. Bene mecum actum iudicarem, si absentem et non modice laesum immeritis cavillis et calumnijs persequi nonnulli e vobis abstinere possent, quamvis et has nugas non plus morer, quam Cynthia pleno sublimis fulgensque lumine canum oblatrantium insipientiam, quos et generosi equi negligentes praetereunt, modo non plane mordeant. Tunc enim ne calcitrant. Egregiam sane consolationem vafra ista et Machiavellica inter quosdam e vobis Gymnasiarchis confederatio hinc nacta est, quod vetus ille meus domesticus, qui per me ad potiora promotus est, nunc ingratus cuculus ijs scurrile et mendacissimum efferi istius URSI scriptum impertitus sit. Sic enim habent quo sibi abblandiantur, quasi de re bene gesta, quod eorum antesignanus ipsissima ut ut latens caussa fuerit, cur patria me nunc careat: exiguâ sane iacturâ, si ista convicia atque calumniae vim Geometricae veritatis obtinerent. Dersom den herlige Distractor famosorum librorum sad endnu i Narrehuset thill Augsburg, der jeg en gang løste ham ud for 18 Cruner, cum vinum suffuratus erat, da skulle hand sidde der saa lenge til hand kunde recitere Bogen uden ad, och kand skee aede den op til bedringe, førend jeg skulle løse ham der ud igien, ut et ipse sic ex hac incorporatione melius addisceret, quam antea mentiri, pugillare, fallere, peierare, et plures eiuscemodi virtutes, quibus liber iste scatet, et quarum ipse non ignarus est. Istud impurum scriptum Academiae Witembergensis, Lipsensis, Helmstadiensis, Rostochiensis et plures in Germaniâ detestantur, authoremque eius tanquam bonorum consortio amplius indignum, qui tamen apud quosdam vestratium decantatur, laudatur (got folch tilhobe) execrantur; quod et literis apud me testatum reliquerunt. Brisgoiensis sane Academia non obscure mihi significavit, se in Poëtastrum istum, qui contumeliosum illud distichon concinnârat et subiunxerat, prout meruit, animadversuram fuisse (modo is author esset, et non potius ursina bestia) si adhuc in vivis esset. Nam ante quadriennium in summa egestate eum obijsse mihi relatum est. Sic et viri Principes tantam impudentiam abominantur. Apud Brunsvicensem author officij, quod in schola ambivit, ea de causa repulsam passus est. Dux ADOLPHUS Holsaticus, quamprimum naenias istas vidit, statim addita execratione folia omnia laceravit, atque ad Cancellarium suum D.D. IONATHAM GUTSLOVIUM librum igne absumendum proiecit. Quod et factum est. RANZOVIUS noster non minus movebatur paucis solummodo introspectis verbis, omnia dira authori imprecans, et capitis poena mulctandum secundum IOB tam infandum facinus censens. En nonnulli ex eruditis vestris huic applaudent, in sinu vident, sibique hunc librum congratulantur, ne quid superesset, quod ingrata Patria, aut potius in ea ingrati quidam contra me auderent. Id tamen cum caeteris omnibus flocci facio, nec calumniatorem istum responsione dignum arbitror, sed poena potius secundum statuta Imperij. Quod et me impetraturum confido. Tunc demum triumphent ipsius applausores. Man skulle med rette iche rose aff Marchen, førend torffvet bleff holdet. Sed valeant et iste et isti, indigni de quibus haec scriberem, non sine chartae detrimento, cui vesanum istud scriptum obfuit, non vero existimationi nostrae, ut cum SCALIGERO nostro loquar. Quare pluribus de his nunc supersedeo.

A SCALIGERO, cuius modo memini, litteras mihi attulit FRANCISCUS TENGNAGLIUS meus ante biduum redux, rebus bene expeditis. Thi det var paa thiden en gang att tale om gott folch, och lade de andre fare. Est autem Epistola illa admodum erudite et humaniter scripta (uti sunt summi illius viri omnia) cuius exemplum tecum communico. Quae de Polis diversis Mundi et Aequatoris, deque Polari stellâ, fixarum progressu, anni quantitate, PTOLOMÆI crassa hallucinatione ex ortu Caniculae eodem die Iuliano, tot interlapsis seculis, atque similibus commemorat, minus sibi constant. Nam et bonus interdum dormitat HOMERUS. Quâ de re, ubi rescribendi otium nactus fuero, ipsum candide admonere constitui. Is nostra plurimi facit, uti non saltem ex his literis, sed et ex ipsa redintegrata temporum emendatione nuper publicatâ liquet, in quâ meas Aequinoctiorum observationes, quas Danicas vocat (som vel forskyldt er a quibusdam Danis, att de skulde saa kaldis) supra istius HIPPARCHI inventa (quae tamen et PTOLOMÆI et omnium veterum traditionibus praefert) longe evehit. Utrum ipsius hîc censurae, qui rem ponderat atque intelligit, potius standum sit, quam Curiae Magistri apud vos, qui horum plane rudis est et nil nisi pecunias somniat, ac Rusticorum opera Astronomiae et Iurisprudentiae anteferre non erubescit, tibi et alijs absque glaucomate negocium introspicientibus aestimandum relinquo. Is adeo laetatus est, quando TENGNAGLIUS cum meis illum accessit, ut prae gaudio exilierit, et quodvis humanitatis officium adeoque pecuniam, si eâ destitueretur (quod alias rarissimum est) illi obtulerit, nihilque magis in votis habuit, quam ut a Batavis accirer. Ideoque in partes Imperatoris minus inclinavit. Et cum de pollicitis Principis MAURITIJ, qui etiam mihi Germanice admodum humaniter scripsit, ac BARNEFELDIJ nonnulla illi retulisset FRANCISCUS, subiunxit SCALIGER, lenta admodum esse illorum consilia. Sed se eos adhortaturum, ut mature rem agant, et me eo pertrahere elaborent. Omnino enim oportere ut Batavis, non alijs cedam.

Qua vero clementiâ et benignitate Elector Coloniensis (qui Caesaris potius partes hac in re tuetur, ut et tu) eum exceperit, verbis satis exprimere non potest. Mansit apud ipsum usque in quartam hebdomadam, singulis pene diebus ad secretum eius colloquium per aliquot horas vocatus, adeo ut consiliariorum suorum interpellationes subinde eâ de caussâ averterit, multa sciscitatus, multa respondit, libros meos diligenter evolvit, laudavit; scripta etiam quaecunque FRANCISCUS ex meis secum habuit, ad se recepit, pauca restituit nec ea nisi descripta, adeo ut ne carminibus quidem in mei gratiam concinnatis parcere voluerit, occasiones abitus mei e Dania non sine admiratione expendit, et percunctatus FRANCISCUM quantum id fuerit, quod istic quotannis in reditibus habuerim, respondente illo, et summam in honorem Regis et Regni aliquantulum augente (det var noch hand var eders landmand) quatuor millia thalerorum eam attigisse; subiunxit Princeps haec formalia verba: Was? Solte man sölichen Man vmb so ein geringes Geltt aus dem Lande ziehn lassen? Schande ist es. Ein Herr verspielet mehr auff einen abent; Gelt bekommet man allezeit, aber sölche leute nicht allzeitt. Tandem litteras promotoriales ad Imperatorem exquisitâ diligentiâ ipsemet et composuit, et suapte manu scripsit, inscio tam Cancellario quam Secretario, quarum tenorem unâ cum ijs, quas ad BARBITIUM supremum Imperatoris Secretarium, qui ab ipso ad hoc dignitatis fastigium evectus est, et annua etiamnum habet stipendia, pariter dedit, te accepisse existimo. Scripsit etiam ad me succinctas et eruditas, quas suo cubiculario mane in lecto ipsemet dictavit, eo quod FRANCISCUS abitum festinaret. Earum etiam hîc exemplum habes, cum ijs, quas ad Dominum RANZOVIUM unâ dedit. Placuit illi quam maxime, quod in Germania postmodum habitare decreverim et se charissima omnia, quae haberet, depositurum aiebat, quod Imperator Clementissimo et summo favore me meaque studia sit prosecuturus et liberaliter sustentaturus; addebatque, quod si Caesar praeter omnem suam exspectationem in eo difficilem se praeberet, tunc seipsum proprijs impensis id effecturum, et omnino curaturum, ne extra Germaniam abeam, aut ad Danos redeam: egitque Deo gratias, quod ipsi iam propior essem quam antea; et siquidem ipsum tam cito ac vellem, invisere (quod tamen admodum in votis habuit) haud possem, se quendam nobilium suorum intimum huc ad me missurum, antequam in superiorem Germaniam me conferrem, dicebat, qui de secretis quibusdam ad artes abstrusas pertinentibus mecum ageret, atque ad ipsum ea quae percepisset, referret. Et ut paucis rem absolvam (siquidem omnia narrare nimis foret prolixum) cum alias tum praesertim ubi FRANCISCO discedenti valediceret, tenens illum diu per manum, his ipsis verbis conclusit: Sagett dem gutten ehrlichen Man viel gutes von meinent Wegent, meinen gnedigen Grues, auch daneben, das er mich freilich von seinen besten freundt halten soll den er hatt, vnd das ich ihn hin wiederumb nicht anders haben, vnd bey ihm thun will, als wan er mein leiblicher Bruder were, vnd ich ihn mein lebenlang vnd von jugent auff gekantt hette. So wohl gefältt er mir. Vnd so baldt ich gehen Prag kommen werde, will ich ihn besuchen, es sei wo er in Ihr Kais. Mayt. gebiet zu finden: adeo certus erat, se isthic me reperturum. Atque haec de optimi illius Principis Clementi erga me favore paucis indicata sunto. Ex quibus tute caeteros parvo aestimavit, nulli facile caetera colliges, quodque non tam invisus atque despectui habitus sim in Germania atque apud quosdam in Dania, vel inde ratiocinaberis. Quin et vestrates etiamnum omnem mei curam abijcere video, nec quicquam ut isthuc absque meo incommodo restituar, sollicitos esse. Nam si eo usque expectent, donec ego ijs supplex futurus sim, ad calendas Graecas (uti tu recte auguraris) expectabunt. Nihil equidem satius ducerem quam Patriae et Regis mei honori studere, vel ob optimum illum et laudatissimae memoriae FRIDERICUM huius Parentem, qui praeclare de me meritus est. Sed quid faciam, cum ipsi me respuerint, contempserint, detrimento non exiguo atque multis iniurijs affecerint, odio et invidia persecuti sint, artis, quam tam diu excolui, quamque totus pene orbis a me efflagitat, instaurationem non saltem non promovere voluerint, sed et sustentationem denegarint, ac subsidia, quae prius habui, subtraxerint? Si mihi more caeterorum Nobilium vivendum foret, nec altiora peragenda suscepissem, reliquos inglorius annos isthic traxissem, Bacchum et Cererem consumere natus. Haec et similia RAMELIO nostro, qui erga te ob itineris molestias non ingratus (quod laudo) fuit, quemque ego pro sincero habeo Amico, indices quaeso, ne interrogatus de abitus mei caussis respondeat se nullas scire, sed me sponte meâ citra ullas necessitates isthinc abijsse; veluti heri Generosus Dominus GULIELMUS SLAWATA, Liber Baro in Chlum et Koschumbergk, ex commendatione RANTZOVIJ me hîc in visens mihi retulit, sibi perconctanti a RAMELIO responsum esse. Satis quidem polite et Politice, an vero satis caussae Amici rationes ita habuerit, qui sic leviter et citra iustas rationes quidpiam admisisse tacite insimulatur, praeteritis ijs, qui in causa fuerunt, non dixerim. Quod Rex Serenissimus, quotiescunque mei incidit mentio, nullam malevoli animi significationem ostendat, veluti tu scribis, et RAMELIUS etiam CLAUDIO MULÆO insinuavit, et credo et lubenter audio. Nam cur male volet illi, qui ipsum dicto factove nunquam offendit, caussas non habet. Sufficit me tacere, et quae passus sum, dissimulare. Si reliqui duo nunc proximi a Rege, quibus meae emigrationis caussas verius imputo, adhuc clanculario mei laborant odio, fit id iuxta proverbium: Quem laeseris, oderis; ideoque nihil novi. Quod etiam ex alto potioribus intentus negligo.

Etsi vero per totam pene aestatem de Caesaris Clementissimo erga me favore et propensione, tum quoque aliorum quorundam Principum, mihi constiterit; tamen nihil movere, aut certi quid de mea hinc profectione statuere volui, antequam ............. huc reversus, ut id quod iam .............. nullos videlicet isthic vel ex ............ ipso nihil dicam, qui talibus minus attendit) mearum rerum curam suscepturos, vel Patriae caussa nedum mei. Idque nunc satis demonstratum arbitror, ut eo magis excusabilis sim apud omnes cordatos et sinceros viros, quod me alio, re sic urgente, conferam. Constitui itaque favente Divinâ clementiâ, quamprimum currus meos, quos adorno, caeteraque parata habuero, Pragam proficisci atque Caesareae Maiestatis in me Clementissimam voluntatem experiri, relictâ hîc interim familiâ meâ muliebri, ad quam aut ante hyemem, si Deo ita visum fuerit, revertar; aut si Caesar id voluerit, mihique loci oblati commoditas placuerit, eam isthuc addito certo et fideli comitatu evocari curabo. Si conditiones apud Caesarem meis desiderijs non arriserint, ad Coloniensem me convertam. Et si nec ibi, ad Batavos. Pollicitus enim est Princeps MAURITIUS, se apud ordines confoederatos meâ de caussa sedulo acturum. Idem et BARNEVELDIUS addixit se facturum, quamprimum ex Anglia redierit, ubi nunc Legatum agit. Sed FRANCISCUS, qui cum utroque oretenus hac de re egit, ut secreta omnia haberent, nec quidpiam Ordinibus proponerent, eos tacite monuit, donec de mea voluntate certi quid resciscerent. Aequum enim esse, ut Imperatori meam operam honoris caussa primum offeram. Quam si renuerit, tunc demum de alijs medijs cogitandum. In Angliam quibuscunque tandem pollicitationibus allectus vix iturus sum. Nam et mihi mores gentis suspecti sunt, praeter id quod Reginae vetulae obitus magnos motus ciebit peregrinis maxime formidabiles et perniciosos. Quae omnia propterea fusius tibi scribo, ut de meis consilijs mearumque rerum statu te (quod non dubito) sollicitum certiorem facerem.

Quod literae Electoris Soceri Regis non sunt exhibitae, parum moror. Nam et parum habent ponderis, nimisque Politice et aulice conscriptae. Non amplius mare Balthicum transibunt. Videbo tamen, quomodo apud Electorem et Electorissam, qui mihi favere videri volunt, id civiliter excusem. At libros quos Regi destinavi, maluissem oblatos esse certis de causis: non tamen quod ego aut sperem aut cupiam, Regem eos magni facturum, qui alias videtur literis non admodum delectari. OLIGERUM etiam nostrum instruxi, quomodo is eos statim dono obtineret, ne in aliorum potestatem devenirent; non quod inimicorum cavillationes et malevolas interpretationes verear, talibus satis assuetus, sed ne ij, quos indignos reputo, meis gaudeant laboribus. Quocirca eos adhuc Regi per OLIGERUM ............ uti et illi scripsi. Si tamen id facere non audeat ............ ita tamen ut libri OLIGERO, si Rex eos (u ti ........... alij cedant. Habebit RAMELIUS, si voluerit, altera Exemplaria. Ad caetera quae scribis, quia me parum concernunt, non respondeo. Id saltem superaddam, si PETRUS BRAHE cognatus meus, quem fratris loco semper dilexi, propterea corbizatori et magno meo bono foedifrago isti valetudinem primogeniti sui filij, de cuius indole multa praeclara inaudieram (adeo ut sperârim eum familiae nostrae, modo disciplinae isthic aliquem locum mererentur, decus futurum) concredidit, eumque sic amisit, proh dolor (cum plus saepe periculi a Medico quam morbo, praesertim tam vulgari) certe male pro se ultus est iniuriam, si qua erat, quod cum CRASSÆO bis vel ter Rostochij locutus sum, et nullum tamen aliud officium aut operam ipsi praestiti. Neque enim ab omni humanitate tam alienus esse poteram, ut tergiversatione eius alloquij satis superque afflicti afflictionem adderem, qui commiseratione potius quam odio dignus est. Et si Salvatoris nostri monitis auscultaret PETRUS, culpam improvisam tam enixe deprecanti Dei saltem et conscientiae caussâ remitteret. Sed fit ut plurimum id quod infers; volumus nobis maxima a Deo debita condonari; nos autem proximo ne minima quidem. Vere igitur nostri Deus misereatur, atque hanc etiam obstinationem, et ab eius voluntate aversionem clementer ignoscat. Cui te tuamque ELSULAM et liberos omnesque tuos commendo. Vale, et prolixitatem hanc boni consule. Dedi tibi sic exemplum, ut et tu solito fusius ad me scriberes. Ignoramus enim an postea, cum longius disiuncti fuerimus, ea dabitur, quae nunc, literas inter nos commutandi oportunitas. Quae de Caesare et Coloniense, meisque rebus nunc indicavi, sorori meae, ESCHILLO BILLEO et ROSÆCRANTZIO nostro secreto impertire, nec ullis alijs. Iamque iterum unâ cum ijs caeterisque Amicis, qui forte (uti fit) nunc mihi de multis unus et alter erunt, quam felicissime vale.

Dabantur Wandesburgo die 20. Augusti st. vet. Anno 98.
Tuus TYCHO BRAHE,
manu propria.

To the most excellent and outstanding man, Lord Niels Krag, Doctor of Both Laws, Royal Historian and distinguished Professor of the Copenhagen Academy, his one intimate and sincerely beloved friend.

Heartiest greeting.

Eight days ago Mule first returned here, and brought your letter, most excellent and most loving Sir — much later than I had hoped. He was indeed detained by Eske and by Accius, but not prevented from signifying meanwhile by letter the causes of the delay — which I had seriously charged him to do. Nor were such secrets or mysteries to be written by him that it mattered into whose hands at last those letters came. And — what displeases me most of all — he has returned with the chief business, namely the money, unaccomplished. Which, however, I impute not so much to him as to the two named men, and to Accius rather than to Eske. For the latter sent his servant to Flensburg to obtain two thousand joachims there on my account, but in vain. The former shuffles over the payment of the sum owed, and asks for a postponement on account of his dead brother's debts and I know not what other expenses — although he is in good circumstances, and now rejoices in being related by marriage to the Chancellor, whom long avarice and tenacity have made rich enough. How then can Axel be in straits? But I again press for the restitution of the whole sum; for I will have no more of my money stuck fast in Denmark, especially in that hateful affinity. I have indeed written to this Accius not incautiously hitherto. For although a not obscure familiarity has subsisted between him and me, beyond the tie of blood (which in this age has degenerated into water even among brothers themselves — and so I reckon it the less), yet we know each other well enough, and have found that there is more in his words than in his deeds. Had he handled the matter seriously — my own brothers and the rest of my kinsmen keeping silent — there would perhaps have been preserved, both for the Kingdom and the King and his Father, that honour of which you write. For he, almost alone, more rightly knows what and how much I accomplished on that island — partly because of the nearness of his dwelling, while he was Governor of Landskrona, partly because he was wont sometimes to visit me, and to inspect those things, and to hear me reporting on them. Yet to the letters sealed to me, which he had jointly signed, he kept silent no less than the rest — greatly indeed to the honour of all of them, and to be commended to posterity in the histories! O ancient faith! O obsolete constancy of the old Danes! But it now goes just as our Germany lately wrote to your Denmark:

Each man cares for himself alone,
not for others, and fears for himself, if another's cause be approved,
however just it be.

But would that this could be said of Accius alone — [a thing] which overflows to very many others, who ought to have been more closely bound to me than he. It would be a good thing, if there were none worse than he. If he furnishes not much good, yet he does not harm as the rest do. And that is a great grace in these times. And he even seems sometimes to console me, and to give hope of better fortune — which, however, I neither expect nor seek in your Denmark. I should reckon myself well dealt with, if some of you could refrain from persecuting an absent and not lightly injured man with undeserved cavils and calumnies — though I heed these trifles no more than Cynthia [the Moon], on high and shining with full light, [heeds] the folly of the barking dogs, which even generous horses pass by, neglecting them, so long as they do not actually bite. For then they do not even kick. A fine consolation indeed has that sly and Machiavellian confederacy among certain of your Gymnasiarchs got from this — that that old member of my household, who was promoted by me to better things, now an ungrateful cuckoo, has shared with them the scurrilous and most lying writing of that savage Ursus. For so they have wherewith to flatter themselves, as though over a thing well done, that their standard-bearer was the very (though hidden) cause why the fatherland now lacks me — a slight loss indeed, if those revilings and calumnies were to obtain the force of geometric truth! If that splendid distributor of libellous books were still sitting in the madhouse at Augsburg — where I once ransomed him for 18 crowns, when he had stolen wine — then he should sit there until he could recite the Book by heart, and perhaps eat it up for his improvement, before I should ransom him out again; so that he too might thus, from this incorporation, better learn than before to lie, to brawl, to deceive, to perjure, and the more such virtues with which that book teems, and of which he himself is not ignorant. That impure writing the Universities of Wittenberg, Leipzig, Helmstedt, Rostock, and many in Germany detest, and they execrate its author as no longer worthy of the fellowship of good men (though among some of your countrymen he is sung and praised — fine folk all together!); which they have also left attested to me in letters. The Academy of Freiburg-im-Breisgau, indeed, signified to me not obscurely that it would have proceeded, as he deserved, against that Poetaster who had composed and appended that insulting distich (if he were the author, and not rather the ursine beast), had he still been among the living; for I am told he died four years ago in the utmost poverty. So too princely men abominate such impudence. At Brunswick the author suffered, for that cause, a rebuff in an office he sought in the school. Duke Adolf of Holstein, as soon as he saw those dirges, immediately, with added execration, tore up all the leaves, and threw the book to his Chancellor, Doctor Jonathan Gutslovius, to be consumed by fire — which was done. Our Rantzau was no less moved, having looked into only a few words, calling down all curses on the author, and judging that so unspeakable a deed deserved, according to Job, to be visited with capital punishment. Yet behold, some of your learned men applaud this, see it in their bosom [with secret delight], and congratulate themselves on this book — lest anything should be left which the ungrateful fatherland, or rather certain ingrates in it, might dare against me. But all this, with everything else, I count as a straw, nor do I judge that calumniator worthy of an answer, but rather of punishment according to the statutes of the Empire — which I am confident I shall obtain. Then at last let his applauders triumph. One should rightly not boast of the field before the harvest is gathered in. But let both that one and these others fare well — unworthy that I should write these things about them, not without harm to the paper, which that frenzied writing has injured, but not our reputation, to speak with our Scaliger. Wherefore I now forbear to say more of these things.

From Scaliger, whom I just mentioned, my Frans Tengnagel — returned two days ago, his business well dispatched — brought me a letter. For it was high time, at last, to speak of good folk, and let the others go. That letter is written most learnedly and humanely (as are all the things of that supreme man), of which I share a copy with you. What he recounts about the diverse Poles of the World and of the Equator, and about the Pole-star, the progress of the fixed stars, the quantity of the year, Ptolemy's gross hallucination from the rising of the Dog-star [Sirius] on the same Julian day after so many centuries have elapsed, and the like — these are less consistent. For even good Homer sometimes nods. On which matter, when I have gained leisure to write back, I have resolved to admonish him candidly. He values my work most highly, as is clear not only from this letter, but also from the very renewed Emendation of Times lately published, in which he exalts my observations of the equinoxes — which he calls "Danish" (as is well deserved by certain Danes, that they should be so called — !) — far above the findings of that Hipparchus (which, however, he prefers to the traditions of Ptolemy and of all the ancients). Whether one should rather stand here by his judgment, who weighs and understands the matter, than by that of the Master of the Court among you — who is utterly raw in these things and dreams of nothing but money, and does not blush to prefer the labours of peasants to Astronomy and Jurisprudence — I leave to be estimated by you, and by others who look into the business without cataract. He was so gladdened when Tengnagel approached him with my things that he leapt up for joy, and offered every office of kindness, and even money, should he be destitute of it (which otherwise is most rare), and wished for nothing more than that I should be summoned by the Dutch. And so he inclined less to the Emperor's side. And when Frans had related to him some things about the promises of Prince Maurice — who also wrote to me in German most kindly — and of Barneveldt, Scaliger added that their counsels are very slow; but that he would urge them to act in good time, and to labour to draw me thither. For by all means I ought to yield to the Dutch, not to others.

With what clemency and kindness the Elector of Cologne (who in this matter, like you, rather upholds the Emperor's side) received him, he cannot sufficiently express in words. He stayed with him into the fourth week, called almost every day for some hours to his private colloquy, so much so that he repeatedly turned away the interruptions of his councillors for that cause; he inquired much, answered much, diligently turned over my books, praised them; and whatever writings of mine Frans had with him, he took to himself, restored few, and those only when copied — so that he would not even spare the poems composed in my honour; he weighed the occasions of my departure from Denmark, not without wonder, and having asked Frans how much it was that I had yearly in revenues there — Frans answering (and, for the honour of the King and Kingdom, somewhat augmenting the sum — it was enough that he was your countryman!) that it reached four thousand thalers — the Prince added these very words: "What? Should one let such a man leave the country for so paltry a sum? It is a shame. A lord gambles away more in one evening. Money one can always get, but such people not always." At length he himself both composed, with exquisite diligence, and wrote in his own hand, the promotory letters to the Emperor — unknown to both his Chancellor and his Secretary — the tenor of which, together with those he likewise gave to Barvitius, the Emperor's supreme Secretary (who was raised by him to this height of dignity, and still has yearly stipends [from him]), I think you have received. He also wrote to me a succinct and learned [letter], which he himself dictated to his chamberlain in the morning in bed, because Frans was hastening his departure. Of these too you have a copy here, with those he gave at the same time to Lord Rantzau. It pleased him exceedingly that I had resolved henceforth to live in Germany; and he said he would lay down all the dearest things he had [to ensure] that the Emperor would pursue me and my studies with most clement and supreme favour, and liberally maintain them; and he added that, if the Emperor — beyond all his expectation — should prove difficult in it, then he would himself accomplish it at his own expense, and altogether take care that I should not go outside Germany, or return to the Danes; and he gave thanks to God that I was now nearer him; and since I could not visit him as soon as I wished (which, however, he greatly desired), he said he would send one of his intimate nobles here to me, before I betook myself into Upper Germany, who should deal with me about certain secrets pertaining to the abstruse arts [alchemy], and report back to him what he had learned. And, to dispatch the matter in few words (since to narrate everything would be too prolix), both at other times and especially when he bade farewell to the departing Frans, holding him long by the hand, he concluded with these very words: "Tell the good honest man many good things on my behalf, my gracious greeting; and besides, that he shall surely hold me for one of the best friends he has, and that I in turn will hold him, and act toward him, no otherwise than if he were my own brother, and as if I had known him all my life and from youth. So well does he please me. And as soon as I shall come to Prague, I will visit him, be it wherever he is to be found in His Imperial Majesty's domain." — so certain was he that he would find me there. And let these things be indicated in few words concerning that excellent Prince's clement favour toward me. From which you will easily gather the rest, and reason out, that I am not held so hateful and contemptible in Germany as among some in Denmark. Nay, I see that your countrymen even now cast off all care of me, nor are solicitous that I be restored there without my disadvantage. For if they wait until I shall become a suppliant to them, they will wait until the Greek Kalends (as you rightly augur). I should indeed reckon nothing better than to be zealous for the honour of my fatherland and my King — even for the sake of that excellent Frederik of most praiseworthy memory, this [King's] father, who deserved excellently well of me. But what am I to do, when they have spurned me, despised me, afflicted me with no slight loss and many injuries, persecuted me with hatred and envy, and were willing not only not to promote the restoration of the art which I have so long cultivated, and which almost the whole world demands of me, but even denied its maintenance, and withdrew the supports I had before? If I had to live in the manner of the other nobles, and had not undertaken higher things to be carried through, I should have dragged out my remaining years inglorious there, born to consume Bacchus and Ceres. These and the like, I pray you, indicate to our Ramel — who was not ungrateful to you for the troubles of the journey (which I praise), and whom I hold for a sincere Friend — lest, asked about the causes of my departure, he should answer that he knows none, but that I went away thence of my own accord, without any necessity; as yesterday the Noble Lord Wilhelm Slawata, Free Baron of Chlum and Košumberk, visiting me here on Rantzau's commendation, reported to me that Ramel had so answered him when he inquired. Politely indeed, and politicly; but whether a friend's reasoning had cause enough so to do — [a friend] who is thus tacitly insinuated to have admitted something lightly and without just reasons, those being passed over who were the [real] cause — I would not say. That his most Serene Majesty the King, whenever mention of me falls, shows no sign of a malevolent mind — as you write, and as Ramel also intimated to Claus Mule — I both believe and gladly hear. For he has no cause to wish ill to one who never offended him by word or deed. It suffices me to be silent, and to dissemble what I have suffered. If the two now nearest the King — to whom I more truly impute the causes of my emigration — still secretly labour at hatred of me, it is according to the proverb: Whom you have wronged, you hate; and so [it is] nothing new. Which too, intent from on high on better things, I disregard.

But although throughout almost the whole summer I have been assured of the Emperor's most clement favour and inclination toward me, and likewise of certain other Princes', yet I was unwilling to move anything, or to determine anything certain about my setting out hence, before [lacuna] returned here, so that what already [lacuna] — namely that none there, or from [lacuna] (of him himself I say nothing, who attends less to such things) — would take up the care of my affairs, even for the fatherland's sake, let alone for mine. And this I think is now sufficiently demonstrated, so that I am the more excusable before all sensible and sincere men in betaking myself elsewhere, the matter so pressing. I have resolved therefore, by the favour of the Divine clemency, as soon as I have my carriages (which I am fitting out) and the rest ready, to set out for Prague and to test His Imperial Majesty's most clement will toward me — leaving here meanwhile my womenfolk, to whom I shall either return before winter (if God so see fit), or, if the Emperor wish it, and the convenience of the place offered please me, I shall have them summoned thither with a sure and faithful escort added. If the conditions with the Emperor do not smile upon my wishes, I shall turn to the Cologne [Elector]. And if not there either, to the Dutch. For Prince Maurice has promised that he will deal diligently on my account with the confederated Estates; and Barneveldt has likewise pledged to do the same, as soon as he returns from England, where he is now acting as Legate. But Frans, who dealt with both of them on this matter by word of mouth, quietly admonished them to keep everything secret, and to propose nothing to the Estates, until they should learn something certain of my will. For it is fair that I should first offer my service to the Emperor for honour's sake; and if he should refuse it, then at last other means must be thought of. To England, by whatever promises enticed, I shall scarcely go; for the manners of the nation are suspect to me, besides that the death of the old Queen will stir great commotions, most fearful and pernicious especially to foreigners. All which I therefore write to you more fully, that I might make you — solicitous, as I doubt not you are — more certain of my counsels and the state of my affairs.

That the letters of the Elector, the King's father-in-law, were not presented, I little mind; for they have little weight, and are too politicly and courtly composed. They shall cross the Baltic Sea no more. I shall see, however, how I may civilly excuse it with the Elector and Electress, who wish to seem to favour me. But the books which I destined for the King, I should have preferred to have been offered, for certain reasons — not, however, that I either hope or desire that the King will value them greatly, who otherwise seems not much delighted by letters. I have also instructed our Holger how he might at once obtain them as a gift, lest they fall into the power of others — not that I fear enemies' cavils and malevolent interpretations, being well enough used to such, but lest those whom I reckon unworthy should enjoy my labours. Wherefore [I have arranged that] they still [be presented] to the King through Holger [lacuna], as I have also written to him. If, however, he dare not do it [lacuna], yet so that the books fall to Holger, if the King [lacuna] [or to] others. Ramel shall have, if he wish, the other copies. To the rest of what you write, since it little concerns me, I do not reply. This one thing I will add: if Peter Brahe, my kinsman — whom I have always loved in a brother's stead — on that account entrusted to that corbizator and great breaker-of-faith toward my good [name] the health of his firstborn son (of whose disposition I had heard many excellent things, so that I hoped he would be an ornament to our family, if only the disciplines there deserved any place), and so lost him — alas (since there is often more danger from the Physician than from the disease, especially so common a one) — certainly he ill avenged for himself the injury, if any there was, in that I spoke with Crassaeus two or three times at Rostock, and yet rendered him no other office or service. For I could not be so estranged from all humanity as, by a refusal of conversation, to add affliction to one already more than enough afflicted, who is worthy of compassion rather than of hatred. And if Peter would hearken to our Saviour's admonitions, he would, for God's and conscience's sake at least, remit an unforeseen fault to one begging pardon so earnestly. But it mostly happens as you infer: we wish our own greatest debts to be forgiven us by God, but [will forgive] our neighbour not even the least. Truly, then, may God have mercy on us, and clemently forgive this obstinacy too, and this turning-away from His will. To whom I commend you, and your Elsula, and your children, and all yours. Farewell, and take this prolixity in good part. I have thus given you an example, that you too might write to me more fully than usual. For we do not know whether afterwards, when we shall be parted farther off, there will be given the opportunity of exchanging letters between us that there is now. What I have now indicated about the Emperor and the Cologne [Elector], and my affairs, share secretly with my sister, with Eske Bille, and with our Rosenkrantz — and with no others. And now again, together with them and the rest of my Friends, who perhaps (as it happens) will now be, of many, but one or two to me, fare most happily.

Given at Wandsbek, the 20th of August, Old Style, the year [15]98.
Your Tycho Brahe,
with his own hand.

I have added, besides what I sent last, two further copies of a letter of Thaddaeus [Hagecius], which I received three days ago, so that from this you may see how earnestly they look for me.

When Johannes Styggius, who is now at Hamburg, had at last learned from whatever quarter that certain of the professors among you make much of that notorious writing about me, and that Olburgensis had procured it for them, being unable to bear so great an injustice, he directed a letter to him [Olburgensis], deliberately written so as to appear to approve and praise this deed — just as the author of the book which they call "Beinkorf" commends the Pontiffs while feeling the contrary. Mulaeus sends you the copy which Styggius himself handed over, to be shared with you. But keep it all secret. Do not communicate anything of this matter except with our Oliger [Rosenkrantz], or also with [my] sister. And meanwhile see to it, quietly, that inquiry is made how this letter has been received by the faction to which Olburgensis adheres. I ask that you be willing to further this German amanuensis of mine in the business he has to transact there in my name, and to press him to return here as soon as possible — within fourteen days, if it can be managed at all — and that you send back with him that same notorious writing. For I cannot do without it, on account of the praises of me it proclaims, since I have but this one copy, and its author sees to it that it is circulated not openly but in secret.

I have added a German copy of the reasons for my departure from Denmark, which you may, on some occasion, give to Lord Ramelius to read; so that, if he is unaware what grounds I had for leaving, and has forgotten what I said to him at Rostock, he may learn it from this and call it back to memory; otherwise perhaps I shall set down these very matters more fully and at greater length another time. Meanwhile, keep this paper, once you have it back from Ramelius, secretly by you, as a record of a thing well managed — since you are a historian. For scarcely anywhere else will you find truer documents; though you may not dare relate the history of Hven, especially of its later years, because truth breeds hatred. Yet others, perhaps, will do it. For this transformation is not unknown, nor is anger elsewhere as silent as it is among us. Many indeed write and speak of the matter, whom I would rather see silent.

  • Edition of record. Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia vol. VIII (Epistolae); the letter first appeared in Tychonis Brahei et ad eum doctorum virorum epistolae ab anno 1588. MS witness: A = Copenhagen, Det Kongelige Bibliotek, a copy (Afskrift) in an exemplar of P. J. Resen's Inscriptiones Hafnienses (1668). The witness is defective at two points (lacunae).
  • Date / place. Dateline "die 20. Augusti st. vet. Anno 98" — explicitly Old Style (stylo veteri), from Wandsbek (the Rantzau estate near Hamburg), Tycho's residence in 1598. Same Wandsbek season as brahe-t_15980810001 (to Holger Rosenkrantz, 10 Aug 1598).
  • Recipient. Niels Krag (Nicolaus Cragius), Doctor of Both Laws, Danish Royal Historian and Copenhagen professor — "his one intimate and sincerely beloved friend"; his wife is "Elsula" (Else).
  • The patronage-auction (the letter's spine). A vivid record of the bidding for Tycho among three powers: (1) Emperor Rudolf II (whose side both Krag and the Elector of Cologne favour); (2) the Elector of Cologne (Ernst of Bavaria), who received Tengnagel for four weeks, wrote autograph promotory letters to the Emperor and to Barvitius (the supreme imperial secretary, the Elector's own protégé), and pledged to fund Tycho himself if the Emperor proved difficult — his outburst "Should one let such a man leave the country for so paltry a sum? … money one can always get, but such people not always" is one of the most-quoted period testimonials to Tycho; (3) the Dutch — Joseph Scaliger (at Leiden) pressing Prince Maurice of Nassau and Johan van Oldenbarnevelt (then Legate in England) to summon Tycho. Tycho's stated order of preference: offer himself first to the Emperor for honour's sake; then Cologne; then the Dutch; England he rules out (suspect manners; the coming upheaval at "the old Queen['s]" — Elizabeth I's — death). This is the same campaign as brahe-t_15980706001 (Tengnagel).
  • The Ursus libel, received across Europe. A catalogue of revulsion at Ursus's (Nicolaus Reimers Bär's) De astronomicis hypothesibus (1597): detested by the universities of Wittenberg, Leipzig, Helmstedt, Rostock; the Freiburg-im-Breisgau academy would have punished the "Poetaster" who wrote the insulting distich (dead four years, in poverty — if he, not "the ursine beast," was its author); a rebuff at Brunswick; Duke Adolf of Holstein tearing up the leaves and having his Chancellor Dr. Jonas Gutslovius burn the book; Henrik Rantzau judging it a capital crime "according to Job." Tycho's celebrated vignette: he had once ransomed Ursus from the madhouse at Augsburg for 18 crowns after Ursus stole wine, and wishes him back there to "eat the book for his improvement." Only "certain Gymnasiarchs" (school-heads) in Copenhagen relish it — having got it from "that old member of my household … now an ungrateful cuckoo" (an unnamed former famulus of Tycho's who passed Ursus's tract to the Copenhagen faction). Continuous with the Ursus material in brahe-t_15991209001 (Kepler) and brahe-t_15990918001 (Vedel).
  • Scaliger & De emendatione temporum. Tengnagel brought a learned letter from Joseph Scaliger; Tycho notes that Scaliger's recently republished Opus de emendatione temporum (2nd ed., 1598) exalts Tycho's equinox observations — which Scaliger calls "Danish" — above Hipparchus (whom Scaliger in turn prefers to Ptolemy). Tycho judges parts of Scaliger's letter (on the world- and equator-poles, the Pole-star, precession, year-length, Ptolemy's Sirius-rising error) "less consistent — for even good Homer sometimes nods," and means to admonish him candidly. He contrasts Scaliger's competent judgment with that of the Danish Master of the Court (Curiae Magister) — "utterly raw … dreams of nothing but money … prefers peasants' labour to Astronomy" — almost certainly Christoffer Valkendorf, the Lord Steward/Treasurer and Tycho's chief Danish antagonist (left unnamed in the letter).
  • Persons named. Mule (Mulaeus / Claudius Mulaeus = Claus Mule, Tycho's agent); Eske Bille (Eschillus); "Accius" (Axel) — a wealthy kinsman/debtor delaying repayment, now allied by marriage to the Chancellor (identity not certain in the text — most likely Axel Gyldenstierne or Axel Brahe; the Danish aside "How can Axel be in straits?" gives the vernacular name); the Danish Chancellor (unnamed); Joseph Scaliger; Frans Tengnagel (Franciscus Tengnaglius, Tycho's envoy); the Elector of Cologne (Ernst of Bavaria); Barvitius (Barbitius, imperial supreme secretary); Prince Maurice of Nassau (Mauritius) and Oldenbarnevelt (Barneveldius); Henrik Rantzau (Ranzovius); Duke Adolf of Holstein (so named — possibly a slip for the reigning Johann Adolf of Holstein-Gottorp) and his chancellor Jonas Gutslovius; Wilhelm Slawata, Baron of Chlum and Košumberk (the Bohemian noble — later a victim of the 1618 Prague Defenestration — who visited Tycho at Wandsbek); Henrik Ramel (Ramelius, Danish Senator); King Christian IV and his father Frederik II ("of most praiseworthy memory"); "the two now nearest the King" whom Tycho blames for his exile (unnamed — the Chancellor and the Steward); Peter Brahe (Petrus Brahe, kinsman who lost his firstborn to a physician); Crassaeus (a physician at Rostock, the "corbizator"/faith-breaker — unidentified); the Elector of Brandenburg (Joachim Friedrich, the King's father-in-law) and the Electress; Holger Rosenkrantz (Oligerus); Tycho's sister (Sophie Brahe).
  • Verse, proverbs & tags. The cited distich "Curat se quilibet unum…" ("Each cares for himself alone…") Tycho attributes to a German verse "lately written to your Denmark" (a pasquinade; not securely sourced). "bonus interdum dormitat Homerus" — Horace, Ars Poetica 359. "ad calendas Graecas" — "at the Greek Kalends," i.e. never (Augustus, per Suetonius). "Quem laeseris, oderis" — "whom you have wronged, you hate" (proverbial; cf. Seneca, Tacitus). "Bacchum et Cererem consumere natus" — echo of Horace, Epistles 1.2.27 (fruges consumere nati). The Moon-and-barking-dogs image (Cynthia … canum oblatrantium insipientiam) is a classical commonplace of serene contempt. Danish asides (kept and translated): "Huor kand da Axel staa til at trenge?"; "Det var en gott, om der var ingen værre end hand"; "Och det er en stor naade i denne tid"; the Augsburg-madhouse passage; "got folk tilhobe"; "Man skulle med rette ikke rose af Marken, førend tørvet blev holdet" ("one should not boast of the field before the harvest is in"); "det var nok han var eders landmand"; "som vel forskyldt er…". German quotations (kept and translated): the Elector of Cologne's two speeches.
  • Lacunae (the crux). Two passages are defective in the witness: (1) in the deliberation on his departure — "antequam … huc reversus, ut id quod iam … nullos videlicet isthic vel ex … " (the sense: he would decide nothing before [his agent] returned, having learned that none in Denmark would take up his cause); (2) in the books-for-the-King passage — "eos adhuc Regi per OLIGERUM … uti et illi scripsi. Si tamen id facere non audeat … ita tamen ut libri OLIGERO, si Rex eos (uti …) alij cedant" (the sense: the books are to reach the King via Holger, but in any case fall to Holger). Reproduced as dots in the source and marked [lacuna] in the translation; the surrounding sense is recoverable, the exact wording is not.
  • Postscript to Niels Krag, Wandsbek, 20 August 1598 (O.S.). Niels Krag (d. 1602) was the Danish crown's historiographer; the postscript is addressed to him explicitly in that capacity — Tycho invokes it ("cum sis historicus") when entrusting him with a private memorandum of the "reasons for my departure from Denmark," evidently hoping that Krag, or "others" after him, would one day write the true "historia Huennensem" (history of Hven) that current politics made too dangerous to publish ("veritas odium parit," truth breeds hatred). This is a first-person statement of Tycho's own historiographical anxiety about how his Hven years would be remembered — not stated so directly elsewhere in the already-published body of this letter.
  • Enclosures. Besides two further copies of letters from Thaddaeus Hagecius (Tadeáš Hájek, the Prague imperial physician and Tycho's chief advocate at Rudolf II's court, urging him eastward — "how earnestly they expect me"; the register of Tycho's correspondence confirms Hagecius as a close, longstanding correspondent, with a letter from him to Tycho as early as 1576), Tycho encloses (1) a copy of a defamatory printed piece ("famosum istud scriptum") about him then circulating among professors in Rosenkrantz's circle, said to have been procured by one "Olburgensis" and forwarded via intermediaries Johannes Styggius (at Hamburg) and Mulaeus; and (2) a German-language account of his own reasons for leaving Denmark, which he authorizes Krag to let "Lord Ramelius" read, to refresh what Tycho had already told him "at Rostock."
  • The "Bienenkorb" allusion. Tycho compares Styggius's letter of feigned approval to "the author of the book they call 'Beinkorf'" — i.e. the Bienenkorb der Heiligen Römischen Kirche ("Beehive"), the well-known mock-encomium that "praises" the Papacy by pretending to hold the opposite of the true reformed view. Corpus (Tycho's printed Opera Omnia, vol. 8) confirms this exact passage in the letter's textus receptus, so the allusion is textually secure.
  • Layered secrecy. Every enclosure carries explicit confidentiality instructions: the defamatory tract and the Hagecius letters are for Rosenkrantz ("Oligerus") and Tycho's sister [Sophie Brahe] only; Krag is told to make quiet inquiry into how Styggius's letter was received "by the faction to which Olburgensis adheres," and to keep the returned Ramelius document "secretly with you... as a memorial of a thing well managed."
  • Textual note. The printed Opera Omnia (Dreyer's edition, vol. 8) reads "Pontificios contrarium sentiendo commendat," where the recovered segment supplied for this translation reads "Pontifices" — a minor one-word variant, flagged here for the collator and left untouched per house rule.
  • Open identifications. Johannes Styggius, "Olburgensis," Mulaeus, and Ramelius/Rameel could not be securely matched to named figures in the works consulted (including Christianson's On Tycho's Island prosopography); "Olburgensis" ("the man of Aalborg") most likely designates a Danish churchman or academic hostile to Tycho, tied to the pamphlet war that followed his 1597 departure, but this remains an educated guess, not a finding.
  • Correction. Five readings have been corrected against Dreyer, TBOO VIII pp.94, 98-99: the Chancellor's name restored as 'Jonathan' (not 'Jonas') Gutslovius, matching the Latin's already-correct 'IONATHAM'; 'cameraque' corrected to 'caeteraque' ('and the rest', not 'and my chamber'), with the English fabricated 'chamber' removed accordingly; 'mactandum' corrected to 'mulctandum' (standard 'poena mulctare' idiom); the signature's letter-transposition 'BRHAE' corrected to 'BRAHE'; and 'in lis' corrected to 'in ijs'. The latter three carry no translation impact.

5 defects found:
- mistranslation (medium). IONATHAM is the accusative of Ionathas (Jonathan), not of Ionas (Jonah/Jonas); the chancellor's name is mistranslated. At: “his Chancellor, Doctor Jonas Gutslovius”.
- text error (medium). Dreyer prints "cæteraque" ("and the rest"), not "cameraque" ("and my chamber"); our transcribed original's corruption produced a fabricated detail ("chamber") that the translation then rendered literally. Dreyer prints: quamprimum currus meos, quos adorno, cæteraque parata habuero (TBOO VIII p.98).
- text error (low). Dreyer's standard legal idiom is "poena mulctare" (to punish with the penalty of); the transcribed original has "mactandum." Sense (death penalty) survives in translation either way. Dreyer prints: et capitis pœna mulctandum ſecundum IOB tam infandum facinus cenſens (TBOO VIII p.94).
- text error (low). Letter-transposition typo in the transcribed original's signature line (BRHAE for BRAHE); translation correctly reads "Brahe" regardless. Dreyer prints: Tuus TYCHO BRAHE, manu propria. (TBOO VIII p.99).
- text error (low). Dreyer reads "ijs" ("those things"); the transcribed original has "lis" ("lawsuit"), which does not fit the sentence. Translation follows the correct sense ("the business he has to transact") regardless. Dreyer prints: hunc amanuenſem meum Germanicum promovere in ijs, quæ iſthic meo nomine expedienda habet (TBOO VIII p.99).

Repaired. All five defects were verified directly against the Dreyer page images: pp.94 for the Gutslovium name and 'mulctandum', p.98 for 'caeteraque', and p.99 for 'BRAHE' and 'in ijs'. Every reading is clearly legible in the type facsimile and unambiguous. No crux remains in this letter.
