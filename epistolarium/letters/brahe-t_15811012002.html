Tycho Brahe to Thaddaeus Hagecius.

Greeting.

A whole year is now nearly elapsed, dearest Master Doctor Thaddaeus, most distinguished man, since I last wrote to you — and that by way of Paulus Wittich of Wrocław, as he was returning home from here. But because in the meantime I have received no letter from you — when that mutual goodwill with which we once embraced one another at the Diet of Regensburg, and that affection toward your friends which is inborn in you and constant, by no means admits so long a delay in writing back — I could not but suspect that those letters had not reached your hands through the man to whom they had been entrusted. And what makes me now incline rather to believe just that is your little book on the latest comet, which Bartholomaeus Scultetus — bound to me by an old friendship from Leipzig — saw to it, two days ago, that it should be conveyed to me by way of a certain man who was bringing us from there [Leipzig] some spagyric [chemical] vessels we had hired [or: ordered]. For since in this writing you seem to want my opinion concerning the parallaxes of both comets, derived from sure observations — an opinion which nevertheless at that earlier time I disclosed to you plainly, and (as I hope) no less truly, in brief — I judge that it cannot have happened that those letters were delivered to you, as I had hoped. For I cannot persuade myself that you — a man both eager for these matters and understanding of them — so suddenly forgot the things I had signified in that letter (even if no occasion for writing back had meanwhile presented itself), or that you wholly neglected them.

Now the things which at that time I unlocked to you in that letter about the last two comets, from our observations, and which I now again set forth for your judgement, are roughly of this kind: In the comet which began to shine in the year [15]77, from all our observations — which were sought out with great diligence by means of various instruments exquisitely wrought from solid metal and exactly capable of [reading] single minutes [of arc], and were reduced to numbers by a varied method of demonstration — I never found that parallax, the one that occurs at the horizon, to have reached at most a third part of one degree, but very often it proved much less than this, as I shall sufficiently demonstrate to the understanding, in its proper place, by various arguments. And whereas in the little book then published about the same comet you had granted a parallax of 5 degrees [partium], and on that account had judged that comet to be sublunar, I warned you as a friend in those same letters that the method of Regiomontanus for investigating parallaxes — which you assert you used — although demonstrable enough, is nonetheless less adequate and suited to so subtle a business. For many things which are truly demonstrated speculatively, if they be carried over into practice and the mechanical handling of the senses, will stray very far from the mark aimed at — especially where, as happens in this case, the things that are greatest are investigated from the least. Nay, even if that speculation of Regiomontanus had had some place here, and an interval of time taken exactly enough had agreed with altitudes and azimuths duly given (in each of which, however — even though great and exquisite instruments be at hand, which yet are seldom ready — to be captured exactly with the precision that is fitting, how much diligence is needed, and how much difficulty befalls those not well practised, no one can persuade himself save one who has long and assiduously laboured in this mechanical wrestling-school of astronomy) — nonetheless, from so narrow an interval of time, which you assumed to be only 18 minutes [scrupulorum], it was impossible sensibly to discern parallaxes that in so little time vary imperceptibly. And so, while it seemed [good] to evade the hindrances of the proper motion by the brevity of time, the whole matter was reduced to a kind of impossibility. We, to be sure, have more than twenty times resolved into numbers that method of Regiomontanus for discerning parallaxes, from various and sufficiently sure observations, with no slight labour (as you know). But nowhere did we find a parallax as great as the Moon admits even when most remote from the earth — and very often none at all. And therefore I could not subscribe to your verdicts, even when I had used the same method (although less adequate to so very subtle a business) as you did.

Nor was it even possible to confirm the parallax by that very thing which you add for its corroboration — namely from the [comet's] culmination [coeli mediatione] together with the bright star of the Vulture [Altair]. For even if their simultaneous transit across the meridian, which then took place in the daytime, had been observable, no one will by any reasoning gather a parallax from this, unless more data were present — as through carelessness you taught, less considerately, amid your other occupations, in your Dialexis. But that the parallax of that comet not only was not so great, but was not even much perceptible, is attested by your own observations, in which you found that, over the distances of the comet from the mouth of Pegasus, with several hours intervening, [the distance] nevertheless varied very little — so that in truth on that account it can be proved, even by your own observations (to which, however, you were admitting contrary [conclusions]), that the parallax, if there was any, was tiny, and that the comet far exceeded [was far beyond] the orb of the Moon. These things, I say, I wrote out to you at that time about the earlier comet.

But I see that in this latest writing you agree with us, and that — having considered the contradictions which at that time you were not noticing — you have recalled your opinion and have found more inwardly the kernel of the matter to be investigated; on which I heartily congratulate you and the lovers of truth (who could be drawn by an abuse of your authority, and by less exquisite observations and demonstrations, to subscribe to the false opinion of the Peripatetics about comets). As for Scultetus's demonstrations, which inferred nearly the same parallax as you, since they rest on an uncertain foundation and are built upon false data (for, to say nothing of other matters, it was impossible that the comet should have stood that day, above the horizon, in the same vertical circle with those fixed stars which he assumes; and the distance of the stars too, which he presupposes, is in error by more than ¾ of one degree — so that from this one may easily gather what is to be thought of the rest) — we shall plainly show, in its proper place, that they are by no means to be accepted for the investigation of the truth.

But as far as concerns this latest comet, which shone in the year [15]80, you seem to me to have struck the mark itself far more exactly, and to have demonstrated the truth of the matter more plausibly with surer observations. In which I would wish at least this much, for a more inward investigation toward exquisite precision: that you had not neglected the change of the comet's proper motion between two observations. For since the comet, in the interval of two hours that day, shifted its place by 27′ in its own circle (the daily motion having indeed been found by us [to be] 5°23′), I judge this difference not to be passed over, if the matter is to be treated precisely, since in this case we reason about far greater things from the least. The instrument too, on this account, with which you take the altitudes — since it admits of at least ten or five minutes [of error] — can by no means be adequate in so subtle an inquiry. Yet your observations, and the demonstrations resting on them, are of such a kind that, even if from these it cannot be made out exactly to a hair how many minutes the parallax was, and in which of the celestial orbs precisely that comet moved, nevertheless that it was so great that it did not pass beneath the regions below the Moon [Lunae inferiora] — this they will be able to persuade, without difficulty, anyone skilled in these matters.

As far as concerns our observations of this comet, I indicated my opinion at that time no less than I now [indicate it] to you by letter — namely, that throughout the whole time of [its] apparition, from observations diligently made and examined with all our most exquisite instruments of various kinds, I was nowhere able to perceive any notable parallax of this comet. For, to be silent about the rest (to be recalled in a more convenient place), I certainly found that comet to have held exactly the same declination from the Equator both on the Meridian, when it was highest, and near the Horizon, at the very point of the western Azimuth — except insofar as the proper motion, proportional to the daily motion, varied it in the meantime, which could by no means have happened, given so great a difference of altitudes, if any sensible quantity of parallax had been present. This very thing too we certainly found to behave plainly in the same way, both by all the reasonings set forth by Regiomontanus in his little book that would have application here, and by certain others, more exact and less liable to error, devised by us concerning the measurement of parallaxes — as I shall demonstrate at leisure, copiously and with firm reasonings, to those who understand the mathematical sciences.

I have also, on receiving your little book, compared several things with our observations, and in them I found a marvellous agreement, which could serve to confirm the certainty of the parallaxes. For on 13 October, when the comet was near the Meridian, I found its distance from the brighter [star] of the head of Pegasus — both by the cross-staff [radius] and by the Astronomical Sextant (a particular instrument invented by us with such a contrivance that by it the intervals of stars are taken far more easily, conveniently, and exactly than by the cross-staff) — to be 1° 16′, greater than your distance by only one minute [scrupulo], almost imperceptible; which difference, however, for this distance of the terrestrial orb, would have grown to as much as the fifth minute [scrupulum] if the comet had been at the very convexity of the orb of the Moon — and far more, if it had blazed either in the fiery region (supposing there were any) or in the uppermost part of the air, as all the Aristotelians long falsely supposed. So too on 21 October, when the comet was nearest the bright star of the Eagle [Altair], standing over that star from the zenith [a vertice imminens], I found exactly the same distance as you. For although at the 8th hour this seemed to us one minute less, yet, continuing this observation up to the tenth [hour], it appeared exactly 6° 22′ — which agrees admirably with your annotation. And just as it could by no means have happened that, in the interval of at least two hours, that distance of the comet from the fixed star should vary by one minute, if a quite sensible parallax had been present, so too no one understanding the mathematical sciences will be persuaded that some body lower than the Moon could, in the interval of 6 degrees of the terrestrial orb — which lies between your observation-place and ours — at the same time precisely hold the same distance from the fixed stars.

Moreover, on that day the wished-for convenience of the [comet's] coma presented itself for examining the parallaxes against the star of the Vulture [Altair], because the comet was then situated at the place of its own circle which was least distant from the star, and looked toward it at right angles; whence too, for some hours, scarcely any sensible difference of intervals was introduced by the proper motion of the comet — and therefore the distance ought necessarily to have varied sensibly by reason of parallax at least (if there had been any), both in different and far-apart Horizons, and in the same [Horizon], where the altitudes of the stars would have changed very much. Yet the contrary of this was found by both of us, with a fine and exquisite agreement — so that even by this single argument it can be proved that that comet chose its seat for itself far above the orbs of the Moon, in the aether itself. And let it suffice to have now conferred with you briefly thus much about this latest comet.

This much at least I will add by way of admonition: that the declination of the brighter star in the head of Pegasus has been assigned by you nearly 8 minutes [scrupulis] greater than is right. For we, several times here — where the Pole is elevated 55° 54⅔′ — have found its maximum altitude to be exactly 38° 20′. And therefore it declines from the Equator 4° 14½′ — whether [the cause be] the smallness of your quadrant and a use not exact enough, or the elevation of the Prague pole, which you, perhaps less scrupulously handed down by the ancients, receive as true, or whether also some error lurk in both of these — which I leave to you yourself to examine and correct. For concerning the certainty of our observation we do not hesitate even by a single minute. By the like reasoning, we on the same 13th observed the declination of the comet itself, from the meridian altitude — which was found by us [to be] 37° 7½′ — [to be] 3° 2′, and therefore 5 minutes less than yours. The difference of the meridians, since it is scarcely greater than a quarter of an hour, [and] the declination varying imperceptibly by at least one minute on account of the comet's proper motion, I willingly pass over. Yet this difference of declinations between our observations cannot detract from the credibility of the agreement above, by which we proved the smallness of the parallax. For there the observation was made by distances, not by altitudes. And although by the quadrant too you measured the distance of the comet and the star from the difference of each one's maximum altitude, nevertheless — whatever error may lurk in the altitude of the Pole, or whatever may be lacking to a correct observation in the quadrant itself — since in both places, both in the comet and in each star, they behave in the same way, they cannot introduce a sensible error into the distance of the comet and the star itself. But in eliciting the declinations of each, to deviate in this way (as we showed before) was nonetheless possible.

Now concerning this whole business I shall shortly — God favouring our endeavours — bring forth into the open, during the winter leisure, very many observations, and those most certain, elaborated with various demonstrations; in which I shall set forth, to the understanding, by most certain and infallible reasonings, the motions, appearances, parallaxes, and distances from the Earth both of that new star and of each of the two subsequent comets — comparing also the principal observations of others, and, insofar as they are consonant with the truth or deviate from it, calling them back into the balance [weighing them]; so that I shall not have hesitated at last to conclude universally that all comets are generated in the aether itself, not in the Elementary region, as has hitherto been believed in vain on the authority of the Peripatetics — notwithstanding that Regiomontanus and Vögelin put forth something different concerning two comets. For I shall bring forth into the open probable and sufficient reasons that each of them deviated from the mark — unless someone wish to fight by authority alone, denying that to err is human, and [denying] that sometimes even good Homer nods.

But that I have not hitherto finished that work I proposed, and have not satisfied the desire of the many who eagerly long for our observations and the apodictic conclusions resting on them — the cause of this has been very many affairs in which I was meanwhile entangled, on account both of the King and of my noble kinsmen and friends. But above all, architectural cares and continual occupations in exquisitely elaborating astronomical machines [instruments] did not allow me to continue and finish the task begun. Yet I hope that this procrastination, God willing, will shortly be made good with interest. But I seem to have overstepped the bounds of a letter, by conferring with you so long about this material, in which I know you take delight — a prolixity which nonetheless I trust is not unwelcome to you. It will be for you to take in good part this, whatever it is; and if anything besides has become established for you about this material — whether from your own [findings] or from the verdicts of other eminent men — or has been published in writing, to share it with me kindly, and to preserve, as you do, the memory of us, and to renew our friendship by letters not interrupted so long as hitherto. I, for my part, will bring it about that nothing pertaining to the office of a candid and constant friend can anywhere be found wanting in me by you.

Farewell.

Given at Uraniborg, on the Island of the Danish Hellespont, Hven, on 12 October, the year 1581.

T[uus]. Tycho Brahe.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Thaddaeus Hagecius = Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku (Thaddaeus Hagecius ab Hayck, 1525–1600), Prague physician, astronomer, and imperial counsellor to Rudolf II; the leading Bohemian observer of the 1572 nova and the comets of 1577 and 1580, and Tycho's principal Prague correspondent. His Dialexis de nova et prius incognita stella (1574) and his comet tract are both referenced below.
  • Sender's self-naming. The letter opens with the heading "TYCHO BRAHE AD THADDAeUM HAGECIUM" run together with the first word "ANNUS" in the transcription; reproduced as printed. The subscription "T.TYCHO BRAHE" is read T[uus] Tycho Brahe ("Your Tycho Brahe").
  • Persons named.
  • Paulus Witichius Wratislaviensis = Paul Wittich of Wrocław (Vratislavia), the mathematician who had visited Hven in 1580 and carried Tycho's earlier (now-apparently-lost) letter homeward. (Tycho's later coolness toward Wittich postdates this letter.)
  • Bartholomaeus Scultetus = Bartholomäus Scultetus (Barthel Schultz, 1540–1614) of Görlitz, mathematician and cartographer, Tycho's friend "from Leipzig"; he forwarded Hagecius's tract. His own cometary demonstrations are criticised below.
  • Regiomontanus = Johannes Müller of Königsberg (1436–1476), whose method of determining cometary parallax (in De cometae magnitudine longitudineque) Hagecius had used.
  • Vogelinus = Johannes Vögelin (Vogelin, d. 1549), Vienna mathematician, author of a tract on the comet of 1532 (Significatio cometae … 1532), here paired with Regiomontanus as a predecessor who reached a non-aethereal result.
  • Homerus = Homer; the closing tag bonus … dormitet Homerus echoes Horace, Ars Poetica 359 (indignor quandoque bonus dormitat Homerus), "I am vexed whenever good Homer nods" — i.e. even the greatest authorities sometimes err.
  • Works referred to.
  • libellus tuus de ultimo Cometa — Hagecius's tract on the comet of 1580, identified in the literature consulted (Mosley, Bearing the Heavens; Norlind) as his Apodixis physica et mathematica de cometis (1581), forwarded by Scultetus.
  • libello … de eodem Cometa edito (with the 5° parallax) — Hagecius's earlier tract on the comet of 1577, his Descriptio cometae … 1577 (Prague, 1578).
  • tua Dialexi — Hagecius's Dialexis de nova et prius incognita stella (Frankfurt, 1574), here cited for a careless inference about parallax from culmination.
  • The astronomical substance (the central crux of the Tychonic comet programme). Tycho rebuts Hagecius's 1578 finding of a 5° parallax for the 1577 comet (which would place it sublunar, in the Aristotelian "elementary region"). Tycho's own instruments give a horizontal parallax of at most ⅓ of a degree, often much less, sometimes none — proving the comet was supralunar, in the aether. The argument is the same anti-Peripatetic one Tycho would publish in his comet book (De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis, 1588).
  • Technical terms (editorial glossary–5.2). parallaxis → parallax; altitudo → altitude; azimuthus → azimuth (kept); declinatio → declination; Aequator → Equator; meridianus → meridian; coeli mediatio → culmination (the star's transit of the meridian); orbis Lunae → orb of the Moon; aether / aethere → aether; Elementaris regio / regio ignea → Elementary region / fiery region (Aristotelian sublunary physics); motus proprius → proper motion; motus diurnus → daily motion; circulus verticalis → vertical circle; a vertice → from the zenith. Instruments: Radius → cross-staff (radius astronomicus); Sextans Astronomicus → Astronomical Sextant (Tycho's own bipartite-arc sextant, here advertised as his invention); quadrans → quadrant; instrumenta e solido metallo … singulorum minutorum adamussim capacia → instruments of solid metal capable of single arc-minutes.
  • Units of arc. gradus / partes (here "degrees"); scrupulum / minutum / scrupulorum are arc-minutes (or, with "18 scrupulorum," minutes of time for the observation-interval — note the ambiguity at the 18 saltem scrupulorum passage: in context "an interval of time … of only 18 minutes" it is time-minutes; elsewhere scrupulum is arc-minutes). Figures are reproduced exactly as written: parallax "5 partium" (5°); proper motion "27′" in two hours; daily motion "5° 23′"; Pegasus-distance "1° 16′"; Altair-distance "6° 22′"; pole-elevation of Hven "55° 54⅔′"; star max-altitude "38° 20′"; star declination "4° 14½′"; comet meridian altitude "37° 7½′"; comet declination "3° 2′."
  • Greek. parallaxeωs (line: "si sensibilis aliqua parallaxeωs adfuisset quantitas") — the genitive of parallaxis written with a Greek-letter ending (-εως) embedded in the Latin word; reproduced as printed, translated "of parallax."
  • Stars (period star-names → modern identification). lucida Vulturis / Vulturis stella = "the bright star of the Vulture" = Altair (α Aquilae; "Vultur volans" was the classical name); lucida Aquilae = "the bright star of the Eagle" = likewise Altair; os Pegasi / lucidior capitis Pegasi = "the mouth / brighter [star] of the head of Pegasus" = Enif (ε Pegasi). The comet's position is fixed by its distances from these.
  • Place-formula. Uranienburg in Insula Danici Hellesponti Huaena = "Uraniborg, on the island of the Danish Hellespont, Hven" — Tycho's standard conceit, the Øresund as the "Danish Hellespont." Comitia Ratisbonensia = the Diet of Regensburg (1575), where Tycho and Hagecius had met in person.
  • Editorial apparatus markers in the source. The transcription carries scattered superscript "A" sigla attached to words (e.g. ex conductoA, tibiA, indicoA, eamA, illaA, parallaxinA, … BRAHE.A). These are lemma-anchors of the critical edition's variant apparatus (recording readings of MSS A and B), not part of Tycho's text. Per the edition's convention(2) they are reproduced as printed in the Source block, but are not rendered in the translation. Likewise the source's fi (for si) and satissactum (for satisfactum) are retained as printed; satissactum is an evident typographic doubling for satisfactum ("made good/satisfied"), translated accordingly.
  • Calendar. Dateline die 12. Octobris Anno 1581 — Old Style (Julian; Denmark kept O.S. until 1700). This agrees with the catalogue label (1581.12.oktober). The in-text observation dates "13. Octobris," "21. Octobris" are likewise O.S. and refer to October 1580 (the apparition of the comet "which shone in the year [15]80"), not 1581; no silent correction made. No in-letter date discrepancy.
  • Correction. Restored the epistolary salutation formula S. (Greeting), printed by Dreyer between the heading and the letter's opening word (TBOO VII p.64) but absent from our source Latin and English; added to both texts.
