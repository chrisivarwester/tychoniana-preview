The original

TYCHO BRAHE AD OLIGERUM ROSENCRANTZIUM.

S. P,

AD eas, quas antequam Witeberga discederem, pauculis interiectis septimanis, a te accepi literas, Exoptatissime mi OLIGERE, tunc responsum misi, speroque id tibi iam pridem redditum. Abij autem inde cum meis omnibus circa Solstitium Aestivum, neque enim diutius isthic haerere ausus fui, utut viatico, quod e Dania expectabam, non satis instructus. Scripsit enim aliquoties ad me THADDÆUS noster, Caesarem pene singulis diebus de me, an advenissem, percontatum esse. Nec tamen prius licuit profectionem instituere, partim quod tertius ille liber, qui luminarium restitutos motus exhibet, Imperatori destinatus, antea absolvi nequiverit, partim quod filia mea natu maior, quae periculose ibi decubuit, paulo ante id temporis primum convaluerit. In itinere autem Dresdam a nobili et Magnifico viro Domino IOHANNE [LOe]SERO, quem procul dubio nosti, Bretziae in Arce eius humaniter invitati et laute tractati sumus. Cumque biduo post Dresdam venissemus, relicto illic apud civem quendam primarium mihi notum, Virum integrum et officiosum, Gynaeceo meo, ipsemet Pragam, cum filijs et reliquo comitatu me contuli. Ubi statim ac advenissem, a multis praecipuis Viris perhumaniter et honorifice exceptus sum, inprimis vero a Magnifico Domino IOHANNE BARVICIO Imperij supremo Secretario, Caesareae Maiestati a Consilijs intimis, qui unus pene omnia agit apud Caesarem. Is in horto quodam, non multum ab Arce remoto (siquidem non longe a Caesare abesse audet) tam nomine Imperatoriae Maitis quam Consiliariorum et proprio, de adventu meo humanissimis verbis mihi congratulatus est, admotumque acceptum esse Caesari meum accessum referebat, eiusque erga me etiam, necdum visum, clementem animum depraedicabat. Cumque libros tres, Caesari humillime offerendos, unâque Literas Electoris Coloniensis et Ducis Magnopolitani commendatitias monstrassem, se de ijs Caesarem percontaturum aiebat, ignorare enim, e cuius manu illa accipere velit; responsumque dedit sequenti die, Caesaream Maiestatem a nemine alio quam me ipso ea velle suscipere, brevique in Arcem ad se me vocaturum. Ostendi etiam Domino BARVICIO quasdam literas, ab Illustrissimo Duce OTTONE, Harburgi prope Hamburgum habitante, ad Illustrem et Generosum Dominum RUMPHIUM Curiae Caesareae Magistrum, praecipuaeque post Caesarem isthic authoritatis virum, mea de caussa scriptas. Suasit Dominus BARVICIUS, ut ipsemet eas illi intra diem unum vel alterum (quod alias per filium TYCHONEM fieri volebam) offerrem. Captata itaque commoditate, in Arcem curru vectus, ad eundem Dnum RUMPHIUM mox introductus sum, qui etiam benignissime me excepit, et perhumaniter affatus est. Inter alia vero aiebat, se multa relatione Praestantium virorum prius de me inaudivisse; gaudere autem, quod nunc praesentem videat; sed non posse se et alios satis mirari, quod Rex me e Dania dimittere voluerit, idque eius iuvenili aetati, quod Marti potius quam Arti operam daret, tribuendum censuit; at ego e contra Regem excusavi, quamque virtute et ingenio polleat, extuli. Mox ille subiunxit: oportet igitur eos, qui ab illo secundi, et autoritate isthic praevalent, aut bonarum Artium rudes, aut malevolos et invidos esse, cum Regis et Patriae honori non rectius consulant; ad quae verba ego non multa respondi, id saltem subijciens, me non dubitare, si Regi omnia de me rite proposita fuissent, ac ab ijs, quos intime habet, illi commendata, ipsum non minus, quam Pater antea, Laudatissimae memoriae FRIDERICUS, Astronomica mea excercitia in suo Regno conservaturum; sed forte peculiari Dei providentia sic factum esse, ut ea, quae in Astronomicis diu multumque molitus sum, nunc alio, atque ad ipsum Imperatorem, Imperij caput, transferenda sint. Haec et similia cum dicto Domino RUMPHIO (qui elegantissime Latine loquitur) verba commutans, literas praenominati Principis illi tradidi, isque, discedentem aliquantulum deducens, meas res sibi curae futuras, seque eas apud Caesarem promoturum, pollicitus est. Insinuavit autem quidam ex Amicis postea, id quod Dominus BARVICIUS adeo ursit, ut in Arcem ascendens ipsemet literas Domino RUMPHIO inscriptas ei in manus exhiberem, id propterea factum, quod Caesar e conclavi suo, quod e regione est, me hac occasione antea videre vellet, quam ad se vocaret. Quamprimum vero ex Arce descenderem, mox obviam factus Dnus BARVICIUS in aedes quasdam splendidas et magnificas (quas prior Imperij Cancellarius, Dominus IACOBUS CURTIUS laudatae memoriae Italica architectura exstruxerat, et horto pulcherrimo cinxerat expensis maioribus quam 20 Ioachymicorum) iussu Caesaris introduxit singulaque in ijs monstravit, subiungens, Caesaream Maiestatem eas mihi a vidua procurare velle, si placerent, in ijs a Domino CURTIO turriculam, quam cernerem, ob id exstructam, ut observationibus coelestibus inserviret, et esse eas aedes Arci atque habitationi Caesaris vicinas, ut promptior sit accessus. Verum cum is ex taciturnitate mea, et quod dicerem, turriculam eam vix uni ex meis instrumentis sufficere, nedum pluribus, colligeret animum meum non admodum eo inclinari, ulterius inferebat: Si forte Pragae habitare mihi non luberet, concessurum facile Imperatorem Arcem quampiam ex suis, unius vel dimidij diei itinere ab urbe distantem, ubi pacatius agerem, extra invidiam aliorum positus. Id cum magis mihi arridere animadverteret, praesertim cum dicerem, me, ut otio fruerer Philosophico, non nimium ab alijs interpellatus, ob id maluisse in Dania insulam eam inhabitare, subiunxit, se Caesari id relaturum, et iam intellexisse, eius animum in has alteras partes inclinare; Caesarem etiam de salario annuo mihi clementer impertiendo constituisse; de quo ubi audientiam, uti vocant (quod intra duos vel tres dies futurum existimavit) nactus essem, plenius mecum agendum. Elapsis mox pauculis diebus, Caesar me in Arcem ad se accersi iubet: constitutum vero fuit antea in Senatu, ut aulae magister Dns RUMPHIUS me ad Caesarem solemniter introduceret, quod hoc esset magis honorificum; at Caesar id tunc temporis renuit, sive quod quidam ex ministris RUMPHIJ ijs diebus peste infectus et in civitatem delatus obijsset, sive quod ipsius Maiestas cum me solo, nemine praesente, loqui maluerit, quod et factum est; nam cum ad ianuam conclavis, in quo Caesar esset, me duxisset Dns BARVICIUS, se non ingredi, nisi a Caesare vocatum, insinuans, extra remansit. Ingressusque solus ego ad Caesar. Matem, quam solam etiam mensae cuidam in superiori conclavis parte dorso innitentem conspexi, nemine alias in toto conclavi praesente, et ne quidem puero famulante, exhibitaque debita reverentia, Caesar me statim nutu capitis propius ad se vocavit; cum accessissem, clementer manum mihi porrigebat; moxque paululum recedens, oratiunculam habui Latino sermone, qua, ex ipsius Caesar. Matis clementissimo mandato, per literas sui Cancellarij CORADUCIJ me vocatum nunc comparere, quod et prius, si res meae id ullo modo ferre potuissent, fecissem, dixi, utque hanc moram clementissime excusatam habere dignaretur, et me atque ea, quae diu multumque tractavi, studia Caesareo favore prosequi ac sibi commendata esse velit. Quo etiam nomine literas a Serenissimo Coloniensi Electore et Illustrissimo Duce Megapolitano scriptas, ipsi humillime porrigerem, moxque literas illas eius Maiestati in manus tradidi, quas et clementer accepit meque praesente aperuit. Verum ijs in mensam repositis, et nondum lectis, statim ipsius Caesarea Maiestas copiosiore etiam sermone, quam ego eam alloquebar, mihi clementissime respondit; et inter alia gratum sibi esse meum accessum, atque me cum meis studijs sibi curae futurum promisit, ac inter loquendum blanditer quasi arrisit, ut favorem eximium ipso vultu ostenderet. Verum omnia, quae referebat, verba capere non potui, tam submisse ipsa natura loquitur. Deinde ego, pro hac clementissima animi sui indicatione humillime gratias agens, de libris tribus, quos ipsius Maiestati subiectissime dedicatos in promptu haberem, mentionem feci; quos cum se lubenter a me accepturam clementissime responderet, ego mox eos a filio TYCHONE, qui in exteriori conclavi expectans secum habuit, ad me recipiens, denuoque ingressus, Caesareae Matem, quae adhuc mensae astabat, in manus exhibui; quae postquam eos clementissime accepisset, atque prope se in mensa reposuisset, ego rursus brevi narratione, quid singuli eorum continerent, summatim indicavi; rursusque ipsius Caesarea Maiestas sermone satis luculento excepit, atque eos gratos sibi futuros benignissime indicavit. Sicque praestito honore discessi; et BARVICIUM ante ianuam adhuc praestolantem reperi, qui statim audito signo ad Caesarem introvocatus, me, ut, antequam rediret, non descenderem, rogavit, statimque reversus dixit, Caesarem automatum illud, quod in curru haberem (hoc enim, cum in Arcem curru veherer, per fenestram conspexerat) sibi monstrari cupere, illud itaque per filium e curru mox allatum, BARVICIO tradidi, atque eius usum Caesari ostendendum monstravi. Quod cum fecisset, atque ad me reversus esset, aiebat, Caesarem dixisse, se unum vel alterum eiuscemodi habere, quorum tamen nullum tam magnum esset, neque penitus ea ratione factum, posse aliquem e suis Astronomis sibi ad huius imitationem quoddam conficere, sed nolle me eo privari; et addebat insuper BARVICIUS, Caesarem singulari Clementia et gratia erga me affectum esse, brevique negotio ad consiliarios relato, velle mecum de salario annuatim numerando atque habitatione idonea transigi curare. Interim iuberent, Gynaeceum meum, quod Dresdae me reliquisse intellexerat, Pragam ad me advehi faciam, ipsius Caesaream Maiestatem nobis omnibus ita prospecturam, ut ad commode vivendum nihil sit defuturum. Quare sequenti mox die filium TYCHONEM unâ cum CLAUDIO MULÆO, pro adducendo Gynaeceo Dresdam misi, quod post octiduum Pragam advenit. Intera de salario mecum per eundem Dominum BARVICIUM et sequentibus etiam aliquot diebus, per intervalla, actum est, siquidem is sit occupatissimus, et nonnulli e Senatoribus Imperatoriae voluntati non satis cito, uti fit, auscultent, lenteque omnia in illa aula ob negotiorum magnitudinem et copiam procedant. Cupiente vero BARVICIO ex Caesaris mandato, ut familiam meam, quanta sit, et eorum, qui ad Artis sustentationem requirerentur, summam consignarem, atque magnitudinem horum omnium in taxa, uti aiunt, deducerem, primum quidem lubens feci; at alterum Caesareae Matis Clementissimae voluntati et libero arbitrio humillime commisi. Quae paucis post diebus 3000 aureorum in certa quadam pecunia mihi benignissime deputavit, et insuper quaedam alia accidentaria ...... pro oeconomia et alijs rebus, quae 1000, si computentur, excedunt. Haec Senatus consulto peracta sunt, quibusdam id facile approbantibus, at pluribus, uti fieri solet, nonnihil reluctantibus, quod nemo ex illis, qui pluribus annis Caesari inservijssent, tantum haberent salarium. Vicit tamen Caesaris ........ci ut sic fieret ursit, cumque per praedictum Dominum BARVICIUM mihi hoc significari curasset, iussit insuper dici, si haec mihi non sufficerent, et in posterum quidpiam desiderarem, nec sibi esse accurtatam manum, nec mihi obturatum os, quod his ipsis verbis ad me idem retulit BARVICIUS. Quin et feudum secundum constitutionem Imperij haereditarium, ut liberis et familiae meae post me consuleretur, Clementissime nec invite addixit, quam primum aliquod in suis ditionibus vel Imperio vacarit, quod mihi et meis commodum esset. Qua etiam de re literas, quas vocant promissivas, brevi accepturus. Et quoniam me Pragae aedes Curtianas non admodum cupere (habui enim meas rationes, cum quod viduam, quae eas adhuc inhabitat, mea de caussa emigrare nolim, tum quod nimijs visitationibus, in tanta praesertim civitate, prope Arcem obnoxius essem) intellexit Caesar, promisit nihilominus clementissime aliam in urbe habitationem mihi oportunam, quam, ubi eo accederem, sive vocatus, sive sponte, cum meis commode incolere possem, et propria quadra vivere minoreque sumptu; forte tamen Curtianam illam suo tempore accipiam, ubi vidua sponte emigrarit, quae nunc, uti audio, novas affectat nuptias, more iuvencularum viduarum. Iussit insuper Caesar, praeterquam quod Salarium meum ab eo tempore, quo ab ipsius Caesarea Mate vocatus sum Pragam, etiamsi multo interiecto tempore non advenerim, incipere deberet, ut mihi 2000 florenorum statim honorarij loco pro introitu darentur, quos et accepi. Dixit quoque inter alia aliquando ad me BARVICIUS [si] foret, ut ante decennium huc ad Caesarem advenissem, [cum] omnia erant meliora et promptiora, nec ita difficultatibus obnoxia, uti nunc ob bellum in Hungaria, tunc Caesarem potuisse multo faciliorem et liberaliorem se erga me praebere; cumque hoc semel atque iterum insinuaret, ego quidem respondi, nihil adhuc periculi esse: Caesarem Turca fere semper superiorem fuisse, et Deum in posterum suum Christianismum protecturum. Verum tacite mecum cogitavi, neminem me ipso magis dolere, quod non ante decennium statim ab obitu laudatissimae memoriae FRIDERICI Regis Patria excesserim, atque huc me contulerim, quod et tunc fecissem, nisi literae a toto Senatu ....... magno meo incommodo, maximoque rei familiaris dispendio, me ....... de his alias agemus, non iustum sit, ut aliqua mihi fiat pro tantis expensis compensatio. Quibus transactis, per Capitaneum quendam Caesareum, eius Mate sic iubente, in Arces prope Pragam dispositas deductus sum, ut ex ijs eligerem aliquam, quae mihi et meis studijs commodior foret. Nomi........ et aliae; sed quoniam ut ex ijs una cum civitatibus propinquis p.........arat, et tertiam in edito positam, Caesar .........enter accederet, qui quondam Bohemici Aulae Magistri fuerat, et Caesar secundum iuris ten.........ata, dissuasit Dominus BARVICIUS earum perlustrationem. Eae vero, quas v........um unius vel etiam dimidij diei profectione Praga absunt, quarum prima dicitur Brandeiss, estque potissima Caesaris Arx in vicinia sita, utpote tribus saltem miliaribus Praga distans, in quam se delectationis gratia interdum se ....... venationes habet circumquaque praecipuas. Altera Lyssa dicta est, etiam .......da, 4 milliaribus ab urbe remota. Tertia Benachia appellatur, qua ....... sub initium huius anni a .......ruvij cuiusdam senio confecti et defuncti .......nis, producentis quindecim .....................int, illi enim, non satis concordes, haereditatem alias inter se dividere nequiverunt, sed sponte Caesari haec bona vendiderunt. Perlustratis itaque his tribus, deducente Capitaneo, Arcibus, et in singulis benigne et honorifice exceptus, praetuli Benachiam, 6 horarum itinere (est enim via plana) Praga semotam, quae ob loci amoenitatem et fluviolum Isaram circumlabentem atque in Albim postea se exonerantem, Bohemorum vernacula lingua sonat, ac si diceres Venetiae Bohemorum, estque in edito et aprico loco sita, iucundissimo prospectu, adiacente parva quadam civitate, in qua religio, quam Evangelicam vocant, praevalet, plurimis tamen ex Picardis, qui Calvinisticae (quam Sectatores orthodoxam nuncupant) appropinquant, in eadem degentibus. Cum Caesar intellexisset, me hanc Arcem prae ceteris eligere, mox per praedictum Capitaneum, qui Brandusijs praeest, me huc introduci curavit, ubi nunc cum familia, commode et pacate, quod Deo acceptum fero, solus habito, nemine obstrepente aut interturbante, sed dicto Capitaneo ex voluntate Caesaris omnia officia mihi lubenter exhibente. Qua etiam de caussa intra unam vel aliam septimanam Brandusio huc accurrit, ut mihi in ijs, quae requiruntur, praesto sit. Mandavit etiam Caesar eidem Capitaneo, ut omnia necessaria pro observatorio, meis instrumentis omnibus rite disponendis idoneo, actutum procuret, quo iuxta meam ordinationem omnia ante hyemem apposite exaedificentur, tum quoque ut laboratorium, ea qua voluero ratione, mihi oportuno loco confici curet, et omnia, quae alias a Mechanicis fieri velim, solvat. Si haec mihi in Patria evenissent, quantis sumptibus pepercissem, pro quibus nunc mihi nulla restat gratia, sed potius ingrata omnia [nihil feci]sse benigne. Obtulit insuper Caesarea Maiestas sponte sua, se novum Uraniburgum Arcem in suo territorio suisque sumptibus aedificaturum, quod Danico illi non cederet, sed multis parasangis excederet. Delectatur enim ipsius Caesarea Matas summopere non saltem Divina illa Astronomia Mathematicisque studijs, sed et architectonicis structuris, quae e Geometria et Perspectiva fluunt. Cumque ipsius Maiestas Libros sibi a me humillime oblatos (de quibus antea memini) diligenter perlegisset, adeo ut in multam noctem primis diebus, referente id BARVICIO, ijs occuparetur (neque enim tam abiecti apud ipsum erant, quemadmodum in vestris oris) a me multa de eorum contentis per eundem BARVICIUM sciscitatus est, et placuit illi valde structura Uraniburgi in primo tomo designata. Sed cum laboratorium in ea tam cito reperire non posset, id sibi indicari voluit. Placuerunt caetera omnia, ita ut Dominus BARVICIUS eos Caesari acceptissimos esse mihi retulerit.

Ex iam recensitis, Amantissime OLIGERE, rerum mearum statum, qualis nunc sit, intellexisti. Agoque Deo propitio gratias, quod sic mihi et meis prospexerit, ut ex adversitatibus illis, quas in Patria diu multumque passus sum, eluctari potuerim, et mihi et meis rectius consulere. Speroque in posterum, eodem Divino Numine favente, adhuc prosperiora. Caeterum de honoribus et dignitatibus Politicis mihi oblatis, imo antequam Pragam venirem, cum adhuc Witebergae essem, deputatis (prout forte ex Domino Doctore FRANTZIO, Domini Doctoris VITI WINSHEMIJ genero, aut oretenus; aiebat enim te, in Orientalem Frisiam abiturus, invisere velle; aut per literas intellexisti, quemadmodum is ex Domino CORRADUCIO Procancellario haec, cum Pragae esset, se accepisse aiebat, ut mihi referret, quo promptius accederem) nihil nunc addere volo. Suaserunt Pragae amici quidam, ut talia susciperem, quae tamen semper ex alto despexi. At si facturus sum, propter vestrates, qui me tam insolenter contemptui habuerunt, facturus sum; quod et THADDÆO nostro urgenti dixi; verum is sic subiunxit: Non tantum propter tuos Danos id admittes, sed etiam propter nostros Bohemos, adeoque in ipsa aula aliqui tam obtuso et rustico ingenio, et non minus quam vestrates ignorent discernere inter aurum et orichalcum, attamen aliqui inveniuntur, qui rectius iudicent, quique, licet aeque atque tu honores Politicos, respectu virtutis et doctrinae eximiae, parvi ducant, ob vulgarium tamen iudiciorum captum, quo commodius in mundo vivatur, ita faciendum existimant. Qui, quamvis pauci reperiantur, tamen prae caeteris iudicio valent, tuique sunt amantissimi, quos partim hactenus pro primo hoc congressu nosti, partim in posterum plenius tibi innotescent. In hunc modum ille. Verum de his postmodum dispiciam, si non ea prorsus despiciam; neque enim in ijs quid consulto et discrete fieri potest ante Magci Domini CORRADUC1J Procancellarij reditum e Roma, ubi Legatum Caesaris nomine apud P. M. agit, qui circa Michaëlis festum expectatur. Neque etiam volo, ut prius quid in talibus concludatur; est is mihi admodum favens, licet de facie ignoti adhuc simus, Mathematicarum quoque rerum, ut dicitur, amantissimus, hac in re antecessoris CURTIJ aemulus.

Habes nunc, charissime mi OLIGERE, historiolam mearum rerum, ex quo e Germania in Bohemiam cum meis migravi; de ijs, quae antea facta sunt, meoque e Dania discessu probe omnia nosti, ut ea repetere non sit opus, quae meminisse animus horrens iterare refugit. Et certe videor historiolam potius quam literas conscripsisse, dum calamo lapso teque fusius alloquor, quo [de] rebus meis plenius edoceare. Quam tamen prolixitatem tibi non ingratam fore, cum alias literas humaniores, adeoque ingentia librorum volumina, non, ut alij, qui Nobiles haberi volunt, fastidias, mihi persuadeo. De libris vero tibi promissis (ut tandem aliquid subiungam) sic habe. Non immemor sum pacti, nec difficilis ad servandum; sed cum exemplaria omnia de Mechanica Astronomia, quotquot coloribus exornata, sive uti vocant illuminata, ................................. Pragae suis coloribus imbui, quod ubi factum fuerit, obtinebis non saltem hunc primum tomum, sed et alterum de affixarum restitutione, tum quoque tertium de luminarium a me correcto motu, quem tamen ultimum denuo e prima conceptione describi curare oportet, cum nullum mihi ad manus mundum sit exemplar, postquam illud, quod Witebergae exaratum est, Caesi Mati unâ cum reliquis obtulerim. Ne sis itaque huius morae impatiens, mi OLIGERE, habebis et haec et alia ex meis, favente Numine, quam primum fieri poterit, nec sine foenore. Interim relinque sorori nostrae SOPHIAe eos, quos Regi destinaram (hos enim repudiatos et RAMELIO donatos ipsam consequi posse intellexi) quod etiam non displicet, malo etenim sic fieri, quam ut in invidorum et in osorum manus pervenirent.

Superest, ut calamum sistam, nisi hoc unicum superaddere luberet. Insulae Huennae, quam istic inhabitavi, resignationem voluntariam, quam suasisti (de qua etiam me, cum huc venirem, plenius rescripturum .......ui) nullatenus approbare animus est, dissuadentibus etiam ijs, qui Politicis non perfunctorie imbuti sunt, imo quibusdam id mihi vitio datum iri, tanquam prae contumaci insolentia, postquam ad Caesareas partes transivi, hoc facerem, quasi Danos meos, et quae isthic haberem, praefracto animo negligerem. Si vero insulam ij, qui ........ praesunt, mihi auferre voluerint, ob id quod ad Caesarem me contulerim, ipsius Caesaream Maitem aut operam daturam, ut Rex Daniae nihil damni mihi sua de caussa inferre patiatur, aut id, quicquid erit, alia ratione compensaturam. Obiectent vestrates, quicquid velint, facilis erit responsio, si modo iustitiae et veritati sit locus; sin minus, transeat id cum caeteris, et iustus iudex omnia decernat. Iamque cum coniuge tua, Nepte mea dilecta, quam felicissime vale, integerrime OLIGERE, salutata unâ cum hac Matre tua Nobilissima et honestissima ac simul alijs amicis, si qui in Cimbria vestra mei memores adhuc supersint, quos aliquos saltem adhuc restare crediderim. Iterumque et saepius quam optime vale, meque de tuis rebus luculenter etiam prima quaque occasione certiorem effice.

Dabantur ex Arce Caesarea Benachia die 9. Septembris Stylo novo, Anni Domini 1599.
Totus Tuus

Tycho Brahe to Holger Rosenkrantz.

Heartiest greeting.

To the letter I received from you, most longed-for Holger, a few weeks before I left Wittenberg, I then sent a reply, and hope it was long since delivered to you. I departed thence with all my people about the summer solstice, for I did not dare to linger there longer, however ill-furnished with the travelling-money I was expecting from Denmark. For our Thaddaeus wrote to me several times that the Emperor inquired about me almost daily, whether I had arrived. Nor yet was I able to set out sooner — partly because that third book, which presents the restored motions of the luminaries, destined for the Emperor, could not be finished beforehand; partly because my elder daughter, who lay dangerously ill there, had first recovered only a little before that time. On the road to Dresden we were courteously invited and sumptuously entertained by the noble and Magnificent Lord Johann Löser — whom you doubtless know — at his castle of Bretzia. And when, two days later, we had come to Dresden, leaving my Gynaeceum [womenfolk] there with a certain leading citizen known to me, an upright and obliging man, I betook myself to Prague with my sons and the rest of my company. Where, as soon as I had arrived, I was received most courteously and honourably by many leading men, but above all by the Magnificent Lord Johann Barvitius, supreme Secretary of the Empire, of His Imperial Majesty's inmost counsels, who alone manages almost everything with the Emperor. In a certain garden not far from the castle (since he dares not be far from the Emperor) he congratulated me on my arrival in most humane words, both in the name of the Imperial Majesty and of the Councillors and his own, and reported that my coming was very welcome to the Emperor, and extolled his clement disposition toward me even before I was seen. And when I had shown him the three books, to be most humbly offered to the Emperor, together with the commendatory letters of the Elector of Cologne and the Duke of Mecklenburg, he said he would inquire of the Emperor about them, for he did not know from whose hand he wished to receive them; and he gave answer the next day that His Imperial Majesty would take them up from no one but myself, and would shortly summon me up to him in the castle. I also showed Lord Barvitius certain letters written on my behalf by the most illustrious Duke Otto — dwelling at Harburg near Hamburg — to the illustrious and noble Lord Rumpf, Master of the Imperial Court, the man of chief authority there after the Emperor. Lord Barvitius advised that I present them to him myself within a day or two (which I had otherwise meant to do through my son Tycho). Seizing the opportunity, then, carried up to the castle by carriage, I was soon introduced to that same Lord Rumpf, who likewise received me most kindly, and addressed me most courteously. Among other things he said that he had previously heard much of me from the report of distinguished men; that he rejoiced now to see me present; but that he and others could not sufficiently marvel that the King had been willing to dismiss me from Denmark — and he judged this should be attributed to the King's youthful age, that he gave his efforts to Mars rather than to Art; but I, on the contrary, excused the King, and extolled how strong he was in virtue and talent. He soon added: those, then, who are second to him and prevail in authority there must be either raw in the good Arts, or malevolent and envious, since they do not better provide for the King's and the fatherland's honour; to which words I did not answer much, only subjoining that I did not doubt that, had everything about me been duly set before the King, and commended to him by those he holds intimate, he would no less than his Father before him — Frederik of most praiseworthy memory — have preserved my astronomical exercises in his Kingdom; but that perhaps by the special providence of God it had been so brought about, that what I had long and much laboured at in astronomy should now be transferred elsewhere, and to the Emperor himself, the head of the Empire. Exchanging these and like words with the said Lord Rumpf (who speaks Latin most elegantly), I handed him the aforenamed Prince's letter, and he, conducting me a little way as I departed, promised that my affairs would be his care, and that he would promote them with the Emperor. One of my friends afterwards intimated that the reason Lord Barvitius so pressed that, going up to the castle, I should myself present into Rumpf's hand the letters addressed to him, was this: that the Emperor wished, by this occasion, to see me beforehand from his chamber, which is opposite, before he summoned me to him. And as soon as I came down from the castle, Lord Barvitius, meeting me, brought me, by the Emperor's order, into certain splendid and magnificent houses (which the former Imperial Chancellor, Lord Jacob Curtius of praised memory, had built in the Italian architecture, and surrounded with a most beautiful garden, at a cost of more than 20 joachims), and showed me each thing in them, adding that His Imperial Majesty wished to procure them for me from the widow, if they pleased — that in them was a little tower, which I saw, built by Lord Curtius for the purpose of serving celestial observations, and that the houses were near the castle and the Emperor's dwelling, so that access might be the readier. But when, from my silence — and because I said that the little tower would scarcely suffice for one of my instruments, let alone several — he gathered that my mind was not much inclined that way, he further proposed: if perhaps it did not please me to live at Prague, the Emperor would readily grant some one of his castles, a day's or half a day's journey from the city, where I might live more peaceably, set beyond the envy of others. When he noticed that this pleased me more — especially since I said that, to enjoy philosophical leisure not too much interrupted by others, I had for that reason preferred to inhabit that island in Denmark — he added that he would report it to the Emperor, and that he had already understood his mind to incline to this latter course; that the Emperor had also resolved to bestow a yearly salary on me most kindly; about which, when I had obtained an audience (as they call it) — which he thought would be within two or three days — there should be fuller dealing with me.

A few days later, the Emperor orders me summoned up to him in the castle. It had indeed been earlier decided in the Senate that the court-master Lord Rumpf should solemnly introduce me to the Emperor, as being more honourable; but the Emperor at that time declined it — whether because one of Rumpf's servants, infected with plague in those days and carried into the city, had died, or because His Majesty preferred to speak with me alone, no one present, which is what happened. For when Lord Barvitius had led me to the door of the chamber in which the Emperor was, intimating that he did not enter unless called by the Emperor, he remained outside. And entering alone to His Imperial Majesty — whom I beheld also alone, leaning with his back on a certain table in the upper part of the chamber, no one else present in the whole chamber, not even a serving-boy — and having made due reverence, the Emperor at once called me nearer with a nod of the head; when I had approached, he kindly extended his hand to me; and soon, withdrawing a little, I delivered a little oration in Latin, in which I said that, by His Imperial Majesty's most clement command, by the letters of his Chancellor Coraduz, I now appeared having been summoned — which I would have done sooner, had my affairs been able in any way to bear it — and [begged] that he would deign most clemently to hold this delay excused, and would be willing to pursue with imperial favour me and the studies I have long and much handled, and to hold them commended to him. On which score, too, I would most humbly present to him the letters written by the most Serene Elector of Cologne and the most illustrious Duke of Mecklenburg; and soon I handed those letters into His Majesty's hands, which he both clemently accepted and opened in my presence. But having laid them back on the table, and not yet read them, His Imperial Majesty at once replied to me most clemently, with even more copious speech than I had used in addressing him; and among other things promised that my coming was welcome to him, and that I, with my studies, would be his care; and while speaking he smiled, as it were, graciously, to show his exceptional favour by his very countenance. But I could not catch all the words he uttered, so softly does he speak by his very nature. Then I, most humbly giving thanks for this most clement declaration of his mind, made mention of the three books which I had ready, most submissively dedicated to His Majesty; and when he most clemently replied that he would gladly accept them from me, I soon, receiving them from my son Tycho — who, waiting in the outer chamber, had them with him — and entering again, presented them into the hands of His Imperial Majesty, who was still standing by the table; and after he had most clemently accepted them, and laid them on the table near himself, I again, in a brief account, summarily indicated what each of them contained; and again His Imperial Majesty took it up in speech sufficiently clear, and most kindly signified that they would be welcome to him. And so, the honour being rendered, I withdrew; and found Barvitius still waiting before the door, who, as soon as a signal was heard, was called in to the Emperor, [and] asked me not to go down before he returned; and returning at once, he said the Emperor desired to be shown that automaton which I had in my carriage (for he had seen it through the window when I was carried up to the castle by carriage); so I, having it soon brought from the carriage by my son, handed it to Barvitius, and showed how to demonstrate its use to the Emperor. When he had done so and returned to me, he said the Emperor had said that he had one or two of the kind, none of which, however, was so large nor made quite on that principle; that one of his Astronomers could make him something in imitation of this, but that he did not wish me to be deprived of it. And Barvitius added besides that the Emperor was affected with singular clemency and grace toward me, and that, the business being briefly referred to the councillors, he wished to have it arranged with me about a salary to be paid yearly and a suitable lodging. Meanwhile they should order that I have my Gynaeceum — which he had understood I had left at Dresden — brought up to me at Prague; and that His Imperial Majesty would so provide for us all that nothing would be lacking for living comfortably. Wherefore the very next day I sent my son Tycho, together with Claus Mule, to Dresden to bring the Gynaeceum, which after eight days came to Prague. Meanwhile there was dealing with me about the salary, through the same Lord Barvitius, and over several following days too, at intervals — since he is most busy, and some of the Senators do not (as it happens) hearken quite quickly enough to the Imperial will, and everything in that court proceeds slowly, on account of the magnitude and abundance of affairs. Barvitius desiring, by the Emperor's order, that I set down how large my family is, and the sum of those required for the maintenance of the Art, and reduce the magnitude of all these into a "taxa" (as they say), I did the first gladly; but the second I most humbly committed to His Imperial Majesty's most clement will and free judgment. Which, a few days later, most kindly assigned me 3,000 gold pieces in a certain fixed money, and besides certain other perquisites [lacuna] for the household and other things, which, if reckoned, exceed 1,000. These were carried through by Senate decree, some readily approving it, but more, as is wont, somewhat resisting, because none of those who had served the Emperor for several years had so large a salary. Yet the Emperor's [will] prevailed [...] urged that it be so; and when he had had this signified to me through the aforesaid Lord Barvitius, he ordered it said besides that, if these did not suffice me, and I should desire anything more hereafter, neither was his hand shortened, nor my mouth stopped — which Barvitius reported to me in these very words. Nay, he most clemently and not unwillingly promised a fief, hereditary according to the constitution of the Empire, that provision might be made for my children and family after me, as soon as any should fall vacant in his dominions or in the Empire that would be convenient for me and mine; concerning which, too, I shall shortly receive letters, which they call promissory. And since the Emperor understood that I did not much desire the Curtius houses at Prague (for I had my reasons — both that I did not wish the widow who still inhabits them to move out on my account, and that I should be exposed, especially in so great a city, near the castle, to too many visitations), he nonetheless most clemently promised me another suitable lodging in the city, which, when I should come there, whether summoned or of my own accord, I could conveniently inhabit with my people, and live at my own table at less expense; though perhaps I shall in time get that Curtius house, when the widow has of her own accord moved out — who now, as I hear, seeks new nuptials, in the manner of young widows. The Emperor ordered besides — over and above that my salary should begin from the time when I was summoned to Prague by His Imperial Majesty, even though I did not arrive until much time had elapsed — that 2,000 florins be given me at once by way of honorarium for my entry, which I received. Barvitius also once said to me, among other things, that, had it been that I had come here to the Emperor ten years ago, when everything was better and readier and not so subject to difficulties as now, on account of the war in Hungary, the Emperor could then have shown himself much easier and more liberal toward me; and when he intimated this once and again, I indeed replied that there was as yet no danger: that the Emperor had almost always been superior to the Turk, and that God would hereafter protect his Christendom. But silently I thought to myself that no one grieved more than I that I had not left the fatherland ten years ago, immediately upon the death of King Frederik of most praiseworthy memory, and betaken myself here — which I would have done then, had not letters from the whole Senate [lacuna], to my great inconvenience and the greatest loss of my household estate, [lacuna] me [lacuna] — but of these we shall deal elsewhere — [and] that it is not just but that some compensation be made me for such great expenses. These things transacted, by a certain imperial Captain, His Majesty so ordering, I was conducted to the castles disposed near Prague, that I might choose from them one more convenient for me and my studies. [It was named lacuna] and others; but since [lacuna], together with the neighbouring towns [lacuna], and a third set on a height, which the Emperor [lacuna] would [not?] approach, [a castle] which had once been the Bohemian Court-Master's, and the Emperor according to the [lacuna] of law [lacuna], Lord Barvitius dissuaded the survey of them. But those which [lacuna] are a day's or even half a day's journey from Prague — of which the first is called Brandeis, and is the chief imperial castle situated in the vicinity, being only three miles distant from Prague, into which [the Emperor] sometimes [betakes himself] for the sake of recreation [and] has the chief hunting grounds all round; the second is called Lissa, also [lacuna], four miles distant from the city; the third is named Benátky, which [lacuna] at the beginning of this year [was acquired] from [the heirs] of a certain [Rudský?], worn out by age and deceased [lacuna], leaving fifteen [lacuna]; for they, not sufficiently in accord, could not otherwise divide the inheritance among themselves, but of their own accord sold these goods to the Emperor. Having surveyed these three castles, then, the Captain conducting [me], and being kindly and honourably received in each, I preferred Benátky, six hours' journey (for the road is level) removed from Prague — which, on account of the pleasantness of the place and the little river Iser flowing around it and afterwards discharging into the Elbe, sounds in the Bohemians' vernacular tongue as if you said "the Venice of the Bohemians"; and it is situated on a high and sunny spot, with a most delightful prospect, with a small town adjoining, in which the religion they call Evangelical prevails — though very many of the Picards, who approach the Calvinist [persuasion] (which the Sectaries call orthodox), live in it. When the Emperor had understood that I chose this castle before the rest, he soon had me introduced here through the aforesaid Captain, who governs Brandeis, where I now dwell with my family, comfortably and peaceably (which I take as accepted of God), alone, no one making noise or disturbing, but the said Captain, by the Emperor's will, gladly performing all offices for me. For which cause too, within a week or two, he hastens here from Brandeis, to be at hand for me in what is required. The Emperor also ordered the same Captain promptly to procure everything necessary for an observatory suitable for setting up all my instruments duly, that everything might be fitly built before winter according to my plan; and likewise that he have a laboratory made for me in a suitable place, in whatever manner I should wish, and pay for everything else I should wish made by the mechanics. Had these things befallen me in my fatherland, what expenses I should have spared — for which no gratitude now remains to me, but rather everything [is] thankless, [to have done] nothing graciously. His Imperial Majesty offered besides, of his own accord, that he would build in his territory and at his own expense a new Uraniborg castle, which should not yield to that Danish one, but exceed it by many parasangs. For His Imperial Majesty delights greatly not only in that divine Astronomy and the mathematical studies, but also in architectural structures, which flow from Geometry and Perspective. And when His Majesty had carefully read the Books most humbly offered to him by me (of which I mentioned before), so much so that in the first days he was occupied with them far into the night (Barvitius reporting it) — for they were not so despised with him as in your shores — he inquired of me much about their contents through the same Barvitius, and the structure of Uraniborg drawn in the first volume pleased him greatly. But since he could not so quickly find the laboratory in it, he wished it indicated to him. All the rest pleased [him], so that Lord Barvitius reported to me that they were most welcome to the Emperor.

From what I have now recounted, most loving Holger, you have understood the state of my affairs, such as it now is. And I give thanks to God propitious, that he has so provided for me and mine, that I have been able to struggle out of those adversities which I long and much suffered in the fatherland, and to provide better for me and mine; and I hope hereafter, the same Divine Power favouring, for still more prosperous things. As for the political honours and dignities offered to me — nay, assigned even before I came to Prague, while I was still at Wittenberg (as you have perhaps understood either by word of mouth from Doctor Frantzius, son-in-law of Doctor Vitus Winshemius — for he said that, about to go off into East Frisia, he wished to visit you — or by letter, as he said he had received this from Lord Coraduz the Vice-Chancellor, when he was at Prague, to report to me, that I might come the more readily) — I wish to add nothing now. Certain friends at Prague urged me to undertake such things, which, however, I have always looked down on from on high. But if I am going to do it, it will be for the sake of your countrymen, who have held me in such insolent contempt; which I also said to our Thaddaeus when he pressed me. But he subjoined thus: You will admit it not only for the sake of your Danes, but also for the sake of our Bohemians — and indeed in the court itself some are of so dull and rustic a wit, and no less than your countrymen are ignorant how to discern between gold and brass; yet some are found who judge more rightly, and who, although they (as much as you) reckon political honours of small account in respect of virtue and exceptional learning, yet think it should be so done, for the sake of the common judgment, that one may live the more conveniently in the world; who, though few are found, yet prevail in judgment before the rest, and are most loving toward you — some of whom you have known thus far from this first meeting, some will become more fully known to you hereafter. Thus he. But of these I shall look later, if I do not altogether look down on them; for nothing in such matters can be done advisedly and discreetly before the return of the Magnificent Lord Coraduz the Vice-Chancellor from Rome, where he acts as Legate in the Emperor's name before the Supreme Pontiff, [and] who is expected about Michaelmas. Nor do I wish anything to be concluded in such matters beforehand. He is much favouring toward me, though we are as yet unknown by face, and most loving (as is said) of mathematical things too, in this a rival of his predecessor Curtius.

You now have, dearest Holger, the little history of my affairs, since I migrated with my people from Germany into Bohemia; of what was done before, and my departure from Denmark, you know well everything, so that there is no need to repeat what the mind, shuddering, shrinks from rehearsing. And certainly I seem to have written a little history rather than a letter, while, the pen slipping, I address you more fully, that you may be more fully informed of my affairs. Which prolixity, however, I persuade myself will not be unwelcome to you, since you do not, like others who wish to be held Nobles, disdain humane letters, and even huge volumes of books. But about the books promised you (that I may at last subjoin something), take it thus. I am not unmindful of the pact, nor hard to keep it; but since all the copies of the Mechanics of Astronomy, as many as are adorned with colours, or, as they call it, illuminated [lacuna] to be steeped in their colours at Prague — which, when it is done, you will obtain not only this first volume, but also the second, on the restitution of the fixed [stars], and likewise the third, on the corrected motion of the luminaries (which last, however, must be newly copied out from the first conception, since I have no clean copy at hand, after I offered the one written out at Wittenberg to His Imperial Majesty along with the rest). Be not, therefore, impatient of this delay, my Holger; you will have both these and others of mine, the Power favouring, as soon as it can be done, and not without interest. Meanwhile, leave to our sister Sophie those which I had destined for the King (for I have understood that she can obtain these, repudiated and given to Ramel) — which also does not displease me; for I prefer it so done, than that they should come into the hands of enviers and haters.

It remains that I stay my pen, unless it pleased me to add this one thing. The voluntary resignation of the island of Hven, which I inhabited there, which you advised (about which too I [said] I would write back more fully when I came here) — my mind is by no means to approve it, those also dissuading who are not perfunctorily versed in political matters; nay, [I am told] it would be imputed to me as a fault by some, as though out of contumacious insolence, after I had crossed over to the imperial side, I did this — as if I neglected my Danes, and what I had there, with a stubborn mind. But if those who [lacuna] are in charge should wish to take the island from me, on the ground that I betook myself to the Emperor, His Imperial Majesty would either take pains that the King of Denmark suffer no loss to be inflicted on me for his sake, or would compensate it, whatever it be, in another way. Let your countrymen object whatever they will, the answer will be easy, if only there be a place for justice and truth; but if not, let it pass with the rest, and let the just Judge decide all. And now, with your wife, my beloved niece, fare most happily, most upright Holger — she being greeted together with this most noble and honourable Mother of yours, and at the same time other friends, if any in your Cimbria still survive mindful of me, of whom I should believe at least some still remain. And again and oftener, fare most well, and make me, at the very first opportunity, fully informed of your affairs too.

Given from the imperial castle Benátky, the 9th of September, New Style, the Year of the Lord 1599.
Wholly yours,
Tycho Brahe,
with his own hand.

There is no leisure to read it over again; you yourself must look it over.

When these words had scarcely been set down, the Captain of Brandeis, who was dining here with me, was summoned to Prague to the Emperor; and after he had been there for ten days, having returned to me, he related that he had been summoned there solely on my account — so that, in the Emperor's absence (who was daily making ready to depart from Prague, on account of the plague and dysentery growing strong there, toward Linz), he might render me whatever services [I needed], and procure whatever I should desire, whether for building works or other things; and moreover indicate that whatever in my affairs could not be so quickly dispatched because of the Emperor's hurried departure should be completed afterward, once the Emperor had come to a quieter place. Meanwhile, however, while he was there, the Emperor, having learned that he had received from me as a gift some antidote against these epidemic diseases, demanded it from him through Lord Barvicius and kept it for himself, and gave orders that I should be written to, that I myself should instruct him in writing how it is prepared. Accordingly, in a letter given forthwith in Latin to His Imperial Majesty (of which you have here the substance), I most humbly set out what His Most Clement Majesty had desired. So you too have the description of the remedy devised by me and put to use against the present epidemic plague. Another copy I have shared with my sister Sophia, to be disclosed to her by you alone — which I also noted in the letter sent to her; otherwise keep it a secret on both sides.   • Approach. Met by Barvitius in a garden by the castle; the three books + the Cologne/Mecklenburg commendatory letters to be received "from no hand but Tycho's own"; the letter from Duke Otto of Brunswick-Harburg to Rumpf (Master of the Court), whom Tycho visits — Rumpf marvels the King let Tycho go ("attributing it to the King's youth, preferring Mars to Art"), and concludes that those "second to the King" must be "ignorant of the good arts, or malevolent and envious." A friend later reveals Barvitius engineered Tycho's castle visit so the Emperor could observe him in advance from the opposite chamber.
  • The private audience. The planned solemn introduction by Rumpf is dropped; Tycho enters alone to find Rudolf II alone, leaning on a table, not even a serving-boy present; the Emperor beckons with a nod, extends his hand; Tycho gives a Latin oration; presents the princely letters (opened in his presence) and the three books (fetched from son Tycho in the antechamber), with a summary of each; the Emperor replies "more copiously than I addressed him," smiling — "but I could not catch all the words, so softly does he speak by nature." Afterward the Emperor asks to see the automaton/clock in Tycho's carriage (glimpsed through a window).
  • Salary & fief. Via Barvitius over several days: 3,000 gold + >1,000 in perquisites, by Senate decree over the objection of long-serving courtiers; the Emperor's assurance "neither is his hand shortened, nor your mouth stopped"; a hereditary fief promised (promissory letters to come); 2,000 florins entry-honorarium; salary backdated to the summons.
  • Lodging & castle. Declines the Curtius house (Italian-built by the late Chancellor Jacob Curtius, >20,000 joachims, with an observation tower "scarcely big enough for one instrument"; a widow lives there, "now seeking new nuptials in the manner of young widows"); is shown three castles — Brandeis (3 mi., chief imperial seat, prime hunting), Lissa (4 mi.), Benátky (6 hrs)— and chooses Benátky, glossed (as in the Kepler letter) as "the Venice of the Bohemians" (the river Iser/Jizera ringing it before it joins the Elbe), in a town where the "Evangelical" faith and many Picards (Bohemian Brethren) live.
  • New Uraniborg. The Emperor's spontaneous offer to build, at his own cost, "a new Uraniborg castle exceeding the Danish one by many parasangs," he himself delighting in astronomy, mathematics, and architecture; he read Tycho's books "far into the night," and especially liked "the structure of Uraniborg drawn in the first volume."
  • Persons named. Thaddaeus = Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku (Thaddaeus Hagecius), the Bohemian imperial physician-astronomer and Tycho's Prague conduit; Rudolf II; Johann Barvitius (supreme Secretary); Johann Löser (host at Bretzia castle, en route); Duke Otto of Brunswick-Harburg; Wolfgang Rumpf (Rumphius, Master of the Court); Jacob Curtius (former Imperial Chancellor, builder of the Curtius house); the widow there; Coraduz (Corraducius, Vice-Chancellor, then Legate to the Pope at Rome, expected back by Michaelmas — "a rival of his predecessor Curtius" in loving mathematics); the Captain of Brandeis; Claus Mule (Claudius Mulaeus); Dr. Frantzius, son-in-law of Dr. Vitus Winshemius (Veit Winsheim), the conduit for the "political honours" offer; the Elector of Cologne (Ernst of Bavaria) and Duke of Mecklenburg (Ulrich III); King Christian IV and Frederik II; Sophie Brahe ("our sister") and Henrik Ramel (the King's repudiated books to pass to Sophie via Ramel); Holger's wife (Tycho's niece) and mother.
  • The three books. Tycho's gift-set, here itemised: (1) the Astronomiae Instauratae Mechanica (the illuminated/coloured copies to be finished at Prague); (2) the volume "on the restitution of the fixed stars" (the Progymnasmata star-catalogue material); (3) "on the corrected motion of the luminaries" (the lunar/solar theory) — the last to be re-copied from the first draft, since the Wittenberg fair copy went to the Emperor.
  • Latin tags & allusions. "Venetiae Bohemorum" — "Venice of the Bohemians" (Benátky ≈ Czech for Venice; cf. brahe-t_15991209001). "nec sibi esse accurtatam manum, nec mihi obturatum os" — the Emperor's assurance (echo of Isaiah 59:1; also in the Vedel relatio). "quae meminisse animus horrens iterare refugit" — echo of Virgil, Aeneid 2.12 (animus meminisse horret). "multis parasangis excederet" — "exceed by many parasangs," i.e. by far. "discernere inter aurum et orichalcum" — to tell gold from brass/copper-ore (true worth from false). "more iuvencularum viduarum" — "in the manner of young widows." P. M. = Pontifex Maximus (the Pope). "Picardis" — the Picards, a pejorative name for the Bohemian Brethren (Unitas Fratrum).
  • Lacunae (the crux). The witness is defective chiefly in: (a) the perquisites figure ("accidentaria …"); (b) "Vicit tamen Caesaris …ci ut sic fieret ursit"; (c) the Senate-letters / compensation passage ("nisi literae a toto Senatu … me …"); and most heavily (d) the castle-tour passage — the naming of the rejected castles, and the provenance of Benátky (acquired by the Emperor at the start of 1599 from the discordant heirs of a deceased lord, "a …ruvij cuiusdam senio confecti et defuncti" — the name reads like Rudský / Rožmberk?, irrecoverable here); and (e) the Mechanica-colouring clause and (f) "ij, qui … praesunt" (those in charge of Hven). All are reproduced as dots in the source and marked [lacuna] in the translation; surrounding sense is given, exact wording is not.
  • Postscript, badly damaged. The recovered segment is the closing postscript of Tycho's letter to his nephew-in-law Holger ("Oliger") Rosenkrantz, written from the imperial castle at Benátky nad Jizerou (Tycho's residence outside Prague), dated 9 September 1599 N.S. The first half is intact and coherent; the second half (from "Vides insuper..." on) survives only in fragments separated by lacunae that break individual clauses, not just paragraph joins — consistent with damage or loss in the underlying source rather than a simple digitised text layer gap.
  • The Captain of Brandeis and imperial favor. The unnamed "Capitaneus Brandeisensis" is the castellan of Brandýs nad Labem, the crown castle that would later become Tycho's own Bohemian residence (the same title recurs elsewhere in Tycho's 1599 correspondence — Tycho Brahes Breve). Here he acts as Rudolf II's personal intermediary to Tycho during the Emperor's flight from a plague/dysentery outbreak in Prague toward Linz, empowered to "procure whatever [Tycho] should desire" for building works and other needs.
  • The plague antidote and Barvicius. Rudolf II, on learning that Tycho had given away an antidote against the "epidemic diseases" then raging, had it reclaimed for himself through Jan Barvitius — Rudolf's supreme secretary and privy councillor, and Tycho's regular high-level contact at court (cf. Tycho's own later letter of 7 May 1600 addressed to "IOHANNI BARVITIO, Sacrae Caesareae Maiestatis Secretario supremo", preserved in the register of Tycho's correspondence). Tycho reports writing directly and confidentially to the Emperor himself, under seal, "so that none of the Councillors might open it" — a striking index of his private access to Rudolf — and says he sent a second copy of the antidote recipe to his sister Sophie Brahe, "to be disclosed to her by you [Holger] alone."
  • Imperial court in flight; war news from the east (uncertain reconstruction). The fragmentary remainder appears to report the Emperor's court "moved/transferred" away from plague-stricken Prague, and news of fighting involving "the hero of Wallachia" and a "Cardinal" to whom "Sigismund" is said to have ceded or appointed something. If the surviving words are read rightly, this most plausibly garbles the Transylvanian crisis of 1599 — Sigismund Báthory's cession of Transylvania to his cousin Cardinal Andrew Báthory, and the resistance of Michael the Brave, voivode of Wallachia (who defeated and killed the Cardinal that autumn). This identification is the translator's own historical inference from the surviving fragments, not verified against the literature, and should be checked against a specialist account of the Long Turkish War before being asserted as fact.
  • Named associates: Cramer and Duncan. Two further individuals surface in the ruined final third — "Cramerum tuum meumque" (a "Cramer" known to both Tycho and Holger) and "Duncan," almost certainly Duncan Liddel, the Scottish mathematician-physician who studied under Tycho and later taught at Rostock/Helmstedt, well attested elsewhere in Tycho's circle (Mosley, Bearing the Heavens; Christianson, On Tycho's Island; Thoren, Lord of Uraniborg) — but the surrounding clauses are too damaged to recover what is being said about either man.
  • A Danish fragment. Embedded in the Latin is one broken sentence of Danish, which cannot be reconstructed across the lacuna and is rendered here as literally as the surviving words allow.
  • Correction. English corrected from "more than 20,000 joachims" to "more than 20 joachims", matching both the house Latin transcription and Dreyer's printed text (TBOO VIII, p. 164, line 37) — a thousandfold translation error.

The translation and its checking

Grade Secure · collated against Dreyer's page images, Opera Omnia VIII pp. 162–172; defects the audit noted: mistranslation (1) (the translator's own grade before the audit was Possible).

Collated against Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia (the edition of record), reading the printed page images. Read Dreyer TBOO VIII pp.162-172 in full via the page images and collated the opening, closing, and every proper name, number, date, sum of money, and place-name against both the transcribed original and the Dreyer print. Confirmed the flagged defect: Dreyer prints '20 Ioachymicorum', not 20,000 — the published English's '20,000 joachims' is a threefold-zero inflation with no counterpart in either the transcribed original or Dreyer. All other numbers (3000 aureorum, 1000, 2000 florenorum, three/four miles, six hours, ten days, eight days, quindecim/fifteen, two decennium references, the 9 September 1599 date) and all proper names (Rosenkrantz, Thaddaeus, Löser, Barvitius, Rumpf, Duke Otto, Jacob Curtius, Coraduz/Corraducius, Frantzius, Vitus Winshemius, Claus Mule/Claudius Mulaeus, Friderich, Sophie, Ramel, Brandeis, Lissa, Benátky/Benachia, Cramer, Duncan, Sigismund Báthory) check out against the Dreyer print. Bracketed lacuna-supplements in the badly mutilated closing postscript (pp.171-172) are properly marked as editorial and are not counted as defects.

1 defect found:
- mistranslation (high). Both our transcribed original and Dreyer's printed page read '20 Ioachymicorum' (20 Joachimsthalers) for the cost of building and gardening the Curtius house; the English inflates this by three orders of magnitude to '20,000 joachims' with no textual warrant. At: “at a cost of more than 20,000 joachims”.

Repaired. Verified directly against the Dreyer page image (TBOO VIII, p. 164), which prints 'expenfis maioribus quam 20 Ioachymicorum'; the house Latin transcription already agrees with Dreyer at '20 Ioachymicorum', so only the English required repair. No other defects were flagged for this letter's opening pages (162-165), which were also reviewed in the course of locating the defect site.
