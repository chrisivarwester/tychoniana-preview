English translation machine translation, Secure

Tycho Brahe to Christen Longomontanus.

Greeting.

Dearest Master Christen, I have written to you twice since I came here to Wittenberg — both which letters I think you have received, or at least one of them. I could wish that you would come to me here, before I undertake the journey toward Prague again. It will be done to your profit. If you lack travel-money, the carrier here will supply it to you; I will repay him. I understood from your [letter] written to me before you departed from Wandsbek that you desire a position in some Academy here in Germany. [Signify to me whether] you wish to succeed that fellow at Prague who boasts himself the Emperor's Mathematician (for he will shortly withdraw himself, if it has not already been done); but if it should not please you, I will bring it about that […] be summoned thither — and he does not shrink from undertaking [this] office; [then] I will give my effort that you be substituted here at Wittenberg in his place […], since, with you present, [I could say] more things to […]. By whatever other means seek […]; for your advantages, as much as is in me, I will strive.

Farewell.
Hastily, at Wittenberg, amid very many occupations, the 11th day of January, in the year [15]99.
Tycho Brahe,
in his own hand.

[Postscript:] POSTSCRIPT. Here you have letters from Braschius the Poet, sent to me from Dresden, and opened by accident.

Apparatus

  • Recipient. Christen Sørensen Longomontanus (Christian Severin "Longomontanus," 1562–1647), Tycho's longest-serving and ablest Danish assistant, later professor at Copenhagen and author of the Astronomia Danica (1622). At the start of 1599 he had recently left Tycho's service (after the Wandsbek period) and was seeking an academic post in Germany; Tycho here tries to draw him back before departing for Prague.
  • "That fellow at Prague who boasts himself the Emperor's Mathematician" = Nicolai Reimers Bär (Ursus), the Imperial Mathematician and Tycho's plagiarism-adversary (see brahe-t_16000727001). Tycho — confident that Ursus "will shortly withdraw himself" (Ursus left Prague in 1599 and died in 1600) — dangles the imperial mathematicianship before Longomontanus, with a fallback scheme to install Longomontanus at Wittenberg instead.
  • viaticum — travel-money; auriga — the carter/driver who would advance it.
  • ⚠ Textual lacunae. The witness (a Langebek transcript) is damaged, with several gaps marked by the editor's ....... (and one bracketed restoration, s[ignifica mihi an]). The lost passages name (a) the substitute Tycho would have "summoned" to Prague in Longomontanus's stead, and (b) the precise Wittenberg arrangement; these are not reconstructed here. Gaps are rendered "[…]"; the bracketed editorial restoration is kept as printed.
  • Calendar. Witebergae … die 11. Ian. Anno 99 = 11 January 1599, Wittenberg (Lutheran Saxony) = Old Style. Header agrees with the label. (Written two days after the Mecklenburg quittance brahe-t_15990110001, also from Wittenberg.)
  • A postscript recovered entire. This letter's existing English translation currently ends flatly at the signature, with no postscript at all; the gap restores the opening of one, enclosing a letter (or letters — Latin "literas" is grammatically plural but idiomatically often covers a single letter) from "Braschius the Poet," sent from Dresden, which Tycho says he had opened by accident.
  • Identifying Braschius. An independent letter from Nicolaus Braschius to Tycho Brahe, dated 8 August 1600, Rostock, is on record (Basel, Universitätsbibliothek, G I 23; brahe-t_16000808001 in this corpus) — confirming Braschius as a real, otherwise-attested correspondent active in Tycho's circle in this period, evidently a poet with Saxon/Dresden connections.
  • The courier detail. "Fortuito resignatas" (opened by chance) is a small courtesy-note: Tycho is explaining, to Longomontanus, why an enclosure addressed elsewhere arrives already unsealed.

The translation and its checking

Grade Secure · collated against Dreyer's page images, Opera Omnia VIII pp. 135–136 (the translator's own grade before the audit was Possible).

Collated against Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia (the edition of record), reading the printed page images. Read TBOO VIII p.136 (Dreyer, 'Tycho Brahe ad Christianum Longomontanum') as a page image; letter entire on this page. The Latin is genuinely damaged in the original (Dreyer himself prints '.......' lacunae and bracketed conjectural supplements '[hoc]', '[tunc]', 's[ignifica mihi an]'); our transcribed original reproduces every lacuna, bracket and conjectural word in the same positions as Dreyer, letter for letter. The English translation handles every gap correctly with matching bracketed/ellipsis markers and does not smuggle in invented content to fill the damaged spans; the intact clauses (travel money, the succession to the 'Caesaris Mathematicum' at Prague, the postscript about Braschius' letter from Dresden) are all rendered accurately. Despite the letter's the translator's own grade Possible (presumably a caution flag for the heavy lacunae), collation found no fabrication, omission, or mistranslation — the damaged passages are honestly marked as such in both source and translation, which is the correct practice, not a defect.
