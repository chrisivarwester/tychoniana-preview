NOBILISSIMO ET VIRTVTE SAPIENTIA ACA INGENIOSA ERVDITAQVE DOCTRINA, CLARISSIMO VIRO DOMINO TYCHONI BRAHE, DOMINO DE KNVDSTRVP, EQVITI DANO, MATHEMATICO NOSTRO SECULO PRÆSTANTISSIMOA, ETC. DOMINO SUO CUM OBSERUANTIA COLENDO, ETC. S.P.

MAXIMA Illustrissimus Princeps noster affectus est lætitia Literis tuis, Vir Nobilissime et Doctissime, Mathematicorum præstantissime, tum quod reconditiorem Astronomiæ Scientiam ac Regium Ingeniosorum Instrumentorum apparatum, acutissimumqueA de Obseruationibus longe aliter instituendis iudicium continerent; tum quod abs te, quem propter summam in Astronomicis rebus peritiam summe diligit, proficiscerentur. Etsi autem Illustriss. ipsius Celsitudo seorsim Literis tuis est responsura; mihi tamen mandauit, vt et ipse, de Mathematicis præsertim, tuæ excellent. responderem. Ne igitur longis vtar ambagibus, cum Illustrissimus Princeps Scriptum tuum de Cometa nancisceretur, mihi id inspicere non licuit, Illustriss. ipsius Celsitudine prohibente, donec et ipse meum de eodem Cometa scriptum attulissem, in quibus cum Obseruationes in tam longe dissitis locis institutas, sibi inuicem ad minutum vsque respondere videremus, dici vix potest, quanto simus gaudio perfusi. Quo igitur et tu eandem concordantiam videre possis, tibi vicissim, meum de eodem Cometa Scriptum transmitto, præsertim cum Capite V. contineat disputationem de Refractione Stellarum, cuius tu in Literis tuis mentionem facis, quanquam nondum sit absolutum, deficientibus nimirum in fine tribus Capitibus, quæ propter occupationes absoluere non potui. Quod si quædam differentia in locis Cometæ constitutis reperitur, ea esse potest, et propter locorum, in quibus nostras Obseruationes habuimus, disiunctionem, et propter loca Stellarum fixarum non eodem forte modo assumta, et propter diuersam Zodiaci obliquitatem, quam ego in meis Obseruationibus assumsi 23 gr. 30 min. tantam nimirum quantam tot annos Illust. Princeps noster adinuenit. Quanquam autemA Instrumenta in ijs Obseruationibus non adeo, vt nunc, fuerunt exacta, nec etiam Parallaxeos aut Refractionis ratio habita, malui tamen Obseruationibus, quam Tabularum annotatis respondentem Zodiaci obliquitatem assumere, præsertim cum ipse adinuenerim, Obseruationes eas non multum a Veritate abesse. Quanquam ipse Solstitialem Solis Altitudinem in æstate propter ventosam et nubilosam Aëris constitutionem nondum exacte obseruare potuerim. Etsi veroA non dubito, quin tua, quam asseris Zodiaci obliquitas sit vera: eam tamen et ego, DEO benignum successum, benignamque Aëris constitutionem largiente, diligentissime obseruabo, vt ita Veritas diuersis diuersorum locorum fulcita Obseruationibus, eo maiorem fidem apud posteritatemA mereatur. Ad Obseruationum calculum in CometaA assumsi Oculum ♉ in 4 grad. 7 min. ♊, cum Latitudine 5 grad. 29 min. Meridionali. Tertiam ♈ Stellam in 1 grad. 58 min. Tauri, cum Latitudine Septentrionali 9 gr. 57 min. etc. vt nos diligentissimis nostris Obseruationibus adinuenimus, nec video eas multum a tuis discrepare. De barba Cometæ, quod scribis, eam 21 Octobris non habuit. At 22 Octobris sub Occasum eam quoque hîc animaduerti. Verum cum fallaciam ex Lunæ Radijs esse iudicarem (vergebat enim ad Lunam) nec tale quicquam antea depræhendissem, eam Scripto meo inserere nolui. Quod autem Cometam adhuc 12 NouembrisA obseruasti, ipse profecto 10 Nouembris eum ob nimiam raritatemA per Instrumenti rimulas obseruare non potui, nec etiam 10 Nouembris amplius videre; quanquam Aër erat impurus acA vaporosus. Forte eum ob nimiam raritatem vaporibus similem, præ vaporibus depræhendere non potui. Nullam omnino Parallaxin tam per accuratissimum illum, quem Scripto meo inserui, Parallaxes obseruandi modum, quam per tempus et distantiam eius a Meridiano, depræhendere in eo potuiA, quamuis diligentissime eam venarer. Differentia, quæ exigua in extremis Cometæ fulsionibus inter nostras Obseruationes interuenit, fortassis est a nimia Cometæ raritate. Equidem semper proportionalem in eo adinueni motum. Sed hæc hactenus de Cometa. Ad Instrumenta nostra quod attinetA, attulit nihil WITTICHIVS, præter partitionemA (quæ, quicunque Autor sit, ingeniosa certe estA) et Sextantis nomen, et Pinnacidiorum rimulasA. Et quanquam Wittichius putabat Instrumenta eaA correctiora fieri non posse, nos tamen post eius discessum multis modis ea correximus. Fecerat enim Wittichius per Automatopæum nostrum simplices tantûm in visorijs rimulas, in Quadrante quidem duas, alteram in superna visorij parte, alteram ad Latus, in Sextante vero tantum vnicam, in centro scilicet visus. Verum cum Instrumenta in vsum deducerem, multa tandem experientia inueni, eiusmodi rimulas non sufficere, nec quicquam certi depræhendi ijs posse. Quapropter in Sextante duas alias adhuc rimulas, ad Latera Pinnacidiorum, prope oculum fieri curaui, vt distantiæ Stellarum per rimulas Parallelas vtrinque cernerentur. Cumque Wittichius nullam rationem Anguli visionis habuisset, huic quoque malo succurrimusA, atque visoria Centro Instrumenti ita apposuimus, vt semper eandem ab illo Sextantis Centro distantiam retineant. Centrumque semper præcise in medio mediæ rimulæ reperiatur. Ac ne Angulus visus sit minor eo quem Instrumentum nobis exhibet, singulis Pinnacidijs superioribus interne tantum detraximus, quantum inferius respondens Pinnacidium a Centro distat. Ita enim per lineas parallelas eundem Angulum cernimus, quemA Instrumentum nobis exhibet, nec vlla differentia inter Angulum visum et Angulum Instrumenti esse potest. Præterea cum antea media Regula in Sextante manibus hinc inde ducenda, in Obseruationibus plurimum negocijA nobis facesseret, multumqueA temporis requireret, priusquam in eum, quo debebat, locum essetA perducta, Catenulam artificiose Instrumento apposuimus, per quam in quemcunque locum et Angulum regulam mediam facilime absque negocio deducere possumus. In Quadrantis quoque inferiore Pinnacidiorum parteA, adhuc rimulam inferne fieri curaui, vt Altitudinem Stellæ, et superne et inferne paralleliter videre possim. Nisi enim Stella per rimulas paralleliter circa Pinnacidium æqualiter secta conspiciatur, nequaquam certa erit eius Altitudo. Iamdudum etiam ad Latera Pinnacidiorum rimulas Parallelas exaptaui, vt transitum Stellæ vtrinque videre possem. Quod si Wittichius rimulas illas Parallelas apud Tuam Excell. vidit, nec Instrumentis nostrisA apposuit, multo nos inani labore onerauit. Cum enim visoria Instrumentorum Mense Martio præteriti Anni corrigeremus, inuenimus ObseruationesA ante visoriorum correctionem institutas, prorsus fuisse incertas et falsasA. Quapropter omnes de nouo iterare coacti sumus. Reliqua quæ de Instrumentis nostris desideras, ex domestico tuo cognoscere poteris. De diuerso Ætheris et Aëris diaphano velim etiam atque etiam cogites. Ipse profecto rem in omnes partes voluens ac reuoluens, nihil tale reperire potui. Sed cum Sententiam ea de re meam in Tractatu de Cometa Capite V satis superque declarauerim, nolo hîc actum agere: Tantum peto a te maiorem in modum in salutem et certitudinem Astronomiæ etiam atque etiam, vt ea diligenter examines, atque ad ea mihi respondere nonA dedigneris. Video enim ob nullam aliam rem magis huic pulcherrimæ Arti detrahi, quam quod (vt ij, qui quid Hypotheses Astronomicæ sint, non intelligunt, arbitrantur) futilibus et falsis Hypothesibus sit referta, perque falsas HypothesesA contra naturam, contraque naturæ Leges verum Planetarum motum demonstret. Vt igitur veternum illum ex animis vulgi eriperem, atque quid sibi vellent Hypotheses Astronomicæ, ostenderem, luculenter et perspicue, vt opinor, ex ipsa rei natura, quantum per occupationes licuit, de ijs disserui, præsertim cum diu multumque expertus sim, etiam eos, qui se multum in hac Arte profecisse voluntA, in sinistra hac inueniri opinione, seductiA, vt arbitrorA a futilitatibus Aristotelis, qui contra experientiam tantum futilibus coniecturis, de his rebus disputat. Refractionem vsque ad 30 ab HorizonteA gradus in Sole reperire non potui, nec etiam in Horizonte dimidij gradus, sed plerunque 15, aut ad summum 20 minutorum. Sed eam maiore in posterum diligentia non tantum in Sole, verumetiam in Stellis fixis obseruabo. Quemadmodum et ex dextro Latere Persei in Altitudine 9 gr. 42 min. inueni Refractionem 2⅓ minutorum. Ex Cauda Cygni in Altitudine 5 gr. 18 min. inueni Refractionem 5⅔ minutorum. Inuenio etiam ex Obseruationibus diligentissimis, Stellas fixas suas mutare LatitudinesA, contra aliorum traditionemA, idque fieri proportionaliter secundum mutationem obliquitatis Zodiaci, ita vt quo magis ad Solstitia accedant, eo magis Latitudines suas mutent, adeo vt Stellæ immotæ videantur; Ecliptica vero pro mutatione obliquitatis hinc inde ad easA accedere et recedere. Quod veroA interdum quædam Stellæ huic suppositioni reclament, id (vt arbitror) Obseruationibus veterum tribuendum erit, in quibus Sextantes, aut etiam Trientes graduum non fuerunt apud ipsos magni momenti, vel etiam eiusmodi error in transcribendo interuenire potuit; quanquam videam, dum recentiores Ptolemæum in Stellis fixis emendare voluerunt, in longe maiores errores esse delapsos, vt ex Alanac, Algorab, etc. manifestum est. Motum Solis in Ephemeride tua positum satis exacte cum nostris Obseruationibus invenio congruere: a quibus tamen Tabulæ Prutenicæ circa Æquinoctium vernum vltra dimidium gradum absunt. Ipse etiam tantam fere Solis Eccentricitatem reperio, quantam tu asseris. Altitudo Poli hîc Cassellis est 51 grad. 20 min. Inueni enim per diligentissimas Obseruationes Stellam Polarem, in superioriA Meridiani parte, Altitudinem obtinere 54 grad. 16 min. in inferioreA 48 grad. 24 minut. Pectus Cassiopeæ superne 87 gr. 3 min. inferne 15 gr. 37 min. In Ascensu medio Cathedræ Cassiopeæ superne 84 gr. 26½ min. inferne 18 gr. 13½ min. Ad genua Cassiopeæ superne 83 gr. 16½ min. inferne 19 gr. 23½ min. In tibia Cassiopeæ superne 79 gr. 44½ minut. inferne 22 grad. 55 min. Quod si Eclipsin illam, quæ Anno 1584, 7 Nouembris apparuit, obseruasti, iam quoque differentiam Longitudinum inter nostra loca habebimus. Obseruauit enim WITTICHIVS hîc Cassellis eius medium incidisse in 13 horam 3 minut. post Meridiem; quanquam video, iustam diligentiam in ea Obseruatione non esse adhibitam. Ac toties iam dolui, me eo tempore hîc nondum fuisse (octiduo enim post huc veniebam) vtinam per distantias ad Stellas fixas non tantum Eclipsin, verumetiam motum et Parallaxin Lunæ obseruasset. Sin eam Eclipsin obseruare non potuisti, alia nobis via ingredienda erit, vt differentiam inter Meridianos nostrorum locorum nanciscamur; quod commode per Lunam fieri posse, puto, si eam vna et eadem Die aut etiam diebus non multum a se distantibus vterque simulA diligenter Obseruauerimus. Ita enim separata Parallaxi, per motum ex Obseruationibus inuentum facile quæsitum patebit. Sin per calculum malueris (quæ ratio tamen mihiA minus certa videtur) poteris calculum Lunæ Meridiano nostro congruentem ad me mittere. Ita enim si Lunam circa nonagesimum grad. obseruauero, ex tuo constituto motu, si isA Apparentijs satisfacit, facile quæsitum inueniam. NarrauitA quoque mihi PETRVS IACOBI domesticusA tuus, te per Studiosum tuis correctissimis Instrumentis, Eleuationem Polarem et Altitudinem Meridianam Spicæ ♍ FruenburgiA Borussiæ, vbi COPERNICVS plerasque suas Obseruationes habuit, obseruasse. Cum igitur perlibenter scirem, quænam fuisset certitudo Instrumentorum, quibus in suis Obseruationibus vsus essetA Copernicus, et an ea FruenburgiA esset Poli Eleuatio, quam Copernicus Libro III. Capite 2 asserit, peto a te maiorem in modum, vt eas mecumA communicare nonA dedigneris. Vicissim tibi mea officia polliceor ac defero. Illustrissimus Princeps mittit ad te Obseruationes suas de Cometis, et Altitudines Solis Meridianas per vetera Organa obseruatas, quas pro tua prudentia ad vsum accommodare, et cum tuis conferre poteris. Mittit item nostras Stellarum fixarum per noua Organa institutas a me Obseruationes, quas calculo per Studiosos tuos examinabis, deque ijs nos admonebis. D. IOACHIMVS CAMERARIVS tibi salutem; cuius Literas ad te perscriptas inter reliqua reperies. His bene et feliciter vale.

Cassellis 18 Calend. Maij, Anno Christi 1586.
T. T.

English translation machine translation, Probable

To the most noble and, in virtue, wisdom, and ingenious and erudite learning, most renowned man, Master Tycho Brahe, Lord of Knudstrup, Danish knight, the most outstanding mathematician of our age, etc., his lord to be honoured with [all] observance, etc. — heartiest greeting.

Our Most Illustrious Prince was filled with the greatest joy by your letter, most noble and most learned man, most outstanding of mathematicians — both because it contained the more recondite Science of Astronomy and the royal apparatus of ingenious Instruments, and a most acute judgement concerning Observations to be conducted in a far different manner; and because it came from you, whom, on account of your supreme skill in astronomical matters, he most highly esteems. And although His Illustrious Highness himself will reply to your letter separately, he has nevertheless charged me to reply to Your Excellency on my own part, especially regarding mathematical matters. So then, not to use long circumlocutions: when the Most Illustrious Prince received your treatise on the Comet, I was not permitted to inspect it — His Illustrious Highness forbidding it — until I too had brought forward my own treatise on the same Comet; and when in these [treatises] we saw the Observations conducted in places so far apart correspond to one another down to the [very] minute, it can scarcely be said with how great a joy we were flooded. So that you too may see this same concordance, I send you in return my treatise on the same Comet, especially since in Chapter V it contains a disputation on the Refraction of the Stars, of which you make mention in your letter — although it is not yet finished, three Chapters being lacking, of course, at the end, which on account of [my other] occupations I could not complete. But if some difference is found in the positions established for the Comet, it may arise both from the distance between the places at which we had our Observations, and because the positions of the fixed Stars were perhaps not assumed in the same way, and because of the differing obliquity of the Zodiac, which I assumed in my Observations [to be] 23 degrees 30 minutes — just so much, indeed, as our Illustrious Prince has found over so many years. And although the Instruments in those Observations were not so exact as now, nor was account even taken of Parallax or Refraction, I nevertheless preferred to assume the obliquity of the Zodiac corresponding to the Observations rather than to the annotations of the Tables, especially since I myself have found that those Observations are not far from the Truth — although I have not yet been able to observe the solstitial Altitude of the Sun exactly in summer, on account of the windy and cloudy condition of the Air. And though indeed I do not doubt that the obliquity of the Zodiac which you assert is the true one, I too, [if] God grants a favourable outcome and a favourable condition of the Air, will observe it most diligently, so that the Truth, thus supported by the various Observations of various places, may merit the greater credit with posterity. For the calculation of the Observations on the Comet I assumed the Eye of Taurus [♉] at 4 degrees 7 minutes of Gemini [♊], with a southerly Latitude of 5 degrees 29 minutes; the Third Star of Aries [♈] at 1 degree 58 minutes of Taurus, with a northerly Latitude of 9 degrees 57 minutes, etc., as we have found them by our most diligent Observations; nor do I see them differ much from yours. As to what you write about the beard of the Comet, it did not have it on 21 October. But on 22 October, towards sunset, I too noticed it here. Yet since I judged it to be a deception from the Rays of the Moon (for it inclined towards the Moon), and had not detected any such thing before, I did not wish to insert it into my treatise. As for your having observed the Comet still on 12 November — I for my part could not observe it on 10 November through the slits of the Instrument, on account of its excessive faintness, nor could I see it any longer [even] on 10 November, although the Air was impure and vaporous. Perhaps, on account of its excessive faintness, [it being] like the vapours, I could not detect it against the vapours. I could detect in it no parallax at all, neither by that most accurate method of observing parallaxes which I inserted into my treatise, nor by the time and its distance from the Meridian, though I hunted for it most diligently. The difference, slight, that occurs between our Observations at the extreme [points of] the Comet's shinings, is perhaps from the excessive faintness of the Comet. I, for one, always found its motion proportional in it. But so much for the Comet.

As regards our Instruments, Wittich brought nothing except the partition (which, whoever its Author be, is certainly ingenious), and the name of the Sextant, and the slits of the Sights. And although Wittich thought that those Instruments could not be made more correct, we nevertheless, after his departure, corrected them in many ways. For Wittich had made, by means of our Instrument-maker, only single slits in the sight-vanes — in the Quadrant indeed two, one in the upper part of the vane, the other at the Side, but in the Sextant only one, namely at the centre of sight. But when I brought the Instruments into use, I found at length, by much experience, that slits of that kind do not suffice, nor can anything certain be detected by them. Therefore in the Sextant I had two further slits made, at the Sides of the Sights, near the eye, so that the distances of the Stars might be discerned through Parallel slits on both sides. And since Wittich had taken no account of the Angle of vision, we relieved this fault too, and so fitted the sights to the Centre of the Instrument that they always keep the same distance from that Centre of the Sextant; and [so that] the Centre [of the eye] is always found precisely in the middle of the middle slit. And lest the Angle seen be smaller than that which the Instrument shows us, we took away from each of the upper Sights, on the inner side, just as much as the corresponding lower Sight stands from the Centre. For thus, by parallel lines, we discern the same Angle that the Instrument shows us, and there can be no difference between the Angle seen and the Angle of the Instrument. Moreover, since formerly the middle Rule in the Sextant, having to be moved this way and that by hand, used to cause us very much trouble in Observations and to require much time before it was brought to the place where it ought [to be], we ingeniously fitted to the Instrument a little chain, by which we can most easily, without trouble, bring the middle rule to whatever place and Angle [we wish]. In the lower part of the Sights of the Quadrant too, I had a further slit made below, so that I might see the Altitude of the Star both above and below in parallel. For unless the Star is seen, through the parallel slits, cut equally around the Sight, its Altitude will by no means be certain. Long since, too, I fitted Parallel slits to the Sides of the Sights, so that I might see the transit of the Star on both sides. But if Wittich saw those Parallel slits with Your Excellency, and did not fit them to our Instruments, he burdened us with much vain labour. For when we were correcting the sights of the Instruments in the month of March of last year, we found that the Observations conducted before the correction of the sights had been utterly uncertain and false. Therefore we were compelled to repeat them all anew. The rest that you desire concerning our Instruments you will be able to learn from your servant. About the differing transparency of the Aether and the Air, I would have you think again and again. I myself, indeed, turning and turning the matter over on all sides, could find nothing of the sort. But since I have declared my opinion on that matter in my Treatise on the Comet, Chapter V, abundantly and more than enough, I do not wish here to do what has [already] been done. I only ask you, all the more, for the welfare and certainty of Astronomy, again and again, that you examine these matters diligently, and do not disdain to reply to them for me. For I see that nothing more detracts from this most beautiful Art than that (as those who do not understand what Astronomical Hypotheses are suppose) it is stuffed with futile and false Hypotheses, and through false Hypotheses, contrary to nature and contrary to the Laws of nature, it demonstrates the true motion of the Planets. So then, to snatch that drowsiness from the minds of the common crowd, and to show what Astronomical Hypotheses mean, I have discoursed of them clearly and perspicuously, as I think, from the very nature of the thing, so far as my occupations allowed — especially since I have long and much experienced that even those who claim to have advanced far in this Art are found to be in this perverse opinion, seduced (as I judge) by the futilities of Aristotle, who, against experience, disputes of these things only by futile conjectures. I could not find a Refraction in the Sun [greater than] up to 30 degrees from the Horizon, nor [even] at the Horizon [as much as] half a degree, but for the most part 15, or at most 20 minutes. But I will observe it in future with greater diligence, not only in the Sun but also in the fixed Stars. Just as, from the right Side of Perseus, at an Altitude of 9 degrees 42 minutes, I found a Refraction of 2⅓ minutes; from the Tail of the Swan [Cygnus], at an Altitude of 5 degrees 18 minutes, I found a Refraction of 5⅔ minutes. I find also, from most diligent Observations, that the fixed Stars change their Latitudes, contrary to the tradition of others; and that this happens proportionally according to the change of the obliquity of the Zodiac, so that the more they approach the Solstices, the more they change their Latitudes — to such a degree that the Stars seem unmoved, while it is the Ecliptic that, with the change of obliquity, approaches and recedes from them this way and that. And as for the fact that sometimes certain Stars protest against this supposition, that (as I judge) will have to be ascribed to the Observations of the ancients, in which Sextants, or even Thirds, of degrees were not of great moment with them, or else an error of that kind may have crept in in the transcribing — although I see that, while the more recent [astronomers] wished to emend Ptolemy in the fixed Stars, they have fallen into far greater errors, as is plain from Alnath, Algorab, etc. I find the motion of the Sun placed in your Ephemeris to agree quite exactly with our Observations — from which, however, the Prutenic Tables differ, around the vernal Equinox, by more than half a degree. I too find almost as great an Eccentricity of the Sun as you assert. The Altitude of the Pole here at Kassel is 51 degrees 20 minutes. For I have found, by most diligent Observations, that the Pole Star, in the upper part of the Meridian, has an Altitude of 54 degrees 16 minutes, in the lower [part] 48 degrees 24 minutes. The Breast of Cassiopeia above 87 degrees 3 minutes, below 15 degrees 37 minutes. In the mid-Ascent, the Throne [Chair] of Cassiopeia above 84 degrees 26½ minutes, below 18 degrees 13½ minutes. At the knees of Cassiopeia above 83 degrees 16½ minutes, below 19 degrees 23½ minutes. In the shin of Cassiopeia above 79 degrees 44½ minutes, below 22 degrees 55 minutes. But if you observed that Eclipse which appeared in the Year 1584, on 7 November, we shall now also have the difference of Longitudes between our places. For Wittich observed here at Kassel that its middle fell at the 13th hour [and] 3 minutes after Noon — although I see that due diligence was not employed in that Observation. And so often have I grieved that I was not yet here at that time (for I came hither a week later); would that he had observed, by distances to the fixed Stars, not only the Eclipse but also the motion and Parallax of the Moon! But if you could not observe that Eclipse, another way will have to be entered upon by us, so that we may obtain the difference between the Meridians of our places; which I think can conveniently be done by means of the Moon, if we both observe it diligently together on one and the same Day, or even on days not far distant from one another. For thus, the Parallax being separated out, the thing sought will easily be made plain through the motion found from the Observations. But if you prefer [to proceed] by calculation (a method which nevertheless seems to me less certain), you will be able to send me a calculation of the Moon agreeing with our Meridian. For thus, if I observe the Moon around the ninetieth degree, [then] from your established motion — if it satisfies the Appearances — I shall easily find the thing sought. Peter Jacobi, your servant, also told me that you, through a Student [of yours], with your most accurate Instruments, observed the Polar Elevation and the Meridian Altitude of Spica [♍] at Frauenburg in Prussia, where Copernicus had most of his Observations. Since, then, I would most gladly know what the certainty was of the Instruments which Copernicus used in his Observations, and whether the Elevation of the Pole at Frauenburg was that which Copernicus asserts in Book III, Chapter 2, I ask you, all the more, that you not disdain to communicate these things with me. In turn I promise and offer you my services. The Most Illustrious Prince sends to you his Observations of the Comets, and the Meridian Altitudes of the Sun observed by the old Instruments, which, by your prudence, you will be able to adapt to use and to compare with your own. He sends likewise our Observations of the fixed Stars, conducted by me by the new Instruments, which you will examine by calculation through your Students, and of which you will advise us. Master Joachim Camerarius [sends] you greeting; whose letter, written out to you, you will find among the rest. With these [words], fare well and happily.

At Kassel, the 18th [day before] the Kalends of May, in the Year of Christ 1586.
Yours truly,
Christoph Rothmann of Bernburg.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Christophorus Rothmannus Bernburgensis = Christoph Rothmann (c. 1550/60 – c. 1600), of Bernburg, court mathematician-astronomer to Landgrave Wilhelm IV of Hesse-Kassel. This is one of his earliest letters in the great Rothmann–Brahe technical exchange (begun 1586), printed by Tycho in the Epistolarum astronomicarum libri of 1596.
  • Recipient / titulature. Tychoni Brahe, Domino de Knudstrup, Equiti Dano = Tycho Brahe, Lord of Knudstrup, Danish knight (the edition's convention, Knudstrup as is). The honorific stack and S.P. (= salutem plurimam, "heartiest greeting,") are rendered, not dropped.
  • The Illustrious Prince. Illustrissimus Princeps noster / Celsitudo = Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel, Rothmann's patron and a practising observer; he is sending Tycho his own cometary and solar observations alongside this letter. Celsitudo rendered "[His] Highness" by the edition's convention
  • Persons named. Wittichius = Paul Wittich (d. 1586), the itinerant Silesian mathematician who had been at Hven and then at Kassel (1584–85), and whose role in transmitting Tycho's instrument designs is the subject of Rothmann's pointed remarks here. Petrus Iacobi = Peter Jacobsen (Flemløse), one of Tycho's assistants/famuli ("your servant," domesticus tuus). Copernicus = Nicolaus Copernicus; the reference is to De revolutionibus Book III, ch. 2, and to his observing site at Fruenburg/Fruenburgi Borussiae = Frauenburg (Frombork) in Prussia. Ioachimus Camerarius = Joachim Camerarius the Younger (1534–1598), the Nuremberg physician-humanist. Aristoteles named as the source of the "futilities" Rothmann attacks.
  • Places. Cassellis / Cassell = Kassel. Fruenburgi Borussiae = Frauenburg (Frombork), Royal/Ducal Prussia.
  • Comet. The comet under discussion is the comet of 1585 (observed Oct.–Nov. 1585 at Hven and Kassel). barba Cometae rendered "the beard of the Comet" (the cometary tail/coma extension); fulsiones = "shinings" (flashes/appearances of brightness), kept descriptive; raritas = "faintness/thinness," the comet's tenuity. Scriptum / Tractatus de Cometa = Rothmann's treatise on the comet (his Observationum stellarum… cometary tract), here described as incomplete (three chapters wanting).
  • Astronomy — concepts (glossary). Parallaxis → parallax; Refractio → refraction; Eccentricitas → eccentricity; obliquitas Zodiaci → obliquity of the Zodiac/ecliptic; Altitudo → altitude; Meridianus → meridian; Longitudo / Latitudo → (celestial) longitude / latitude; motus → motion; Ecliptica → ecliptic; Aequinoctium vernum → vernal equinox; Eclipsis → eclipse; Altitudo Poli / Eleuatio Polaris → altitude / elevation of the pole (the observer's latitude). Aether / Aër diaphanum → "the transparency of the Aether and the Air" — a genuine physical-optical question (whether the heavens and the air have different refractive transparency), kept literal.
  • Astronomy — instruments. Sextans → sextant; Quadrans → quadrant; Organa / Instrumenta → instruments; Pinnacidia → sights; visoria → sight-vanes / sights; rimulae → slits (the sighting-slits in the vanes); Regula media → the middle rule (the sextant's moving radius); Catenula → little chain (the fine-adjustment chain Rothmann fitted). partitio → partition (the graduated division of the arc — the transversal-point division Wittich is credited with carrying). Automatopaeus → Instrument-maker / mechanic (the court's instrument-builder; literally "maker of self-acting devices"); rendered "our Instrument-maker." Angulus visionis / Angulus visus → "the Angle of vision / the Angle seen" (the parallax-of-sight error from eye placement that Rothmann's twin-slit fix addresses).
  • Symbols. ♉ = Taurus ("the Eye of Taurus" = the star Aldebaran, α Tauri); ♊ = Gemini; ♈ = Aries ("the Third Star of Aries"); ♍ = Virgo ("Spica," α Virginis). The fractions 2⅓ and 5⅔ minutes are kept as written. Trientes graduum = "thirds of degrees" (a unit of arc precision).
  • Star names. Alanac, Algorab = the fixed stars Alnath (β Tauri / γ Aurigae) and Algorab (δ Corvi) — cited as examples where post-Ptolemaic emenders erred. Alanac is the source's printed form (for Alnath/Elnath); kept as printed, identified here. Pectus / Cathedra / genua / tibia Cassiopeae = the Breast, the Throne (Chair), the knees, the shin of Cassiopeia (the standard descriptive star-positions of the constellation).
  • Tables. Tabulae Prutenicae = the Prutenic Tables (Reinhold, 1551); Ephemeris tua = Tycho's [solar] ephemeris/computed solar motion. Tabularum annotata = "the annotations of the Tables" (tabulated/received values), contrasted with direct observation.
  • Dateline crux (primary). The subscription reads "Cassellis 18 Calend. Maij, Anno Christi 1586" = a.d. XVIII Kalendas Maias 1586 = 14 April 1586 (O.S.; Hesse-Kassel and Denmark were on Old Style). The Roman-calendar idiom is rendered with the idiom and glossed. The edition label ("1587. 18. maj. Kassel") disagrees on both counts: it reads the year as 1587 (the source says 1586) and silently mis-renders "18 Calend. Maij" as "18 May" rather than the inclusive-count Roman date (a.d. XVIII Kal. Mai. = 14 April). The header therefore leads with the as-written date (14 April 1586) and appends the catalogued date. The reading 14 April 1586 is independently corroborated by A. Mosley, Bearing the Heavens (Cambridge, 2007), which cites this very letter as "Christoph Rothmann to Tycho Brahe, 14 April 1586." The identifier encodes the (incorrect) catalogue date 1585-05-18 and is retained as an opaque identifier only.
  • In-letter dating note (not reconciled). Rothmann discusses two distinct events with their own dates: the comet observations of Oct.–Nov. 1585 (21–22 Oct., 10/12 Nov.), and a lunar eclipse of 7 November 1584 observed by Wittich at Kassel ("Eclipsin illam, quae Anno 1584, 7 Nouembris apparuit"), proposed as a means of fixing the longitude difference Kassel–Hven. These dates are reproduced as written; none is converted.
  • Negation check. The negations are reproduced one-for-one and none added or dropped — notably the polite-request formula "ad ea mihi respondere non dedigneris" / "eas mecum communicare non dedigneris" = "do not disdain to reply / to communicate" (a single negation each, an idiom of courteous request, not a prohibition); "mihi id inspicere non licuit" = "I was not permitted"; "eiusmodi rimulas non sufficere, nec quicquam certi" (two coordinated negations) = "do not suffice, nor [can] anything certain…"; "Wittichius nullam rationem Anguli visionis habuisset" = "had taken no account"; "Nullam omnino Parallaxin… depraehendere… potui" = "I could detect no parallax at all." The contrary-to-fact wish "vtinam… obseruasset" ("would that he had observed") is kept as an unfulfilled wish, not asserted as fact.
  • Conditional/mood note. Rothmann's two-branch proposal for fixing the longitude difference is kept in its conditional structure: "Sin eam Eclipsin obseruare non potuisti…" ("But if you could not observe that Eclipse…") → the lunar-distance method; "Sin per calculum malueris…" ("But if you prefer [to proceed] by calculation…") → send a Moon-calculation. The parenthetical hedge "(quae ratio tamen mihi minus certa videtur)" ("a method which nevertheless seems to me less certain") is preserved, not sharpened.
  • Epistolary subscription. T. T. = Tuus totus / Tibi totus ("Yours wholly / truly"); rendered "Yours truly." Bernburgensis = "of Bernburg" (Rothmann's origin), kept as a cognomen.
  • Transcription artefact (flagged, not in the original text). The working transcription carries stray capital "A" characters fused to word-endings throughout (e.g. PRAeSTANTISSIMOA, acutissimumqueA, Quanquam autemA, vale.A, 1586.A, ROTHMANNVS Bernburgensis.A). These are footnote/variant-apparatus reference markers from the digital base text, not part of Rothmann's Latin; they are reproduced verbatim in the Source block by the edition's convention(2) but are ignored in establishing the reading and do not appear in the translation. A doubled-text dittography in the transcription — "Stellas fixas suas mutare Latitudinessuas mutare Latitudines" — has been read once ("Stellas fixas suas mutare Latitudines", "the fixed Stars change their Latitudes"); the printed-as-received form is preserved in the Source block and the single correct reading explained here.
