The original

CLARISSIMO ET PRÆSTANTISSIMO VIRO DOCTORI IO. IACOBO GRYNÆO, APUD BASILIENSES VERBI MINISTRO ET IN INCLYTA ILLA ACADEMIA PROFESSORI CELEBERRIMO, AMICO SUO HONORANDO. BASEL.

CLARISSIME et praestantissime vir, quas in Decembri superioris anni ante semestre Basileâ ad me dedisti literas, recte quidem accepi, quibus tamen rescribendi nulla sese obtulit interea commoditas, antequam idem, qui tuas reddidit tabellarius, e Dania reversus, et ad vos denuo pergens, hac transiret, quare moram hanc candide accipias rogo. Non possum non ingentes tibi agere gratias, optime GRYNÆE, quod de me, utut longa viarum intercapedine dissito, tibique de facie ignoto, adeo benevole et sincere sentires, et ea quae mihi ob quorundam in Patria livorem et malevolentiam, crassâ veri ignorantiâ, sive per imprudentiam, sive datâ operâ, non dixerim, coniunctam ab inexpectato, et aliter quam forte promeritus eram, evenerunt, tibi adeo cordi esse sinas, ut huic rerum nostrarum conturbationi condoleas, meque consoleris et confirmes. Debemus equidem omnes prae caeteris Regionibus, plurimum Patriae, quae nos edidit, excepit et fovit. At quid faciendum, si haec gratitudinem nostram respuat, neque bene merita agnoscat, imo pro his quaevis incommoda et damna machinetur otiumque et commoditatem virtuti rebusque bonis operam navandi interturbet atque praeripiat? Et quis quaeso Patriae suae mores atque iudicia subinde perversa in hac mundanae scenae confusione et irregularitate unquam corriget? praesertim hoc saeculo, quando omnia videntur in peius ruere. Ferenda igitur sunt forti et constanti animo, quae caveri et emendari nequeunt, et de verâ illa ac Coelesti potius Patriâ omnis turbulentiae et enormitatis experte, serio cogitandum eoque anhelandum. Interim vero dum in hoc terrestri versamur exilio, eas quae nobis obveniunt graviores interdum adversitates, quam ijs sustinendis pares simus, siquidem avertere non datur, loci mutatione declinare atque evitare licitum existimo; ideoque et ego a gravioribus iniurijs, quas non paucas a quibusdam nostratium, qui nunc fatis Reipublicae ferme soli praeesse volunt, pertuli, totque fluctibus et tempestatibus in Germaniam discedendo, auxiliante Coelesti Numine, me vindicavi: sperans tranquilliorem et tutiorem me hîc inventurum portum. Quam arduae vero caussae me eo impulerint, verbis eloqui non facile datur, nec satis consultum arbitror, si quis ea, quae in Insula illa Hellesponti Danici, quam inhabitavi, molitus sum tot praeterlapsis annis, quaeque abiens isthic effecta reliqui, conspexerit expenderitque, non obscure vel hinc colliget gravissima fuisse illa, quae me haec omnia posthabere et Patrium solum deserere totamque familiam in Germaniam transferre adegerunt. Habet praesens tabellarius secum librum quendam hîc per otium hybernum excusum, quem meo permissu Nepoti ex fratre OTTHONI BRAHE, qui cum suo praeceptore in vestra laudatissima Academia aliquandiu antea versatus est, filius meus natu maior dono mittit; hunc si tantum tibi a gravioribus officij tui curis superest otij, ut quaedam in eo perlustrare vacet, mandavi tabellario, ut dum isthic moratur, tibi inspiciendi copiam faciat; cognosces hinc utcunque, veluti opinor, praesertim ex ijs, quae appendicis loco de structuris addidi, et quae infra Insulae topographiam consignata sunt, mihi antea non fuisse in animo talia unâ cum tota Patria totque in ea sanguine iunctis et amicis derelinquere. Adiuncta sunt eidem libro quaedam carmina manu scripta, quae hanc mutationem aliquatenus attingunt, quod si ea etiam percurrere commodum fuerit, erit tibi id liberum. Sed desino de his plura.

Ego hanc sortem, quae mihi contigit, lubens et fortiter, Divinâ favente clementiâ, feram, nec quidpiam inde movebor aut cursum a virtutis tramite remittam, quamvis mihi unâ cum meis extra Natale solum in peregrinis oris reliquum vitae, prout Deo visum fuerit, transigendum sit, nec enim Patriam hisce terreni globi angustis limitibus metior. Sed perinde esse duco, ubi quis in huius terrae superficie degat, modo bene et commode degat, nec contra conscientiam quidpiam agere aut admittere cogatur. Domino HENRICO RANZOVIO, affini et amico meo honorando, literas tuas praelegi curavi, illique ex te salutem dixi; is vicissim te peramanter resalutat, gratumque illi fuit, quod tam honorifice de illo scripseris et senseris. ANTHONIUM RANZOVIUM nondum convenire licuit, neque mihi constat, ubi is moretur; cum dabitur occasio, huic etiam tuo nomine salutem nunciari curabo. Plura nunc ob occupationum molem addere non vacat. Haec itaque qualiacunque subito effusa boni consulas, et amicitiam inter nos semel per literas contractam hac etiam ratione, siquidem aliter non liceat, conservare non desinas. Deus te cum tota domo incolumem conservet et a malis tueatur.

Dabantur Wandesburgo die 7. Iunij Anni 98.
Tuae excell. studiosiss.
TYCHO BRAHE,
manu propria.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the most renowned and excellent man, Doctor Johann Jacob Grynaeus, minister of the Word among the people of Basel and most celebrated professor in that renowned Academy, his honoured friend. Basel.

Most renowned and excellent man: the letter you sent me from Basel half a year ago, in December of last year, I did indeed duly receive — but no convenient means of writing back offered itself in the meantime, until the same carrier who delivered yours, returning from Denmark and proceeding again to you, passed this way; wherefore I beg you take this delay in good part. I cannot but give you the greatest thanks, excellent Grynaeus, that — of me, though sundered by a long stretch of road and unknown to you by face — you should think so benevolently and sincerely, and should let those things which befell me, through the envy and malevolence of certain men in my fatherland (out of gross ignorance of the truth, whether through imprudence or by design I will not say — combined, unlooked-for, and otherwise than perhaps I had deserved), so touch your heart, that you grieve with me at this disturbance of my affairs, and console and strengthen me. We all owe, beyond other regions, most to the fatherland that brought us forth, received and cherished us. But what is to be done, if it spurns our gratitude, and does not acknowledge good deserts — nay, contrives against them all manner of harms and losses, and disturbs and snatches away the leisure and convenience for devoting one's labour to virtue and good things? And who, pray, will ever correct the manners and the often perverse judgements of his fatherland, in this confusion and irregularity of the worldly stage — especially in this age, when all things seem to rush to the worse? The things, then, that cannot be guarded against and amended must be borne with a brave and constant mind; and we must think seriously of, and pant after, that true and Heavenly fatherland, free of all turbulence and enormity. But meanwhile, while we dwell in this earthly exile, the graver adversities that sometimes come upon us — heavier than we are equal to bearing — if we are not granted to avert them, I hold it licit to decline and avoid by a change of place. And so I too, from the graver injuries (not few) which I suffered from certain of my countrymen — who now wish to preside almost alone over the fortunes of the Commonwealth — and from so many waves and tempests, by departing into Germany, with Heaven's aid, vindicated myself, hoping to find here a calmer and safer harbour. By what hard causes I was driven thither it is not easy to express in words; yet I think it not ill-advised [to say that] whoever shall have beheld and weighed what I wrought, over so many past years, on that Island of the Danish Hellespont which I inhabited, and what I left finished there on departing, will gather even hence, not obscurely, that those things were most grievous which forced me to set all this behind me, to desert my native soil, and to transfer my whole family into Germany. The present carrier has with him a certain book, printed here over the winter's leisure, which, with my permission, my elder son sends as a gift to my brother's son, Otto Brahe — who for some time previously studied, with his tutor, in your most praised Academy. If you have so much leisure left over from the graver cares of your office that you may scan some things in it, I have charged the carrier, while he stays there, to give you the chance of inspecting it. You will recognise hence in some measure, as I think — especially from what I added by way of appendix, concerning the structures, and what is set down below as the topography of the Island — that it was not formerly in my mind to abandon such things, together with my whole fatherland and so many bound to me there by blood and friendship. There are also joined to the same book certain poems in manuscript, which touch this change [of fortune] in some degree; if it be convenient to run through these too, that is free to you. But I cease to say more of these matters.

This lot which has befallen me I will bear gladly and bravely, by Divine clemency favouring, nor will I be moved by it at all, nor slacken my course from the path of virtue, although I, together with mine, must pass the remainder of my life outside my native soil, on foreign shores, as it shall seem good to God; for I do not measure my fatherland by these narrow limits of the terrestrial globe. But I judge it all one where a man lives on the surface of this earth, provided he live well and conveniently, and be not compelled to do or admit anything against conscience. I had your letter read aloud to Lord Heinrich Rantzau, my honoured relation and friend, and gave him your greeting from you; he in turn most lovingly returns your greeting, and it was welcome to him that you wrote and thought so honourably of him. Anton Rantzau I have not yet been able to meet, nor do I know where he stays; when occasion is given, I will see that a greeting be announced to him too in your name. More I have no leisure to add now, on account of the mass of occupations. So take in good part these things, of whatever quality, suddenly poured out; and do not cease to preserve, by this means too (if it may not be otherwise), the friendship once contracted between us by letter. May God preserve you, with your whole house, unharmed, and guard you from evils.

Given at Wandsbek, the 7th of June, the year [15]98.
Most zealous of your excellency,
Tycho Brahe, in his own hand.

Apparatus

  • Recipient. Johann Jacob Grynaeus (1540–1617), the Basel Reformed theologian, antistes and professor — an admirer who had written to console Tycho (a stranger by face) over his troubles. The exchange is courteous and warm; this is Tycho's reply.
  • Significance — Tycho's apologia for leaving Denmark (one of the central documents of the self-exile). The letter is Tycho's reasoned, dignified justification for abandoning Hven:
  • The meditation on Patria: we owe most to the fatherland that bore us, but "if it spurns our gratitude … contrives all manner of harms … snatches away the leisure for virtue" — who can correct a fatherland's perverse judgements? One must bear what cannot be amended and long for the "true and Heavenly fatherland."
  • The doctrine of licit departure: when adversities exceed our strength and cannot be averted, "I hold it licit to decline them by a change of place" — hence Tycho's withdrawal "from the graver injuries I suffered from certain of my countrymen, who now wish to preside almost alone over the fortunes of the Commonwealth" (a pointed allusion to the regency faction under Christian IV's minority — Christoffer Valkendorf and the council).
  • The cosmopolitan close: "I do not measure my fatherland by these narrow limits of the terrestrial globe" — it matters not where on earth one lives, provided one live well and not against conscience.
  • Hven named — "the Island of the Danish Hellespont." Insula illa Hellesponti Danici, quam inhabitavi = Hven, in the Øresund (the "Danish Hellespont," the Sound). Tycho invokes "what I wrought there over so many years, and what I left finished on departing" as silent proof that he never meant to abandon it.
  • The enclosed book = the Astronomiae Instauratae Mechanica (Wandsbek 1598) — librum quendam hîc per otium hybernum excusum, "a book printed here over the winter's leisure," whose self-description clinches the identification: it contains an appendix "concerning the structures" (the descriptions of Uraniborg and Stjerneborg) and "the topography of the Island" (the Hven map) — exactly the Mechanica's closing matter. Tycho's elder son sends a gift copy to Otto Brahe (Tycho's brother's son, formerly a student at Basel), routed via Grynaeus. (A documented Mechanica gift-distribution event — see cross-references below.)
  • The manuscript poems. Carmina manu scripta … quae hanc mutationem … attingunt — handwritten poems "touching this change of fortune," i.e. Tycho's elegiac verses on his departure from Denmark (the Elegia addressed to Dania / Urania that circulated with the exile), sent loose with the book.
  • The Rantzaus. Heinrich Rantzau (Henricus Ranzovius, 1526–1598), the great Holstein statesman-humanist and Tycho's host-patron (Wandsbek was his estate), to whom Tycho read Grynaeus's letter aloud — Rantzau returns warm greetings. Anton Rantzau, a kinsman, not yet met.
  • Persons & places named. Johann Jacob Grynaeus; Otto Brahe (nephew, at Basel); Tycho's elder son (unnamed; Tycho Brahe the Younger); Heinrich Rantzau; Anton Rantzau; the unnamed Danish regency faction. Places: Wandsbek, Basel, Hven ("Island of the Danish Hellespont"), Denmark, Germany.
  • Cross-references. A documented Mechanica distribution event — the gift copy (Tycho the Younger → Otto Brahe, via Grynaeus, Basel, June 1598) belongs to the documented distribution history of the Mechanica; the apologia itself belongs with the Hven-surrender / self-exile thread.
  • Calendar. Holstein was Old Style in 1598; no stylo marker; dateline 7 June 1598 read Old Style (per the catalogue label). Header left unqualified to match.
  • Correction. Latin corrected against Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Opera Omnia VIII, page 70: "aliquando" corrected to "aliquandiu"; the English clause describing Otto Brahe's time at Basel, which had followed the corrupted reading, corrected from "once before studied" to "for some time previously studied".
