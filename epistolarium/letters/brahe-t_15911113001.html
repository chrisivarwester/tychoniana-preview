The original

TYCHO BRAHE AD IOANNEM METELLUM.

GRATISSIMUM mihi fuit, Magnifice Domine IOANNES METELLE, quod comitando Serenissimum Regem vestrum me in hac Insula invisere non gravatus sis, quodque discedens, illa ingenij exquisitam acrimoniam redolentia Ianuæ annotâris Epigrammata, quæ a pluribus postea ob acumen et elegantiam descripta sunt. Quod si pari aut etiam uberiore (ista enim obiter et inter aliud agendum subito concinnata erant) venâ nostram effigiem, cuius exemplar existimo me tibi antea misisse (ut tamen certo habeas, en alterum addidi) decorâris, rem inprimis acceptam feceris, teque ipsum in quibusdam meorum Operum Cithara Apollinea personantem, circa ipsum Frontispicium cognosces. Scripsi et hoc nomine Domino PETRO IUNIO, deque quibusdam alijs, in quibus et tuum expeto, sicubi opus fuerit, unâ cum consilio, auxilium. Si illi insuper Hortulanum et Aucupem primo vere mihi procurare commodum non fuerit, ut per te officij hoc mihi præstetur, peramanter rogo. Unicum insuper a te petam, quod et sponte mihi, cum hîc esses, pollicebaris, ut videlicet Dominum IOANNEM CRAIGIUM Medicinæ Doctorem hancque Edenburgi facientem et Mathematum insuper excellenter peritum, quantum in te est (est autem quamplurimum) ad potiora promovere, eiusque rei familiari, tua apud Regiam Maiestatem intercessione, ut commode et bene degat, consulere, si modo id hactenus oblivione quadam prætermissum est, ne graveris. Dignus enim is mea sententia est, qui ametur, iuvetur, promoveatur. Quamvis enim ex omnibus Europæis Mathematicis, ad quos noster liber secundus de recentioribus Ætherei Mundi Phænomenis (quem hîc, ni fallor, vidisti) pervenit, is solus inventus est, qui contentis eiusdem nonnullis contradicere, mecumque de eisdem per literas disputare attentârit, tacentibus, imo de sua opinione prorsus cedentibus, eamque revocantibus, nostris non invite subscribendo, præcipuis peneque cunctis inter illos, quorum ego in eodem scripto Placita sub veritatis incudem revocaveram, deviationesque aperueram et modeste taxâram. Attamen tantum abest, ut id ullo pacto in ipso moleste feram, quod etiam hoc mihi apprime gratum sit, læterque me in illo invenisse Antagonistam, qui ausus fuerit nostris nonnulla quocumque tandem ratiocinationis prætextu exponere. Sic enim veritas ipsa, expensis semotisque ijs, quæ in contrarium adduci a quoquam plausibiliter poterint, rectius et plenius elucescit. Quemadmodum ex Apologia nostra, quam etiam (si recte memini) hîc inspexisti, illius argumentationes affatim diluente, non obscure liquet. Quam et magnificus Dominus IACOBUS CURTIUS, Cæsareæ Maiestatis atque Imperij Procancellarius, in literis ad me datis, quarum exemplum Domino PETRO IUNIO transmisi, tum etiam alij eruditi in Germania Mathematici, libere et lubenter approbârunt. Si itaque effeceris, ut vir ille sentiat meam commendationem apud te pondus habuisse, sibique emolumento fuisse, non tam illi quam mihi rem plurimum acceptam exhibueris, meque tibi vel hoc nomine, utut alias de te bene mereri satagentem, arctius devinxeris. Vale, plura enim non addam, ne te gravibus et varijs administrationis publicæ munijs occupatum nimium interpellem.

Iterum atque iterum quam felicissime vale.
Datæ Uraniburgi 13. Novembris Anno 1591.
TYCHO BRAHE

TYCHO BRAHE TO JOHANNES METELLUS.

It was most gratifying to me, Magnificent Master Johannes Metellus, that in accompanying your Most Serene King you did not think it a burden to visit me on this Island, and that on departing you inscribed upon my Gate those Epigrams that breathe an exquisite keenness of wit, which afterwards were copied out by many for their acuteness and elegance. But if you would adorn my likeness — a copy of which I think I sent you before (yet, that you may have it for certain, see, I have added another) — with a vein equal or even more abundant (for those [epigrams] were composed in passing, on the spur of the moment and amid other business), you would do a thing especially welcome to me, and you would recognise your very self, sounding forth on Apollo's lyre, around the very Frontispiece of certain of my Works. I have written on this account also to Master Peter Young, and concerning certain other matters, in which I seek your help too, wherever there may be need, together with your counsel. If, besides, it should not be convenient for him to procure me a Gardener and a Fowler by early spring, I most affectionately ask that this service be rendered me through you. One thing further I shall ask of you, which of your own accord you promised me when you were here: namely, that — so far as lies in your power (and it is very much) — you not be reluctant to advance Master John Craig, Doctor of Medicine, practising the same at Edinburgh and besides excellently skilled in the mathematical sciences, to better things, and to see to his domestic estate, by your intercession with the Royal Majesty, so that he may live comfortably and well — if only this has hitherto been passed over through some forgetfulness. For he is, in my judgement, worthy to be loved, helped, and advanced. For although, out of all the European mathematicians to whom my second book on the more recent Phenomena of the Aetherial World (which here, unless I am mistaken, you saw) has reached, he alone has been found who has attempted to contradict some of its contents and to dispute with me about them by letter — while the rest stayed silent, nay, wholly yielded their own opinion and recanted it, subscribing not unwillingly to mine, [I mean] the foremost and almost all among those whose Tenets I had in that same writing called back to the anvil of truth, whose deviations I had laid open and modestly censured — yet so far am I from taking this amiss in him in any way, that it is even highly pleasing to me, and I rejoice to have found in him an Antagonist who dared to set forth some objections to my views under whatever pretext of reasoning at last. For thus truth itself, once the things that anyone could plausibly bring against it have been weighed and set aside, shines forth more rightly and more fully. As is plainly evident — not obscurely — from my Apology, which too (if I rightly remember) you examined here, and which abundantly dissolves his argumentations. This [Apology], moreover, the magnificent Master Jacob Curtius, Vice-Chancellor of the Imperial Majesty and of the Empire, in a letter sent to me — a copy of which I forwarded to Master Peter Young — and then too other learned mathematicians in Germany, have freely and gladly approved. If therefore you bring it about that that man perceives my recommendation to have carried weight with you and to have been to his profit, you will have done a thing most welcome not so much to him as to me, and you will have bound me to you more closely, on this account too, however much I am otherwise eager to deserve well of you. Farewell — for I shall add no more, lest I interrupt you too much, occupied as you are with the weighty and various duties of public administration.

Again and again, may you fare most happily.
Given at Uraniborg, 13 November, the year 1591.
TYCHO BRAHE

Apparatus

  • Sender / recipient. Tycho Brahe writing from Uraniborg to Johannes Metellus. Metadata discrepancy flagged: the edition's recipient field reads "John Maitland," but the source heading (TYCHO BRAHE AD IOANNEM METELLUM) and the vocative (Magnifice Domine IOANNES METELLE) unambiguously address Metellus, not Maitland. The translation follows the source. Iohannes Metellus = Jean Matal / Johannes Metellus (c. 1520–1597), the Burgundian-born humanist and geographer settled at Cologne, who had visited Hven in the train of the Scottish king and there inscribed Latin epigrams on the gate of Uraniborg. (John Maitland of Thirlestane, James VI's Chancellor, was a genuine but separate Tycho correspondent; the edition has evidently mislabelled this letter.)
  • Serenissimum Regem vestrum — "your Most Serene King": James VI of Scotland (later James I of England), who visited Uraniborg in the spring of 1590 while in Denmark for his marriage to Anne of Denmark; Metellus was in the royal party.
  • Petrus Iunius — Peter Young (1544–1628), James VI's former tutor and his ambassador/envoy to Denmark; the intermediary through whom Tycho repeatedly worked on Scottish matters. The errand of procuring "a Gardener and a Fowler" (Hortulanum et Aucupem) reflects Tycho's recruitment of skilled servants for the Hven establishment.
  • Ioannes Craigius — John Craig (d. 1620), Scottish physician to James VI, formerly Professor of Logic and Mathematics at Frankfurt an der Oder, then practising medicine at Edinburgh. From 1588 he was Tycho's lone correspondent-critic on the comet of 1577; Tycho's whole point here is to commend him for patronage despite — indeed because of — that dispute.
  • liber secundus de recentioribus Aetherei Mundi Phaenomenis — the second book of De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis (Uraniborg/Wandsbek, 1588), Tycho's treatise on the comet of 1577, in which (esp. the closing chapters) he reviewed and "censured" the placita (tenets) of earlier comet-writers. Rendered "the more recent Phenomena of the Aetherial World."
  • sub veritatis incudem revocaveram — "called back to the anvil of truth": a metalworking metaphor (the views re-forged/tested on truth's anvil); kept as image. Placita = "Tenets / [settled] opinions"; deviationes = "deviations [from the truth]"; modeste taxâram = "I had modestly censured" (Tycho's own hedge on the sharpness of his criticism — preserved).
  • Antagonistam — "an Antagonist": the Greek-derived term kept; Tycho frames Craig positively as the only adversary willing to engage, by whose opposition "truth itself … shines forth more rightly and more fully."
  • Apologia nostra — "my Apology": Tycho's defence of the comet book against Craig's objections (the Apologia later worked into the dispute material; cf. Tycho's long 1595 answer to Craig). illius argumentationes affatim diluente = "abundantly dissolving his argumentations" — diluere in the sense of refuting/dissolving an argument.
  • Iacobus Curtius, Caesareae Maiestatis atque Imperij Procancellarius — Jacob Kurz (Curtius) von Senftenau (d. 1594), Imperial Vice-Chancellor (here Procancellarius) under Rudolf II, an astronomically learned official and Tycho correspondent who had approved the Apology. Procancellarius rendered "Vice-Chancellor."
  • Cithara Apollinea personantem, circa ipsum Frontispicium — "sounding forth on Apollo's lyre, around the very Frontispiece": Tycho invites Metellus to compose verse to appear on the frontispiece engraving of his Works (the engraved author-portrait, nostram effigiem, of which he encloses a fresh copy). Frontispicium kept as "Frontispiece."
  • Magnifice Domine … Magnificus Dominus — the honorific Magnificus ("Magnificent") rendered as such for both Metellus and Curtius, per the dignity of the address. Domino Petro Iunio etc.: Dominus → "Master," by the edition's convention
  • Epistolary formulae. Vale … Iterum atque iterum quam felicissime vale = "Farewell … Again and again, may you fare most happily" (the doubled valediction; the edition's convention). Datae Uraniburgi = "Given at Uraniborg."
  • Calendar. 13. Novembris Anno 1591 — 13 November 1591, Old Style (Denmark kept the Julian calendar). This agrees with the edition label ("1591. 13. november. Uraniborg"); no in-letter date discrepancy. The header date is taken from the dateline as written.
  • Place. Uraniburgum → Uraniborg, Tycho's observatory-residence on the island of Hven (hac Insula, "this Island").
