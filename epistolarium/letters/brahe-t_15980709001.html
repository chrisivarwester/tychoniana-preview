The original

IOSEPHUS SCALIGER AD TYCHONEM BRAHE.

S. P.

DISCESSUS tuus ex Dania, Vir generosissime, ut nobis acerbissimus fuit, ita ancipites animi nos semper habuit, quem locum fortunis tuis deligeres. Ubi enim illae sitae forent, ibi et animo tuo et serijs studijs tuis aliquid quietis partum iri confidebamus, idque eo impensius optabamus, quo propensior nostra in te semper fuisset voluntas; itaque cum primum accepissem, te Rostochij subsedisse, non mediocriter me consolabar, quod ex eo, quod vicinum Daniae tuae locum optasses, te cito in patriam rediturum ominabamur. At vero non prius te Rostochium commigrasse renuntiatum, quam ex alijs accepimus, te iam inde pedem extulisse. Ab eo tempore frustra semper quaesivimus de statu rerum tuarum ac de sedibus, quas tibi constituisses. Denique nihil certi ad nos de te ferebatur praeter infelicitatem patriae tuae, quae te carere posset. Quare incerta animi mei de statu rerum tuarum sententia non passa est me officio illo erga te fungi, quod tu prior occupasti. Testis est mei consilij iuvenis quidam Mathematicus, qui hîc agit et in vestris domesticis se fuisse profitetur. Ab eo primum ante aliquot dies te in Arce Ranzoviana degere rescivi et recte se curaturum, si quid ad te literarum vellem. Id meditantem oppressit me adventus Nobilis adolescentis, FRANCISCI TEGNAGLIJ domestici vestri, qui tuas plenas amoris, humanitatis, eruditionis mihi attulit, ut nondum constituerim, utrum mihi gratuler de tuis illis humanissimis literis, an doleam hoc officium a te praereptum fuisse.

Quod de meo opere de emendatione temporum aliter iudicas ac imperiti, quanquam in eo agnosco amorem tuum in me, tamen exprimet confessionem ab illis, me non operam lusisse, vel eo nomine, quod tanto Viro mea placuerint, praesertim φιλαληθεστάτῳ et harum rerum intelligentissimo. Invidia neque nunc primum neque primi afflati sumus, cui opportunum se facit, quisquis aliquid supra captum plebeculae facit. Livore ab huiusmodi perstringi, est laborum suorum fructum percipere. Si scirent, quantum addant gloriae nostrae importunitate sua, cautius agerent, quam agunt. Ego horum neminem vidi, nisi imperitissimum, et quod pluris est, nequissimum. Quicquid scribunt, id vero chartae detrimento est, non existimationis nostrae. De simiolo Heydelbergensi, quem trophaea MILTIADIS non sinunt dormire, minus mirarer, si THEMISTOCLES esse posset. Sed imitari bonos aliorum hominum est, non stercoreorum et barbarorum et ab omni morum humanitate remotissimorum. Sed quid mirer, cum inveniat secutores et admiratores? εὗρεν ἄλοκας τὸ πῶμα. Quid in literis praestiterimus, non est pudoris nostri dicere. Hoc unum ingenue profitemur, neminem vivere, qui plures ad insaniam scriptis suis adegerit. Sed, ut fit, non unius generis homines sunt. Alij a nobis discunt et dissimulant, ne nobis aliquid acceptum ferant. Alij palam carpunt, et tamen nihil illis tam aegre est, quam quod illorum auctores non sint, propter quae tam hostili animo nos petunt. Sed quid de hac faece generis humani te morer? igitur de illo opere nostro, quod institueram dicere, fateor me plus laboris, quam industriae illo contulisse. Vix enim aliter mihi persuadere possum, et nobiscum praeclare agi putamus, si studiosiores quam doctiores habemur. Moneo autem me hominem imbecillis ingenij non potuisse tantam telam percurrere, ut non aliquando humanitus dormitarim, cum aliud pro alio contra animi nostri sententiam nobis excidit: quorum partem in Erratis correximus, partim imprudentes omisimus, quale illud, »Saturnum in annuo motu duodecimam partem unius gradus efficere«, quum vellemus dicere duodecim gradus, de quo et similibus possum iurare sancte: ἡ χεὶρ βέβληκεν, ἡ δὲ φρὴν ἀπόσκοπος. Quare, vir generosissime et doctissime, eiusmodi παροράματα potius quam ἁμαρτήματα, qualia multa in EUDOXO divinus HIPPARCHUS civiliter excusat, et quae ingenui Lectores emendare, non exagitare debent, tuum quoque est aut ἀπροσκόπως καὶ ἀπταίστως praeterire, aut, si per graviores tuas occupationes tibi licet, corrigere. At illa parva dictu menda retrimentis istis sunt argumenta novae foeturae librorum. Quod Septembrem vocamus, quem modo Octobrem, et feriam quartam, quam modo tertiam dixeramus, hoc typographias excalfacit. Barbaria et impudentia invenit ibi quod agat. Divinae aequinoctiorum observationes tuae locum invenerunt in calce librorum nostrorum, non utique quem debebant, sed quem potuerunt. Non enim prius illas acceperamus. Et tamen ibi sunt a nobis collocatae, ubi convenientissime cum Hipparcheis conferri possunt. Sane illud continuum quinquennium omnium priscarum observationum dignitatem et fidem elevat, cum tuae sibi constent, Hipparcheae quadrante diei plus aut minus distent, quod quidem illa aequinoctia valde suspecta nobis facit. Nos ex septem illud delegimus, quod ab autore ἀκριβέστατον vocatur: quemadmodum et anni forma ALFONSINI GELALÆAe quanquam elegantissimae nobis praelata est. Tuus annus Coelestis pauxillo minor GELALÆO. PTOLEMÆI vero observationes tam nobis falsas esse constat, quam multa alia apud illum scriptorem eximium. Ecce anno 886 Nabonassari, Pharmuthi IX, feria prima, Februarij 23, Christi 139, Sol, inquit, erat ἀκριβῶς in 3° 30' Piscium. At in meridie prima Thoth primi, Sol erat ἀκριβῶς in 3° 20' Piscium, eodem teste, nempe 26 Februarij. Ergo in 886 annis προήγησις tridui tantum facta fuerit. Quid absurdius? Haec et plura ἀτοπήματα diligenter in PTOLEMÆO annotavimus. Quare lubricae sunt eius observationes et male illis fiditur. Itaque non miror, si in 285 annis, tot scilicet, quot inter illius et HIPPARCHI observationes intersunt, unius diei progressionem aequinoctia tantum fecisse dicit, utpote qui sibi persuasisset in 300 annis id fieri. Itaque hinc illae lacrimae. A prima meridie Nabonassari ad aequinoctia ab se observata, sunt anni noningenti, et proinde triduum tantummodo aequinoctia εἰς τὰ προηγούμενα promota esse, quare omnes loci Solis ab eo notati ab hac haeresi pendent, et propterea falsi. Neque hac fine stat eius sententia. Si 23. Februarij est 3° 30' Piscium, ergo aequinoctium in 23. Martij, quia anno sequenti ita observarat. Ergo ab hac Epocha aequinoctij verni retro proportione 300 annorum addet diebus Iulianis, ut locum Solis designet, quod facit in omnibus eclipsibus veteribus. Quare qui sequitur illas Epochas Solis, sciat se 300 annorum προηγήσεις sequi. Arabes, qui mentem et consilium PTOLEMÆI non animadverterunt, ex illis aequinoctialibus PTOLEMÆI observationibus μορμολυκεῖον illud Trepidationis pepererunt, ut ex praegressione aequinoctiorum nihilo monstrosior motus octavae Sphaerae εἰς τὰ ἑπόμενα natus est. Neque enim ideo ullus motus Trepidationis assignandus, quod PTOLEMÆO Instrumenta sua imposuerunt; neque Sphaeram octavam ab occidente in orientem ideo promoveri putandum, quod puncta aequinoctialia in Ecliptica in antecedentia promoventur. Circuli enim aequinoctiales ad illa describuntur. Puncta vero mobilia sunt, ergo et ipsi circuli ad ea descripti sunt mobiles, et consequenter polus aequinoctialis mobilis. Itaque tempore HIPPARCHI polus aequinoctialis aberat a cauda Cynosurae gr. 12 24'. Hodie minus distat, quam tribus gradibus. Et tamen omnes putant polum mundi esse non aequinoctialis circuli, et quidem HIPPARCHUS primus huius eximiae rei observator in ea haeresi fuit. At error est, neque unquam circulus arcticus ullius loci parallelus fuit Tropicis aut aequinoctiali. Circuli arctici et antarctici polus idem est cum polo mundi. Circuli aequinoctialis polus variat, ut diximus. Hactenus igitur nunquam polus aequinoctialis fuit polus mundi; sed fore aliquando, fidem facit accessus propior ad Cynosurae caudam, sive ea est polus mundi, sive aliud punctum non longe ab ea remotum. Quid tui iudicij sit, quando illi duo poli convenerint, libenter audiverim. Nam magna res est. Item velim ex te scire an κατάχρησις illa et confusio poli aequinoctialis cum polo mundi aliquem errorem invexerit in collocatione Asterismorum in Sphaera solida. Multa enim in illis desidero et HIPPARCHUM ostendemus ea re deceptum. Edituri sumus in calce MANILIJ nostri aureolum libellum PTOLEMÆI adhuc ἀνέκδοτον περὶ ἀπλανῶν ἀστέρων, in quo per singulos dies anni Actiaci et Iuliani, quae stella matutino aut vespertino oriatur occidatve in quinque Climatibus, diligentissime designatur. Sed recentiores Asterismorum Astrothesiae valde illis repugnant, neque bene cum illis pariant tabulae directionum praeclari Artificis REINHOLDI. Nos omnes omnium Asterismorum Hipparcheas observationes accuratissime collectas in suos dies Iulianos digessimus, in quibus perfectissimus ille artifex notat, quae prima stella cuiusque Asterismi, quaeve ultima oriatur aut occidat, et cum qua parte Eclipticae. Deprehendimus male illi cum PTOLEMÆO convenire, in qua lite componenda, tuo, Vir clarissime, aut nullo auxilio opus est. Vix tandem vanitas anni siderei autore THEBITHIO nobis comperta est; qui scimus eadem die Iuliana eandem stellam semper oriri vespertino et occidere matutino, quod vere docebat nos Aegyptiorum sententia, quae semper eodem tempore Iuliano Caniculam oriri sciscit. Quod verum est. Nos tamen illi commentitio anno Sidereo ascensionem accommodavimus, ut et προηγήσει ἰσημερινῇ unius gradus per 70 annos, cuius opinionis auctor COPERNICUS, PTOLEMÆUS observat unum gradum promotionem fieri in centum annis. Ego vereor, ne in pluribus. Sed tu haec melius, a quo et alia expectamus. Nam nos homunciones procul spectamus, et intra vestibula sacrarij consistimus, quia nondum ἐπόπται sumus, neque spes unquam fore. Itaque sufficit nobis summorum virorum vestigia adorare, quorum tu Princeps, qui talia diu observasti. Nos vero vobis tantum a labore adesse possumus, quantum a peritia nobis desumus. Itaque quod sedulitas nostra nos docuit, id non pigebit in medium proferre. Aio igitur tempore HESIODI Arcturum vespertino oriri solitum in Boeotia et Attica VIII, die Martij Iuliani, eademque die in eodem tractu ortum illum observatum a METONE et EUCTEMONE, et postremo notatum saeculo PTOLEMÆI. Hoc confirmat sententiam nostram de confutatione anni siderei, et Aegyptiorum scitum, qui annum, quem Iulianum nos vocamus, κυνικόν vocabant, quod per plus, quam mille quingentos annos Caniculam semper eodem anni Iuliani die oriri prodiderunt. Haec observatio, ut spero, tibi ingrata non erit. Magnam a nobis inibitis gratiam, si paucularum stellarum, quae propiores sint Eclipticae, ut Basilisci et πρόποδος τῶν Διδύμων observationes vestras nobis communicare libuerit cum latitudinibus, longitudinibus et die Iuliana. Fortasse non infeliciter cum illis Hipparcheis et Ptolemaicis conferentur. Annum coelestem verum a te prodi unico posse certo scimus, a quo plura in Astrologia quotidie expectamus. Quam ineptus et imperitus fuit auctor Anni Pontificij, non dico nunc. Magis miror, tot millia hominum errores non deprehendisse. Sed te moramur.

Vale, Vir Praestantissime.
Lugduni Batavorum VII. Eid. Quintiles Iulianas, CIↃ. IↃ. XC VIII.
Tibi devotissimus et tuae amplitudinis studiosissimus

JOSEPH SCALIGER TO TYCHO BRAHE.

Heartiest greeting.

Your departure from Denmark, most noble Man, as it was most bitter to us, so it always kept our minds in suspense [as to] what place you would choose for your fortunes. For where they should be settled, there we trusted some rest would be gained both for your mind and for your serious studies — and this we wished the more earnestly, the more inclined our goodwill toward you had always been. And so, when I first heard you had settled at Rostock, I consoled myself not a little, since from your having chosen a place near your Denmark we augured you would soon return to the fatherland. But it was not reported that you had moved to Rostock before we learned, from others, that you had already withdrawn your foot thence. From that time we always asked in vain about the state of your affairs, and the seats you had established for yourself. In the end, nothing certain was brought us about you, except the misfortune of your fatherland in being able to lose you. Wherefore the uncertain judgement of my mind about the state of your affairs did not allow me to perform that duty toward you which you have anticipated first. Witness of my intention is a certain young Mathematician who acts here, and professes that he was among your household; from him first, some days ago, I learned that you dwell in a Rantzau castle, and that he would duly see [to it] if I wished any letter to you. While I was meditating this, the arrival of the noble youth Franz Tengnagel, your household-member, surprised me — who brought me your [letter], full of love, humanity, and erudition — so that I have not yet decided whether to congratulate myself on that most humane letter of yours, or to grieve that this duty was snatched from me [that you wrote first].

That you judge of my work On the Emendation of Times otherwise than the unskilled — though in it I recognise your love toward me — yet it will extort a confession from them that I did not waste my labour, even on this ground, that my [work] pleased so great a Man, especially one most-truth-loving [φιλαληθεστάτῳ] and most intelligent in these matters. By envy we are afflicted neither now first, nor [are we] the first — [envy], to whom whoever does anything above the grasp of the little crowd makes himself opportune. To be carped by the malice of such [men] is to reap the fruit of one's labours. If they knew how much they add to my glory by their importunity, they would act more cautiously than they do. Of these I have seen none but the most unskilled and (what is more) the most worthless. Whatever they write is to the detriment of paper, not of my repute. About that Heidelberg little-ape, whom the trophies of Miltiades will not let sleep, I should wonder less, could he be a Themistocles. But to imitate the good belongs to other men — not to dung-born barbarians, most remote from all humanity of manners. But why should I wonder, since he finds followers and admirers? "The lid found its furrows" [εὗρεν ἄλοκας τὸ πῶμα — like finds like]. What I have achieved in letters, it is not for my modesty to say. This one thing I frankly profess: that no one lives who has driven more [men] to madness by his writings. But, as it happens, men are not of one kind. Some learn from us, and dissemble it, lest they credit us with anything; others openly carp, and yet nothing is so painful to them as that they are not the authors of those things for which they attack us with so hostile a mind. But why do I detain you about this dregs of humankind?

So, about that work of mine which I had begun to speak of: I confess that I put more labour than skill into it. For I can scarcely persuade myself otherwise; and we think we are dealt with splendidly, if we are held more studious than learned. But I warn that I, a man of weak talent, could not run through so great a web without sometimes humanly dozing — when one thing fell from me for another, against our mind's intention — part of which we corrected in the Errata, part we imprudently omitted; such as that "Saturn, in its annual motion, makes a twelfth part of one degree," when we meant to say twelve degrees — of which, and the like, I can swear holily: "the hand threw, but the mind was off the mark" [ἡ χεὶρ βέβληκεν, ἡ δὲ φρὴν ἀπόσκοπος — a slip of the hand, not the mind]. Wherefore, most noble and learned man, such oversights [παροράματα] rather than errors [ἁμαρτήματα] — like the many that the divine Hipparchus civilly excuses in Eudoxus, and which noble Readers ought to emend, not assail — it is yours too either to pass over without offence [ἀπροσκόπως καὶ ἀπταίστως], or, if your weightier occupations allow, to correct. But those faults — small to tell — are, to those refuse-men [critics], arguments for a new brood of books. That we call September what [we had just called] October, and the fourth feria [Wednesday] what we had said the third [Tuesday] — this heats up the printing-presses. Barbarity and impudence find there something to do.

Your divine observations of the equinoxes found a place at the end of our books — not, indeed, the place they deserved, but the place they could [find]; for we had not received them earlier. And yet they are placed by us where they can most fittingly be compared with the Hipparchean. Truly that continuous quinquennium [5-year run] elevates the dignity and trust of all the ancient observations — since yours are self-consistent, while the Hipparchean differ by a quarter-day, more or less, which indeed makes those [Hipparchus's] equinoxes very suspect to us. We chose, from the seven, that one which is called by the author "most accurate" [ἀκριβέστατον] — just as the Alphonsine form of the year was preferred by us to the Jalali [GELALAEA, the Persian Jalali calendar], however most elegant. Your celestial year [is] a little less than the Jalali. But Ptolemy's observations are as false to us as many other things in that eminent writer. Behold: in the year 886 of Nabonassar, Pharmuthi 9, the first feria [Sunday], 23 February, [year] 139 of Christ, the Sun, he says, was accurately [ἀκριβῶς] in 3° 30′ of Pisces. But at the noon of the first Thoth of the first [year], the Sun was accurately in 3° 20′ of Pisces, by the same witness — namely 26 February. So in 886 years a precession [προήγησις] of only three days would have been made. What more absurd? These and more absurdities [ἀτοπήματα] we diligently noted in Ptolemy. So his observations are slippery, and ill-trusted. And so I do not wonder if he says that in 285 years (as many, that is, as lie between his and Hipparchus's observations) the equinoxes made only one day's progression — since he had persuaded himself it happens in 300 years. Hence those tears. From the first noon of Nabonassar to the equinoxes observed by himself are nine hundred years, and accordingly the equinoxes were advanced backward [εἰς τὰ προηγούμενα] by only three days; wherefore all the Sun-places noted by him depend on this heresy, and are therefore false. Nor does his view stop here. If 23 February is 3° 30′ Pisces, then the equinox is on 23 March, because he had so observed it the following year. So from this epoch of the vernal equinox, backward by a proportion of 300 years, he will add to the Julian days, to designate the Sun's place — which he does in all the old eclipses. Wherefore whoever follows those Solar Epochs, let him know that he follows 300-year precessions [the wrong rate].

The Arabs, who did not notice Ptolemy's mind and plan, from those equinoctial observations of Ptolemy brought forth that bugbear [μορμολυκεῖον] of Trepidation — as, from the precession of the equinoxes, a no-less-monstrous motion of the eighth Sphere toward the consequents [εἰς τὰ ἑπόμενα] was born. For no Trepidation-motion is to be assigned, because Ptolemy's instruments imposed upon him; nor is the eighth Sphere to be thought advanced from west to east, because the equinoctial points are advanced in the Ecliptic toward the antecedents. For the equinoctial circles are described with reference to those [points]. But the points are mobile; therefore the circles described to them are also mobile; and consequently the equinoctial pole is mobile. So in Hipparchus's time the equinoctial pole was distant from the tail of Cynosura [the Pole-star region] 12° 24′; today it is distant less than three degrees. And yet all think the pole of the world is [the pole] not of the equinoctial circle — and indeed Hipparchus, the first observer of this eminent matter, was in that heresy. But it is an error: never was the arctic circle of any place parallel to the Tropics or the equinoctial. The pole of the arctic and antarctic circle is the same as the pole of the world; the pole of the equinoctial circle varies, as we said. Hitherto, then, the equinoctial pole was never the pole of the world; but that it will be, sometime, the closer approach to Cynosura's tail gives faith — whether that [tail] be the pole of the world, or another point not far removed from it. What your judgement is, [as to] when those two poles will converge, I should gladly hear; for it is a great matter. Likewise I should wish to know from you whether that misuse [κατάχρησις] and confusion of the equinoctial pole with the world's pole has introduced any error in the placing of the Asterisms on the solid Sphere [the globe]. For I miss many things in them, and we shall show Hipparchus deceived in this. We are about to publish, at the end of our Manilius, a golden little book of Ptolemy, still unpublished [ἀνέκδοτον], On the Fixed Stars [περὶ ἀπλανῶν ἀστέρων — the Phaseis], in which, through each day of the Actian and Julian year, what star rises or sets, morning or evening, in five Climates, is most diligently designated. But the more recent star-placements [Astrothesiae] of the asterisms strongly conflict with them; nor do the direction-tables of the excellent artificer Reinhold agree well with them. We have digested all the Hipparchean observations of all the asterisms, most accurately collected, into their Julian days — in which that most perfect artificer notes what first star of each asterism, and what last, rises or sets, and with what part of the Ecliptic. We find he agrees ill with Ptolemy — in settling which dispute, most renowned Man, your help, or no one's, is needed.

Scarcely at last has the vanity of the sidereal year — by its author Thebit [Thabit ibn Qurra] — been discovered by us, who know that on the same Julian day the same star always rises in the evening and sets in the morning; which the view of the Egyptians truly taught us, which holds that the Dog-star [Sirius] always rises at the same Julian time. Which is true. Yet we accommodated the ascension to that fictitious Sidereal year, as also to an equinoctial precession [προηγήσει ἰσημερινῇ] of one degree per 70 years — of which opinion Copernicus is the author; Ptolemy observes one degree's progression to be made in a hundred years; I fear [it is] in more. But you [know] these better, from whom we expect other things too. For we manikins look from afar, and stand within the vestibules of the sanctuary, because we are not yet initiates [ἐπόπται], nor is there hope we ever shall be. So it suffices us to adore the footsteps of the highest men, of whom you are the Prince, who have long observed such things. We can be of service to you only so much in labour as we fail you in skill. So what our diligence has taught us, it will not irk [us] to bring forward. I say, then, that in Hesiod's time Arcturus was wont to rise in the evening, in Boeotia and Attica, on the 8th day of March Julian; and on the same day, in the same region, that rising was observed by Meton and Euctemon, and lastly noted in Ptolemy's age. This confirms our view on the confutation of the sidereal year, and the Egyptians' knowledge, who called the year which we call Julian the "Dog-year" [κυνικόν — Sothic], because for more than 1500 years they reported the Dog-star always to rise on the same day of the Julian year. This observation, I hope, will not be unwelcome to you. You will gain great favour with us, if it please you to communicate to us your observations of a few stars closer to the Ecliptic — such as Basiliscus [Regulus] and the "forefoot of the Twins" [πρόποδος τῶν Διδύμων, Gemini] — with the latitudes, longitudes, and the Julian day. Perhaps they will be compared not unsuccessfully with the Hipparchean and Ptolemaic. We know for certain that the true celestial year can be revealed by you alone, from whom we expect more in Astronomy daily. How inept and unskilled was the author of the Pontifical Year [the Gregorian calendar], I do not say now. I wonder more that so many thousands of men have not detected the errors. But we are detaining you.

Farewell, most excellent Man.
Leiden, the 7th [day before] the Ides of July [9 July], Julian [reckoning], 1598.
Most devoted to you, and most zealous of your greatness,
Joseph Scaliger.

Scaliger thanks Tycho for esteeming his great chronological work above "the unskilled," and rails at his detractors (notably a "Heidelberg little-ape" — a chronology-critic at Heidelberg). He candidly confesses errata (e.g. "Saturn's annual motion a twelfth of a degree" for "twelve degrees"; September/October, Wednesday/Tuesday slips) — "oversights, not errors" (παροράματα not ἁμαρτήματα) — and asks Tycho "to emend, not assail."
  • Tycho's equinox observations praised. Tycho's five-year run of equinox observations (placed at the end of the De emendatione) is "divine" and self-consistent, exposing Hipparchus's equinoxes (varying by ¼-day) as "very suspect"; Tycho's celestial year is "a little less than the Jalali."
  • The Ptolemy precession critique. Scaliger shows Ptolemy's solar data imply an absurd 3-day precession in 886 years, so "all his Sun-places are false," resting on the wrong 1°/100-year (= 300-year-per-degree) precession.
  • ★ The trepidation debunking and the moving equinoctial pole (the Diatriba seed). Scaliger demolishes the Arab "bugbear of Trepidation" and the eighth-sphere motion, arguing instead that the equinoctial pole itself is mobile (distinct from the fixed world-pole) — now within 3° of Cynosura's tail (12°24′ in Hipparchus's time) — and converging on the Pole-star. He asks Tycho's judgement on when the two poles will meet — the central thesis he develops in brahe-t_16000310001 (the Diatriba) and that Tycho rejects in brahe-t_16000723001.
  • The Ptolemy Phaseis edition. Scaliger announces his edition of Ptolemy's hitherto-unpublished On the Fixed Stars (the Phaseis), appended to his Manilius, and the conflict between ancient (Hipparchus/Ptolemy) and modern (Reinhold) star-placements — needing Tycho's help.
  • The sidereal-year debunking. Scaliger rejects Thabit ibn Qurra's sidereal year, affirming the Egyptian/Sothic ("Dog-year") constancy (Sirius rising the same Julian day for 1500 years), confirmed by Hesiod/Meton/Euctemon's Arcturus rising (8 March). He asks Tycho for observations of Regulus and the "forefoot of Gemini" to compare with the ancients, and disparages the Gregorian ("Pontifical") calendar.
  • The deference. The famous self-positioning: "we manikins … stand within the vestibules of the sanctuary … not yet initiates; it suffices us to adore the footsteps of the highest men, of whom you are the Prince."
  • Cross-references. The opening of the Scaliger–Tycho exchange (→ brahe-t_15990417001, brahe-t_16000310001, brahe-t_16000723001); the precession/trepidation/sidereal-year and chronology threads (also brahe-t_16000327002, brahe-t_16000107001).
  • Persons & works named. Joseph Scaliger (De emendatione temporum, the Manilius, the Ptolemy Phaseis); Tycho; the young Leiden mathematician (former Tycho assistant); Franz Tengnagel; the "Heidelberg ape" (a chronology-critic); Miltiades, Themistocles (allusion); Hipparchus, Ptolemy, Eudoxus, Reinhold, Thabit ibn Qurra (Thebithius), Copernicus, Meton, Euctemon, Hesiod; the Nabonassar era; the Jalali and Alphonsine calendars; the Gregorian ("Pontifical") calendar; Regulus (Basiliscus), Gemini. Places: Leiden, Rostock, the Rantzau castle (Wandsbek). The Greek phrases are reproduced throughout.
  • Calendar. Lugduni Batavorum VII. Eid. Quintiles Iulianas, CIↃIↃXCVIII — "the 7th before the Ides of July, Julian [= Old Style]" = 9 July 1598 [O.S.] (Scaliger pointedly dates Julian; CIↃIↃXCVIII = 1598). Header and label agree.
