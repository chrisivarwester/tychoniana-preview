The original

NOBILI ET GENEROSO VIRO, DOMINO TYCHONI BRAHE OTTONIDI A KNUDSTORP, AMICO SUO SEMPER OBSERVANDO.

GENEROSE et clarissime vir, Scotus hîc quidam in hoc postremo bello Danico et Suetico stipendia sub Capitaneo IOHANNE CLARICO Scoto meruit, et parum, uti opinor, illi restat, ipsi etiam serenissimo Regi aut quaestoribus eius incognitum et memoria elapsum. Quod si tu hunc afflictum tua gratia et favore apud Regem aut per aulicos amicos iuvare possis, obnixe te oro, ut id facias. Et hoc etiam tibi persuadeas, quod tibi hac de re nec scripsissem, nec molestus fuissem, si literae, quas tibi BUCHANANUS, serenissimi Scotorum regis Praeceptor, nunc scribit, ad id me non impulissent; cuius preces magnum apud te pondus habituras esse spero, cum ille Poëta sit doctissimus, et Theologus minime seditiosus, nec Reipublicae perturbator, nec etiam ARISTOTELI nimium addictus, quo etiam quidam Theologiam profitentes abutuntur. Scotus ille responsum hîc a te expectabit, et literas ad aulicos amicos, ut negotia isthic tutius et facilius expedire possit. Postea si voles, BUCHANANO rescribas. De Astronomicis quibusdam libenter te audiam disserentem, ubi simul affuerimus. Nam Theorias Planetarum revolvere, et faciliori a te excogitata Methodo discere plurimum aveo. Astrologica, quibus tu moderate uteris, nec nimium (quod laudo) ijs tribuis, non valde vitupero quidem, sed neque admodum suspicio, ut multi alias solent, siquidem a Domino CALVINO olim in his absterritus sum, cum Argentinae simul essemus, quando istis vaticinijs admodum addictus fuissem. Habent enim nescio quid, quod hominem trahit ad se, ut ijs nimium adhaereat et fidat. Is enim, cum vidisset, me eo credulitatis in hisce praedictionibus pervenisse, ut de adulterijs etiam mulierum hinc nonnulla, quae, ut fatear quod verum est, raro falsos habuerint eventus, praedicere me posse iactarem, pro sua autoritate et gravi iudicio me increpavit, et male mihi minatus est, si ista non relinquerem. Quare cum eum suspicerem, atque in pretio haberem, mecumque STURMIUS plurimum illis tribueret, non ausus sum talia ulterius serio attingere. Astronomica tamen semper plurimi aestimavi, atque uti merentur, magnifeci, et etiamnum magnifacio. Quare in ijs aliqua etiam hac invalescente aetate de praeceptore discere plurimum desidero, quod et te mihi non denegaturum spero.

Vale, Vir integerrime, et me, quod plura non scribam, excusatum habe, Uraniamque tuam meis verbis reverenter saluta.
Haffniae 17. die Novembris Anno 1576.
Tibi deditissimus
Carolus Danzeus.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the noble and well-born gentleman, Master Tycho Brahe, son of Otte, of Knudstrup, his friend ever to be esteemed.

Well-born and most distinguished sir, a certain Scot here served for pay, in this last Danish and Swedish war, under Captain John Clark, a Scot, and there remains owing to him, as I think, but little, [a matter] unknown even to his most serene Majesty himself or to his treasurers, and slipped from memory. If you can help this afflicted man by your goodwill and favour with the King, or through friends at court, I earnestly beg you to do so. And let yourself be persuaded of this too: that I should neither have written to you on this matter nor have troubled you, had not the letter which Buchanan, the tutor of the most serene King of the Scots, is now writing to you, driven me to it; whose entreaties I hope will carry great weight with you, since he is a most learned poet, and a theologian by no means seditious, no disturber of the commonwealth, nor yet too much addicted to Aristotle — whom certain professors of theology also abuse. That Scot will await here a reply from you, and letters to friends at court, so that he may dispatch his affairs there more safely and easily. Afterwards, if you wish, you may write back to Buchanan. On certain matters astronomical I shall gladly hear you discourse, when we are together. For I greatly long to turn over the theories of the planets, and to learn [them] by an easier method devised by you. As for astrological matters — which you use in moderation, and to which you do not (a thing I praise) attribute too much — I do not greatly disparage them, to be sure, but neither do I altogether look up to them, as many on other occasions are wont to do; since I was once frightened off these things by Master Calvin, when we were together at Strasbourg, at a time when I had been quite addicted to those prognostications. For they have some I-know-not-what that draws a man to them, so that he clings to them and trusts them too much. For he [Calvin], when he had seen that I had come to such a pitch of credulity in these predictions that I would even boast that I could foretell from them some things, indeed, concerning the adulteries of women — which, to confess what is true, had rarely had false outcomes — by his authority and grave judgement rebuked me, and threatened me harshly if I did not give those things up. Wherefore, since I looked up to him and held him in esteem, and since Sturm too with me attributed much to those [prognostications], I did not dare to take up such things seriously any further. Yet astronomical matters I have always valued most highly, and have, as they deserve, esteemed greatly, and still esteem greatly. Wherefore I greatly desire to learn something even in these, in this my advancing age, from a teacher — which I hope you too will not refuse me.

Farewell, most upright sir, and hold me excused that I write no more; and reverently greet your Urania in my words.
At Copenhagen, the 17th day of November, the year 1576.
Most devoted to you,
Charles de Dançay.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Carolus Danzeus = Charles de Dançay (c. 1510–1589), the long-serving French (royal) ambassador (resident) at the Danish court, a Huguenot humanist and a close friend and supporter of Tycho. The biographical literature confirms the letter's own testimony that Dançay "had been a Protestant since youth, when he had studied with Johann Sturm and John Calvin" (J. R. Christianson, On Tycho's Island, p. 54) — directly corroborating the references to Calvinus, Sturmius, and Argentina (Strasbourg) below.
  • Ottonidi a Knudstorp — "son of Otte, of Knudstrup": Ottonides, a Greek-style patronymic for Otte Brahe, Tycho's father; Knudstrup (Knutstorp), the Brahe family seat in Scania.
  • hoc postremo bello Danico et Suetico — "this last Danish and Swedish war": the Northern Seven Years' War (1563–1570) between Denmark–Norway and Sweden, in which Scottish (and other) mercenaries served the Danish crown.
  • Capitaneo Iohanne Clarico Scoto — "Captain John Clark, a Scot" (Latin Ioannes Claricus): a Scottish mercenary officer in Danish service; the Scotus quidam of the opening is an unnamed soldier of his company seeking arrears of pay. The individual is not further identifiable from the letter; crux flagged (see Confidence).
  • serenissimo Regi … quaestoribus eius — "his most serene Majesty … his treasurers": Frederik II of Denmark and the crown's fiscal officers, who have overlooked the soldier's outstanding pay.
  • Buchananus, serenissimi Scotorum regis Praeceptor = George Buchanan (1506–1582), the Scottish humanist, poet, and historian, then tutor (Praeceptor) to the young James VI of Scotland (the serenissimus Scotorum rex). The characterisation — "a most learned poet, a theologian by no means seditious … nor too much addicted to Aristotle" — is Dançay's vouching for Buchanan's standing as he forwards Buchanan's (separate, enclosed/forthcoming) letter of recommendation to Tycho.
  • nec etiam Aristoteli nimium addictus, quo … quidam Theologiam profitentes abutuntur — "nor too much addicted to Aristotle, whom certain professors of theology also abuse": a humanist's jab at the scholastic over-reliance on Aristotle; abutuntur in its period sense "make wrong/excessive use of."
  • Theorias Planetarum revolvere … faciliori … Methodo — "to turn over the theories of the planets … by an easier method": Theoricae planetarum is the planetary-theory genre; Dançay wishes to learn the planetary theories by some simpler method of Tycho's own devising. revolvere "to turn over (in study), peruse."
  • Astrology. Astrologica … directio-type technical vocabulary is absent here; the passage is about astrology as a practice (vaticinia, praedictiones — "prognostications, predictions"). Dançay's confession that he had grown so credulous as to predict adulteria mulierum ("the adulteries of women") is rendered literally; the hedge raro falsos habuerint eventus ("had rarely had false outcomes") is preserved, not sharpened.
  • a Domino Calvino olim … absterritus sum, cum Argentinae simul essemus = "frightened off … by Master [John] Calvin, when we were together at Strasbourg": Argentina (Argentoratum) is the Latin name of Strasbourg, where Calvin resided 1538–1541 and where the young Dançay studied. The episode dates Dançay's renunciation of judicial astrology to that period.
  • Sturmius = Johann Sturm (1507–1589), the humanist educator and rector of the Strasbourg gymnasium; here named as one who, with Dançay, had once "attributed much" to astrological prognostications.
  • hac invalescente aetate — "in this my failing age": invalescens here in the sense of growing weak / declining (not the commoner classical "growing strong"); Dançay, then in his mid-sixties, contrasts his advanced years with his wish still to learn astronomy. The reading is contextually secure but the word is Janus-faced; noted.
  • de praeceptore discere — "to learn … from a teacher": de praeceptore "from a teacher," i.e. with Tycho as his instructor.
  • Uraniam tuam … reverenter saluta — "reverently greet your Urania": Urania, the Muse of astronomy, here standing figuratively for Tycho's astronomical pursuit (and, by extension, his observatory programme); a learned flourish, not a person. Cf. the Sacris Uraniae sign-off idiom.
  • Epistolary formulae. S. (in the heading sense) — the address-formula closes Amico suo semper observando ("his friend ever to be esteemed"); Vale, Vir integerrime → "Farewell, most upright sir"; Tibi deditissimus → "Most devoted to you".
  • Place / calendar. Haffniae 17. die Novembris Anno 1576 = "at Copenhagen (Hafnia), the 17th day of November, 1576" (O.S.; Denmark kept the Julian calendar). The dateline as written agrees with the edition's catalogue label (1576. 17. november. København); no date discrepancy.
  • Edition / witnesses. Edition of record: Tychonis Brahei … epistolae … 1568–1587 (= Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII). MS witnesses: A = Vienna, ÖNB cod. Vindob. lat. 10686.7; B = Vienna, ÖNB cod. Vindob. lat. 10686.66.
  • Correction. Corrected the English rendering of "hac invalescente aetate" from "this my failing age" to "this my advancing age" — the Latin participle denotes age gaining ground, not declining.

1 defect found:
- mistranslation (low). "Invalescente" (from invalesco, to grow strong/increase) means an age that is advancing or gaining on him, not one that is weakening. "Failing age" imports a connotation of decline that the Latin participle does not literally carry; a more neutral rendering would be "this advancing age of mine" or "as age grows upon me". At: “Wherefore I greatly desire to learn something even in these, in this my failing age, from a teacher”.
