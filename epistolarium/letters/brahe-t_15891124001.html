CLARISSIMO ET ERVDITISSIMO
VIRO, D. CHRISTOPHORO ROTHMANNO,
ILLVSTRISSIMI PRINCIPIS WILHELMI LANDTGRAVII HASSIAe, ETC. MATHEMATICO EXIMIO, AMICO
SUO PLURIMUM DILECTO. S. D.

ECQVID id est mi ROTHMANNE, quod tam inuito animo accipias, adeoque sinistre interpreteris meas vltimo ad te datas literas, nimisque rhapsodice ad eas respondeas, cum amicae inter nos collationis, atque veritatis in abscondito latentis penitius eruendae gratiâ, bona intentione scriptae sint: Neque enim ego te vspiam in illis impietatis accusaui. De pietate vel impietate cuiusuis, nullum mihi arrogo iudicium, Id scrutatori cordium omnium, vti par est, relinquens. Nec etiam te authoritati sacrarum literarum, quasi data opera, aliquid detrahere absolute dixi, saltem quod Mosis verba de Luna, luminari minore, incompetenter ad corporis, ipsis plaerisque Stellis reuera minoris, magnitudinem transtuleris, cum potius de lucis Terras contingente praeeminentia accipienda veniant, te admonui. Rationibus quoque vt existimo non incongruis adiectis. Quod etiam Prophetis nimium deroges, eos non plusquam vulgus imperitum in Astronomicis et Physicis intellexisse asseuerando, vtvt diuinitus, immeditate instructi fuerint, et praeterea antiquiss. Patrum visibilem a DEO conditarum rerum excolentium traditiones non paucas, nostris procul dubio ab Ethnicis acceptis certiores et meliores in promptu habuerint, non approbandum duxi, in qua etiamnum persisto sententia. Neque enim vnquam leuiter de his decernendum censui. Multa quidem et non posthabenda, de ijs quae Physice in sacris literis continentur, nuper etiam secundum vulgarium Philosophorum ratiocinationes, explicuit Franciscus Vallesius, neque absque solerti, quo ad ea principia, labore; Licet et multo plura in his longe aliter secundum veriorem Physicam, forte quoque ex his ipsis non minima de parte potius petendam, enucleanda delitescant. Quin et Lambertus Danaeus, vir sane apprime eruditus, et de instituendis alijs optime meritus, Physicam suam Theologicam, tam ex veteri quam nouo Codice non inutili labore extruxit. Possentque fortasse multa de his aliâs dici, quae peculiare et reuera Metaphysicum, non quale nobis Stagyrita iste Graecus obtrusit, requirerent volumen. Literas tuas quin satis caperem, nec morbus, licet et ille grauis erat, corporis tamen non ingenli, nec occupationum, quamuis et hae non paucae implicationes, adeo vt putas, impedierunt. Nulla enim in illis irresolubilia latebant aenigmata, re omni dudum satis perspecta; Nec quicquam tibi obijcio, quod ex literis illis colligi non possit, imo forte plusquam opus est, parco. Conferantur saltem tuae et meae, dijudicentque alij, affectibus non indulgentes, neque sibijpsis nimium placentes. Quatenus sacris literis contraria induxeris, et antea et iam aliquo modo indicaui, te vbique studiose id facere non dixi. Nec ego licet Theologiam non profitear, neque quispiam eorum qui eam solide et sincere exercent, tuis assertionibus e Dedomenis sacrarum literarum assentiri possumus: Frustra itaque praetendis hoc diuerticulum, Cum duo dicunt vnum, non tibi concedi vt sint vnum, Neque enim alijs concedendum duco, quod hîc reprehendo. Augustinum satis libere de sacris nonnunquam pronuntiasse, interdum non incomposite, interdum vero frigidiuscule pro eâ temporibus Apostolorum viciniore aetate, non equidem Dei gratiâ sum ignarus. Sed quid ista omnia huc conferunt, aut ad rem de qua agitur, faciunt. Quaestio mouebatur, an mobilitas Terreni Globi, eademque triplex a Copernico asserta reuera constare possit, anque illa inuentioni nostrae praeferenda foret, et vtrum sacrae literae illius imaginationi contrariarentur, necne: Si quid igitur habes e sacris Oraculis, aut horum Interpretibus, vtpote Augustino vel alijs Patribus, pro hac Copernicea assertione, tuaque astipulatione faciens, ex eorum scriptis id cita. Modo iustae fuerint rationes, ijs non refragabor. Id satis scio, Augustinum, quem solum nominas, motum Terrae, neque annuum neque diurnum vsquam concessisse, ex quo rotunditati eius tanquam minus Mathematicus, Antipodas denegando, detraxerit. Quod tamen illi condonandum est. Quare cum rotundam esse non perceperit, motum etiam rotunditati competentem longe minus admittere potuit. Theologorum vestratium opinationes, et de his censuras, etsi extra eorum forte Professionem, minusque deliberatas, non equidem contemno. Sed cum videam illos tantum hoc nomine accersitos atque interrogatos, an liquiditas Caeli e sacris literis, sine orbium realitate astrui possit (prout tua clausula innuit) cumque scias me etiam longe antequam de his somniâris idipsum concessisse, atque pronuntiasse, miror, cur haec nihil huc facientia obijcias. Neque enim existimo vestris Theologis de terna Terrae volubilitate propositam esse quaestionem, cum paucissimi ex illis, eam etsi quam maxime vellent, caperent, vtvt Terrenis, quemadmodum et alibi fit, non parum forte addicti. Cum igitur de mobilitale Terrae ex Aristarcho et quibusdam alijs veteribus per Copernicum renouata introductaque controuersia solummodo inter nos mouebatur, qua quaeso cohaerentia impulsus eam cum limpiditate Caeli confudisti, quam ego nunquam denegaui, sed primus forte omnium id ita se habere non saltem asseuerare, sed e certissimis demonstrationibus probare elaboraui. An hoc est Mathematicum, disparia coniungere atque implicare. Quod ne quidem Dialectici, siue Artificiales, siue naturales in diuersis terminis admiserint.

Liquidissimam et vbique peruiam esse Aetherei Mundi substantiam e sacris literis probari posse, vel ipsis silentibus Theologiae Professoribus, iam dudum compertum habeo. Multaque in eius rei testimonium consignaui diuinitus patefacta Oracala. Quin et Clarissimus ille, deque Doctrina publica egregie meritus vir D. CASPARVS PEVCERVS literis ad me hoc praesertim nomine datis, plurima sacrarum literarum dicta, huc facientia collegit, meaeque sententiae circa tenuissimam, limpidissimam et nihilominus superelementarem Caeli materiam erudite atque egregie subscripsit. Idque potissimum, vt Hypothesium mearum inuentionem minime absurdam esse inde quoque comprobaret. Quando ego meas de Caeli materia vbique peruia nullisque orbibus solidis conferta, vt in gratiam veterum hactenus creditum est, et nihilominus Aëre Elementari atque animali velut Ioannes Pena Gallus, tu et quidam alij promulgare audetis, non coinquinatas ratiocinationes plenius, quam adhuc factum est, in publicum edidero, alijsque dijudicandas concessero, vtique sacrarum literarum, quae primas in omni sapientia stabilienda, atque scientia condenda obtinere merentur, autoritatem citare, vel te non monente, nequaquam intermissurus sum. Dei enim solius gloriam in omnibus spectandam semper censui, id quod et tu recte approbas, cum veritas absque ipsius patefactione et dono introspici nequeat; Omne enim donum perfectum a Deo. Meam quintam Essentiam, quam Aetheri, apud Chymicos locutione non ignota, neque veteribus etiam Philosophis, adeoque ne ipsi Aristoteli (licet in his non vbique satis perspicaci) improbata, tribuo, salse mihi exprobare videris; Sal vero dulce, limpidum, omnique terrestreitate defaecatum, exaltatum, nullaque ex parte insipidum, aut graueolens, sed perpetuitati Circulari analogum, nondum degustati; vbi olfeceris, aut saporem eius perceperis, tum primum de his sententiam profer; Interea satius est immaturo supersedere praeiudicio. Rogas, ne de Crepusculis te caecum efficiam, neque temeritatis accusem, quasi illa scribas, quae non expertus sis. At ego nec te occaecare, nec temerarium pronuntiare vnquam praesumsi; Vtinam et me et te atque alios a caligine mentis, qua plusquam credimus offuscati sumus, temeritateque Mundana liberari liceret, aeque atque oculorum externam imbecillitatem, vel Chymicis praesertim medijs emendare non vsque adeo difficile est, sane in eo, quo ad fieri posset, quamplurimum elaborandum censerem. Neque enim opera illa inutiliter collocata foret. Scire te ais Crepusculum desinere, Sole P. 17. sub Horizonte depresso, vulgariter Crepusculum pro luce dubia accipiendo. Existimas itaque me vulgo saltem approbatam et hactenus in Astronomorum libris contentam opinionem venditare, nihilque e proprijs aduersionibus huc contulisse; At quae haec est insimulatio. Tamdiu ne tecum per literas egi, vt adhuc me veritatis esse studiosum, neque aliunde quam vel iusta ratiocinatione atque experientia, vel quod potissimum est, patefactione diuina eam conquirere, neque data opera falsum pro vero astruere, incompertum habeas. Sufficienter colligere potuisti, me ne in maioribus quidem, et ad rem Astronomicam plus facientibus caecutire velle, nedum vt in Crepusculis, facilimo negotio animaduertibilibus, irrita et Antiquorum saltem sententiae vulgari colludendo quippiam citra propriam animaduersionem pronuntiarem. Crepusculum cum nulla Aëris pars vndiquaque circa Horizontem vel minimum albescat, determinandum, satis sciebam, quod etiam tum a me ipso, tum a mihi septenis vel octonis ad minimum in rebus Astronomicis inseruientibus studiosis, quam diligentissime aliquoties factitatum est, ita vt nullum, ne quidem minimum vestigium Aëris a Sole illuminati in vlla citima Horizontis superficie dignosci potuerit, quando tempora Crepusculorum denotata sunt; Adhibitis Stellarum Aequatorijs a Meridiano remotionibus, Solisque exacto loco, et omnibus per trutinam Geometricam diligenter expensis, tibique si oculos aperire velles, eandem ob caussam, ne pro libito aut vulgari opinione constituta, persentisceres, liberaliter communicatis. Si itaque tu duobus oculis, et binis quoque saltem Organis acrius haec discernere potes, quam nos omnes sedecim ad minimum luminibus, totidemque et pluribus sicubi opus fuerit, Instrumentis, equidem hanc lincaeitatem atque mediorum modicam requisitionem tibi non inuideo, miror magis. Imo maius adhuc acumen plenioremque sufficientiam congratularer. Tu itaque de Crepusculis, tuo sensu abundando tibi quicquid libuerit, persuade, Ego ea quae indicaui, et pluribus etiam experimentis satis rata esse, nullique verae refragationi obnoxia, minime haesito. Vide saltem ne aliorum non indubias animaduersiones eleuando, vt tuis assertionibus de Aëris atque Aetheris consimili existentia (quam magna ex parte ob vaporum in diuersis Horizontibus discrepantiam, et in Borealioribus magis quam Australibus incrassatam sublimatamque probare conaris) patrocineris, pluribus vaporibus, obscuritatibus atque nebulis veritatem ipsam, ad quam tendere deberemus, inuoluas, quam omnes circa Horizontem terrestrem, quouis in loco, siue Australi siue Boreo densae et fuscae vaporositates. Proinde, cur Refractionibus non ad amussim subscribam, nescire vis, cum alias per Instrumenta eruere non potueris, ideoque nihil hîc in mei gratiam faciendum et propterea iniqua me postulare hoc postulando, asseueras. At quando ego a te vnquam petij, vt posthabita veritate, quidpiam in mei gratiam proferres, aut adeo iniqua vrsi? id enim reuera iniquum foret, si hoc fecissem. Imo multo potius de veritate ipsa penitius enucleanda semper admonui, cum haec sit virtutum omnium pene pulcherrima praestantissimaque. Refractiones a te assertas, cur omni ex parte approbare nequeam, et mea me docent Instrumenta: Si in vno atque altero, ijsdemque saltem bicubitalibus, vtvt ex mea inuentione profectis, et per Wittichium vobis propalatis, neque etiam tunc satis maturis, haec et alia quam ego plusquam vicenis Organis, plerisque eorum quadricubitalibus et quincubitalibus, pluribus quoque adhibitis Obseruatoribus visus acumine satis valentibus, accuratius collimare solus potes, est quod tibi et hanc praerogatiuam non inuideam; Quin et sic erit, cur mihi gratias agas, quod te per a me excogitata Instrumenta (neque enim id iam ignoras) tam perspicacem reddiderim. Id saltem tibi persuade, quemadmodum in palestrica Arte fieri solet, nullum Athletam Progymnastis suis, ea confestim indicare, quae sibi ipsi in exercitio isto defectu aliquo vnquam obessent; Sic etiam nec Wittichium nec vllos alios omnia mea, quae veritati peruestigandae ad amussim sunt necessaria ita diuulgasse, quin adhuc potiori ex parte apud suum Autorem sarta tecta maneant. Frustra itaque de tuis Instrumentis atque Obseruationibus nimium gloriaris: Forte si hîc esses omniaque ipsemet per longe plura minimeque dubia Organa, vel tuis solius oculis, me praesente intuereris, aliud diceres. Sed et Refractiones tuas nimium modicas esse et Crepuscula nimis lata, non satis inter se conueniunt. Oportet itaque in alterutris vel vtrisque errorem non modicum latere, aut sane Aërem in hoc parum Borealiore loco, quantum ad Crepuscula attinet, aliquanto defaecatiorem, quam illîc esse, quo ad Refractiones vero turbidiorem, id quod vel te ipso iudice simul consistere nequit. Attende igitur his diligentius, vel si minus lubet, aut in procinctu non est, acquiesce saltem aliorum minime dubiae decisioni neque hîc tibi quidpiam immature decernendum praesume. Titulum libri mei alias in dubium vocatum, aut etiam attentatum, nunc approbas, nec amplius reprehensione dignum aestimas; Licet illum quem allegasti, non publice ediderim, aut vlli libro praefixerim, sed saltem in literis quibusdam ad Illustriss. Principem tuum datis illum obiter annotârim. Gratulor itaque et tibi et mihi, quod tam pulchre resipueris, antea enim obiecisti, me corruptionem Caelo introducere, cum generationis illic circa Cometam vocabulo vterer, nimis rigide verba mea vrgendo. At vide potius, ne de industria nunc asserendo, Cometas in Caelo generari, alicubi praeter opinionem pecces, non tam Grammatice quam Physice, modo vocabuli proprietatem retinere deceat. Quod non assertiue sed dubitanter atque interrogatiue ista proposuisti, vt meam sententiam plenius explicarem, id nihil apud me noui est; Nam et Wittichius et plaerique alij sic mecum egerunt, obijciendo ea quae nullum mererentur dubium, vt alia quaedam expiscarentur. Verum postquam ego in eo, tum etiam alijs idipsum mature experirer, cautior factus sum, vtvt non pauca in Germania hincinde meis sumptibus et laboribus comparata, idque Dei solius gratiâ ab alijs pro suis etiamnum venditentur, dictitenturque: Quod parui facio. Neque aegre fero aliquos riuulos ad Exteros deriuari, ipso fonte penes suum Dominum adhuc Dei beneficio scaturiente. Non tamen sunt falsa proponenda, vt cognoscantur vera; Vera enim et falsa sunt dissentanea, nisi quis, vti apud Geometras fit, cum assertiue nihil probari possit, per negationem, rem in absurdum deducat. Sic etiam quae de Cometarum cauda proposuisti, tum in scripto illo tuo de Cometa Anni 85. tum literis ad me datis nunc reuocas, neque te illa tanquam vera vrsisse, sed tantum, vt meo iudicio subijceres, asseris. Licet vero et scriptum illud et literae tuae quiddam aliud prae se ferant, attamen quod et hîc in viam redieris bene me habet. Placet enim, quod ratiocinationes tuas et quae ex Illustrissimi Principis Obseruatione de Cometa Anni 58. comprobationis loco adduxisti, quam parum valida sint (ex quo ista tibi in superioribus literis ob oculos posuerim) iam satis videas, adeo vt te ista non scripsisse, ipsemet, vti apparet, malles. Quae excusandae istius Obseruationis caussa vltra ea quae ego produxi, adfers, apud me facilem merentur locum, qui non tam Obseruationi aliquantulum deuiae, sed potius, quod tu hanc non satis circumspecte nimium vrseris, omniumque aliorum in plurimis Cometis inuenta hinc eludere conatus sis, imputem. Rogas vt ego haec non publicem, vtque tuam de Cometarum generatione sententiam scriptis meis non immisceam. Atqui te hîc celare non possum, me per literas rogatum a Nobilissimo viro D. HENRICO RANZOVIO, Vicario Regio in Holsatia, tum etiam Clarissimo et Eruditissimo D. CASPARO PEVCERO, Philosopho eximio, atque quibusdam alijs non ineruditis viris id expetentibus approbantibusque, peculiari Tomo vno vel altero Epistolas Astronomicas mihi a varijs hincinde perscriptas, vna cum mea ad singulas responsione publicem. Quod et illis non denegandum duxi. Cumque dictus RANZOVIVS principaliter Illustrissimi Principis tui, atque tuas Typis consignari expetisset, et per se mererentur, vt priorem et principaliorem voluminis partem occuparent, iamque liber ille sub Praelo sit, ea quae alijs addixi, rursus in tuam gratiam detractare et facta infecta reddere nequeo. Nec video cur id a me expetendum fuerat, curque id concederem, cum nihil hac in re, tuo Illustrissimo Principi, quod obsit, aut vlla ex parte deroget, sed saltem quae in ipsius Celsitud. praedicationem, vti par est, potius cedant, admittatur. Nec etiam tibi quicquam hinc creatur periculi aut detrimenti, cum honorifice semper de te loquar et sentiam. Quod si tu quaedam in prioribus scriptis et literis proposita, postea mutas, et re rectius deliberata corrigis, tuae existimationi hinc nihil decedit, etiamsi priora publicentur. Imo praestat alios cognoscere, quam difficile sit, etiam ingeniosis et industrijs viris veritatis latebras extricare, et quod non omnia quae prima fronte recte se habere putantur, vndiquaque rite constent. Quodque dies diem doceat, et posteriores cogitationes, vt plurimum, licet non semper, priores antecellant. Reuocârunt alij admodum eximij viri multa a se prius minus considerate pro veris habita, etiam in ijs materijs, vbi non adeo difficilis erat atque hîc veritatis peruestigatio. Quando ex professo circa totius Operis Epilogum, quod de Ascititijs Caeli Phaenomenis prae manibus habeo, Cometarum naturam et procreationem, iuxta omnium quotquot mihi innotuerunt opinationes disquiro, hanc etiam tuam, cum alijs quibusdam communem praetermittere, vt quam incongrua sit, quod et alias tibi scripsi, ne indicem, haud possum. Attamen cum videam te in his teipsum corrigere atque priores conceptiones antiquare, faciam eius quoque illic mentionem, idque conuenienter excusabo. Ego tuum scriptum de Cometa publici iuris non faciam: Nihilominus nonnulla hinc citare atque expendere in laudabilem tui mentionem potius cedet, quam quod reprehensioni erit. Dicis quidem me pugnaturum cum muto, si id agam. At non mutus eras cum haec scriberes, literisque ad me datis consignares; Sunt enim notae scriptae, praesertim tua propria manu exaratae, pro ipsis verbis et loquela, imo et plus merentur ponderis. Sic etiam quantum ad meas literas attinet, quas publici iuris reddere, nisi rogatus ab amicis, vt dixi, nunquam constitueram, cum saltem obiter, prout in buccam venerat, inter plurima negotia exaratae erant, scias me non adeo earum penitere, quin tibi liberum relinquam, siue priuatim, siue publice, quocunque libuerit modo, quae in his contenta sunt, discutere, probare vel improbare. Si quid iustis rationibus, vel tu vel quispiam alius non rite in his alijsue meis constare sufficienter demonstrarit, ego illa corrigere non erubesco. Neque ego cum praeiudicio vllo, quippiam in tuis disquirendis aggrediar. Vide vt eodem candore circa mea quoque vtaris, nec iuuenili quodam feruore sententiam immaturam pronunties. Gratulor tibi, quod ex cogitatione materiae Stellarum et viae lacteae ad aliam opinionem diuerteris circa Cometarum conformationem, quam ea erat, qua antea imbutus parum Physica, longeque minus Mathematica: Es enim hîc in bona via, si eam ingredi callueris, omniaque hospitia noueris. Nec enim satis mirari poteram, qua iudicij nebula tantas absurditates astruere, vt corpus Cometarum, vel teipso fatente, prope Reuolutiones Saturninas a Terris distans (vt de eo qui Anno 85. apparuit, consignasti) ideoque necessario et Terra et Mari omnibusque circumfluis vaporibus multis parasangis maius existens, prout ferunt Geometricae demonstrationes, nihilominus hinc in supremum Aethera ascendisse, nescio quo incognito raptu, et partem esse suo toto multo maiorem, pronuntiare ausus sis; idque prorsus contra rationes Mathematicas. Si Physicis ita sentire lubet, coniungant has naenias caeteris. Bene igitur superinducis, quod ea, quae de Trabibus discurrentibus, quarum forte occasione in tam absona prolapsus es, cum reuera huc nihil conferant, tantumque haec inuicem distent, quantum Caelum et Terra, prorsus falsa esse deprehenderis. Habebam in animo, te vltimis literis et de his admonere, quod quomodo neglexerim, non recordor. Nam in scripto tuo de Cometa Anni 85. vbi talia asseuerare conaris, ad marginem memoriae caussa haec annotâram, quae vel tunc vel alias per opportunitatem commonefacienda duxi. Halitus isti siue Chasmata longe sunt nobis propiora, quam quod hyberno tempore a Sole nimium infra Horizontem depresso, illuminari queant, veluti ratio docet Geometrica et Optica. Quin et Anno 1588. eius rei euidens feci periculum die 6. Decemb. circa octauam vespertinam. Animaduerti enim tunc accurata inspectione Chasmata quamplurima hincinde versus Septentrionem volantia albicantiaque, ita vt lumen quoddam vmbram etiam Terrae illustrans prae se ferrent, longe nobis viciniora esse, quibusdam densioribus nubibus circa Horizontem Boreum segregatim dispersis, adeo vt Chasmata illa inter nos et nubes, discurrere euidenter apparerent, ita vt et alij ex meis, quibus monstrabam, id satis perspicue discernerent. A Sole igitur versus Antipodas submerso, ista vaporum illuminatio fieri nequibat; cum ne nubes quidem longe a Terra remotiores ipsius lumen percellere potuerit, quod facile Geometrice haec mensurando patebit. Nubes autem illae, de quibus loquor, non fuerunt continuae, sed circa Horizontem sparsim dispositae, ad Altitudinem, quo ad visibilem Horizontem, circiter graduum 10. Cum igitur Radij Solares partem vmbrae Terrae nobis adeo propinquam in ista eius sub Horizontem demersione non contingant, frustra asseritur, per illuminationem ab hoc quippiam eiuscemodi contingere. Simile quid saepius consideraui, praesertim hoc ipso Anno die 2. Ianuarij, cum quoque circa octauam vespertinam, quando plurima ad Septentrionem hîc spectabantur Chasmata, quae nubibus longe propiora cernebantur. Quapropter frustra hinc aliquid subsidij petijsti, pro innaturali illa Cometarum generatione stabilienda. To the most renowned and most learned man, Master Christoph Rothmann, distinguished mathematician of the Most Illustrious Prince Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, etc., his most beloved friend — heartiest greeting.

What is the meaning of this, my dear Rothmann, that you take so unwillingly, and interpret so unfavourably, the letter I last sent you, and reply to it in so disjointed [rhapsodice] a fashion — when it was written with good intent, for the sake of friendly comparison between us and of drawing out more deeply the truth that lies hidden? For nowhere in that letter did I accuse you of impiety. Of anyone's piety or impiety I arrogate to myself no judgement, leaving that, as is fitting, to the searcher of all hearts. Nor did I say absolutely that you detract anything from the authority of Holy Scripture as if of set purpose; I only admonished you that you had transferred Moses's words about the Moon, the "lesser light," incompetently to a magnitude of body — though it is in fact smaller than most of the stars — whereas they ought rather to be understood of the pre-eminence of its light as it touches the Earth. I added reasons too, not, I think, unfitting ones. And that you also derogate too much from the Prophets — in asserting that they understood no more in astronomy and physics than the ignorant crowd, however much they were divinely and immediately instructed, and that besides they had ready to hand not a few traditions of the most ancient Fathers, who cultivated the study of the visible things created by God, traditions surer and better than ours, which were doubtless received from the heathen — this I judged not to be approved, and in that opinion I still persist. For I have never thought one ought to decide lightly about these matters. Indeed Franciscus Vallesius has lately expounded many things, and not to be despised, concerning what is contained physically in Holy Scripture — even following the reasonings of the common philosophers — and that, as regards those principles, not without skilful labour; although many more things in these matters lie hidden, to be unfolded far otherwise according to a truer physics, and perhaps too to be sought rather, in no small part, from these very [Scriptures]. Moreover Lambert Daneau, a man truly most highly learned and excellently deserving in the instruction of others, has built up his Physica Theologica from both the Old and the New Codex with no useless labour. And many things could perhaps be said about these matters elsewhere, which would require a special and truly metaphysical volume — not of the kind that that Greek man of Stagira [Aristotle] has thrust upon us. As for my not sufficiently understanding your letter — neither illness, though it was grave (an illness of the body, however, not of the mind), nor occupations, though these too were no few entanglements, hindered me to the degree you suppose. Let yours and mine at least be compared, and let others judge — others who do not indulge their passions, nor are too well pleased with themselves. To what extent you have introduced things contrary to Holy Scripture, I indicated in some way both before and now; that you do this studiously everywhere I did not say. Nor can I — though I do not profess theology — nor can any of those who practise it solidly and sincerely, assent to your assertions out of the data [Dedomenis] of Holy Scripture. In vain, therefore, do you put forward this evasion, that, when two say "one," it is not granted to you that they be "one"; for neither do I hold that to be granted to others which here I censure. That Augustine sometimes pronounced rather freely about sacred things — at times not inelegantly, at times indeed somewhat feebly, allowance being made for that age nearer the times of the Apostles — of this, by God's grace, I am not ignorant. But what do all these things contribute here, or what do they do toward the matter under discussion? The question raised was whether the mobility of the terrestrial globe, and that threefold [mobility] asserted by Copernicus, can really hold together; and whether it ought to be preferred to our own discovery; and whether Holy Scripture contradicts that imagining or not. If, therefore, you have anything from the sacred Oracles, or from their interpreters — Augustine, say, or other Fathers — making for this Copernican assertion and for your endorsement of it, cite it from their writings. Provided the reasons be just, I shall not resist them. This much I know well: that Augustine — whom alone you name — nowhere conceded a motion of the Earth, neither annual nor diurnal, since, as a man somewhat less of a mathematician, he detracted from its roundness by denying the Antipodes. Which, however, must be pardoned him. Wherefore, since he did not perceive it to be round, far less could he admit a motion suited to its roundness. The opinions of your theologians, and their verdicts on these matters — though perhaps outside their profession, and less deliberated — I do not, to be sure, despise. But since I see that they were summoned and questioned only on this head, whether the fluidity of the heaven can be established from Holy Scripture without the reality of the orbs (as your clause hints), and since you know that I, long before you dreamed of these things, had myself conceded and pronounced the very same — I wonder why you object these things that contribute nothing here. For I do not suppose that the question of the Earth's threefold whirling was put to your theologians, since very few of them would grasp it, however much they might wish to — being, as happens elsewhere too, perhaps not a little addicted to earthly things. Since, therefore, only the controversy about the mobility of the Earth — renewed and reintroduced by Copernicus out of Aristarchus and certain other ancients — was being moved between us, by what coherence, I ask, were you driven to confound it with the limpidity of the heaven, which I never denied, but rather perhaps first of all men laboured not merely to affirm but to prove, from the most certain demonstrations, that it is so? Is this "mathematical," to join and entangle disparate things — which not even the dialecticians, whether artificial or natural, would have allowed in different terms?

That the substance of the aetherial world is most fluid and everywhere passable can be proved from Holy Scripture, even with the professors of theology silent — this I have long held ascertained. And I have recorded many divinely-revealed Oracles in testimony of that fact. Moreover that most renowned man, excellently deserving of public learning, Master Caspar Peucer, in a letter sent to me especially on this account, gathered very many sayings of Holy Scripture making toward this, and learnedly and excellently subscribed to my opinion concerning the most tenuous, most limpid, and nonetheless super-elemental matter of the heaven. And this chiefly, so that he might thereby also prove that the invention of my hypotheses is by no means absurd. When I shall have published, more fully than has hitherto been done, my uncontaminated reasonings about the matter of the heaven — that it is everywhere passable and packed with no solid orbs (as has hitherto been believed to gratify the ancients), and yet not [made] of an elemental and animate air, as you, and a certain John Pena the Frenchman, and certain others dare to proclaim — and when I shall have handed them over to others to judge, I shall by no means refrain — even without your prompting — from citing the authority of Holy Scripture, which deserves to hold the first place in establishing all wisdom and in founding all science. For I have always held that the glory of God alone is to be regarded in all things — which you too rightly approve — since truth cannot be inspected without His revelation and gift; for every perfect gift is from God. You seem to reproach me wittily [salse, with a pun on sal, "salt"] for assigning my "quintessence" to the aether — a manner of speaking not unknown among the chemists, nor disapproved by the ancient philosophers either, nor even by Aristotle himself (although in these matters he was not everywhere sufficiently perspicacious). But the salt is sweet, limpid, purged of all earthiness, exalted, in no part insipid or rank-smelling, but analogous to circular perpetuity — and not yet tasted [by you]. When you have smelled it, or perceived its savour, then for the first time pronounce judgement on these things; meanwhile it is better to refrain from premature prejudice. You ask that I not make you blind concerning twilights, nor accuse you of rashness, as though you write things you have not yourself experienced. But I have never presumed either to blind you or to pronounce you rash. Would that it were permitted both to me and to you and to others to be freed from that fog of the mind by which we are obscured more than we believe, and from worldly rashness — just as the external weakness of the eyes can be mended, especially by chemical means, with no great difficulty; truly, in that, so far as it could be done, I should think the very greatest labour ought to be spent. For that labour would not be uselessly bestowed. You say you know that twilight ends when the Sun is depressed 17 degrees below the horizon, taking "twilight" in the common sense for doubtful light. You suppose, then, that I am peddling an opinion at least approved by the crowd and hitherto contained in the astronomers' books, and have contributed nothing to it from my own observations. But what an accusation is this! Have I dealt with you by letter so long that you should still hold it unascertained that I am a devotee of the truth, that I seek it from no source other than either just reasoning and experience, or — what is chief — divine revelation, and that I do not of set purpose set up the false as true? You could sufficiently have gathered that I do not wish to see blindly even in greater matters that bear more on astronomy, far less that in the case of twilights — most easily observable things — I should pronounce anything idle, colluding merely with the common opinion of the Ancients, without observation of my own. I knew well enough that twilight is to be determined when no part of the air anywhere around the horizon grows even the least bit white — which was done most diligently several times, both by myself and by at least seven or eight students serving me in astronomical matters, so that no trace, not even the least, of air illuminated by the Sun could be discerned on any nearest surface of the horizon when the times of the twilights were noted; the stars' equatorial distances from the meridian being applied, and the Sun's exact place, and all things diligently weighed by a geometrical balance — and freely communicated to you, for this same reason, that, if you were willing to open your eyes, you might perceive [these times] not to have been fixed at pleasure or by common opinion. If, then, you with two eyes, and with at least a pair of instruments, can discern these things more keenly than all of us with sixteen eyes at the least, and with as many instruments and more wherever it was needed — I do not, to be sure, envy you this lynx-sightedness [lincaeitas] and modest requirement of means; rather I marvel. Indeed, I should congratulate you on a yet greater acuity and fuller sufficiency. So then, about twilights, persuade yourself of whatever you please, abounding in your own sense; I do not in the least hesitate that the things I have set forth are sufficiently confirmed, by many experiments too, and liable to no true refutation. Only see to it that, in disparaging the not-doubtful observations of others — so as to defend your own assertions about the like existence of air and aether (which you try in great part to prove from the discrepancy of vapours in different horizons, and more thickened and sublimated in the more northerly than in the southerly regions) — you do not enwrap the very truth, toward which we ought to tend, in more vapours, obscurities, and mists than all the dense and dusky vaporosities around the terrestrial horizon, in any place whatever, whether southern or northern. Accordingly, you wish not to know why I do not subscribe to the refractions exactly, since you could not otherwise extract them by instruments, and you assert that therefore nothing here is to be done to gratify me, and that in demanding this I demand unfair things. But when did I ever ask of you that, with the truth set aside, you should bring forward anything to gratify me, or press for things so unfair? For that would indeed be unfair, had I done so. On the contrary, I have always admonished, much rather, that the truth itself be more deeply unfolded, since this is well-nigh the fairest and most excellent of all the virtues. Why I cannot in every part approve the refractions asserted by you, my instruments too teach me. If, with one and another — and those at least two-cubit ones, sprung though they were from my own invention and disclosed to you through Wittich, and not even then sufficiently matured — you alone can collimate these and other things more accurately than I with more than twenty instruments, most of them four-cubit and five-cubit, and with several observers employed too, sufficiently strong in keenness of sight: well, there is reason why I should not envy you this prerogative either. Indeed it will even be so that you should give me thanks, because through the instruments devised by me (for you are not now ignorant of that) I have made you so perspicacious. Persuade yourself of this much at least: just as it is wont to happen in the art of wrestling, that no athlete forthwith discloses to his trainers those things which might at any time, through some lack, work against himself in that exercise — so neither has Wittich nor any others published all my matters that are exactly necessary for tracking down the truth, but they still, for the greater part, remain safe and whole [sarta tecta] with their author. In vain, therefore, do you boast too much of your instruments and observations. Perhaps, were you here and saw all things yourself, through far more and least-doubtful instruments, or with your own eyes alone, in my presence, you would say otherwise. But also your refractions being too small and your twilights too wide do not sufficiently agree with each other. It is necessary, therefore, that no slight error lurk in one or the other, or in both — or else that the air in this place a little more to the north, as concerns twilights, be somewhat more purged than it is there, but as concerns refractions more turbid — which, even with you yourself as judge, cannot stand together. Attend, therefore, more diligently to these matters; or, if it pleases you less, or it is not in readiness, at least acquiesce in the not-at-all-doubtful decision of others, and presume to decide nothing here prematurely for yourself. The title of my book, which you elsewhere called into doubt, or even assailed, you now approve, and no longer reckon worthy of reproach — although I did not publish that [title] which you alleged, nor prefix it to any book, but only noted it in passing in certain letters sent to your Most Illustrious Prince. I congratulate, therefore, both you and myself that you have so handsomely come to your senses; for before you objected that I introduce corruption into the heaven, since I used the word "generation" there concerning the comet, pressing my words too rigidly. But take care rather, lest now, in asserting deliberately that comets are generated in the heaven, you somewhere sin against [accepted] opinion — not so much grammatically as physically — provided it be fitting to retain the proper sense of the word. That you proposed those things not assertively but doubtfully and interrogatively, so that I might more fully explain my opinion — that is nothing new to me; for Wittich too and most others have dealt with me thus, objecting things that merited no doubt, in order to fish out certain others. But after I had carefully tested this in that case, and in others too, I became more cautious — although not a few things acquired here and there in Germany at my expense and labour, and that by the grace of God alone, are still peddled and proclaimed by others as their own; which I make little of. Nor do I take it ill that some rivulets are diverted to foreigners, while the very fountain still gushes, by God's bounty, in the keeping of its lord. Yet false things are not to be put forward in order that true things be known; for true and false are at odds — unless someone, as is done among the geometers, when nothing can be proved assertively, reduces the matter to absurdity by negation. Likewise, the things you proposed about the comet's tail — both in that writing of yours on the Comet of the year [15]85 and in the letters sent to me — you now retract, and assert that you did not press them as true, but only so as to submit them to my judgement. But although both that writing and your letters bear another aspect, nonetheless it pleases me well that here too you have returned to the road. For it pleases me that you now see well enough how little force your reasonings have, and the things you adduced by way of confirmation from the Most Illustrious Prince's Observation of the Comet of the year [15]58 (since I set those before your eyes in my earlier letter) — so much so that, as appears, you yourself would prefer not to have written them. The things you bring forward, beyond what I produced, to excuse that observation, find easy place with me — I who would impute [the discrepancy] not so much to an observation a little astray, but rather to the fact that you pressed this one too incautiously, and tried thereby to evade the findings of all others in very many comets. You ask that I not publish these things, and that I not mix your opinion about the generation of comets into my writings. But I cannot conceal this from you here: that I have been asked by letter, by the most noble man Master Heinrich Rantzau, the King's vicar [governor] in Holstein, and also by the most renowned and learned Master Caspar Peucer, an outstanding philosopher, and by certain other not unlearned men, who request and approve it, to publish in a special volume or two the astronomical letters written to me from various quarters here and there, together with my reply to each. Which I judged ought not to be denied them. And since the said Rantzau had requested that chiefly your Most Illustrious Prince's letters, and yours, be set in type — and since they deserve of themselves to occupy the earlier and more principal part of the volume — and since that book is now under the press, I cannot, for your sake, retract what I have assigned to others and make done things undone. Nor do I see why this should have been demanded of me, or why I should grant it, since nothing in this matter is admitted that would be harmful to your Most Illustrious Prince, or that derogates from him in any part, but rather only such things as redound, as is fitting, to His Highness's commendation. Nor is any danger or detriment created for you from this, since I always speak and think of you honourably. But if you change certain things proposed in your earlier writings and letters, and correct them on better deliberation, nothing departs from your reputation thereby, even if the earlier things be published. Indeed it is better that others learn how difficult it is, even for ingenious and industrious men, to disentangle the hiding-places of truth, and that not all things which at first sight are thought to be right hold good in every respect. And that day teaches day, and that later thoughts, for the most part — though not always — surpass earlier ones. Other quite outstanding men have retracted many things previously held by them, less considerately, as true — even in those matters where the tracking-down of truth was not so difficult as here. Since, in the Epilogue of the whole Work, which I have in hand concerning the Adscititious Phenomena of the Heaven [Ascititia Caeli Phaenomena], I inquire professedly into the nature and procreation of comets, according to the opinions of all, however many have come to my knowledge, I cannot but indicate this opinion of yours too — shared with certain others — and how incongruous it is, as I wrote to you elsewhere. Nevertheless, since I see you correcting yourself in these matters and superseding your earlier conceptions, I shall make mention of it there too, and shall fittingly excuse it. I shall not make your writing on the comet public; nonetheless to cite and weigh some things from it will redound rather to your laudable mention than be a reproach. You say indeed that I shall be fighting with a mute man, if I do so. But you were not mute when you wrote these things and consigned them in letters sent to me; for written marks, especially those penned in your own hand, stand for the words and speech themselves — nay, they deserve even more weight. So too, as regards my own letters — which I had never resolved to make public, unless asked by friends, as I said, since they were penned only in passing, as it came to my mouth, amid very many affairs — know that I do not so repent of them but that I leave you free, whether privately or publicly, in whatever manner you please, to discuss, approve, or disapprove what is contained in them. If, by just reasons, either you or anyone else shall sufficiently demonstrate that something in these or others of my [writings] does not hold rightly, I do not blush to correct them. Nor shall I, with any prejudice, set about inquiring into yours. See that you use the same candour about mine too, and do not, with a certain youthful ardour, pronounce a premature opinion. I congratulate you that, from considering the matter of the stars and the Milky Way, you have turned to a different opinion about the conformation of comets than that with which you were before imbued — one little physical, and far less mathematical. For here you are on the good road, if you have learned how to enter it and know all its lodging-places. For I could not sufficiently marvel by what fog of judgement you dared to assert such great absurdities — that the body of comets, by your own admission distant from the Earth nearly as far as Saturn's revolutions (as you recorded of the one that appeared in the year [15]85), and therefore necessarily greater by many parasangs than the Earth and Sea and all the surrounding vapours, as the geometrical demonstrations bear — should nonetheless have ascended thence into the highest aether, by I know not what unknown snatching-away, and should be a part much greater than its own whole; and that quite against the mathematical principles. If the physicists like to think so, let them join these doleful ditties [naeniae] to the rest. Rightly, therefore, do you draw the further conclusion that the things about running beams [Trabes discurrentes] — by occasion of which you perhaps slipped into so discordant a view — since they really contribute nothing here, and these are as far apart from one another as heaven and earth, you have caught out to be wholly false. I had it in mind to admonish you of these things too in my last letter; how I neglected it, I do not recall. For in your writing on the Comet of the year [15]85, where you try to assert such things, I had noted these matters in the margin for memory's sake, which I judged ought to be brought to mind either then or otherwise as occasion offered. Those exhalations [halitus] or chasms [chasmata] are far nearer to us than that they could be illuminated, in winter time, by a Sun depressed too far below the horizon, as geometrical and optical reasoning teaches. Moreover in the year 1588 I made an evident trial of this on 6 December, about the eighth hour of the evening. For I observed then, by careful inspection, very many chasms flying and whitening here and there toward the North — so that they bore a certain light that even illumined the Earth's shadow — to be far nearer to us than certain denser clouds dispersed separately around the northern horizon; so much so that those chasms evidently appeared to run between us and the clouds, so that others of my people too, to whom I showed it, discerned it clearly enough. Therefore, with the Sun submerged toward the Antipodes, that illumination of the vapours could not take place; since not even the clouds, more remote from the Earth, could have been struck by its light — which will easily be plain by measuring these things geometrically. Now those clouds of which I speak were not continuous, but disposed sparsely around the horizon, to an altitude — as regards the visible horizon — of about 10 degrees. Since, therefore, the Sun's rays do not, in that submersion of it below the horizon, touch the part of the Earth's shadow so near to us, it is asserted in vain that anything of this kind happens through illumination from it. I have considered something similar rather often, especially in this very year on 2 January, likewise about the eighth hour of the evening, when very many chasms were seen here toward the North, which were perceived to be far nearer than the clouds. Wherefore in vain did you seek some support from this for establishing that unnatural generation of comets. And if you wish to know what I think about these chasms: I judge it to be a certain terrestrial sulphurousness, burning and running in the nearby air, and sometimes filling the whole heaven by ascending to the zenith — yet at a modest distance from our sight — forerunners of winds, or of dryness and cold soon to follow; sometimes too, if they happen often and last too long, indications of air inclined to the contagion of an epidemic pestilence. For that disease has not a little in common with the sulphurous nature; whence too it is more readily dissolved by terrestrial sulphur excellently purified and exalted — especially if it be reduced into an agreeable liquor — as like by its like. For that maxim of the Galenists is not always true: that contraries are cured by contraries. These things about these matters I have thought it good now to bring to your mind. Most rightly, therefore, will you have done, if you change, as you promise, that whole chapter on the matter of comets and their illumination, and treat it more considerately. Nor is there need that yet another comet be awaited. For those which have passed most recently, even in our own age, abundantly convince [us] that your earlier opinion can in no way stand; which our observations in these matters will easily teach you, provided they can merit some credence. But if you judge that our decisions — though sought with what diligence could be, and not, as I hope, brought forward beside the point — are not such that you think one should safely rely on them: come then, await yet another comet, if any should occur meanwhile, and scrutinize it more diligently by measuring it, provided that be granted you. If you then find something different from our conclusions, there will be time enough to bring it forth, whatever it shall be; meanwhile, if you suspend judgement — provided it displease you to acquiesce in ours — it will not be ill-advised. That the planets hang [pendere] in the most subtle and purest air (though that is said quite improperly, since in truth they do not hang) — that you no longer wish to dispute this further with me, I gladly accept; and you do well in no longer calling the matter of the heaven animate and elemental. For here you will never obtain anything from me. If my reasons — partly already brought forward, partly to be further declared elsewhere, and adduced from lower and palpable things, yet analogous to the higher — do not suffice for you, [then] as far as I am concerned you are free to think whatever you wish. Treat this question wherever you please, and whether you involve me or not, it will be the same. I shall not, when occasion to treat of this matter is given, forbear to defend my opinion with sufficient probability. I in no way shun the judgement of most learned men who understand the matter itself; for I do not arrogate so much to myself as to presume to settle this dispute alone. I have always held the love of truth [philalithia] in esteem — perhaps to my greater inconvenience (which, however, I bear not ill) than to my profit. Nor have I ever permitted myself to be obscured, of set purpose, by the bewitchment of prejudice, as you seem to impute; for, as far as it has been divinely granted me to discern true from false, I have continually striven to do so. If you appeal to some third person (for I see you dealing with me juridically in a mathematical matter — for your brother too reported that you incline toward jurisprudence, perhaps because it is more lucrative and more conspicuous to the world) — I too accept the condition, and willingly challenge any philosophers in Germany or elsewhere capable of these matters. And, that someone be named: to the most renowned and excellent man Master Caspar Peucer, if you so please, I leave the whole matter — since he is in the first rank versed in both philosophy and the mathematical disciplines, and not slothfully devoted to the study of theology (whence this question can in great part be decided), and at the same time prevails by grave judgement and age. Nor need it occur to you to suspect that he will be a judge too favourable to my side (for you know, if you wish to be a jurist, that the favour of the judge avails more than ten laws written in the Code) — for he has never been known to me; but with your Most Illustrious Prince — whom, in a letter sent to me, he calls his best benefactor, and not undeservedly — and with you too, when present, he contracted a not ungrateful friendship. If there are others among your countrymen, or even elsewhere, to whom you wish to lodge an appeal, I too appeal to the same — provided they have inspected the matter under discussion more deeply, and have learned to understand both the higher and the lower things, and to reconcile these with one another, knowing the one from the other. For whatever is above, that also is below, as that Hermes truly Thrice-Greatest [Trismegistus] among mortals used to say. That the hidden mystery of the Trinity lurks in all created things, you rightly grant to me — although your philosophy concerning these matters, which you adduce, is more outward than inward, and too obvious to the senses; since that which is chief, and in which the kernel of the matter resides, does not appear at first sight. For it is not enough to say that all lower things consist of Earth, Water, and Air, since these very mothers too have certain other threefold principles, not yet sufficiently perceived by the common philosophers, of which there is now no place to dispute. You add then that you see — that you have now at last obtained — that I have conceded to you that the diaphaneity of pure air differs in nothing from the diaphaneity of the matter poured around the planets; and you do not on that account doubt that truth, the daughter of time, will in its own time teach me your remaining opinion too. But with what eyeglasses [perspicilla] did you ever see this so keenly? Or by what means could you ever gather it from my writings? I marvelled, reading these things, why you should wish to allege that I propose things contrary to myself in this part. So I inspected again the copy of the letter last addressed to you, from which you doubtless wished to gather this. But I find nothing of the kind in it — rather, the plain contrary of your assertion. I there first gave an example, from lower things, which by the benefit of the spagyric art [ars spagyrica] can be exalted into such subtlety that they represent a like diaphaneity, so far as it is discerned by the senses; and nonetheless I showed them to be of a different substance — and therefore that what you intended does not follow, even were it conceded, on account of refractions not tending to the zenith, that there is an imperceptible discrepancy between the heaven and purer air as regards diaphaneity, [it would] therefore [not follow that they] consist of one and the same matter. In this way I reasoned with you, nor on that account did I concede the diaphaneity of air and aether to be entirely the same; nay, I added these words, indicating otherwise: "So too the diaphaneity of heaven and air, even if they were quite the same — which however has not yet been sufficiently established — nothing nonetheless prevents [them] from having a different substance and matter." Why, then, I ask, do you boast that you have obtained from me that which I said in open words has not yet been sufficiently established? Why do you so injuriously invert my meaning? And why do you so inconsiderately precipitate judgement about these tenets [placita]? If truth, the daughter of time, shall ever inform me better, I shall surely not shrink from accepting the truth; and that on whatever occasion it shall at last be granted. Yet I do not yet see why I should assent to any of your opinions about which there is controversy between us. Nor will you evidently demonstrate that I have retracted anything of those things which I have hitherto written to you, even in passing, or otherwise issued, that is of any moment — whereas you not a few things, even in this very letter, formerly keenly defended, not merely call into doubt but wish to be wholly void: As that you did not assert the Prophets to have understood no more in physics than the sluggish crowd; and that Moses, speaking of the two great luminaries, did [something] against the geometrical demonstrations, as if this were not to detract from Holy Scripture; that you entangle the question raised about the Copernican motion of the Earth with the matter of the heaven, and strive to prove this from Augustine and other modern theologians by confounding heaven and earth; that you do not reproach the title of my volumes on the celestial generation of comets, though before you objected that I introduce corruption into the heaven if I assert generation; that you asserted the comets' tails, in so many words, and also from some observation, formerly as though they were parts of the body, consisting of the same matter, and now no longer hold them as true — nay, wish the very observation, [though] written out, to be undone; that the whole opinion about the generation of comets, formerly thrust upon me on account of a certain "Graminaeus," you now so set aside that you judge a whole chapter must on that account be changed in your writing and treated otherwise; that the running beams, formerly illumined by the Sun, you now deprive of its light — and whatever other corollaries there are. For I do not wish to dig out everything too scrupulously. Yet I do not review these things in order to reproach you with acknowledging and amending your deviations. For that I praise, and would that you had done so more often, and had permitted yourself to be instructed by truth the daughter of time, as you rightly said. Thus you would have touched the centre of the circle, which is unique, more on a straight line. If you ever show that I have written anything that needs retraction, I shall be grateful; that I have as yet retracted anything of mine I am not conscious — not that it cannot happen, as we are human, but because what you say you see in my writings has not yet been open to my eyes, nor do I yet acknowledge it. That comparison which I proposed, and somewhat explained to you, between the liquor of Water and of Wine, both purified, with respect to the matter of Air and of Heaven, perhaps deserved a greater reception than that it should be so taken. For in truth the heaven is related to elemental air just as some spirit of Wine or of another generous liquor is to purged water. And although they be almost the same in diaphaneity, yet far different in substance. Nor do I know whether a better comparison can be drawn from terrestrial things. Hence it is that certain of those who philosophize have not hesitated to call the Spirit of Wine especially — moderately exalted and purified several times, and at length freed by continual and long-lasting circulation from elemental intemperance — "Aether," or "Philosophical Heaven"; although whether it can be done in the manner they propose, [and] that certain other things be not wanting, I do not now wish to say. This much at least I would dare openly to affirm: that in each terrestrial substance there lurks something analogous to the celestial nature — in some more, in some less — which savours properly neither of Air, nor Water, nor Earth, nor even the Element of Fire (if such there were in the nature of things), although these elemental qualities too are present in each, in a certain grosser composition. Nor must it be thought that this super-elemental power is in the elemental things only from the Sun or the rest of the stars, although I would not deny this in some part. For some things are more conformed to these, some to other stars; meanwhile, however, the universal essence of the heaven too, in which these astral virtues dwell, [is such that] all lower things — according as they more or less emulate it — [are governed] not otherwise than as the stars themselves are revolved in the heaven, befitting their own nature. But let this much now suffice to have discoursed about these matters, since they do not pertain to the commonly received philosophy, and would perhaps scarcely be accepted there. The Ptolemaic or the Copernican hypotheses, whichever of these you approve, will, as far as I am concerned, be free to you. I would only advise that you form your judgement about the heaven from the heaven itself, not from papers and ingenious demonstrations, perhaps not everywhere consonant with the heaven itself. Let it therefore be permitted me too to pronounce freely what the heaven has taught me in these matters. About the Filcher of my System [Surreptor mei Systematis] I will add no word here, for he is not worth it. I replied to you briefly in my last [letter] to the things you objected to this innovation of hypotheses of ours; a fuller resolution of this kind, and of other doubts, you will obtain when I shall have treated professedly of the motion of the Secondary Movables [Secunda Mobilia], and shall have laboured to render it, as regards the reckoning, exactly conformable to the heaven itself, and shall (the Maker of the heavens favouring) have, as I trust, brought the whole matter to the wished-for end. If you think out anything more meanwhile that you suppose can be brought to the contrary, you will do a not ungrateful thing by freely producing it. For sometimes by contradiction the Truth is rendered more illustrious. The occasions which impelled me to such an ordering of the Celestial Revolutions I have also briefly indicated to you. For I was brought to this neither rashly nor for a light cause, but the heaven itself taught me to think thus of it, with no other Teacher, nor any books, nor jumbles of Tables [Tabularum Farragines] contributing anything here. I conferred too with certain other learned mathematicians in Germany about this our modern invention, which, although it does not much please certain Aristotelians, especially those too addicted to the realities of the orbs, is nonetheless reckoned quite elegant by the more judicious, who weigh the matter without preconceived opinion and inspect it more rightly. Although, to say what the case is, even though I do not despise the judgements of others, yet whether they make for or against, I care not much, provided experience itself, drawn down from heaven, evidently convince [me] that the matter is so. I know how abstruse the inner recesses of Nature have always been, and how far, very often, those things we think we rightly perceive in physics stray from the mark. Therefore I think one should rest not so much in the authority and applause of others, but rather that it befits us ourselves to make trial whether the matter be so or otherwise, and to weigh all things in the balance of Observations and Demonstrations, and to prove them. But there is now no place to discourse more on these things. But since I see that the Copernican opinion about the threefold motion of the Earth much pleases you, I shall propose, in each of these [motions], at least a single doubt — though far more could be given — and one not so laborious. First, as regards the diurnal motion about its own axis, by which the Earth is feigned to be wheeled round in 24 hours, and thus the universal course from east to west is explained away: tell me how it can come about that a leaden globe, let down in due fashion from a very high tower, should touch perpendicularly and exactly the point of the Earth placed beneath it; for that this can by no means happen, the Earth being carried round meanwhile, since its course is most swift, a geometrical computation will teach you: seeing that in one second-scruple of time the Earth must be revolved, even in these more northerly regions, nearly 150 of the larger paces [passus maiores — the greater Roman pace as a unit of distance]. From this reason out the rest. For the fall of the lead does not accompany the air, but violently passes through it. Concerning the second motion of the Earth, the annual — which would remove the eighth Sphere so far that the orb which this [annual motion] would describe vanishes with respect to it — explain whether it seems probable to you here that the space which is from the Sun, then the centre of the universe, to Saturn, is contained more than 700 times within [the space between] this [Saturn] and the Sphere of the Fixed [stars], and that the whole [space] filled with no stars. And yet it is necessary that this be so, if only the annual orb of the Earth is to appear of the size of one minute. Nay, then too the Fixed Stars of the third magnitude, which have one minute in diameter, will necessarily be equal to this whole Annual Orb — that is, they will comprehend in diameter 2284 Semidiameters of the Earth. For they will be distant nearly 7,850,000 of those same Semidiameters. What shall we say of the Stars of the first magnitude, of which some occupy two, certain ones nearly three minutes in visible diameter? And what if the eighth Sphere be removed yet higher, so that the Earth's annual motion vanishes there altogether? Deduce these things, if you please, geometrically, and you will see how great absurdities — even by inferring thus (to say nothing of others) — accompany this assumption. The third [motion], the annual being taken away, falls of itself; or if it seems to you able to hold together with it, how, I ask, can it come about that the Axis of the Earth is gyrated yearly in the contrary direction to the motion of the centre, so correspondingly that it nonetheless appears to be at rest? How too is it granted that the Axis and the Centre are agitated by a double and different motion, in a single and simple body? — to say nothing in reply about that third, diurnal [motion] supervening about the Axis. I am silent too about the entanglements of the librations, [and that they are], not everywhere corresponding to the advancement of the Fixed [stars] to which they are assigned, very discordant. Weigh these and like things carefully with yourself, and, if it be granted, resolve them at leisure. You will have more elsewhere. If, therefore, the Copernican reasoning cannot hold — as indeed it cannot — the Ptolemaic [reasoning], which Copernicus (to say nothing of others) has sufficiently refuted, nothing will hinder our invention from deserving a place. You set forth, finally, the causes why your Most Illustrious Prince has not replied to mine, which I gladly accept. I would now write to His Highness, but nothing occurs [to me] besides what I consigned in my last. And at the same time I fear lest I trouble Him — occupied with the administration of the commonwealth and other cares — with my meagre letters. Frischlin, of whom you write, has not come hither, nor sent any letter from you, though otherwise he would not be received by me unkindly; for I have heard that the man is of varied learning and eloquent. You make mention besides of the Mill suitable for making paper, which I indicated I would build, lest I be so often and so long frustrated for printing-paper to be brought from Germany for striking off my lucubrations; and you say this pleased your Prince, which I readily believe. For in these things too we have a certain mutual confluence. For just as His Highness delights in making architecture, ramparts, ditches, aqueducts, fishponds, gardens and mills and like mechanical structures, so I too, after my own measure, especially in summer time, when it is not permitted to be free for higher matters, and these are more conveniently handled. For these too smack of something geometrical, provided they are to be made with due symmetry. That mill is now ready, with a very high and very broad rampart holding back sufficient water both in summer and in winter time, and a wheel 12 ells in diameter driven by the least water, and performing a double work besides the paper-work. With very many fishponds too disposed in order here and there, so that when it shall be necessary, these mill-pools too may supply waters. And all these things were dry a few years ago. Moreover I am building here another mill for my peasants and workmen dwelling round about in the neighbourhood, where too almost no water is now thought to be at hand. For I do not wish that others should survey the paper-works and the things pertaining to my own domestic affairs, and untimely disturb them. If Victorinus Schönfeldt sends his son hither to visit me, by me it is permitted him; as soon as it shall seem [good] to him or to me that he return to his own, it will be free enough to both. Whether he be promoted to Master of the Liberal Arts or not is all one to me; yet I should prefer him to be such, rather than to be [merely] so called. But this is not displayed at so light a price; it would suffice to be a disciple of these [arts]. But I see, my dear Rothmann, that in replying to your letter — written more tersely and, as appears, with a hurrying pen — I have been too copious; which I admitted the more readily, lest I should seem to wish to avenge your brevity (as if indulging too much in retaliation) by no reply at all, or even a shorter one. For although I am now entangled in more affairs than you were in writing yours, or perhaps ever otherwise, yet I wished to be hindered by no other occupations from replying to yours, and that so amply; which in my last [letter] too, besides the illness I signified, was really so — so that, with so much time elapsed, leisure for replying to you doubtless was at hand, more conveniently and less adversely. If, however, anything of freer admonition is written in these, take it in the better part and ascribe it to the love of our common studies. Farewell, and if you have leisure, write back now at once, so that, God willing, you may obtain a reply again by the next coming Fair. Meanwhile take in good part these things, penned likewise with a hurrying pen.
Again, happily farewell.
Given at Uraniborg, 24 November, in the year 1589.
Tycho Brahe.

[Postscript:] POSTSCRIPT. Since I have learned, both from your letter and from elsewhere, that the most illustrious Prince Landgrave has resolved to make public his observations of the fixed stars over several years, and to employ your labour in carrying out this undertaking: I ask, for the honour and the benefit of Astronomy, that you do not grudge to hasten these labours — perhaps no less than the labours of Hercules — lest, by too much procrastinating, they either perish altogether, or scarcely at last see the light of publication for the common advancement of this Art. For from this both those who are now living and posterity, whom the care of such lofty matters touches, will easily judge how erroneous the places of the "fixed" stars have been, and how little congruent with the heaven, of which (whether through the want or the carelessness of the craftsmen) we have hitherto made use in so many ways, since nothing surer was at hand; and at the same time it may become plain how great a task it was to track down the boundary-markers [Hermas] of the Stars; and, by comparing your observations with mine — which I have resolved shortly (the Maker of the Stars favouring) to publish — you may discern how much more accurate, and with what constancy of labour, this matter has been carried through on both sides. For the fact that your restitutions differ from mine, in almost all the Stars, by abounding as it were by a twelfth of one degree, will easily find its excuse with those who understand the intricate difficulty of the matter. Nor indeed could it be otherwise than that the limits of the Stars fixed by you should somewhat exceed their genuine distance from the equinoctial point, since you carried it out only by means of the evening star Venus (and not likewise the morning star), and not often enough at that, the refraction then insinuating Venus to the eyes a little further in longitude than is right. Whence it follows that the Stars derived from her across that interval grow, in their longitudes too, more than was needful — to say nothing of that slippery way of observing by Azimuths and Altitudes, together with the markings of the times obtained less surely from clocks, in all of which you, as I not without reason suspect, place too secure a trust. But since in our first Volume of the Astronomical Progymnasmata, in the second chapter, where I treat expressly of the exact verification of the fixed stars, I shall set forth abundantly how the limits of the Stars — falling back at last precisely into one and the same place, and most aptly analogous to one another — have been determined by us not only from Venus western of the Sun but also from the same Venus eastern; and likewise, after about eight hundred [stars], in their canonical distribution into fixed places as to longitude and latitude, I shall insert some mention of the Landgravian rectification in these matters too, and why it almost everywhere exceeds ours by some 5 minutes, as I shall show: I am unwilling now to dwell upon this very point further here. Do you only take pains that those who desire them may shortly enjoy your Atlantean labours in this part as well. And so once again, farewell.

Apparatus

  • Sender / recipient. Tycho Brahe, writing from Uraniborg on Hven, to Christoph Rothmann (Christophorus Rothmannus, c. 1555–c. 1600), mathematician at the Hesse-Kassel court of Landgrave Wilhelm IV (Illustrissimus Princeps Wilhelmus Landtgravius Hassiae;). This is one of the principal letters of the Tycho–Rothmann correspondence, in which the two debate the materiality of the heavens, the truth of the Copernican system, the nature of comets, atmospheric refraction, and twilight. It was printed by Tycho himself in the Epistolarum astronomicarum libri (Uraniborg, 1596), the volume named in the source's edition field; that printed text is reproduced (with the Opera Omnia VI critical text as edition of record).
  • Dateline. Datae Vraniburgi 24. Nouemb. Anno 1589 = "Given at Uraniborg, 24 November, in the year 1589" (Old Style; Denmark kept the Julian calendar until 1700). The catalogue label ("1589. 24. november. Uraniborg") agrees with the dateline; no discrepancy.
  • Greek. This letter contains no Greek-script interpolations (the heuristic and several technical terms — philalithia, parasanga, chasma, trismegistus — are Greek loanwords written in Latin letters; see below). They are transliterations, not Greek script, and are kept as printed.
  • Persons named.
  • Franciscus Vallesius = Francisco Valles (de Covarrubias, 1524–1592), Spanish physician-philosopher; his De iis quae scripta sunt physice in libris sacris ("Sacra Philosophia," 1587) is the work meant.
  • Lambertus Danaeus = Lambert Daneau (c. 1530–1595), French Reformed theologian; Physica christiana / Physice Theologica is meant. "Codex" here = the two Testaments ("the Old and the New Codex"), kept literal with a gloss.
  • Stagyrita iste Graecus = Aristotle (of Stagira); the dismissive demonstrative iste ("that Greek of Stagira") is preserved.
  • Caspar Peucer = Caspar Peucer (1525–1602), Wittenberg physician-philosopher and Melanchthon's son-in-law; Tycho repeatedly invokes him as an ally on the fluidity of the heavens and proposes him as arbiter.
  • Ioannes Pena Gallus = Jean Pena (c. 1528–1558), French mathematician; in the preface to his Euclid Optica (1557) he argued the heavens are filled with ordinary (elemental) air — the view Tycho rejects ("not of an elemental and animate air").
  • Wittichius = Paul Wittich (c. 1546–1586), the Wrocław mathematician who visited Hven in 1580 and (Tycho complains) carried instrument-designs and methods to Kassel and elsewhere; he is the unnamed Surreptor mei Systematis ("Filcher of my System") of the later passage — Tycho's standing grievance over priority for the geo-heliocentric system.   • Henricus Ranzovius = Heinrich Rantzau (1526–1598), royal Statthalter (governor; Vicarius Regius) of Holstein, humanist patron; one of those urging Tycho to publish the correspondence.
  • Hermes … Trismegistus = Hermes Trismegistus; the maxim Quicquid est supra, id etiam infra ("whatever is above, that also is below") paraphrases the Hermetic/Emerald-Tablet correspondence doctrine; kept with the Latin.
  • Graminaeus quidam — "a certain Graminaeus": Theodor Graminaeus (Gravius), a 16th-c. writer on comets whose theory Rothmann had (Tycho says) earlier pressed; treated as a proper name, flagged below.
  • Frischlinus = Nicodemus Frischlin (1547–1590), the wandering humanist poet-scholar; Tycho notes he never arrived.
  • Victorinus Schonfeldt = Victorinus Schönfeld (1525–1591), Marburg professor of mathematics/medicine, whose son might visit Hven.

Organa / Instrumenta → instruments; bicubitalia / quadricubitalia / quincubitalia → "two-/four-/five-cubit" (instrument sizes); collimare → collimate (sight along). minutum = arc-minute; Scrupulum secundum temporis = "second-scruple of time" = one second of time (kept literal with gloss).
  • The threefold Copernican motion. mobilitas … triplex a Copernico asserta = the diurnal (axial), annual (orbital), and the third motion (Copernicus's annual conical motion of the axis, the motus declinationis, here librationum intricationes). Tycho's three objections — the falling-lead/tower argument, the immense empty gap to the fixed stars (and the resulting monstrous physical size of stars), and the incoherence of a third axial motion — are his standard anti-Copernican physical arguments; rendered faithfully, with his hedges ("to say nothing of others," "though far more could be given") preserved.

Dates within the letter: trials of 6 December 1588 and 2 January 1589 ("this very year"), both circa octauam vespertinam ("about the eighth hour of the evening"); the Comet of 1585 (Rothmann's tract) and the Landgrave's observation of the Comet of 1558. None silently converted.
