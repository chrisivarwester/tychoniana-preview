The original

ERUDITISSIMO ET HUMANISSIMO
VIRO D. THOMAe CRAGIO I. V. D. AMICO
SUO PLURIMUM DILECTO. EDENBURGI.

S.GRATISSIMAe mihi fuere literae tuae, vir eruditissime, quas accepi nuper refertissimas singularis in me benevolentiae et amoris notis. Quocirca etiam fusius respondere illis decreveram, modo Magnificus D. P. IUNIUS, uti proposuerat, in insula hac me invisisset. Sed spirante mox Austro, Helsingorae eum paratum iam navem ascendere cursim tantum salutare licuit, ita ut hac ratione omnis mihi rescribendi oportunitas tum sublata fuerit, et ne nunc quidem ob varias occupationes aliaque impedimenta otium ullum relictum sit, ut, quantum velim, literis complectar. Nihilominus breviusculas hasce ad te dare censui, ne ad humanissimam tuam Epistolam plane obmutescere viderer. Quod vero ad negotium de Cometis attinet, mihi perinde est, sive quis in coelo eos credat, sive in aëre versari. Sufficiet quod ex quinque huiusmodi crinitis sideribus, quae annis aliquot proxime elapsis, arduo et indefesso labore observavi, nullum infra Lunam fuisse, quin et eorum quaedam ne tantillam paralaxin, qualem Sol efficit, admisisse, ideoque ipso longe altiora et a terris remotiora extitisse invictis rationibus demonstrârim. Unde apertissime liquet omnes Aristotelicos, qui tot iam seculis contrariam tutati sunt sententiam, hac in parte plurimum aberrasse et Cometas sublunares efficiendo toto (prout dici solet) coelo deviasse. Fratri autem tuo non invideo hunc ob autoritatem ARISTOTELIS errorem, quem cum multis alijs communem habet, miseret potius quod cum Mathematicus sit, caeteris oculatior non existat. Ad literas eius respondissem quidem, si ea quae prae se ferunt, sincere et sine arroganti praeiudicio dicta esse comperissem. Verum existimo eas Capnurania ista scoptica non multo mansuetiores, adeo ut inter alia hospitalitatem etiam meam (ne quid dicam amplius) in dubium vocare non vereatur, dum sic scribit: Cupivit doctiss. vir D. legatus ut secum te inviserem, non patiuntur id amici. Si venissem, gratusne fuissem hospes? Caetera per temetipsum et pro tuâ prudentiâ atque humanitate satis intelligis. De meâ vero benevolentia erga te, cuius singularem candorem et virtutem pari doctrinâ coniuntam, cum ex praestantissimi D. PETRI IUNIJ relatione, utut inter nos breviculâ, tum ex proprijs tuis literis atque carminibus perspectam habeo, non est quod addubites, imo si quid tibi quod gratum sit per me praestari queat, invenies me quam paratissimum. Cum autem eleganti poëticâ venâ te praeditum esse ex versibus eiusmodi gratia conscriptis non obscure perspexerim, Epistolam hanc Elegiacam ad imitationem Ovidianarum nupere animi causa sororis nomine a me lusam adiungere volui, ut videas me non semper sublimia et coelestia, sed etiam terricolis, ut plurimum, usitata nonnunquam (obiter tamen) tractare.

Vale.
Datae Uraniburgi 26. Iulij Anno 1594.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the most learned and most courteous Master Thomas Craig, Doctor of Both Laws, his most dearly beloved friend. At Edinburgh.

Greeting. Most welcome to me were your letters, most learned Sir, which I received lately, crammed full with marks of your singular goodwill and affection toward me. For this reason I had even resolved to answer them at greater length, provided that the Worshipful Master P. Junius had, as he had proposed, visited me on this island. But, the south wind soon springing up, I was permitted only to greet him hurriedly at Helsingør as he was already standing ready to board ship — so that in this way every opportunity of writing back was then taken from me; nor even now, on account of various occupations and other hindrances, is any leisure left me to embrace [the matter] in a letter as fully as I should wish. Nevertheless I judged it right to send you these rather brief lines, lest I should seem to fall altogether silent in the face of your most courteous letter. But as for the business concerning the comets, it is all one to me whether one believes them to dwell in the heaven or in the air. It will suffice that, out of five such hairy stars [comets] which over some years now last past I have observed with arduous and unwearied labour, I have demonstrated by unanswerable reasonings that not one was below the Moon — indeed that certain of them admitted not even so much as that tiny parallax which the Sun produces, and were therefore far higher than [the Sun] itself and more remote from the earth. From which it is most plainly evident that all the Aristotelians, who for so many centuries now have maintained the contrary opinion, have in this matter erred most greatly, and, by making comets sublunary, have gone astray (as the saying goes) by the whole heaven. As for your brother, I do not begrudge him this error on the authority of Aristotle, which he holds in common with many others; rather I pity that, being a mathematician, he is no more clear-sighted than the rest. I would indeed have answered his letters, had I found that what they profess was said sincerely and without arrogant prejudice. But I judge them not much milder than that scoffing Capnurania of his — so much so that, among other things, he does not even shrink from calling my very hospitality (to say nothing further) into question, when he writes thus: "The most learned Worshipful Master ambassador wished that I should visit you in his company; my friends do not allow it. Had I come, should I have been a welcome guest?" The rest you understand well enough for yourself, and by your own prudence and courtesy. But as to my goodwill toward you — whose singular candour and virtue, joined with equal learning, I have come to know thoroughly, both from the report of the most excellent Master Peter Junius, brief though it was between us, and from your own letters and poems — there is no reason for you to be in any doubt; nay rather, if anything that may be agreeable to you can be furnished through me, you will find me most ready. And since I have perceived not obscurely, from verses composed for the sake of such [a favour], that you are endowed with an elegant poetic vein, I wished to append this Elegiac Epistle, lately composed by me in play, for my heart's relief, in my sister's name, in imitation of Ovid's [Heroides], that you may see that I do not always treat of lofty and celestial matters, but sometimes also (in passing, however) of things familiar to the dwellers on earth, as is mostly the case.

Farewell.
Given at Uraniborg, 26 July, in the year 1594.

Apparatus

  • Sender. Tycho Brahe, writing from Uraniborg on the island of Hven (Latin Uraniburgum; the edition's convention).
  • Recipient. D. Thomae Cragio I. V. D. = Master Thomas Craig, Doctor of Both Laws (Iuris Utriusque Doctor — of canon and civil law); the eminent Scottish jurist and Latin poet Thomas Craig of Riccarton (c. 1538–1608), at Edinburgh. The salutation singles out his learning and humanity; the body praises his "letters and poems" and his "elegant poetic vein," consistent with Craig the neo-Latin poet rather than with his comet-disputing brother.
  • Fratri autem tuo — "As for your brother": the comet antagonist is John Craig (the physician and Aristotelian, d. 1620), Thomas's brother, with whom Tycho had carried on a bitter epistolary dispute over the placement of comets (c. 1589–94). Tycho here replies courteously to the conciliatory Thomas while declining to engage John's renewed attack. cum Mathematicus sit — "being a mathematician": Tycho pointedly pities that John, though trained in mathematics, is no more clear-sighted than the lay Aristotelians.
  • Magnificus D. P. IUNIUS / praestantissimi D. PETRI IUNIJ — Master Peter (Petrus) Junius, the intermediary who had carried Craig's correspondence and Craig's good report of Thomas to Tycho; named first by initial (D. P. IUNIUS) and later in full (D. PETRI IUNIJ). The text twice calls his contact with Tycho brief (cursim … salutare; utut inter nos breviculâ).
  • Capnurania ista scoptica — "that scoffing Capnurania of his." Capnurania is John Craig's own polemical coinage, a Greek-derived portmanteau (καπνός kapnos, "smoke" + Οὐρανία/Urania, the muse of astronomy / the heavens), insinuating that Tycho's celestial comets are mere smoke or vapour. Tycho retained the term and titled his rebuttal the Capnuraniae restinctio ("the quenching of the Capnurania," ed. in Dreyer, Opera Omnia IV). scoptica (Greek σκωπτικός, "scoffing, satirical") is kept and glossed; the neologism is flagged, not Englished, by the audit's rule.
  • Embedded quotation. Cupivit vir D. legatus … gratusne fuissem hospes? — Tycho quotes John Craig's own words verbatim (the legatus = an ambassador in whose company Craig had wished to visit Hven, but was prevented by his friends), as the instance in which Craig had impugned Tycho's hospitality. Rendered as direct speech in quotation marks; vir D. legatus literally "the man, Master ambassador."
  • Astronomy / the comet argument. crinitis sideribus = "hairy stars," i.e. comets (the literal sense of cometa < κομήτης); rendered "hairy stars [comets]." infra Lunam … sublunares = below the Moon / sublunary — the Aristotelian doctrine that comets are atmospheric (sublunary) exhalations, which Tycho's parallax measurements of five comets refuted. paralaxin = parallax; qualem Sol efficit — Tycho's standard of comparison is the (small) solar parallax. toto … coelo deviasse — "gone astray by the whole heaven," a proverbial Latin idiom (toto caelo errare, to be utterly wrong); Tycho marks it as proverbial with prout dici solet ("as the saying goes"), preserved.
  • Epistolam hanc Elegiacam … ad imitationem Ovidianarum … sororis nomine — "this Elegiac Epistle … in imitation of Ovid's [Heroides] … in my sister's name." Tycho appended to this letter a Latin verse epistle in elegiac couplets, written animi causa ("for diversion / for the heart's relief") and in the name of his sister — almost certainly Sophie Brahe — modelled on Ovid's Heroides (verse letters in a woman's voice). The enclosed poem is not part of this letter's prose text. lusam (from ludere) = "composed in play / as a literary diversion"; the modest framing is preserved, not sharpened into a claim of seriousness.
  • terricolis … usitata — "things familiar to the dwellers on earth," opposed to sublimia et coelestia ("lofty and celestial matters"): Tycho's self-deprecating contrast between his astronomy and this occasional verse. obiter tamen ("in passing, however") is his hedge that such earthly trifling is only incidental — preserved.
  • Orthography (as printed). oportunitas for opportunitas; autoritatem for auctoritatem; coniuntam for coniunctam; perspexerim; the -ij digraph (proprijs, carminibus … IUNIJ, alijs) is the period spelling for -ii; circumflexes (fusiùs etc., here fusius / breviculâ / prudentiâ / venâ) are the editor's. The edition header carries inline lettered footnote markers (superscript "A") on several words (eruditissimeᴬ, refertissimasᴬ, fusiùsᴬ, Capnuraniaᴬistaᴬ, perspexerimᴬ, sororisᴬ); these are apparatus sigla, not part of the text, and are omitted from the Source block.
  • Calendar / place. Datae Uraniburgi 26. Iulij Anno 1594 = "Given at Uraniborg, 26 July 1594" — Old Style (Denmark kept the Julian calendar until 1700; the edition's convention). The edition label ("1594. 26. juli. Uraniborg") agrees with the dateline; no discrepancy. (Edinburgh likewise remained on Old Style in 1594, so no cross-style ambiguity arises with the Scottish recipient.)
  • MS. The sole witness is the Vienna draft (Kladde), cod. Vindob. lat. 9737f.
  • Correction. Latin: restored the dropped abbreviation 'doctiss.' (doctissimus, 'most learned') before 'vir D. legatus', per Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII, p.366. English: added 'most learned' to the corresponding quoted clause.
