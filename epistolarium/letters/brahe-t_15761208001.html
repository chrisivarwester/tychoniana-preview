The original

HENRICUS BRUCÆUS AD TYCHONEM BRAHE.

SERIUS quam velim, nobilissime amicissimeque D. TYCHO, tuis literis respondeo. Nunc nactus idonei Tabellarij potestatem, alijs sepositis, ad scribendum me dedi. Et recte tu quidem inobservata currere sidera, nec tam serenitatem deesse quam perdi scribis. Quae quamvis vere dicantur, quod magna pars plerisque nihil agendo effluat, tamen hisce studijs arctoa Urania occinere videtur. Hoc latus mundi nebulae dirusque Iuppiter urget. Nisi forte Academici in media luce caecutiunt, qui umbras sectantur, vel ut tuis verbis utar, formalitates verborum inanium. Id quomodo in hoc doctrinae genere fieri queat, in quo ornari res ipsa negat, contenta doceri, non perspicio. Sed hoc forte intelligis, auditorum oculos in coelum esse deducendos, aut ipsum docentem non debere scholastico pulvere contentum in illo consenescere. Ego hoc vere affirmare possum, quicquid solidi in eo doctrinae genere hisce temporibus in lucem proditum est, hoc ab Academicis profectum esse. Nam et PURBACHIUS Academicus. Idem REGIOMONTANUS, STOFLERUS. Quid de COPERNICO dicam, quem Bononiae cum DOMINICO MARIA observasse, scripta sua testantur, et Romae docuisse scio. Ut interim REINHOLDUM et RHETICUM taceam. De me nihil habeo quod dicam, quo forte respicis, nisi, non omnes sibi hunc studiorum scopum proponere, et multos destitui necessarijs, aliisque causis impediri. Et tamen hic Academicus fortasse tuis aliorumque observationibus adiutus, facta cum veterum observationibus collatione, accommodatas aliquas hypotheses extruere, et his, Geometria duce, rationem tabulas condendi demonstrare. Et inani sua pompa verborum (sic enim labores nostros vocare videris) deducere suos auditores ad recludenda veri penetralia. Verum ego suum cuique ut sit pulchrum, remitto. Imo gaudeo et laudo aliquos esse, quibus mentem et animum Dij dederunt, ut in nobili et arduo studio incumbendum sibi persuadeant. Utinam mihi ipsorum laboribus et sumptibus frui liceret, velim me dare unâ observatorem. Progredere, mi TYCHO, quo virtus tua te vocat, Dij tibi ingenium, Dij fortunam et indefessum studium dedere, da operam, ut agnoscat posteritas, te non frustra vixisse, et crassum aërem naturae beneficio et studio vince.

Venio nunc ad ea, quae de medicamentis obiter inter nos mota sunt, ubi ignorantiam decoctionum, infusionum et aliorum operum seplasiariorum minime tibi obieci. Sed hoc tum temporis me existimare memini, te necdum satis perspexisse vim et facultatem eorum, quid possint in obstructionibus tollendis, in crassis et viscidis humoribus incidendis, quantum ad intemperies corrigendas, et alia quae quotidie usu veniunt, siquidem praxin Medicam publice non exerceas, uti nos, neque etiam opus habeas. Neque, mi TYCHO, aut spiritu vitrioli aut lapidum pretiosorum viribus omnia absolvi possunt. Quod si tamen quis purius et defecatius vires eliciat separatione puri ab impuro, eum ego seplasiariujs praetulerim, uti et tu facis, et illius laboribus fruar. Non enim ars consistit in illa separatione, uti neque in dococtionibus et alijs praeparationibus, sed morborum causarumque dignotione, in naturae aegrotantis, in medicamentorum recta applicatione. Quamvis illam purificationem ego non privaverim, ut neque medicamentorum praeparationem et longe minus medicamentorum veteribus ignotorum usum utilem. Quae duo priora eius generis sunt, ut existimem, a stupidissimo quoque, sed laborioso, si praeeuntem habeat, percipi posse, tantum abest, ut subtilem ARISTOTELIS naturae interpretis discipulum requirant. Sunt enim haec (loquor de praeparationibus, non de commodo usu) mechanici operis et nullius ingenij et mentis, ubi Doctor peritus et fidelis adsit. Verum illud fuit subtilis verborum indagatoris et sententiae, autorem per scapham ligonem intelligentem et verborum portentis secretiora quaedam medicamenta describentem assequi. Ego autem arbitror, rerum cognitionem satis per se difficultatis afferre, etiamsi verborum tenebris non involvatur. Ob id tibi gratias ago, quod pro nostra amicitia confectionem vitrioli aperueris, quamvis adhuc sola tua descriptione fruamur, quod vitris recipientibus destituamur. Sumtibus enim minime deterreor. Partem alteram de Horologijs velim, si commode fieri queat, ad me mitti cum libello illius nobilis Angli de Radio, si eo carere queas, aut saltem titulum primis literis ad me mittito, quamvis libellum malim. Cum enim in Anglia sit excusus et nunc miserabilis sit Belgicus status, vix alibi consequar. Utrum aliquid librorum Lipsiae sis nactus, scire velim, et quos.

Est hic Magister LUCAS Hamburgensis non ineruditus, et harum artium, quas uterque nostrum summe colit, non imperitus, Musices non ignarus. Hic cuperet tuo beneficio iuvari, et si vel Haffniae conditio offerretur docendi, aut etiam nobilis alicuius liberi forent instituendi, operam suam offert. Uxorem habet, curta est illi supellex, ut laborandum sit illi omnino de auxilio. Itaque hunc habeas de meliore nota commendatum; nam et moribus est integerrimis et eruditus. Non dubito te, cum de omnibus optime mereri studeas, etiam nostro nomine facturum omnia, quae ab optimo nostrique amicissimo expectari possunt. Quod si existimes necessarium, ut illum coram videas, dabo operam, ut ad te transcurrat, cum aut in Insula, aut Haffniae te esse intellexero.

Bene vale, et me, ut soles, ama.
Rostochij VIII. Decembris 1576.
HENRICUS BRUCÆUS, plane tuus.

Heinrich Brucaeus to Tycho Brahe.

I reply to your letter later than I would wish, most noble and most beloved Master Tycho. Now, having got the use of a suitable courier, I have set everything else aside and given myself to writing. And rightly indeed do you write that the stars run by unobserved, and that it is not so much that clear skies are lacking as that they are wasted. Although this is truly said — that for most men a great part [of the clear skies] runs off in doing nothing — yet the northern Urania seems to sing a warning against these studies. This side of the world is pressed by fogs and by a baleful Jupiter [i.e. foul weather]. Unless perhaps it is the Academics who are blind in broad daylight, men who chase after shadows, or — to use your own words — the formalities of empty words. How that can come about in this kind of learning, in which the subject itself refuses to be dressed up, being content to be taught, I do not perceive. But perhaps you mean this: that the listeners' eyes are to be led up to the heavens, or that the teacher himself ought not, content with scholastic dust, to grow old in it. For my part I can truly affirm that whatever solid work in that kind of learning has been brought to light in these times has proceeded from the Academics. For Peurbach too was an Academic. So was Regiomontanus; so Stoeffler. What shall I say of Copernicus, whom — as his own writings attest — observed at Bologna together with Domenico Maria, and who, I know, taught at Rome? To say nothing meanwhile of Reinhold and Rheticus. About myself I have nothing to say — at which you perhaps glance — except that not all men set themselves this goal of study, and that many are deprived of the necessary means and hindered by other causes. And yet this Academic, perhaps aided by your observations and those of others, might — once a comparison has been made with the ancients' observations — build up some fitting hypotheses, and from these, with Geometry as guide, demonstrate the method of constructing tables; and with his "empty pomp of words" (for so you seem to call our labours) lead his listeners on to unlock the inner sanctuaries of truth. But I leave it to each man that his own should seem fair to him. Indeed I rejoice and praise that there are some to whom the gods have given the mind and spirit to persuade themselves that they must apply themselves to a noble and arduous study. Would that it were granted me to enjoy the fruit of their labours and expenses; I should be willing to give myself [to it] as an observer alongside [them]. Press on, my Tycho, whither your virtue calls you; the gods have given you genius, the gods have given you fortune and tireless zeal: take pains that posterity may acknowledge that you did not live in vain, and conquer the thick air by nature's favour and by [your] zeal.

I come now to those matters concerning medicines that were raised between us in passing, where I by no means charged you with ignorance of decoctions, infusions, and the other operations of the apothecaries. But I remember that I thought at that time that you had not yet sufficiently perceived their power and capacity — what they can do in removing obstructions, in cutting through thick and viscid humours, how far [they avail] toward correcting distempers, and in other matters that come up daily in practice — seeing that you do not practise medicine publicly, as we do, nor indeed have need to. Nor, my Tycho, can all things be accomplished either by spirit of vitriol or by the powers of precious stones. But if nevertheless someone elicits the powers more purely and more cleanly by the separation of the pure from the impure, him I would prefer to the apothecaries — as you also do — and I would enjoy the fruit of his labours. For the art does not consist in that separation, just as it does not in decoctions and other preparations, but in the diagnosis of diseases and their causes, in [the constitution] of the ailing nature, in the right application of the medicines. Although I would not have stripped that purification [of its value], nor likewise the preparation of medicines, and far less the useful employment of medicines unknown to the ancients. The first two of these are of such a kind, I think, that they can be grasped by even the most stupid man — provided he is industrious and has someone to lead the way — so far are they from requiring the subtle disciple of Aristotle, the interpreter of nature. For these things (I speak of the preparations, not of their proper use) are of mechanical workmanship and call for no genius or mind, where a skilled and faithful Doctor is at hand. But that other thing was [the work] of a subtle searcher-out of words and meaning: to make out an author who understands a ligo [mattock] by scapha [skiff], and who describes certain more secret medicines by monstrous [coinages of] words. I, however, judge that the knowledge of things brings difficulty enough of itself, even if it be not wrapped up in the darkness of words. For this I thank you, that for our friendship's sake you have disclosed the confection of vitriol — although as yet we enjoy only your description of it, since we lack the receiving glasses [retort-receivers]. For by the expense I am by no means deterred. The second part, concerning Horologes [clocks/timekeeping], I should like, if it can conveniently be done, to be sent to me, together with that little book of the noble Englishman on the Radius [cross-staff] — if you can do without it — or at least send me the title in the first letter [you write], though I should prefer the book itself. For since it was printed in England, and the state of the Low Countries is now wretched, I shall hardly come by it elsewhere. I should like to know whether you have got hold of any books at Leipzig, and which.

There is here one Master Lucas of Hamburg, not unlearned, and not unskilled in these arts which both of us cultivate to the highest degree, nor ignorant of Music. He would wish to be helped by your kindness; and if a teaching position should be offered, whether at Copenhagen, or even if the children of some nobleman were to be instructed, he offers his services. He has a wife, his furnishings are scant, so that he must by all means labour for [some] support. So have him commended to you under the better mark; for he is both of the most upright character and learned. I do not doubt that you, since you strive to deserve best of all men, will also do on our account all that can be expected from the best of men and our most loving friend. But if you think it necessary that you see him in person, I shall take pains that he run over to you, once I learn that you are either on the Island [Hven] or at Copenhagen.

Fare you well, and love me, as you are wont.
At Rostock, 8 December 1576.
Heinrich Brucaeus, wholly yours.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Henricus Brucaeus = Heinrich Brucaeus (Henry Brucaeus, c. 1530/31–1593), Flemish-born professor of mathematics and then of medicine at the University of Rostock, where the young Tycho had studied; one of Tycho's standing scholarly advisers, repeatedly consulted on instruments, books, and the publication of his work on the comet and the new star. The closing plane tuus ("wholly yours") matches the warm, collegial register throughout.
  • Place / dateline. Rostochij VIII. Decembris 1576 = "At Rostock, 8 December 1576" (O.S.; Denmark and the Empire were still on the Julian calendar). The dateline agrees with the catalogue label (1576. 8. december. Rostock); no discrepancy.
  • MS witness. Recorded as given in the source header: Vienna, ÖNB cod. Vindob. lat. 10686.66. (Note: the same shelfmark string is carried in the edition header for the 1571 Wolf letter; this looks like a edition-level collation artifact rather than a genuine shared folio, and is flagged here without being silently corrected.)
  • Opening sense. inobservata currere sidera, nec tam serenitatem deesse quam perdi — Brucaeus quotes back Tycho's own complaint: the stars "run by unobserved," and clear skies are not so much lacking as wasted (let slip). The whole opening paragraph answers a lost Tycho letter point by point, quoting Tycho's phrases (formalitates verborum inanium, inani … pompa verborum) back at him.
  • arctoa Urania — "the northern Urania": Urania (Muse of astronomy) in her arctoa (Arctic / far-northern) aspect, i.e. astronomy as practised in the cloudy north; rendered literally, the personification kept.
  • dirus Iuppiter — "a baleful Jupiter," a learned periphrasis for bad weather (Jupiter as sky-god / the air), glossed "[i.e. foul weather]"; the astronomical-mythological double sense is preserved rather than flattened to "the weather."
  • The "Academics." Brucaeus defends university men (Academici) against Tycho's evident jibe that they "chase shadows" and the "formalities of empty words." The roll he cites — Purbachius (Georg Peurbach), Regiomontanus (Johannes Regiomontanus), Stoflerus (Johannes Stöffler), Copernicus (with Dominico Maria = Domenico Maria Novara, Copernicus's Bologna teacher), Reinholdus (Erasmus Reinhold), Rheticus (Georg Joachim Rheticus) — are all academically-placed astronomers, marshalled as proof that solid work came from within the universities.
  • accommodatas aliquas hypotheses … rationem tabulas condendi — "some fitting hypotheses … the method of constructing tables": hypotheses in the period sense of geometrical models/parameters, not "conjectures"; tabulae = astronomical tables. Geometria duce = "with Geometry as guide."
  • crassum aërem … vince — "conquer the thick air": the heavy, foggy northern atmosphere of the opening; a closing exhortation that ties back to Hoc latus mundi nebulae … urget.
  • Medicine / alchemy. The second paragraph answers an exchange on medicamenta. Glossary-fixed and period renderings: decoctiones = decoctions; seplasiarii = apothecaries (period sense of the dealers in unguents/drugs); obstructiones = "obstructions"; crassis et viscidis humoribus incidendis = "cutting through thick and viscid humours"; intemperies = "distempers" (humoral imbalances); spiritus vitrioli = "spirit of vitriol" (the distillate of vitriol/sulphuric acid); confectio vitrioli = "the confection of vitriol" (its preparation, which Tycho had disclosed); separatio puri ab impuro = "the separation of the pure from the impure," the alchemical/spagyric refining Brucaeus subordinates to clinical judgement (morborum causarumque dignotio = "diagnosis of diseases and their causes"). The period framing is kept and not modernised to chemistry.
  • vitris recipientibus — "the receiving glasses," i.e. the glass receivers/receiver-vessels of a distillation train (rendered "[retort-receivers]"); Brucaeus lacks the apparatus, not the will or the funds (sumtibus … minime deterreor).
  • The scapha / ligo crux. autorem per scapham ligonem intelligentem et verborum portentis secretiora quaedam medicamenta describentem assequi — Brucaeus jabs at an obscurantist author who hides plain things behind "monstrous words": one who "understands a ligo [mattock/hoe] by scapha [skiff/boat]," i.e. wilfully misnames the commonplace. The phrase is proverbial in tenor (cf. the Greek tag "to call a spade a spade," here inverted — not calling a spade a spade); the exact target author is not named in the text. Rendered with both Latin nouns glossed and the polemical sense kept; flagged rather than resolved to a named work.
  • The Horologia / English Radius book. Partem alteram de Horologijs — "the second part, concerning Horologes": a treatise/section on clocks or timekeeping that Tycho had (or was preparing), of which Brucaeus wants the second part. libello illius nobilis Angli de Radio — "that little book of the noble Englishman on the Radius": a printed English book on the radius astronomicus (cross-staff). Brucaeus notes it "was printed in England" and is now hard to obtain because the Low Countries (Belgicus status) are in upheaval (the Dutch Revolt). The most likely referent is Thomas Digges, who made a noted study of the cross-staff fitted with transversal divisions (Dreyer, Tycho Brahe; cf. J. J. Roche, "The Radius Astronomicus in England," Annals of Science 38 [1981]); a reference to his father Leonard Digges (A geometrical practise, named Pantometria, London 1571) is also possible. The identification is probable, not certain, and is flagged.
  • Magister Lucas Hamburgensis — "Master Lucas of Hamburg," an otherwise here-unidentified learned man (musician and mathematician) whom Brucaeus commends to Tycho for a teaching or tutoring post; de meliore nota commendatum = "commended under the better mark" (i.e. warmly, in the higher class of recommendation).
  • in Insula, aut Haffniae — "on the Island [= Hven] or at Copenhagen (Haffnia)": by Dec. 1576 Tycho was establishing himself on Hven, granted to him by Frederik II that year; Brucaeus already locates him there.
  • Formulae. D. TYCHO = "Master Tycho"; Bene vale, et me … ama = "Fare you well, and love me [as you are wont]"; plane tuus = "wholly yours."
  • Negation check. The dense medical paragraph turns on several negations, all reproduced one-for-one: ignorantiam … minime tibi obieci ("I by no means charged you with ignorance"); te necdum satis perspexisse ("you had not yet sufficiently perceived"); Neque … aut spiritu vitrioli aut … omnia absolvi possunt ("Nor can all things be accomplished either … or …"); non … ars consistit in illa separatione, uti neque in decoctionibus ("the art does not consist in that separation, just as it does not in decoctions"); illam purificationem ego non privaverim, ut neque … et longe minus … ("I would not have stripped that purification [of value], nor likewise … and far less …"); etiamsi verborum tenebris non involvatur ("even if it be not wrapped up in the darkness of words"). No negation added or dropped.
  • Correction. "eximere" corrected to "extruere" (Dreyer, TBOO VII, p.43); English "draw out some fitting hypotheses" corrected to "build up some fitting hypotheses" to match.
