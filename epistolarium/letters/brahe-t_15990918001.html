ERUDITISSIME ET PRÆSTANTISSIME DOMINE M. ANDREA WELLEIE, AMICE DIU MULTUMQUE DILECTE. S. P.

ACCEPI quidem antequam Witeberga abirem, tuas literas, ad quas etiam luculenter respondi. Sed cum ea, quae filio hac de re dictavi, inter componendas res meas ita distracta sint, atque inter alia forte, quae nunc inquirere non vacat, disposita, ut reperiri nunc nequiverint, nec etiam tuae literae nunc ad manus sint, describam ea, quae memoria dictitat, quaeque de rebus meis te scire velim. Atque ut historiolam habeas eorum, quae mihi partim in Patria occasionem discessus dederunt, partim postea in exteris regionibus evenerunt (lubet enim paulo altius ordiri, etiamsi quaedam ex illis tibi anteaA forte perspecta sint) sic habe.

Cum praevidissem Uraniae istic nostrae, cui tam serio tamque impense tot annos litavimus, ruinam instare, praemonui discrete per literas Cancellarium Regis, ut rectius provideret atque ne id fieret praecaveret. Responsum est, Regi Instrumenta ista Astronomica suis impensis conservare non esse integrum, quod et eventus testabatur. Nam praeter beneficium illud Norvagicum, quo, statim ac coronatio peracta est, privatus sum, etiam caetera, quae a Rege habui, adempta sunt, relicta sola Insula, et 11 vel 12 in ea parte Scaniae, quae Culda vocatur, rusticis, quae ducentos tantum quotannis Ioachimicos in certa pecunia nonA pendent, et concessa adhuc aliquantisper Praebenda ista Roeschildensi, quae pro quarta parte meis expensis annuis non sufficiebat, quam tamen, cum vix adhuc littus patrium reliquissem, Cancellarius a Rege pro se impetravit atque in privatos suos usus transtulit, adeo ut multi verosimiliterA inde collegerint quod huc cursus fuit. Nam praeterquam [quod] reditus pene omnes necessarij denegati, multis alijsA gravibus ... et non paucis molestijs praeter exspectationem et meritum pene absque intermissione conquassatus sum, forte quo promptius et citius abirem, adeo ut ne in proprijs quidem et a me exstructis aedibus Hafniensibus, quo instrumenta mea pro maiore parte contuleram atque unâ cum typographia deposueram, studia mea, quae tamdiu in honorem Patriae molitus sum, privatis sumptibus pertexere et quaedam in ijs ad Colophonem deducere permissum sit, tranquillitate praerepta Philosophica et dispositione instrumentorum in turri vicina, mihi integerrimeA a Proregibus et insuper a Magistratu urbis ea de causa concessa, nomine Regis, licet absentis, a Curiae Magistro per urbis Praefectum interdicta, utut is tum, unus e Quatruumviris, literas, quibus Magistratui HafniacoA mandabatur, ut turrim illam in eos usus mihi relinqueretA, subscripserit. Videns itaque, mihi in Patria omnia esse adversa, et adversum eorum animum, qui haec studia, quae in laudem Patriae perpetuam moliebar, promovere debebant, lubens et alacri fortique animo inde cum omnibus meisA discessi, idque iuxta solstitium aestivum, ante biennium a superiore huius anni numeratumA. Ne tamen viderer nimis propere nimisque remote patriam deserere, substiti primum Rostochij per anni quadrantem. Dedit ibi illustrissimus Dux Megapolitanus, avus Regis nostri maternus, literas ad nepotem diligenter scriptas, quibus quatuor adductis non minimis rationibus, eum de restitutione Uraniburgi rogabat et admonebat; quin et ego, scriptis inde ad Regem meum serenissimum literis, meam etiamnumA ipsi et Patriae prae alijs Principibus et nationibus operam subiectissime obtuli, modo tolerabilibusA conditionibus id fieri posset atque indemnitati meae consuleretur. Verum omnia incassum, nihil respondente Rege avo, et meas inclementius, quam sperassem et quam forte meritae erant, accipiente. Postea increbrescente peste Rostochij in Holsatiam a laudatae memoriae viro Domino HENRICO RANTZOVIO, affine meo et amico honorando, per literas evocatus, eo me cum tota familia recepi, atque arcem quandam eius, semisse saltem milliaris Hamburgo distantem, in quam etiam instrumenta, quotquot Hafniae habui, et suppellectilem isticA relictam transtuli, ipso benigne concedente, per integrum annum inhabitavi, volens sic non sine magno rei familiaris dispendio Patriae studere, atque nostratibus rem rectius dispiciendi satis superque multum relinquere temporis, ut in posterum eo magis essem excusabilis, si relicta Dania aliam quaererem Patriam aliumque laborum meorum Mecaenatem.

Interea dum ibi sum, relatum est de me meoque e Patria abitu ad augustissimum Imperatorem RUDOLPHUM Secundum, qui me mox iuxta Pascha praeteriti anni accersiri voluit, sed ante Michaëlis festum iter inde versus Bohemiam non suscepi, quod annuum tempus, prout in animum induxeram, istic complere vellem. Circa initia itaque OctobrisA Pragam versus profectionem instituebam, relicta Wandesburgi familia mea muliebri, donec de Caesaris clementissima voluntate edoctus essem. Dresdam cum advenissem, audivi in Bohemia omnia turbata esse ob pestem et dysenteriam istic grassantes, Imperatorem Pragae non esse, sed in arcem quandam secessisse, ut tutior sit, nec dari cuipiam audientiam (uti vocant) quare, scriptis in aulam literis, quid mihi faciendum restaret, percunctatus sum. Rescriptum est manu magnifici Domini CORADUCIJ Imperij Procancellarij in hunc modum: Caesaream Maiestatem de meo Dresdam adventu perlibenter intellexisse, utque istic aut in vicinia, si Pragam accedere nonA luberet, me continerem, cupere, donec, sedatis morbis epidemicis, aula dispersa recolligeretur, interea, ubi commode collocandus sim, benigne cogitare. Quo accepto responso, Witebergam pedem retuli, atque istic dimidio anno moratus sum, Philippaeas aedes inhabitans. Licet enim statim in fine Ianuarij ex clementissimo Caesaris mandato per dictum magnificum Dominum CORADUCIUM Procancellarium Pragam rursus auguste et honorifice (habes hîc literarum exemplarA) accersitus sim, tamen ante solstitium aestivum huius anni iter eo suscipere non concedebaturA, tum quod nonnullos libros Caesari destinatos prius absolvere nequiverim, tum quod ob filiae natu grandioris invaletudinem ibi ultra voluntatem detinerer, sed circa dictum tempus itineri me accingens, et in transitu Dresdae relicto Gynaeceo meo (quod Witebergam circa brumam antecedentem e Wandesburgo ad me venire feceram) Pragam circa Iohannis festum, veteri numeratione, intraviA.

Quae vero mihi postea istic et alias in Bohemia evenerunt, nunc brevibus commemorabo. Ab ipsius Caesarea Maiestate clementissimo favore acceptus sum, solusque auditus, nemine alio praesente, et respondente ipso Caesare benignissimis verbis, idque Latine. Tres libros, quos ipsius Caesareae Maiestati in proprias manus humillime porrexi (sic enim fieri voluit) lubentissime accepit et sequentibus mox diebus, interdumque usque in multam noctem (referente magnifico Domino BARVICIO) perlegit. Iussus sum mox Gynaeceum meum Dresdae relictum advehi curare. Actum deinde est mecum de annuo salario, quod cum Caesaris clementiae et benignitati liberum relinquerem, deputatum mihi est ab ipsius Maiestate trium millium florenorum in certa pecunia, et in accidentarijs quibusdam alios millenos attingens, reluctantibus quidem nonnullis ex Consiliarijs, quod nemo in aula, etiam ex Comitibus et Baronibus, quidiu inservierunt, tantum quotannis obtineretA, sed urgente Caesare ut sic fieret, nec dissuadente illustri Domino RUMPHIOA, Curiae Magistro, aut Domino TRAUTSONIO, aulae Mareschallo, aut ullis ex praecipuis, ita decisum est, numeratique mox bis mille floreni honorarij loco pro introitu. Quin et ut stipendium meum ab eo tempore, quo primum vocatus sum, inchoaretur, clementissime Caesar indulsit, utut non citius advenerim, siquidem interea nullum alium Dominum agnoscere et acceptare voluerim. Promisitque insuper benignissime et perlibenter feudum aliquod pro me et meis, secundum Imperij consuetudinem, haereditarium, quam primum aliquod in suis ditionibus vacaritA, ut familiae meae post me in peregrinis oris consuleretur. Introductus quidem sum in aedes Curtianas magnifice exstructas, impensis maioribus viginti millibus Ioachimicis, arcique vicinas, etiam antequamA audientiam (uti loquuntur) habueram, idque per praenominatum magnificum Dominum BARVICIUM, Imperij supremum Secretarium Caesarisque intimum, qui pene omnia solus apud ipsum efficit. Verum cum is animadverteret, me eas non admodum affectare (nolebam enim, ut vidua, quae eas etiamnumA inhabitabat, mea de causa emigraret, quodque nimijs visitationibus obnoxiae essent) subiecit, Caesarem facile aliquam ex suisA arcibus mihi inhabitandam concessurum, unius vel dimidijA diei itinere ab urbe distantem (si sic vellemA) et iudicavit pro me hoc esse commodius, quodque seorsimA degens invidiae aliorum minus forem obnoxius. Id cum magis arridere intelligeret, ad Caesarem rem detulit. Advocatus mox Capitaneus Brandeisensis, qui in tres Caesarianas arces me deduceret. Prima Brandeisia, distans ab urbe 3 milliaribus, altera Lissa, 4 remota, tertia BenachiaA, quae 5 milliaribus sive 6 horarum itinere (est enim via plana) inde abest. Quibus perlustratis praetuli hanc Benachiam, in quam paucis post diebus per eundem Capitaneum solenniter introductus sum. Obtulit etiam Caesarea Maiestas sponte sua, se novum Uraniburgum pro me aedificariA curaturum, quod Danico illi, in libro quem obtuleram repraesentato, non cederet, iussitque Caesar mihi per BARVICIUM haec verba conclusionis loco dici: si ea, quae iam deputaratA, mihi non sufficerent, et pluribus in posterum opus haberem, nec sibi esse accurtatam manum, nec mihi obturatum os. At ego subiectissime gratias agens, ijs pro tempore acquievi. Extruximus nunc hîc Benachiae novum observatorium pro omnibus meis instrumentis rite et seorsim disponendis, simulque opportunum laboratorium pro exercitijs Spagyricis, tum quoque alijs, quaecumque voluero, ipso Caesare sumptus benignissime praebente, et quotquot desideraro artifices mechanicos sustentante, quod in Patria proprijs impensis faciendum erat, nulla tamen residua manente gratia proA ijs aut etiam pro longe maioribus. Omnia erant ingrata, nihil fecisse benigne; ideoque ei vale dixi.

Ago vero Deo opt. ter max. ex toto pectore gratias, qui me tam benigne ex istis, quas istic diuA multumque fortiter, licet indigne, sustinui, difficultatibus et adversitatibusA liberarit imo vindicarit, et mihi meisque rectius in alienis oris prospexerit, ut studia haec, quae tanto conamine molior, pacate et commode tractare atque, Deo benedicente, ad exoptatum finem deducere possem in Caesaris atque Imperij honorem, siquidem Dania, Patria mea, hac gloria se ipsam tam inconsiderate privarit. Sunt hîc egregij et politi homines, virtute et dignitate valentes, qui me favore et amore officiose prosequuntur, egoque vicissim illos, exoptantque, ut diu ante advenissem, quod et ego optarimA. Sed obstabant Senatusconsulti literae in Dania mihi statim ab obitu laudatissimae memoriae Regis FRIDERICI concessae et aliter, quam evenit, promittentes, paucorum tamen culpa, indolentibus caeteris viris integris et Patriae honoris studiosis, meique amantibus. Agasque Deo propitio et ter maximoA mecum devote gratias, agantque omnes pij et integriA bonarumque scientiarum amantes, quod in hoc augustissimo et laudatissimo Caesare tam benignum Mecaenatem mihi et meis prospexerit, cui aeviternoA Deo sit laus, honor et gloria in secula.

Mitto iam filium meum TYCHONEM cum MULÆO domestico meo in Daniam, ut instrumenta, istic ob magnitudinem relicta, aliasque res meas ante hyemem per Albim asportari faciant, quo etiam nomine Imperatoria Maiestas ad Magistratum tam Magdeburgensem quam Hamburgensem clementer scripsitA et Dominus BARVICIUS iussu Caesaris in Daniam ad Dominum HENRICUM RAMELIUM, Senatorem Regni, quo negotium istudA promptius procuret. Dedit etiam ipsius Caesarea Maiestas ad illustrissimum Electorem Brandeburgicum literas, quibus eum ut MULLERUM Mathematicum suum per unum aut alterum annum mihi concedat laborumA socium (sic enim inter MULLERUM et me, cum Witebergae me inviseret, convenit) rogat. Quin et alias Caesarea Maiestas, quaecunque in rebus mechanicis vel alijs, ad opus quod molior conducentia, clementissime mihi subministrari iussit, estque praeterea benignissimo et Caesareo favore erga me et meos affecta.

To the most learned and excellent Master Andreas Velleius, my long- and much-beloved friend. Heartiest greeting.

I did indeed receive your letter before I left Wittenberg, and I answered it fully too. But since the notes I dictated to my son on this matter were so scattered amid the packing of my affairs, and stowed among other things which I have no leisure now to search out, that they cannot now be found — nor is your letter now to hand — I shall write down what my memory prompts, and what I would have you know of my affairs. And so that you may have a little history of the things which partly, in the fatherland, gave occasion for my departure, and partly afterwards befell me in foreign regions (for I am minded to begin a little higher up, even though some of them may perhaps have been known to you before) — take it thus.

When I had foreseen that ruin was at hand for our Urania there, to which we had so seriously and so lavishly sacrificed for so many years, I discreetly forewarned the King's Chancellor by letter, that he should provide better and take care it not happen. The answer was that it was not feasible for the King to maintain those astronomical Instruments at his own expense — which the outcome too bore out. For, besides that Norwegian benefice of which I was deprived as soon as the coronation was over, the other things too which I held from the King were taken away, leaving only the Island, and 11 or 12 [farms] among the peasants in that part of Scania called Kullen, which pay only two hundred joachimsdalers a year in fixed money; and that Roskilde prebend, still granted for a little while, which did not suffice for a quarter of my yearly expenses — yet which the Chancellor, when I had scarcely yet left my native shore, obtained from the King for himself and transferred to his own private use, so that many quite plausibly gathered from this which way the course had been tending. For, besides that almost all the necessary revenues were denied, I was battered with many other grave [burdens] and not a few vexations, beyond expectation and desert, almost without intermission — perhaps so that I should go away the more readily and quickly; so that I was not even allowed, in my own Copenhagen house, built by me — to which I had brought the greater part of my instruments and deposited them together with the printing-press — to complete at my own expense the studies I had so long laboured at in honour of the fatherland, and to bring some of them to a colophon [conclusion], philosophical tranquillity being snatched away; and the arrangement of the instruments in the neighbouring tower — granted me for that purpose, in the fullest terms, by the Viceroys and moreover by the city Magistracy — was forbidden, in the King's name (though he was absent), by the Master of the Court through the city Praefect — although the latter, then one of the Four Men [councilmen], had himself signed the letters by which the Copenhagen Magistracy was ordered to leave me that tower for those uses. Seeing therefore that everything in the fatherland was against me, and adverse the mind of those who ought to have furthered these studies which I was labouring at for the perpetual praise of the fatherland, I departed thence gladly and with eager, strong spirit, with all my people — and that about the summer solstice, two years before that of the present year. Yet, lest I should seem to desert the fatherland too hastily and too far off, I first halted at Rostock for a quarter of a year. There the most illustrious Duke of Mecklenburg, our King's maternal grandfather, gave a letter diligently written to his grandson, in which, adducing four weighty reasons, he asked and admonished him concerning the restitution of Uraniborg; and I too, writing thence to my most serene King, most humbly offered my service even then to him and the fatherland before other princes and nations, provided it could be done on tolerable conditions and care taken for my indemnity. But all in vain — the [Duke,] the King's grandfather, getting no answer, and [the King] receiving my [letter] more harshly than I had hoped and than perhaps it deserved. Afterwards, the plague increasing at Rostock, summoned by letter into Holstein by the man of praised memory Lord Henrik Rantzau — my kinsman-by-marriage and honoured friend — I betook myself thither with my whole family, and for a whole year inhabited a certain castle of his, distant only half a mile from Hamburg, into which I also moved the instruments I had at Copenhagen, and the furniture left there, he kindly granting it; wishing thus, not without great loss to my household estate, to serve the fatherland, and to leave my countrymen more than enough time to consider the matter aright, that I might hereafter be the more excusable, if, leaving Denmark, I should seek another fatherland and another Maecenas for my labours.

Meanwhile, while I was there, news of me and of my departure from the fatherland was carried to the most august Emperor Rudolf II, who wished me to be summoned soon, about Easter of last year; but I did not undertake the journey thence toward Bohemia before Michaelmas, since I wished to complete the year there, as I had resolved. So about the beginning of October I set about the journey toward Prague, leaving my womenfolk at Wandsbek until I should be informed of the Emperor's most gracious will. When I had reached Dresden, I heard that everything in Bohemia was disturbed by the plague and dysentery raging there; that the Emperor was not at Prague but had withdrawn into a certain castle, to be safer; and that no one was given audience (as they call it). Wherefore, writing a letter to the court, I inquired what remained for me to do. The reply was, in the hand of the Magnificent Lord Coraduz, Vice-Chancellor of the Empire, after this manner: that His Imperial Majesty had most gladly learned of my arrival at Dresden; that he desired me to keep myself there or in the vicinity, if I did not care to come to Prague, until, the epidemic diseases being settled, the dispersed court should be reassembled; and that he was meanwhile kindly considering where I might conveniently be placed. Having received this reply, I withdrew to Wittenberg, and stayed there half a year, inhabiting the Melanchthon house. For although, immediately at the end of January, by the most gracious command of the Emperor, through the said Magnificent Lord Coraduz the Vice-Chancellor, I was again augustly and honourably summoned to Prague (you have here a copy of the letters), yet I was not allowed to undertake the journey thither before the summer solstice of this year — partly because I could not first finish certain books destined for the Emperor, partly because I was detained there beyond my will by the illness of my elder daughter. But about the said time, girding myself for the journey, and in passing leaving my Gynaeceum [womenfolk] at Dresden (which I had had come to me at Wittenberg from Wandsbek about the previous winter), I entered Prague about St. John's day, by the old reckoning.

But what afterwards befell me there, and elsewhere in Bohemia, I shall now briefly recount. By His Imperial Majesty himself I was received with most clement favour, and heard alone, no one else being present, the Emperor himself replying in most kind words, and that in Latin. The three books which I most humbly handed to His Imperial Majesty's own hands (for so he wished it done) he received most gladly, and read through over the following days, sometimes far into the night (as the Magnificent Lord Barvitius reports). I was soon bidden to have my Gynaeceum, left at Dresden, brought up. Then there was dealing with me about an annual salary, which, since I left it free to the Emperor's clemency and kindness, was assigned me by His Majesty at three thousand florins in fixed money, reaching another thousand in certain perquisites — some of the Councillors indeed objecting, since no one at court, even among the Counts and Barons who had served long, obtained so much yearly; but the Emperor urging that it be so, and the illustrious Lord Rumpf, Master of the Court, not dissuading, nor Lord Trautson, Marshal of the court, nor any of the chief men, it was so decided; and two thousand florins were soon paid by way of honorarium for my entry. Moreover, the Emperor most graciously granted that my stipend should begin from the time I was first summoned, although I had not come sooner, since meanwhile I had been unwilling to recognise and accept any other Lord. And he besides most kindly and gladly promised some fief for me and mine, hereditary according to the Empire's custom, as soon as any should fall vacant in his dominions, that provision might be made for my family after me in foreign shores. I was indeed brought into the Curtius house — magnificently built, at an expense of more than twenty thousand joachims, and near the castle — even before I had had audience (as they say), and that through the aforenamed Magnificent Lord Barvitius, supreme Secretary of the Empire and the Emperor's intimate, who alone accomplishes almost everything with him. But when he noticed that I did not much desire it (for I did not wish that the widow who still dwelt there should move out on my account, and because [those houses] were exposed to too many visitations), he suggested that the Emperor would readily grant me one of his castles to inhabit, a day's or half a day's journey from the city (if I so wished), and judged this more convenient for me, and that, living apart, I should be less exposed to the envy of others. When he understood that this pleased me more, he referred the matter to the Emperor. Soon the Captain of Brandeis was summoned, to conduct me to three imperial castles: the first Brandeis, distant 3 miles from the city; the second Lissa, 4 [miles] off; the third Benátky, which is 5 miles, or 6 hours' journey (for the road is level), away. Having surveyed these, I preferred this Benátky, into which a few days later I was solemnly introduced by the same Captain. His Imperial Majesty also offered, of his own accord, that he would have a new Uraniborg built for me, that should not yield to the Danish one represented in the book I had presented; and the Emperor ordered these words to be said to me through Barvitius, by way of conclusion: that if what he had already assigned did not suffice me, and I should need more hereafter, neither was his hand shortened, nor my mouth stopped. But I, giving most humble thanks, acquiesced in them for the present. We have now built here at Benátky a new observatory for setting up all my instruments duly and separately, and at the same time a convenient laboratory for Spagyric [alchemical] exercises, and others too, whatever I shall wish — the Emperor most kindly furnishing the costs, and supporting as many mechanical artificers as I desire: what in the fatherland had to be done at my own expense, with no gratitude remaining for them, or even for far greater things. Everything was thankless; nothing was [counted] graciously done; and so I bade it farewell.

But I give thanks to God, the best and thrice-greatest, with my whole heart, who has so kindly freed — nay, vindicated — me from those difficulties and adversities which I there long and much endured, bravely though unworthily, and has provided better for me and mine in foreign shores, that I might pursue these studies, which I labour at with so great an effort, peacefully and conveniently, and, God blessing, bring them to the longed-for end, to the honour of the Emperor and the Empire — since Denmark, my fatherland, has so inconsiderately deprived itself of this glory. There are here excellent and polished men, strong in virtue and dignity, who attend me with favour and love, and I them in turn; and they wish that I had come long before, as I too could wish. But there stood in the way the Senate-decree letters granted me in Denmark immediately upon the death of King Frederik of most praiseworthy memory, promising otherwise than has turned out — by the fault, however, of a few, the other upright men, zealous for the fatherland's honour and loving me, grieving at it. And give thanks devoutly with me to God, propitious and thrice-greatest — and let all do so who are pious and upright and lovers of the good sciences — that He has provided for me and mine, in this most august and most praiseworthy Emperor, so kind a Maecenas: to whom, the eternal God, be praise, honour, and glory unto the ages.

I am now sending my son Tycho, with Mule my servant, into Denmark, that they may have the instruments left there for their size, and my other things, carried off before winter by the Elbe — on which score, too, the Imperial Majesty has graciously written to the Magistracy both of Magdeburg and of Hamburg, and Lord Barvitius, by the Emperor's order, into Denmark to Lord Henrik Ramel, Senator of the Realm, that he may the more readily expedite that business. His Imperial Majesty has also given letters to the most illustrious Elector of Brandenburg, asking him to grant me, for a year or two, his mathematician Müller as a partner in my labours (for so it was agreed between Müller and me, when he visited me at Wittenberg). And the Emperor has besides most graciously ordered that whatever in mechanical or other matters conduces to the work I am undertaking be supplied to me; and he is moreover affected with most kind and imperial favour toward me and mine.

You have now, dearest Master Andreas, the little history of my affairs — how, departing from Denmark, I betook myself first into Germany, then into Bohemia, where too I was received most kindly by the Emperor and most courteously by all the best men. And since you have always by your very nature delighted in Histories, I think this little narrative too will not be unwelcome to you. It is written on account of the acquaintance that subsists between us, and of our common love also of astronomical matters. The matter has been narrated as it was done; would that every History were equally certain in itself. Do you here excerpt a little compendium and summary, and enter it in your annals or fasti for the sake of memory to posterity, as many other famous men in Germany and elsewhere do. And many besides, over and above those dealing with me by letter on this matter, burden me with poems — examples of which, if I am allowed to copy them so quickly, I shall append, in case you have perhaps not received some of them before, through Master Christiern or from elsewhere. And if you dare, and if your vein is still vigorous (as I think it is), sing along not only in verses, and increase the heap of the others, but also do not neglect, in due time, the things to be set down in prose for the memory of the well-managed deed. He who succeeded you in the Historian's office said to me, when I was still in Denmark, that he would gladly indeed bring forth to the Histories an Epithalamium [wedding-song] of the island of Hven, but not an Epitaph. You know what is alluded to, and you know the first author of these words — but perhaps to his own harm, not mine. One often does another good, although he means it ill; and the false man strikes his own lord upon the neck. That other Historian said, moreover, that he could be held excused if he made mention only of the Epithalamium and passed over the Epicedium [funeral-song], since he had found that [office] in the time of King Frederik of most praiseworthy memory, down to whose death alone he was enjoined to weave his relations, and no further, as is not the custom. But you, since you were unexpectedly cut off from this Historian's office (certain politicians so willing it), it will be permitted to you, privately if not publicly, to weave the commemoration of both times, and to hint, both of the Epithalamium and of the Epitaph, the things you know and have learned to be true — only not openly, at least tacitly; and though it may perhaps not be granted to publish them, let them be reserved for those who desire to know the truth, whether in our times or afterwards. It costs nothing. And the matter is perhaps of greater moment than is reckoned among us, and the rumour of it has now filled the world, all sensible and upright men marvelling that the fatherland's honour was neglected among us. Wherefore I shall add no more, lest I seem to extol myself and my studies.

You understand from the aforesaid, dearest Master Andreas, most of what should be written by way of reply to your letter received at Wittenberg, and why I should go neither to Breslau, as you advised, nor to Cologne — each too distant from the Emperor and inconvenient for me.

For the rest, what you added about that Ditmarsh Brutus was rightly uttered by you, and I gladly received his rank poem which you sent; and it is certain that Erik Lange, through the carelessness and imprudence to which he is liable even in his own affairs, gave the handle by which I came to be bespattered, beyond my desert, with such great calumnies by so impure a Beast — since he brought him onto my island with him, without consulting me and against my will. But [the Beast] was on that account compelled, at Magdeburg, under a public Notary, to testify what he knew to be true, against that libellous writing — though even this he did grudgingly; and everything was set down in public instruments (as they call them), on parchment, written partly in Latin partly in German, that I might have at hand the means to convict him at Prague, when it should please me to bring an accusation — not indeed through myself, since I judge him unworthy, but through some Procurator, hired with my money; which I had resolved to do before. For it concerns my honour and reputation, which I must defend, even had I not come to Prague. But that man, ill at ease in his own conscience, withdrew himself a little after my arrival, deserting even there the wife he had recently married — an infamous and adulterous one (a lid worthy of the pot). But when opportunity is given, that Beast must be tracked down at the opportune time, when I shall have breathed a little from weightier business, and dragged into court wherever he has lurked. Which will not even be difficult for me, whether it must be carried out at Prague or elsewhere. I have on this matter quietly consulted the Archbishop of Prague — an illustrious man, sprung of distinguished stock — [as to] whether that libellous writing had been printed at Prague with his knowledge, since it is his office to take notice of such things. But he excused himself, and protested that it was done without his knowledge, and wished the author to be summoned to law and punished according to the laws, and added that he would himself promote it. The Machiavellian standard-bearers, the Coryphaei in Denmark, will one day see how vainly they tickled themselves to please their master, in that they received such a writing from Hans Aalborg by way of congratulation, and read it in their little banquets with great applause (if not rather with greater stupidity), as relishes for food and wine, and so that they might prove by one scurrilous [writing] another — namely that the causes of my departure from Denmark, of which some of them were aware (if not authors and promoters), were not incompetently managed. But let both these and that one see to it. His own punishment follows the guilty; and, God providing and protecting, at last the good cause triumphs.

I add no more now, lest I trouble you too much. It is for you, too, to write back to me fully, and to continue and preserve our old friendship at least by letters, since hereafter it can scarcely be otherwise. Farewell. Greet diligently on my behalf all the people of Ribe whom you know to love me, and others too in your Cimbria [Jutland]; and instruct them about my affairs, if only they desire to know, that you may all give thanks with me to God, propitious and almighty, that He has so providently and kindly rescued and vindicated me out of so many difficulties and adversities which I undeservedly endured in the fatherland — to whom alone be honour and praise unto the ages. And now, my most beloved Master Andreas, with your whole house — your wife and children being greeted by me, whom I shall always strive to benefit so far as I can — again, farewell.

Given at Benátky, with hurrying pen, in the imperial castle in Bohemia, the 18th of September, the year 1599.
Your most loving Tycho Brahe, with his own hand.

There was no time to read it over again; you must judge it for yourself.

Apparatus

  • Edition of record. Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia vol. VIII (Epistolae), where the letter is headed Tychonis Brahei relatio de statu suo ("Tycho Brahe's account of his condition"). MS witness: A = Copenhagen, Det Kongelige Bibliotek, Langebeks Excerpter 179 (a later copy). The inline superscript "A" sigla scattered through the source block are Dreyer's witness-A variant markers (digitised text layer artefacts), not part of the text — see the editorial note above.
  • Date / place. Dateline "die 18. Septembr. Anno 1599," from the imperial castle Benátky in Bohemia; catalogue label confirms New Style. Within the letter Tycho dates his entry into Prague "about St. John's day, by the old reckoning" (≈ 24 June O.S.) — an explicit O.S. marker inside an N.S.-dated letter.
  • Recipient & genre. Anders Sørensen Vedel (Andreas Severini Velleius, 1542–1616), the Danish historian and former royal historiographer (and Tycho's old Ribe friend; cf. glossary). This is Tycho's autobiographical relatio de statu suo — the single most sustained first-person narrative of his departure from Denmark and his establishment under Rudolf II. Tycho explicitly asks Vedel, as a historian, to excerpt it into his annals/fasti for posterity — i.e. the letter is consciously composed as material for the historical record.
  • The Danish grievances (the core of the apologia). Tycho's own bill of particulars for the loss of Uraniborg: (1) the Norwegian benefice (the Nordfjord fief) stripped immediately after Christian IV's coronation (1596); (2) reduction to "only the Island [Hven] and 11–12 farms at Kullen in Scania," yielding just 200 joachimsdalers; (3) the Roskilde prebend — already inadequate — seized by the Chancellor for his own use the moment Tycho left; (4) systematic harassment "perhaps so that I should leave the quicker"; (5) the ban, in the King's name though he was absent, on his arranging instruments in the Copenhagen tower the city had granted him — forbidden by the Master of the Court through the very city Praefect who had earlier signed the grant. A primary source for the much-debated circumstances of the Hven catastrophe.
  • The exile itinerary. Departure "about the summer solstice, two years before this year's" (≈ June 1597) → Rostock (a quarter-year), where Duke Ulrich III of Mecklenburg (Christian IV's maternal grandfather) interceded with the King by a four-point letter, and Tycho offered renewed service in vain → Wandsbek (a Rantzau castle ½ mile from Hamburg, a full year), hosted by Henrik Rantzau ("of praised memory" — he had died ~Dec 1598/Jan 1599) → news reaches Rudolf II, who summons him (Easter 1598) → set out ~Oct 1598, but at Dresden learns of the Bohemian plague; Coraduz (Imperial Vice-Chancellor) tells him to wait → retires to Wittenberg (half a year, lodging in Melanchthon's house, Philippaeae aedes) → re-summoned end of Jan 1599 → enters Prague c. 24 June O.S. 1599 (delayed by unfinished books for the Emperor and his elder daughter's illness).
  • The imperial reception (a richly detailed record). Received alone by Rudolf II, who replied "in most kind words, and that in Latin"; presented three books to the Emperor's own hands (read by Rudolf "far into the night," per Barvitius); salary 3,000 florins fixed + c. 1,000 in perquisites (over the objection of councillors — "no Count or Baron got so much"), with 2,000 florins entry-honorarium and the stipend backdated to the first summons; a promised hereditary fief; first lodged in the Curtius house (Prague, built for >20,000 joachims) but, declining it (a widow lived there; too exposed to visitors), offered an imperial castle — shown Brandeis (3 mi.), Lissa (4 mi.), Benátky (5 mi./6 hrs) by the Captain of Brandeis, choosing Benátky; the Emperor's offer of "a new Uraniborg not inferior to the Danish one in the book I presented [the Mechanica]" and his assurance (via Barvitius) "neither is his hand shortened, nor your mouth stopped." A new observatory and a Spagyric (alchemical) laboratory already built at imperial cost.
  • Persons named. Rudolf II (the Emperor); Coraduz (Imperial Vice-Chancellor, Procancellarius); Barvitius (supreme Secretary, "who alone accomplishes almost everything"); Wolfgang Rumpf (Rumphius, Master of the Court / Obersthofmeister) and Paul Sixt Trautson (Trautsonius, court Marshal), who did not oppose the salary; the Captain of Brandeis; the widow in the Curtius house; Archbishop of Prague (Zbyněk Berka of Dubá), who disclaimed knowledge of the libel's printing; Duke Ulrich III of Mecklenburg; Henrik Rantzau (Wandsbek host); King Frederik II ("of most praiseworthy memory," d. 1588 — the Senate-decree promises date from just after his death); King Christian IV (the coronation; the "King"/grandson); the Danish Chancellor (unnamed; took the Roskilde prebend); son Tycho and servant Mule, dispatched to Denmark; Henrik Ramel (Danish Senator, to expedite the shipping); the Elector of Brandenburg (Joachim Friedrich) and his mathematician Müller, lent to Tycho; Master Christiern (a conduit for poems, unidentified). The Historian who succeeded Vedel in the royal office is Niels Krag (royal historiographer from 1594/95) — the source of the barbed "Epithalamium of Hven but not an Epitaph" remark (i.e. willing to chronicle Hven's founding but not its death).
  • The Ursus thread. "That Ditmarsh Brutus / the impure Beast" is Nicolaus Reimers Bär (Ursus); Tycho blames Erik Lange (Sophie Brahe's alchemist betrothed) for having brought Ursus onto Hven, occasioning the feud. Ursus was made to testify under a Magdeburg notary against his own libel; Tycho holds notarised Latin/German instruments to convict him at Prague via a hired Procurator. Ursus fled Prague, deserting his "infamous and adulterous" new wife. The libel had been relished by the "Machiavellian Coryphaei in Denmark," who got it from Hans Aalborg (Johannes Aalburgensis, glossary). Continuous with the Ursus material in brahe-t_15991209001 (to Kepler).
  • Latin & Danish tags / allusions. "nec sibi esse accurtatam manum, nec mihi obturatum os" (the Emperor's assurance) — echo of Isaiah 59:1 ("the Lord's hand is not shortened"). "dignum patella operculum" — "a lid worthy of the pot," a proverbial fit of two bad things (Hieronymus; Erasmus, Adagia). "Sequitur sua poena nocentem" — "his own punishment follows the guilty" (proverbial; cf. Claudian). "tandem bona causa triumphat" — "at last the good cause triumphs," a recurring Tychonic motto (also in brahe-t_15980810001). Philippaeae aedes — Melanchthon's house at Wittenberg. Gynaeceum — the women's quarters/his womenfolk. Spagyrica — alchemical (Paracelsian) operations. Two Danish proverbs are kept and translated: "Der giør tit en den anden got, alligevell hand mener det ont…" ("One often does another good, although he means it ill; and the false man strikes his own lord upon the neck") and "Det taerer intet" ("It costs nothing / does no harm").
  • Correction. Restored Tycho's authorial postscript "Non vacabat relegere. Ipsemet dispicias." ("There was no time to read it over again; you must judge it for yourself"), printed by Dreyer immediately after the signature, TBOO VIII p.181 - previously silently dropped from both Latin and English. Also corrected 'militaribus' to 'milliaribus' (miles) in the Brandeis-distance sentence per Dreyer, TBOO VIII p.178; the English already read 'miles' and required no change.
