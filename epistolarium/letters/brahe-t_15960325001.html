The original

ERUDITISSIMO ET HUMANISSIMO IUVENI IOHANNI ISACIO PONTANO NUNC CANTABRIGIAe IN ANGLIA LITERIS POLITIORIBUS INCUMBENTI SIBI PLURIMUM DILECTO TYCHO BRAHE.
S. P.

EX literis tuis Cantabrigiae 12. Novemb. anni praecedentis ad me datis, dilecte mi IOANNES ISACIDE, cognovi te nunc in Anglia degere et literis humanioribus criticisque operam navare. Non displicet mihi hoc studium, siquidem eo, a Patre tuo olim sic educatus, inclinare videris. Exhibebit Anglia multos in his commilitones, cum fertilis sit liberalium ingeniorum et eloquentiae inprimis deditorum. Admones de Anglico privilegio, et in eo procurando tuam offers voluntatem; idque oportune admones, gratumque est quod offers. Quaero itaque omnem moveas lapidem, ut illud mature obtineam: si ante festum Pentecostes vel paulo post id fieri queat, votis meis apprime responderes. Efficies id per aliquem primariae auctoritatis in Serenissimae Reginae ELISABETHAe Cancellaria, quae cum sit inter Heroinas nostri seculi non solum genere Regio splendidissima, sed etiam varia doctrina, quod alias in hoc sexu perrarum est, decoratissima, nihil addubito, quin doctrinis et studijs liberalibus, praesertim Astronomicis, quae, ut ait noster Manilius,

Regales animos primum dignata movere,
Proxima tangentes coelo fastigia rerum,
clementissime et impense faveat, ea promovere inter alias Regales curas non dedignetur. Quo vero generalis aliqua forma constet scribendi eius diplomatis, mitto exemplum Caesareani, tum quoque Regis Scotorum. Ex quibus perspicax conscriptor facile modum reperiet, quo reliquum illud consignandum sit. Neque tamen opus est praeeuntes omnino sequi, sed fertile Anglorum ingenium facile rationem inveniet, qua singula terse et magnifice, ut artis regiae promotioni accommoda et honorifica sint, concipiantur. Pollicitus est olim optimae memoriae DANIEL ROGERSIUS, amicus meus desideratissimus (in quo eruditio et humanitas pariter certabant) suum in tali privilegio procurando conscribendoque studium, quando is legatione nomine Serenissimae Reginae hîc in Dania functus me semel atque iterum invisere non est degravatus. Sed cum fata nimis immatura hunc tantum nobis praeripuerint, haud dubito, quin in Cancellaria Latina Reginae successor aliquis eius doctrinae et virtuti non impar substitutus sit, cuius officio et beneficio id quod petimus privilegium non invite, uti spero, obtinebimus. Honorarium vicissim meo nomine gratitudinis ergo exhibendum offer et expone. Ego id, quicquid erit, quam primum de eo certior redditus fuero, patri tuo Amstelrodamum, ut ille tibi, restitui curabo. Vide itaque, mi ISACIDE, nequa mora nostris desiderijs aut ulla alia impedimenta obveniant. Habebis me ad tibi vicissim gratificandum non difficilem, et quid expectandum sit, mature significa. Ad quaestionem illam iocosam et nonnihil criticam antiqui LUCRETIJ, cum is sectam Philosophorum Deos eorumque providentiam inficiantem profiteretur, Venerem nihilominus, Aeneadum genitricem, primordio sui operis, eiusque opem imploret, non habeo serio dicere, quomodo haec resolvenda sit, siquidem non ad Veneris sidus coeleste, quod nos unâ cum ceteris subinde scrutamur, sed ad terrestrem illam Venerem, Aeneadum, uti fingebant Poëtae, matrem, et aliorum quoque hominum genitricem pertineat; cuius naturam et genealogiam Anglis ita perspectam esse existimo, tum quoque tuis Batavis, ut non opus sit Danos de his percontari. Meministi me solere dicere Anglos participare ex influentia potissimum ♀ ♃ ☿, unde alacres, mundi et iucundi sunt, tum quoque ingeniosi pariter et foecundi. Est igitur haec quaestio, licet non Angelica, Anglicana tamen et faceta. Quamobrem ab ijs potius quam a nobis eius enodationem edisces. Siquid tamen in his nostri valent lusus, crediderim LUCRETIUM ad imitationem aliorum Poëtarum sic exorsum esse, non quod revera aliquam Deam, quae Venus appellaretur, aut ulla alia numina statueret. Ideoque sub hoc nomine voluptatem corpoream, quam etiam Deam subinde nuncupare non veretur, intellexisse arbitror. Verum tute cum alijs sodalibus haec plenius dispicito. Ante omnia vero voluptatem animi vere Deam omnibus corporis illecebris anteponito. Sic te verae Veneres, Charites et Musae nunquam intermoriturae perpetuo redamabunt et sertis semper virentibus coronabunt. Vale et nostros in Anglia amicos, licet de facie non omnes notos, quos non paucos esse mihi persuadeo, diligenter ex me saluta, inprimis optimum illum et reverendum senem, virum excellentissimum THOMAM DIGGÆSEUM, si isthic locorum is degit. Obtinebunt hi et alij brevi nonnulla ex nostris Operibus, in quibus hos quoque permixtos Ducibus noscetur Olympi. Iamque iterum et multoties vale, mi IOANNES, et me saepius tuis invise, hisque actutum rescribe.

Raptim Uraniburgi die 25. Martij Anni 1596.
TYCHO BRAHE,
manu propria.

Binas eodem hoc tempore ad te per diversos homines misi
literas, uti si alteræ interciderent, unæ saltem ex his tibi traderentur.
Quin et hasce ternas, ut res certior esset, addidi.

Tycho Brahe to the most learned and most courteous young man Johannes Isacius Pontanus, now at Cambridge in England devoting himself to the more polished letters, most dearly beloved of him. Heartiest greeting.

From your letter written to me at Cambridge on 12 November of last year, my beloved Johannes son of Isaac [Isacides], I learned that you are now living in England and applying yourself to the more humane and critical letters. This study does not displease me, seeing that you appear to incline toward it, having long ago been so educated by your father. England will furnish you many fellow-soldiers in these pursuits, since it is fertile in liberal talents and in men especially devoted to eloquence. You advise me about the English privilege, and offer your goodwill in procuring it; and you advise this opportunely, and what you offer is welcome. I ask, therefore, that you move every stone, so that I may obtain it in good time: if it can be done before the feast of Pentecost or a little after, you would answer my wishes exceedingly well. You will accomplish it through someone of leading authority in the Chancery of the Most Serene Queen Elizabeth; and since she, among the heroines of our age, is not only most splendid by royal birth but also most adorned with manifold learning — which is otherwise very rare in this sex — I do not at all doubt that she most graciously and lavishly favours the learned disciplines and the liberal studies, especially the astronomical, which, as our Manilius says,

First deigned to stir the minds of kings,
Who touch the topmost heights of things nearest to heaven,
and that she does not disdain, among her other royal cares, to promote them. But that some general form may be at hand for the drafting of that diploma, I send a specimen of the Imperial one [Caesareani], and also that of the King of the Scots. From these a perceptive drafter will easily find the manner in which the rest of it is to be drawn up. Yet there is no need to follow the precedents altogether; rather the fertile English wit will easily find a way in which each particular may be conceived neatly and magnificently, so that they are suited and honourable to the advancement of the royal art [the art of astronomy]. There once promised — of best memory — Daniel Rogers, my most longed-for friend (in whom erudition and humanity strove on equal terms), his zeal in procuring and drafting such a privilege, when, discharging an embassy here in Denmark in the name of the Most Serene Queen, he did not think it beneath him to visit me once and again. But since fates all too untimely have snatched this great man from us, I do not doubt that in the Queen's Latin Chancery some successor not unequal to his learning and virtue has been put in his place, by whose office and good service we shall obtain — not unwillingly, as I hope — the privilege we seek. Offer and set forth in turn, in my name, an honorarium to be presented by way of gratitude. Whatever it shall be, as soon as I am made more certain of it, I shall see it repaid to your father at Amsterdam, so that he [may repay it] to you. See to it therefore, my son of Isaac, that no delay nor any other impediments befall our desires. You will find me not difficult in gratifying you in turn, and signify in good time what is to be expected. As to that jocular and somewhat critical question concerning the ancient Lucretius — that, while he professed the sect of philosophers who deny the gods and their providence, he nonetheless, at the very beginning of his work, invokes Venus, the mother of the descendants of Aeneas, and implores her aid — I cannot say in earnest how this is to be resolved, seeing that it pertains not to the celestial star of Venus, which we together with the rest now and again scrutinise, but to that earthly Venus, mother of the Aeneadae, as the poets feigned, and begetter of other men too; whose nature and genealogy I judge to be so well understood by the English, and also by your Batavians [the Dutch], that there is no need to question Danes about these matters. You remember that I am wont to say that the English partake especially of the influence of ♀ ♃ ☿ [Venus, Jupiter, Mercury], whence they are lively, elegant, and pleasant, and likewise ingenious and fertile alike. This question, then, though not Angelic, is yet Anglican and witty. Wherefore you will learn its unravelling from them rather than from us. Yet if our jests have any weight in these matters, I should believe that Lucretius began thus in imitation of other poets, not because he really posited some goddess who should be called Venus, or any other divine powers. And so under this name I judge that he understood bodily pleasure, which too he does not fear now and again to call a goddess. But do you, with your other companions, look into these matters more fully. Above all, however, set the pleasure of the mind, truly a goddess, before all the enticements of the body. So shall the true Venuses, Graces, and Muses, never to die, love you back perpetually and crown you with ever-green garlands. Farewell, and diligently greet on my behalf our friends in England, though not all known to me by face, of whom I am persuaded there are not a few — above all that excellent and reverend old man, the most excellent man Thomas Digges, if he is living in those parts. These and others will shortly obtain some of our Works, in which they too will be known to be mingled with the Leaders of Olympus. And now again and many times farewell, my Johannes, and visit me more often with your letters, and write back to this one straightway.

In haste at Uraniborg, the 25th day of March in the year 1596.
TYCHO BRAHE,
in his own hand.

At this same time I have sent you two [sets of] letters through different men, so that, if one set should be lost, at least one of these might be delivered to you. Indeed, I have even added this third, that the matter might be more certain.

Apparatus

  • Sender. Tycho Brahe, writing from Uraniborg on Hven. manu propria = "in his own hand," a subscription formula.
  • Recipient. Iohannes Isacius Pontanus = Johannes Isaacius Pontanus (Jan Isaacsz Pontanus, 1571–1639), born at Elsinore of a Haarlem patrician family that had fled to Denmark for its Protestant faith; Tycho's famulus (assistant) on Hven 1593–95, later a noted historian in Denmark and the Netherlands. Tycho addresses him with the playful Greek-style patronymic Isacides ("son of Isaac"), since his father was Isaac Pietersz, then at Amsterdam (patri tuo Amstelrodamum, "to your father at Amsterdam"). At the date of the letter Pontanus was studying at Cambridge (Cantabrigiae).
  • Salutation. S. P. = salutem plurimam (dicit), "(sends) heartiest greeting". literis politioribus / humanioribus criticisque — "the more polished / the more humane and critical letters," i.e. classical philology and textual criticism (the studia humaniora).
  • Anglicum privilegium — "the English privilege": a royal printing-privilege / copyright protection Tycho sought from Elizabeth I for his published Works (his Opera), to be drafted in the Queen's Latin Chancery. He encloses model documents — exemplum Caesareani ("a specimen of the Imperial [privilege]," i.e. from the Holy Roman Emperor) and Regis Scotorum ("that of the King of the Scots," James VI of Scotland, later James I).
  • omnem moveas lapidem — "move every stone," the classical idiom (Greek πάντα κινῆσαι λίθον); rendered literally to preserve the figure.
  • festum Pentecostes — the feast of Pentecost (Whitsun); a movable liturgical date used as a calendar marker. Not converted.
  • Queen Elizabeth. Serenissima Regina Elisabetha = Elizabeth I of England (r. 1558–1603). Tycho's praise of her learning "rare in this sex" (quod alias in hoc sexu perrarum est) is preserved as written, hedge intact.
  • Manilius quotation. ut ait noster Manilius — Marcus Manilius, Astronomica I.41–42 (the didactic Latin astronomical poem). The two verses are kept as verse by this chapter's convention: Regales animos primum dignata movere, / Proxima tangentes coelo fastigia rerum — astronomy "first deigned to stir the minds of kings, who touch the topmost heights of things nearest to heaven." noster Manilius = "our Manilius," claiming the poet as a kindred astronomer.
  • artis regiae — here "the royal art," i.e. astronomy (the ars regia of stirring kings' minds in the Manilius lines), not alchemy; rendered "the royal art [the art of astronomy]."
  • Daniel Rogers. Daniel Rogersius = Daniel Rogers (c. 1538–1591), the Anglo-Dutch humanist and Elizabethan diplomat who had visited Tycho during embassies to Denmark; here remembered as dead (optimae memoriae … fata nimis immatura hunc … praeripuerint). Cancellaria Latina Reginae = the Queen's Latin Secretariat / Chancery (the office handling the crown's Latin diplomatic correspondence), in which Rogers had served.
  • honorarium — "an honorarium," a fee/gratuity to be advanced by Pontanus and repaid by Tycho via Pontanus's father at Amsterdam.
  • Lucretius question. antiqui Lucretij = Lucretius (De rerum natura), the Epicurean poet who "denies the gods and their providence" (Deos eorumque providentiam inficiantem) yet opens his poem with an invocation of Venus (Aeneadum genitrix, "mother of the descendants of Aeneas," I.1). Tycho distinguishes the Veneris sidus coeleste — "the celestial star of Venus" (the planet, which astronomers scrutinise) — from the terrestris Venus — "that earthly Venus," the mythological/sensual goddess. He resolves the puzzle by reading Lucretius's Venus as a poetic personification of voluptas corporea ("bodily pleasure"). Hedges preserved: non habeo serio dicere ("I cannot say in earnest"), crediderim ("I should believe"), arbitror ("I judge").
  • Veneris sidus — rendered "the star of Venus" (the planet); by this chapter's convention sidus/stella of a planet is the planet itself.
  • Astral-influence remark. Anglos participare ex influentia potissimum ♀ ♃ ☿ — "the English partake especially of the influence of Venus, Jupiter, Mercury." The planet glyphs ♀ ♃ ☿ are kept in the Source block and named in English by this chapter's convention; whence Tycho's characterology of the English as alacres, mundi, iucundi … ingeniosi … foecundi.
  • non Angelica … Anglicana — a pun on Angelicus ("Angelic") / Anglicanus ("English/Anglican"), playing on the old Angli/Angeli topos (Gregory the Great's "non Angli sed Angeli"). The wordplay is kept rather than flattened: "though not Angelic, yet Anglican."
  • verae Veneres, Charites et Musae — "the true Venuses, Graces [Charites], and Muses": the Charites are the three Graces (Greek term kept). sertis semper virentibus = "with ever-green garlands."
  • Thomas Digges. Thomam Diggaeseum = Thomas Digges (c. 1546–1595), the English mathematician and Copernican; Tycho's hedge si isthic locorum is degit ("if he is living in those parts") is preserved — Tycho did not know Digges had in fact died in August 1595, which the hedge inadvertently flags.
  • Ducibus … Olympi — "the Leaders of Olympus," a flourish for the eminent men to whom Tycho will send his Works; the phrase echoes Manilius/poetic diction. Rendered literally.
  • Dateline. Raptim Uraniburgi die 25. Martij Anni 1596 = "In haste at Uraniborg, the 25th day of March, the year 1596" (O.S.; Denmark kept the Julian calendar). The edition label ("1596. 25. marts. Uraniborg") agrees with the dateline; no discrepancy.
  • Edition note. The edition's edition field reads "Contra vitam monasticam ad Bern. Mattium epistola" — this appears to be a mis-carried edition tag (that title is a separate work and does not match this letter); the letter is among Tycho's Epistolae (Dreyer, Opera Omnia). Flagged, not relied upon.
  • What this is. A short paragraph immediately preceding the letter's warm close (greetings to English friends, Thomas Digges, etc., in the published tail) — Tycho explaining his own redundant-delivery practice: this is, by his own account, the third copy of essentially the same letter, sent by three different couriers to guard against loss in transit.
  • The recipient. Johannes Isaacius Pontanus (1571–1639), the young Danish-Norwegian humanist scholar and mathematician who had spent time in Tycho's circle; he later became a historian (author of a major history of Denmark) and remained a lifelong correspondent, addressed here with evident personal warmth ("mi Ioannes" in the published tail).
  • Significance for the corpus of letters. This is direct authorial testimony to a documented hazard of early modern scholarly correspondence — letters routinely lost in transit — and to Tycho's countermeasure of triplicate dispatch by separate carriers, a practice also implicit elsewhere in the edition (e.g. Fabricius's and Scaliger's concern over safe and swift delivery routes).
  • Correction. Source text corrected against Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Opera Omnia VII, page 377: "nostri secuti" corrected to "nostri seculi" (genitive of saeculum, "of our age"). The English translation already read correctly and required no change.
