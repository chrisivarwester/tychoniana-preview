The original

ILLVSTRISSIMO PRINCIPI AC DOMINO, DOMINO WILHELMO, HASSIÆ LANDGRAVIO, COMITI IN CATZENELNBOGEN, DIETZ, ZIEGENHAIN ET NIDDA: PRINCIPI AC DOMINO SUO CLEMENTISSIMO S. P.

QVÆ ad T. Celsit. hoc tempore perscriberem, Princeps Illustrissime, etsi non multa occurrerent, simulque vererer, ne meis importunis literis T. Celsit. Reipub. negotijs occupatiorem molestarem (nam et semel atque iterum iamdudum literas, quibus nihil responsum est, transmisi, vnde T. Celsit. alijs curis auocari, neque his vacare collegi.) Attamen, quoniam nouum quoddam Phœnomenon sese proxime antecedentibus diebus, cœlitus conspiciendum exhibuit: Cometes videlicet aspectu non adeo magnus, sed pallido et subobscuro vultu lucens: Sciens T. Celsit. huius apparentias cognoscendi cupidam esse (ex quo in vltimo, ante quinquennium viso, simile quid a me expetitum sit) non supersedendum duxi, quin aliquid literarum, vel hoc saltem nomine ad T. Celsit. mitterem, veluti paulo ante T. Celsit. Mathematico scribens, quando nonnullas in eodem Cometa obseruationes continuassem, me facturum spopondi. Circa illudipsum enim tempus Cometa apparere cœpit, et expectandam erat, donec ratas aliquot in eo animaduersiones nancisci licuit. Ne igitur res diutius differatur, quæ hactenus sedulâ denotatione explorata habeo, T. Celsit. communicanda duxi, vt cum sui Mathematici in eodem Cometa obseruatis (nec enim dubito eundem etiam a vobis animaduersum esse) conferre queat. Occasio autem qua illum primum conspeximus, erat huiusmodi. Die 23. Februarij, cum Veneris Stella terris descendendo appropinquans, circa maximam a Sole remotionem versaretur, nactus post quatriduanas aëris obscuritates, exoptatam aliquam serenitatem, illius obseruationi sedulo attendendum censui. Siquidem ex dato ipsius loco, in remotissima, respectu Solis, tam Vespertina, quam Eoa dispositione, ad Apogæi atque Eccentricitatis cognitionem perueniri posse, prout quoque a Ptolemæo et Copernico factitatum est, T. Celsit. non ignorat. Non paucas igitur eiuscemodi a Sole digressiones maximas, matutinas atque vespertinas, iam antea aliquoties exploraui, quibus et hanc adiungere satagebam; vixque bis vel ter Venus iam Instrumentis denotata erat, vbi ex improuiso Stellam quandam subobscuram, inter Andromedæ atque Arietis Asterismos, iuxta Piscem Borealem sese præter solitum ostendentem, cum nullam eiuscemodi illic esse oportere satis perspectum haberem, animaduerteremus, quæ etiam subinde radiolos quosdam ad verticem exerere cernebatur. Neque enim Crepusculum vespertinum iam satis præterierat, vt Stellæ minus conspicuæ euidenter discernerentur. Cœpi itaque statim cum meis Studiosis hoc insolitum Phœnomenon per Instrumenta demetiri, et vix elapso horæ quadrante, deprehendi ex motu eius, quem vel tantillo tempore sensibiliter alterabat, tam Declinatione, quam Distantia a Fixis nonnihil variata, non genuinum, sed ascititium quoddam e Cometarum numero Sidus existere, vtut cauda illa tenuior, quam post Crepusculi finem promptius monstrabat, tunc vix oculis perciperetur. Mitto autem T. Celsit. seorsim in adiecta charta breuem quandam eiusdem Cometæ descriptionem, e meis obseruationibus, quas adeptus sum, ex tempore et festinanter concinnatam, quam cum vestris aduersionibus conferre licebit. Rogoque, vt T. Celsit. hanc nostram voluntatem boni consulat. Veneris Stella toto Februario et Martio interdiu, etiam in ipso Meridie apparuit, quod in posterum quoque pro maiori Aprilis parte futurum iudico. Si itaque T. Celsit. Mathematicus illi obseruandæ, tam interdiu, quam noctu operam sedulam nauauit, admodum oportunam, de quinis illis Minutis, quibus in Longitudinibus Stellarum differimus, decernendi arripuit ansam. Ex quo tam Venus quam Sol adeo sublimes obseruationi patuerint, vt Refractionum impedimentis vix implicarentur. Nec dubito, quin vel inde, adhibita solerti circumspectione colligere detur, me Longitudinem Fixarum nequaquam iusto plus anticipâsse. Constitueram T. Celsit. de his omnibus prius scribere, per Mercatores nostrates Francofurdum euntes, sed accidit, vt circa eosdem dies, quibus illi itineri se committebant, Serenissimus Scotorum Rex, cum suis Consiliarijs reliquoque Nobilium Comitatu, me in hac Insula inuisere dignatus sit, et exinde quasi quotidianos hospites habuerim. Hincque factum est, quod tardius atque constitueram, hasce mittere cogar. Quæ tamen mora non sine fænore accidit, nam eo plus temporis concedebatur, per interualla, ea, quæ in dicto Cometa prius subito exararam, plenius, et enucleatius recognoscendi, vt non dubitem, hæc ipsa, quæ mitto, vtut paucula et breuia, omnimode recte sibi constare, atque apparentijs istius Cometæ apprime esse analoga. Sed cum non ignorem T. Celsit. pro sua clementi et candida erga nos affectione, hanc dilatiunculam equi bonique accipere, nolo in eâ excusandâ plus occupari, quam forte opus sit, sed me meaque omnia T. Celsit. commendata cupio.

Vale.
Vraniburgo, Calendis Aprilis, Anno 1590.
T. Celsit.
Addictissimus
TYCHO BRAHE.

English translation machine translation, Probable

To the Most Illustrious Prince and Lord, Lord Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, Count in Catzenelnbogen, Dietz, Ziegenhain and Nidda: his most gracious Prince and Lord — heartiest greeting.

As to what I might write at length to Your Highness at this time, Most Illustrious Prince — although not much offered itself, and at the same time I feared that with my importunate letters I might trouble Your Highness, who are rather much occupied with the business of the commonwealth (for once and again, some while ago now, I sent letters to which no reply has come, from which I gathered that Your Highness is called away by other cares and has no leisure for these things) — nevertheless, since a certain new phenomenon has shown itself in these last few days to be seen in the heavens, namely a comet not so very large to the sight, but shining with a pale and somewhat dim face, and knowing that Your Highness is desirous to learn of its appearances (from the fact that with the last one, seen five years ago, something similar was sought of me), I judged that I ought not to forbear from sending Your Highness at least some letter, if only on this account — just as, writing a little while ago to Your Highness's Mathematician, I gave my pledge that I would do so, once I had carried on some observations on this same comet. For it was about that very time that the comet began to appear, and it had to be waited out until it was possible to obtain a few settled observations upon it. So then, lest the matter be put off any longer, I judged that what I have thus far explored by careful recording was to be communicated to Your Highness, that you may be able to compare it with the observations made by your Mathematician on the same comet (for I do not doubt that it was noticed by you also). Now the occasion on which we first caught sight of it was of this kind. On the 23rd of February, when the star Venus, drawing near the earth as it descended, was about its greatest distance from the Sun, having obtained — after a four-day obscurity of the air — some longed-for clear weather, I judged that careful attention should be given to observing it. For from its given place, when at its greatest distance with respect to the Sun, in both an evening and a morning disposition, one can arrive at the knowledge of the apogee and of the eccentricity, just as was done by Ptolemy and Copernicus too, as Your Highness is not unaware. Not a few greatest elongations of this kind from the Sun, both morning and evening, I had therefore already explored several times before, and I was busy to add this one too to them; and Venus had hardly yet been recorded by the instruments two or three times when, unexpectedly, we noticed a certain somewhat dim star showing itself contrary to custom between the asterisms of Andromeda and Aries, near the Northern Fish — since I held it well enough ascertained that no such star ought to be there — which was also seen from time to time to put forth certain little rays toward the zenith. For the evening twilight had not yet sufficiently passed for the less conspicuous stars to be clearly distinguished. I therefore began at once, with my students, to measure out this unusual phenomenon by the instruments, and scarcely had a quarter of an hour elapsed when I perceived, from its motion — which it appreciably altered even in so brief a time, with both its declination and its distance from the fixed stars somewhat varied — that it was no genuine star, but a certain adventitious one, of the number of comets, even though that thinner tail, which it showed more readily after the end of twilight, was then scarcely perceived by the eyes. But I send to Your Highness separately, on an enclosed sheet, a certain brief description of this same comet, drawn up offhand and in haste from the observations I have obtained, which it will be possible to compare with your own notes. And I ask that Your Highness take this our goodwill in good part. The star Venus appeared all through February and March in the daytime, even at midday itself, which I judge will be so in time to come too, for the greater part of April. If, therefore, Your Highness's Mathematician has devoted careful labour to observing it, both by day and by night, he has seized a most opportune handle for deciding about those five minutes by which we differ in the longitudes of the stars. Since both Venus and the Sun lay open to observation so high up that they would scarcely be entangled in the impediments of refractions. And I do not doubt that even from this, with diligent circumspection applied, it may be possible to gather that I have by no means anticipated the longitude of the fixed stars more than is right. I had resolved to write to Your Highness about all these things sooner, by way of our merchants going to Frankfurt; but it happened that, about the same days on which they were committing themselves to that journey, the Most Serene King of the Scots, with his Councillors and the rest of his retinue of nobles, deigned to visit me on this island, and that thereafter I had, as it were, daily guests. And hence it came about that I am compelled to send this present letter later than I had resolved. Yet that delay did not occur without interest [returned], for so much the more time was granted, in the intervals, for recognizing more fully and more thoroughly the things which I had earlier scratched down hurriedly on the said comet — so that I do not doubt that these very things which I send, though few and brief, are altogether rightly consistent in themselves, and exceedingly well matched to the appearances of that comet. But since I am not unaware that Your Highness, in keeping with your gracious and candid affection toward us, takes this little delay as fair and good, I do not wish to occupy myself in excusing it more than perhaps need be, but I desire myself and all my affairs to be commended to Your Highness.

Farewell.
From Uraniborg, on the Kalends of April [1 April], the year 1590.
Your Highness's
Most devoted,
TYCHO BRAHE.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Wilhelmus, Hassiae Landgravius = Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel (1532–1592), the "astronomer prince," with whose Kassel observatory Tycho maintained his most important scientific correspondence. The titulature Comiti in Catzenelnbogen, Dietz, Ziegenhain et Nidda names the subsidiary Hessian comital domains (Katzenelnbogen, Diez, Ziegenhain, Nidda); the place-names are left in their as-printed forms.
  • Sender / place. Tycho Brahe, writing from Uraniborg (Vraniburgum) on the island of Hven.
  • Salutation. S. P. = salutem plurimam (dicit), "(sends) heartiest greeting". T. Celsit. = Tua Celsitudo, Your Highness; rendered consistently throughout. The subscription Addictissimus = "Most devoted."
  • The phenomenon. nouum quoddam Phoenomenon … Cometes — a comet, that of late February 1590, first detected by Tycho on the night of the Venus observation. non genuinum, sed ascititium … Sidus … e Cometarum numero — "no genuine star, but a certain adventitious one, of the number of comets": the diagnosis turns on the body's measurable proper motion in a quarter-hour. radiolos … ad verticem exerere = "to put forth little rays toward the zenith"; cauda … tenuior = "a thinner tail."
  • in vltimo, ante quinquennium viso — "the last one, seen five years ago": the comet of 1585, on which the Landgrave had earlier sought Tycho's observations. ("Five years" is loose for late 1585 → early 1590.)
  • T. Celsit. Mathematico — Your Highness's Mathematician: Christoph Rothmann, the Kassel mathematician and Tycho's chief observational interlocutor in this period.
  • Venus / greatest elongation. circa maximam a Sole remotionem = "about its greatest distance from the Sun" = greatest elongation; in remotissima … tam Vespertina, quam Eoa dispositione = "at its greatest distance … in both an evening and a morning disposition" (Eous = of the dawn, the morning star). Tycho states the standard method — that from Venus's place at greatest elongation one derives the apogee (Apogaeum) and eccentricity (Eccentricitas) of its orbit, "as was done by Ptolemy and Copernicus too." digressiones maximas = "greatest elongations" (here digression in the technical sense). The daytime visibility of Venus near midday is noted as an observational opportunity.
  • Star positions. inter Andromedae atque Arietis Asterismos, iuxta Piscem Borealem = "between the asterisms of Andromeda and Aries, near the Northern Fish" (Piscis Borealis, the northern fish of Pisces). Asterismus = "asterism" (constellation-figure).
  • Technical terms. Instrumenta → instruments; Declinatio → declination; Distantia a Fixis → "distance from the fixed stars"; Fixae → the fixed stars; Refractiones → refractions; Crepusculum (vespertinum) → (evening) twilight; Meridies → "midday / meridian"; zenith (verticem) kept/named by this chapter's convention Studiosi rendered "students" (Tycho's resident assistants at Uraniborg).
  • The five-minute discrepancy. de quinis illis Minutis, quibus in Longitudinibus Stellarum differimus = "those five minutes by which we differ in the longitudes of the stars": the standing 5-arcminute disagreement between Tycho's and Kassel's stellar longitudes. Tycho argues that the high-altitude daytime observations of Venus and the Sun — being "scarcely entangled in the impediments of refractions" — afford a test that, he expects, will confirm "that I have by no means anticipated [overstated] the longitude of the fixed stars more than is right." His epistemic hedge (Nec dubito, quin … colligere detur "I do not doubt that … it may be possible to gather") is preserved, not sharpened into a claim of proof.
  • The royal visit. Serenissimus Scotorum Rex … me in hac Insula inuisere dignatus sit = "the Most Serene King of the Scots … deigned to visit me on this island": James VI of Scotland (later James I of England), who visited Hven on 20 March 1590 while in Denmark for his marriage to Anne of Denmark — the cause of the letter's delay. per Mercatores nostrates Francofurdum euntes = "by our merchants going to Frankfurt" (the Frankfurt fair as the postal channel to Hesse).
  • non sine faenore — literally "not without interest" (financial metaphor): the delay paid a dividend, in extra time to perfect the comet observations. Rendered with the metaphor kept ("without interest [returned]").
  • adiecta charta — "an enclosed sheet": the separate comet-observation summary accompanying the letter, now lost from / not part of this text.
  • Calendar / dateline. Vraniburgo, Calendis Aprilis, Anno 1590 = "From Uraniborg, on the Kalends of April (1 April), the year 1590" (Old Style; Denmark kept the Julian calendar). The Roman-calendar idiom Calendis Aprilis is the 1st of April; this agrees with the catalogue label (1590. 1. april. Uraniborg), so no date discrepancy arises. The Roman idiom is glossed by this chapter's convention
