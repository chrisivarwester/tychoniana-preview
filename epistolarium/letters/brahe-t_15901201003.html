CLARISSIMO ET EXCELLENTISSIMO VIRO D. IOANNI ANTONIO MAGINO PATAVINO, IN CELEBRI GYMNASIO BONONIENSI MATHEMATUM PROFESSORI EXIMIO, DEQUE RERUM ASTRONOMICARUM LOCUPLETATIONE PRÆCLARE MERITO: AMICO SUO PLURIMUM COLENDO.

QUOD Librum nostrum secundum de recentioribus Mundi Ætherei Phænomenis per GELLIUM SASCERIDEM meum iam dudum obtinueris, vir clarissime, et de re Astronomica, prout hactenus a nostri sæculi artificibus constituta est, excolenda locupletandaque optime merite, ex litteris tuis Bononia Idibus Septembris anni recogniti ad me datis, quas ego 8 Calendarum Decembris anni veteris Iuliani hîc accepi, lubenter cognovi; licet idipsum antea quoque dictus GELLIUS per litteras retulerit, quodque scriptionem tamdiu procrastinâris, te iam satis excusatum habeo. Fuisse autem id, quicquid erat libri, tibi gratum, nec displicuisse, mihi quoque admodum acceptum est. Et quod humanissimis tuis litteris me nunc tandem compellare, singularemque tuam erga me meaque studia benevolentiam atque candorem non obscure in ijs declarare, mecumque de quibusdam ad negotium Astronomicum rectius et solidius, quam antehac præstitum est, instaurandum stabiliendumque conferre, meumque de his iudicium atque experimenta requirere sategeris, mihi nequaquam ingratum est, sed multis nominibus pervolupe. Est igitur, quo tibi pro hoc sincero et officioso erga me animo gratias habeam plurimas. Experiere me vicissim erga te tuosque conatus benignum et tui studiosissimum, amicitiæque semel per litteras contractæ conservatorem promptum et constantem, ne te certasse priorem pœniteat. Etsi vero de ijs, quæ hisce tuis desideras, ante pauculas septimanas præmonito GELLIO, qui eadem ferme tuo nomine a me expetierat, descriptumque tuarum litterarum adiunxerat, quo pleraque nunc repetita comprehendebantur, affatim responderim, unâque ut illa ad te referri curaret, iniunxerim, attamen et nunc quoque his ipsis, quæ propriæ tuæ continent atque a me expetunt exspectantque, responsionem adornare non detrectabo. Elenchum plurimarum inerrantium Stellarum præteritis aliquot annis maxima assiduitate et diligentia a nobis infallibilibus organis atque demetiendi rationibus verificatarum, equidem nunc libenter et liberaliter tecum communicarem, nec tibi tantum thesaurum, utut ingenti pariterque incredibili labore et sumptu plurimisque vigilatis noctibus comparatum, denegarem aut inviderem, quo tuas eximias lucubrationes his ornare locupletareque posses; cederet enim id in Astronomiæ, quam, quocunque modo liceat, promovere studeo, emolumentum; nec etiam mihi obesset, cum te mea mihi, non tibi, ascripturum, nec pro alienis editurum, ex mentis et iudicij tui integritate satis persuasum habeam. Verumenimvero cum nimia sit inter nos locorum intercapedo, multique viarum terra marique anfractus, per varias etiam diversorum dominorum ditiones transeundum, litteræque meæ, antequam ad te pervenerint, plurimorum manus subituræ sint: vereor sane ne alicubi intercidentes, aut etiam reseratæ, alijs, quam vellem, meorum librorum copiam præberent, qui postea eos vel pro suis edituri, vel alijs me invito concessuri essent. Auget hanc suspicionem hybernum tempus, cum iam instet bruma, nec antea tuis circa Æquinoctium autumnale exaratis, cum iam primum eas sim adeptus, respondere licuerit. Scis autem ipsemet difficiliores esse hyberno tempore hinc in Italiam peregrinationes minusque tutas, ut satis certus esse nequeam, an et hæ ad tuas manus sartæ tectæ perventuræ sint. Spero itaque, quod ponderatis hisce rationibus, me ob hoc tempore non transmissa Fixarum restituta loca, pro tuo candore atque æquanimitate sis libenter excusaturus. Ne tamen spe tanta, et erga me eximia confidentia prorsus decidas, utque votis tuis aliquando nec nimis diutina dilatione satisfiat: scias me Tomum primum Cœli recentiorum Phaenomenωn, qui de Stella Nova et alijs quibusdam huc pertinentibus atque penum Astronomicum locupletantibus luculenter agit, prœlis propriæ meæ Typographiæ nunc urgere, fermeque ad colophonem, desideratis saltem paucis quaternionibus, deduxisse. Illic in secundo eius voluminis capite, de Stellarum fixarum accurata et ipsi cœlo ad amussim consona restitutione, studiose et quasi ex professo tracto, subiunctis 500 aut etiam 600 circiter, prout libuerit (nam quaterniones illos Catalogum hunc complexuros inexcusos adhuc relinquo, ne citius quam vellem ad alios pertingant) tam quo ad latum quam longum admodum scrupulose redintegratis confectisque Affixarum locis, nulla earum, quæ circa Zodiacum versantur atque in nostro Horizonte oriuntur, vel minima vixque aspectabili prætermissa: adeo ut in plerisque Asterismis multo plures numerem stellas, quam ab antiquis antecessoribus nostris factitatum est. Hunc librum futura æstate, aspirante nostris conatibus cœlesti Numine, nanciscere. Illic voti abunde compos redderis. Interim hanc morulam atque dilationem æquo et candido perfer animo.

Quapropter consultius iudicarem, ut Ephemeridum tuarum, aut aliorum operum, quibus de fixis syderibus magnopere instituitur quæstio, recognitionem atque sub prœlum revocationem aliquamdiu adhuc protraheres, donec acceptis nostris, in his, inventionibus, rectificationibusque ipsi cœlo congrua proponere, atque alijs aperire concessum fuerit. Quæ enim hactenus in usu fuere, sive Alphonsinæ, sive Coperniceæ Fixarum restitutiones, ab ipso cœlo toto pene cœlo deviant, adeo ut ne una quidem sit inter tot stellas, quæ cœlesti normæ conformem dispositionem sortita sit, cum et ipsa ♍ Spica, qua sola COPERNICUS usus est, minus apposite ab ipso ordinata sit, cæteris omnibus ad imitationem Abaci Ptolemaici perperam in suis intercapedinibus iuxta longum et latum retentis. Sic etiam circa septem Errantium syderum motiones, Canones Astronomici, etiam a prædicto ingenti illo COPERNICO eiusque locupletatore REINHOLDO redintegrati non minus, si modo non plus cœlestibus apparentijs iniuriam inferunt; adeo ut non saltem in pauculis scrupulis, sed et in nonnullis etiam gradibus sæpenumero deprehendatur disconvenientia. Certum quidem est in Apogæis atque Eccentricitatibus, adeoque orbium commensurationibus, non omnia, prout decet, a COPERNICO ad amussim (ob instrumentorum, uti opinor, fallaciam, et observationum exactarum insufficientiam) esse disposita, satisque subtiliter consignata (imo forte ne quidem ab ipso PTOLEMÆO) id quod nostræ evidenter testantur observationes atque ex his derivatæ demonstrationes; adeo ut maiore, quam plerique existimant, in nonnullis adhuc opus sit correctione atque limitatione. Attamen cum alia adhuc subsit inæqualitatis particularis ratio, a veteribus non animadversa, nec etiam ab ipso COPERNICO, utut industrio cœlestium contemplatore, quæ neutris eorum hypothesibus analoga est, aut per has commode excusatur. Idcirco de Apogæis atque Eccentricitatibus orbiumque proportionibus frustra verba facimus, nisi et huius Anomaliæ habeatur respectus; quæ licet in omnibus quinque Planetis aliquatenus sese insinuet, in solo ♂ tamen, eo quod Solaris circuitus ad ipsius orbicularem tramitem magnitudinem obtineat maxime perceptibilem cæterisque multo ampliorem, evidentius oculis incurrit plusque sentitur. Atque hinc non minima ex parte evenit, quod Martium sydus se regulari atque sua vestigia numeris consignari, admittere hactenus respuerit. Scio satis eius stellam inobservabilem vulgariter existimari, quod a PLINIO, qui hoc de ea frustra profert, desumptum autumo. Ego nihilominus in ipsius apparentijs cœlitus denotandis et intermediantibus Geometricis apodixibus in numeros redigendis, non plus difficultatis invenio, quam penes cæteros, imo adhuc minus quam in ☿ aut ♀. Sunt mihi in promptu multiplices et diutinæ tum huius, tum aliorum oberrantium syderum observationes accuratissima ratione et medijs minime fallentibus conquisitæ, e quibus Astronomiæ cœlitus depromptam instaurationem Canonumque Secundorum Mobilium de novo extructionem, favente conatibus nostris cœlestium omnium Opifice ter maximo, ingenti et audenti conamine molior. Hoc nostrum Astronomicæ redintegrationis Theatrum, quam primum solide et totaliter extructum absolutumque fuerit, et publica luce spectari sustinuerit, te eius quoque reddam participem. Illud omnia, imo etiam plura, quam nunc circa Planetarum gyrationes dimensionesque enucleandas desideras, affatim suppeditabit. Expecto adhuc nonnullas superiorum Planetarum acronychas dispositiones desuper plenius examinandas, ut per totum hincinde Eccentricum (lubet enim nunc vulgariter loqui, cum in nostris hypothesibus ferme omnia sint concentrica) inæqualitales atque apparentias difformes penitius scruter, atque undiquaque circumspiciam, illisque convenienter prospiciam: idque potissimum in ipso de quo loqueris ♂. Ideoque præcognoscere aveo huius, quæ in Iunio proxime instantis anni futura est, circa ☉ oppositum, exactam constitutionem, quando circa Perigæum solare (quod non parum interest dignoscere) pernox reddetur: tum etiam biennio post, ubi in Piscibus iuxta medias ferme Solis elongationes eidem contraposita loca peraget, quanto etiam tam ratione Eccentrici, quam Epicycli (ut more usitato hæc dicam) terris plurimum appropinquans solito maior apparebit, atque pro novo quodam iubare ab imperitis reputabitur. Ubi quoque inter calculum Alphonsinum et Copernicæum admodum magna et consueta longe evidentior incidet differentia, utpote quæ novem gradus aliquantulum excedat, ipso cœlo locum aliquot gradibus ab utraque supputatione diversum tunc ostensuro. Has inquam in Martia stella animadversiones, plurimum ad tramitem eius sagaciter investigandum conducentes, adhuc desidero. Licet enim non paucas eiuscemodi acronychas huius syderis dispositiones hactenus iugiter scrutatus sim, unâ cum cæteris in vario, quoad Solem, situ locis, ex quibus eius apparentem motum demonstrare atque in numeros resolvere non est difficile, attamen et has binas superaddere exopto, ut per totum Zodiacum eius versus Solem acronycha incessio multifariam et subtiliter exploretur atque concinnetur. Eccentricitatem eius mutari, et non ob id omnibus sæculis æquationum Tabulas huic superiunctas apte congruere, abs re quidem a te asseritur. Verum id fit per accidens atque aliunde evenit, quemadmodum et in cæteris Planetis, licet in his non tantopere atque in ♂ certis de causis sentiatur. Si igitur universales debent esse canones, quod merito etiam requiritur, et huius quoque mutationis ratio non negligenda venit. Optarem quam maxime PTOLEMÆI atque COPERNICI circa eius Eccentricitatem, adeoque ipsum Apogæum prolata rite constare, et firmo adeoque cœlesti inniti fundamento. Sed enim in utroque horum aliquid satis perceptibilis latere erroris, haud citra rem suspicor, præsertim in Copernicæa decisione, quam videtur potius pro libitu ita ordinasse, ut suæ quadraret assumptioni, quam quod indubijs observationibus ita se exhibere sufficienter compertum habuerit. Quî enim fieri potuit, ut Martis apparens motus intra unius hominis ætatem a cœlesti tramite ita difformis redderetur, ut in tribus vel quatuor nonnunquam gradibus numeri ex ipsius calculatione deprompti a scopo cœlesti digrediantur, si illi suo tempore atque ijsdem annis, quibus observationes exercuit, competenter cœlo conformes erant? Verum de his atque alijs ad totam harmoniam cœlestem concinne in numeros adaptandam facientibus, volumen nostrum Astronomicum de perennium syderum circuitibus tractans, suo tempore, cum divinitus ita prospectum fuerit, te plenius admonebit, certioremque reddet.

Systema universi a nobis ante non multos annos adinventum, et ex ipso cœlo cœlestium pervestigata dispositione atque incessu, non autem libris vel chartis, sive antiquorum, sive recentium Philosophorum, tibi quoque probari et a te in pretio haberi, plurimum mihi ipsi congratulor. Neque enim ignoro perpaucos futuros, præsertim ex ijs, qui in Scholis Peripateticorum educati et instructi sunt, qui hanc nostram mundanæ Symmetriæ atque revolutionum cœlestium ordinationem prima fronte inspectam admissuri sint, utut plurimas a PTOLEMÆO et Veteribus commissas inconcinnitates adeoque superfluitates nimias et a COPERNICO introductas absurditates devitando eliminet. At ubi penitius ipsius penetralia introspicere solertibus ingenijs dabitur, utique non diffido, quin hanc Neotericam nostram constitutionem, repudiatis et antiquatis cæteris, sint approbaturi, præsertim quando apparentias cœlestes illi adeo certo et subtiliter congruere exploratum habuerint. Quod autem Martius orbis Solarem non totaliter ambiat, sed bifariam transeat, te et forte etiam alios nonnihil movet atque ancipites detinet, nihil prorsus apud me moretur scrupuli. Id enim quod hactenus Aristotelica auctoritate sic frustra persuadente creditum est, cœlum ex impervia adamantinaque constare materia, orbibusque realibus et solidis astra sibi infixa rotantibus confertum esse, caret fundamento atque ab ipsa rei veritate alienissimum est; prout ex ipsis saltem Cometis, si non aliunde etiam, sufficienter liquet. Dum enim quinos, accurata diligentia, diversis aliquot annis præteritis in altissimo æthere curriculum suum exercentes et nequaquam sublunari aëre, ut perperam tot sæculis Scholas omnes decepit ARISTOTELES, idoneis et minime fluctuantibus instrumentis observârim, atque invicta Geometriæ certitudine demonstrârim: horum tamen nullus alicuius certi orbis circumagitationem concomitabatur, sed libere in liquidissimo æthere, quo propria ferebat naturaliter indita motionis vis atque energia, undecunque tandem exorta atque agitata, movebantur: idque non perplexo, interrupto, aut tumultuario incessu, sed ordinato atque ad invicem proportionali, cæterorumque Planetarum cursui in plerisque non multum absimili, licet concitatiore nonnunquam impulsu. Fieri itaque non potest ut cœlum sit solidum atque impervium et realiter existentibus compaginetur orbibus. Alias enim Cometæ quoque in cœlo revera, quandocunque effulserint, versantes, ab eo, in quo existerent, orbe circumraperentur illique obsecundarent. Atque hæc non minima est causa, cur ascititiorum cœli Phaenomenωn descriptionem peculiari opere, magno illi, quod Mundo coævorum cœli corporum gyrationes multiplices extricabit atque ob oculos ponet, præmittere satius duxerimus. Inde namque non pauca sequentibus viam sternentia atque hypotheseos nostræ fundamenta, quibus cætera omnia superstruantur, stabilientia depromenda comprobandaque veniunt. Accedit et hoc, quod refractiones Astrorum ad verticem nequaquam pertingentes, sed intra dimidium quadrantem atque adhuc declivius prorsus evanescentes evidenter testentur diaphanitatem cœlestem non versari in solida materia, longe diversa ab aëris liquida diaphanitate, adeo ut illæ, quæ fiunt in luminaribus atque stellis sub decliviori versus Horizontem situ refractiones ex solis pene vaporibus Terram, etiam serenissimo existente aëre, in propinquo ambientibus, insinuentur et originem trahant, ita vel solis opticis ratiocinijs, quæ Geometriæ validis affirmantur munimentis, Aristotelicam de cœli natura atque materia nimis diu inveteratam opinionem aperte eludentibus. Quin et id, de quo sæpedictus GELLIUS noster te commonefecit, Martem in situ acronycho terris ipso Sole reddi propiorem, a nobis iugiter et subtili indagine pervestigatum est, atque ita se habere compertum, præsertim circa finem anni 1582, quando in ♋ sublimis, ipsa quoque latitudine Borea illum plus exaltante, Soli opponebatur. Deprehendi enim tum e multiplicibus et accuratis animadversionibus in vario situ obtentis atque invicem diligenter collatis, ipsum Parallaxin admisisse Solari paululo maiorem, quod nullatenus evenire poterat, si tota eius revolutio in orbibus supra Solis Sphæram extantibus, ut fert Ptolemaica dispositio, absolveretur. Attendi etiam sæpenumero ad ipsius transitus retrogrados, quando acronychius esset, et interiectis aliquot diebus comperi eos aliquanto concitatiores reddi, quam fert Ptolemaica ratiocinatio, congruente potius hîc Copernicæa numeratione, præsertim si verioris Apogæi atque oppositionis cum Solis medio motu fiat applicatio. Atque hinc non obscure satisque certo patuit, Martem Soli aversam cæli partem perambulantem, telluri ipso plus admoveri. Unde necessario consequitur, aut Copernicæas hypotheses veras esse, aut ita cœlestium distributionem ordinandam, quemadmodum a nobis nuper constitutum est. At COPERNICI circa motum telluris triplicem, utut ingeniosam et a cœlestibus apparentijs non admodum abhorrentem speculationem, nequaquam in rei veritate constare (si quis est, qui adhuc hæsitat) suo loco et tempore a nobis irrefragabiliter, non saltem Theologice et Physice, sed etiam Mathematice convincetur: utut is se satis Mathematica Mathematicis atque ab illis irreprehensibilia proposuisse sperabat. Imo vel ipsi Cometæ, de quibus dixi (ut hoc obiter nunc quoque indicem) quatenus aliqui eorum circa oppositam Solis plagam incedebant, motum Terræ annuum a COPERNICO assertum inficiabantur; nihil enim eorum destinato cursui detraxit, nedum ut eos retroagi aut lentiore gressu tardari nobis ita aspectantibus cogeret, de quibus opportuniore loco latius agere decrevimus. Ex his itaque omnibus iam commemoratis Martiæ stellæ circuitum, licet Solarem transeat, ob cœli liquidissimam et ubique perviam substantiam orbiumque realiter existentium abrogationem et systematis Copernicæi absurditatem, non inconcinne aut citra possibilitatem a nobis ordinatum esse, extra omnem controversiam concludere datur.

VERNERI libellum de motu octavæ Sphæræ ex tua donatione nondum accepi. Existimo illum præ locorum inter nos longinqua distantia itinerumque difficultate alicubi intercidisse. Ego nihilominus pro hac quoque tua non contemnenda erga me benevolentia tibi magnas dico gratias. Et ut conscius sis, Librum illum iam dudum a D. FABRITIO Mathematico Cæsareo, intermediante D. Doctore THADDÆO HAGGECIO, etiam Mathematum insigniter gnaro, adeptus sum. Illum introspexi, disquisivi, dilui: Nam ut paucis expediam, quod res est, ternæ illæ stellæ, quibus pro fundamento cæterarum utitur, atque octavæ Sphæræ intricatas mutationes superstruere conatur, non ab illo cœlitus diligenter, uti par erat, licet is ita prætendat (quod tamen cum pace Manium viri alias eruditissimi et de re Mathematica præclare meriti dictum volo) observatæ sunt, sed potius eas ita pro lubitu effinxit atque adaptavit, ut suæ intentioni congruerent. Quod inde satis liquet, ex quo latitudines earundem ubique easdem, quas veteres præsupposuerunt, retineat, et nihilominus assumptis suis declinationibus differentias longitudinum pares cum recepta annotatione efformet. Quæ nequaquam invicem cohærere possunt. Nam nec illæ latitudines earundem stellarum, excepta sola Spica, in qua saltem unicum desideratur scrupulum, cœlitus ita se habent, nec longitudinum intervalla apparentijs congruunt. Hinc patet, quam reliqua, quæ non absque ingenij acumine atque subtilitate superædificare laborat, invalida atque ruinosa evadant. Taceo nunc quod ipsas stellas suas latitudines mutare, ad rationem alteratæ obliquitatis Eclipticæ (prout a nobis indubitate deprehensum est) nec is, nec magnus ille COPERNICUS animadverterint: multo minus, ut per hypothesin aliquam hanc divagationem tutati sint.

Intelligo te Theoricas tuas nunc publice exponere atque copiosius illustrare, quod etiam plurimum laudo. Attigisti in istis Theoricis propius scopum Copernicæum, redigendo ipsius speculationem ad formam Ptolemaicam, quam antea in Tabularum Astronomicarum e Prutenicis resolutione præstitum est, ubi nimium ab ipsius symmetria et ratione aliena admisisti, quod te ipsum iam dudum animadvertisse atque emendasse non dubito. Verum ne hîc quidem ad amussim quadrant, et Æquantum a circulari et regulari motu, qui proprium, non alienum respicit centrum, inconcinnitas parvis quibusdam circellis convenientius excusari poterat, ne sit Mathematicæ perfectioni atque regularitati offendiculo. Realitas etiam et copia tot orbium complentium et trahentium, quæ et quorsum libuerit, non est necessaria, imo prorsus supervacanea, cum ipsi cœlo nihil eiuscemodi revera competat, velut antea innuimus. Recte tamen facis, quod studiosos aliquali saltem cœlestium cognitione imbuis, et eorum ingenia ad solidiora et pleniora capessenda idonea reddis atque præparas. Est aliquid prodire tenus, si non datur ultra, ut inquiebat Poëta. Cum nostræ publicam lucem viderint hypotyposes hisque superinductæ demonstrationes et numeri, has quoque discutere, explanare atque auditoribus tuis proponere declarareque tibi liberum relinquam. Interim utere ijs, quæ in promptu sunt, et si qua, antequam nostra acceperis, intercesserit, uti fit in rebus arduis, mora, eam patienter sustine et candide interpretare. Unum vel alterum adhuc addam, postea scribendi finem faciam.

To the most distinguished and most excellent man, Master Giovanni Antonio Magini of Padua, eminent Professor of Mathematics in the celebrated University of Bologna, and splendidly deserving of the enrichment of Astronomical matters: his much-to-be-esteemed friend.

That you have, some while ago, obtained our Second Book on the more recent Phenomena of the Aethereal World [De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis] through my [agent] Gellius Sascerides, most distinguished Man — [you] who have most excellently deserved of the cultivation and enrichment of the Astronomical [science] as it has hitherto been constituted by the artificers of our age — I gladly learned from your letter, given to me from Bologna on the Ides of September of the reformed year [13 September, N.S.], which I received here on the 8th [day] before the Kalends of December of the old Julian year [24 November, O.S.]; although the said Gellius had also reported the same beforehand by letter, and, that you put off the writing so long, I hold you now sufficiently excused. And that it was welcome to you — whatever there was of [my] book — and did not displease, is also very acceptable to me. And that you have now at last troubled to address me with your most kind letter, and to declare in it, not obscurely, your singular benevolence and candour toward me and my studies, and to confer with me about certain [matters] for the more correct and solid renewal and stabilizing of the Astronomical business than has been done before, and to seek my judgment and experiments about these — this is by no means unwelcome to me, but on many counts most pleasing. There is therefore [reason] why I should hold you the greatest thanks for this sincere and dutiful disposition toward me. You will find me in turn kind toward you and your endeavours, most zealous for you, and a ready and constant preserver of the friendship once contracted by letter — so that you may not regret having been the first to contend [in it]. And although, about those [things] which you desire in this [letter] of yours, I gave a full reply a few weeks ago, having forewarned Gellius (who had sought almost the same [things] from me in your name, and had attached a copy of your letter, in which most of [these things] now repeated were comprised), and enjoined that he take care to convey those [things] to you — nevertheless even now I shall not decline to prepare a response to these very [things] which your own [letter] contains and which seek and await [an answer] from me. The catalogue [elenchus] of very many fixed Stars, verified by us over some years past with the greatest assiduity and diligence, by infallible instruments and methods of measurement — I would indeed now gladly and liberally communicate to you, nor would I deny or begrudge you so great a treasure (acquired, though it was, with vast and likewise incredible labour and expense, and very many wakeful nights), so that you might adorn and enrich your eminent lucubrations with these; for it would tend to the profit of Astronomy, which I strive to promote in whatever way I may; nor would it harm me either, since I am sufficiently persuaded, from the integrity of your mind and judgment, that you will ascribe my [things] to me, not to yourself, and will not publish them as another's. But truly, since there is too great an interval of places between us, and many windings of the roads by land and sea, [and] one must pass through the various domains of diverse lords too, and my letter, before it reaches you, will pass under the hands of very many: I truly fear lest, falling in somewhere — or even unsealed — they should furnish to others, more than I would wish, a supply of my books, who would afterwards either publish them as their own, or, against my will, hand them over to others. The winter season increases this suspicion, since now the solstice presses on, nor was it possible to reply earlier to your [letter] written about the autumn equinox, since I have only now first obtained it. You yourself know that journeys from here into Italy are more difficult and less safe in winter, so that I cannot be sufficiently certain whether even this [letter] will reach your hands safe and sound. I hope, therefore, that, these reasons being weighed, you will gladly excuse me, in accordance with your candour and equanimity, for not transmitting at this time the restored places of the Fixed [stars]. Yet, lest you fall away entirely from so great a hope and from your exceptional confidence toward me, and so that your wishes may some time be satisfied without too long a delay: know that I am now pressing through the presses of my own Printing-house the First Volume of the more recent Phenomena of the Heaven [the Astronomiae Instauratae Progymnasmata, Tome I], which treats amply of the New Star and certain other [matters] pertaining hither and enriching the Astronomical store, and have brought it almost to the colophon, only a few gatherings [quaterniones] being still wanting. There, in the second chapter of that volume, where I treat, studiously and as it were professedly, of the accurate restitution of the fixed Stars, exactly consonant with the heaven itself, with about 500 or even 600 [stars] subjoined, as it shall please (for I am leaving those gatherings which are to comprise this Catalogue still unprinted, lest they reach others sooner than I would wish), the places of the Fixed [stars] most scrupulously restored and worked out, both as to latitude and longitude, with none of those which move about the Zodiac and rise in our Horizon omitted — even the least and scarcely visible — so that in most of the constellations I count far more stars than was done by our ancient predecessors. This book you will obtain next summer, the heavenly Deity favouring our endeavours. There you will be rendered abundantly possessed of your wish. Meanwhile bear this little delay and postponement with an even and candid mind.

Wherefore I should judge it more advisable that you should still draw out for some while the revision, and the recalling under the press, of your Ephemerides, or of [your] other works in which a question is greatly raised about the fixed stars, until — our findings and rectifications in these [matters] having been received — it shall be granted [you] to propose [things] congruent with the heaven itself, and to open [them] to others. For the restitutions of the Fixed [stars] which have hitherto been in use — whether Alphonsine or Copernican — deviate from the heaven itself by almost the whole heaven; so that there is not even one among so many stars which has been allotted a disposition conformable to the celestial norm — since even Spica [♍] itself, which alone Copernicus used, was less aptly ordered by him, all the rest being wrongly retained in their intervals (as to longitude and latitude) in imitation of the Ptolemaic Table [Abacus]. So too, concerning the motions of the seven Wandering stars, the Astronomical Canons — restored even by that vast Copernicus and his enricher [Erasmus] Reinhold — do no less injury (if indeed not more) to the celestial appearances; so that a discrepancy is very often found not merely in a few minutes, but even in several degrees. It is indeed certain that, in the Apogees and Eccentricities, and so [in] the commensurations of the orbs, not everything was disposed by Copernicus exactly (on account, I think, of the fallacy of the instruments and the insufficiency of exact observations) as is fitting, nor subtly enough recorded (nay, perhaps not even by Ptolemy himself) — which our observations, and the demonstrations derived from these, evidently testify; so that in some [matters] there is need of a greater correction and limitation than most suppose. But moreover, since there yet lies beneath another reckoning of a particular inequality, not noticed by the ancients, nor even by Copernicus himself (industrious contemplator of the heavens though he was), which is analogous to neither of their hypotheses, nor is conveniently excused by these: therefore we speak in vain about Apogees and Eccentricities and the proportions of the orbs, unless regard be had to this Anomaly too; which, although it insinuates itself to some extent in all five Planets, yet in Mars [♂] alone — because the magnitude of the Solar circuit, relative to its [Mars's] orbicular track, is most perceptible and far ampler than [for] the rest — strikes the eyes more evidently and is felt more. And hence it comes about, in no small part, that the Martian star has hitherto refused to admit being regulated and having its tracks recorded by numbers. I know well enough that its star is commonly thought unobservable — which I suppose was taken from Pliny, who asserts this about it in vain. I, nonetheless, in noting its appearances from the heaven, and in reducing [them] to numbers by means of intermediate Geometric demonstrations [apodixes], find no more difficulty than [I do] with the rest — nay, still less than in Mercury [☿] or Venus [♀]. I have ready to hand manifold and long-continued observations, both of this and of the other wandering stars, sought out by the most accurate method and by means in no way deceptive — from which I am attempting, with a vast and daring effort, the restoration of Astronomy drawn from the heaven, and the new construction of the Canons of the Second Movables [the planetary tables], the thrice-greatest Maker of all the heavenly [bodies] favouring our endeavours. This our Theatre of Astronomical restoration, as soon as it shall have been solidly and wholly built and completed, and shall have endured to be beheld in the public light, I will render you a sharer of it too. It will abundantly supply everything — nay, even more — than you now desire concerning the unfolding of the Planets' gyrations and dimensions. I still await some acronychal dispositions of the superior Planets, to be examined more fully thereupon, so that through the whole Eccentric on either side (for it pleases [me] now to speak commonly, since in our hypotheses almost everything is concentric) I may scrutinize more deeply the inequalities and the deformed appearances, and may look about on every side, and provide for them suitably — and this especially in that very Mars [♂] of which you speak. And therefore I am eager to foreknow the exact constitution of this [planet], which is to be in June of the year next at hand, about [its] opposition to the Sun [☉], when, about the solar Perigee (which it is of no little interest to discern), it will be rendered visible all night long; and likewise two years after, when it will traverse the places set opposite [to the Sun] in Pisces, near nearly the mean elongations of the Sun — by how much also, approaching the Earth very greatly (both by reason of the Eccentric and of the Epicycle, to say these things in the customary manner), it will appear larger than usual, and be reckoned by the unskilled as some new radiance. There too a difference, very great and far more evident than usual, will fall between the Alphonsine and the Copernican calculation — namely, one that exceeds nine degrees a little — the heaven itself then showing a place several degrees different from both computations. These observations on the Martian star, I say — conducing very much to the sagacious investigation of its track — I still want. For although I have hitherto continually scrutinized not a few such acronychal dispositions of this star, together with the rest, in [its] various place with respect to the Sun (from which it is not difficult to demonstrate its apparent motion and to resolve [it] into numbers), yet I much desire to add these two as well, so that throughout the whole Zodiac its acronychal advance toward the Sun may be explored and arranged in manifold and subtle fashion. That its Eccentricity changes, and that for this reason the Tables of equations superimposed on it do not aptly agree for all ages, is indeed not idly asserted by you. But this happens by accident and comes about from elsewhere, as also in the other Planets — although in these it is not felt so much as in Mars, for certain causes. If therefore the canons ought to be universal (which is rightly required too), then the reckoning of this change must not be neglected either. I should wish most greatly that what Ptolemy and Copernicus have brought forward concerning its Eccentricity, and so [concerning] the Apogee itself, were duly established and rested on a firm — indeed a celestial — foundation. But I suspect, and not without reason, that something of fairly perceptible error lies hidden in both of them — especially in the Copernican determination, which he seems to have ordered thus rather at [his own] pleasure, so that it might square with his assumption, than because he had found it sufficiently established, by indubitable observations, to present itself so. For how could it have come about that the apparent motion of Mars should be rendered, within the lifetime of one man, so deformed from the celestial track that the numbers drawn from his calculation diverge from the celestial mark by three or sometimes four degrees — if those [numbers] were, in his time and in the very years in which he conducted his observations, fittingly conformable to the heaven? But about these and other [matters] making for the apt adaptation of the whole celestial harmony into numbers, our Astronomical volume treating of the circuits of the everlasting stars will, in its time — when it shall have been so provided by Heaven — admonish you more fully and render [you] more certain.

That the system of the universe [Systema universi], discovered by us not many years ago — and [discovered] from the heaven itself, the disposition and advance of the heavenly [bodies] having been investigated [there], and not from the books or papers of Philosophers, whether ancient or recent — is approved by you too and held in esteem by you, I greatly congratulate myself. For I am not ignorant that there will be very few — especially of those who have been educated and trained in the Schools of the Peripatetics — who will admit this our ordering of the world's symmetry and of the celestial revolutions, inspected at first glance, even though, by avoiding [them], it eliminates the very many incongruities committed by Ptolemy and the Ancients, and the excessive superfluities, and the absurdities introduced by Copernicus. But when it shall be granted to skilful talents to look more deeply into its inner chambers, I do not at all doubt but that they will approve this our Neoteric [modern] constitution, the rest being repudiated and made obsolete — especially when they shall have found by examination that the celestial appearances agree with it so surely and subtly. But that the Martian orb does not totally encompass the Solar [orb], but passes through [it] in two ways [bifariam] — [a thing which] moves you somewhat, and perhaps others too, and holds [you] in two minds — let it detain not the least scruple with me. For that which has hitherto been believed, Aristotelian authority thus vainly persuading — that the heaven consists of an impervious and adamantine matter, and is packed with real and solid orbs rotating the stars fixed in them — lacks foundation and is most alien from the very truth of the matter; as is sufficiently clear from the comets at least, if not from elsewhere too. For when I had observed five [comets], with accurate diligence, in several years now past, plying their course in the highest aether and by no means in the sublunary air (as Aristotle wrongly deceived all the Schools for so many centuries), with suitable and in-no-way-wavering instruments, and had demonstrated [it] with the unconquered certainty of Geometry: yet none of them accompanied the rotation of any fixed orb, but they moved freely in the most fluid aether, whither the proper force and energy of motion, naturally implanted [in them] — arisen and stirred up, at last, from whatever source — bore [them]; and that not with a perplexed, interrupted, or tumultuary advance, but with an ordered and mutually proportional [one], not much unlike the course of the other Planets in most [respects], though sometimes with a swifter impulse. It cannot therefore come about that the heaven is solid and impervious and framed of really existing orbs. For otherwise the comets too — really dwelling in the heaven, whenever they have blazed — would be snatched round by that orb in which they existed, and would comply with it. And this is no small cause why I have thought it better to send ahead, in a separate work, the description of the adventitious phenomena of the heaven [the comets], before that great [work] which will disentangle and set before the eyes the manifold gyrations of the heavenly bodies coeval with the World. For thence come [things] to be drawn forth and proven — not a few [of them] paving the way for what follows, and stabilizing the foundations of our hypothesis, on which all the rest are to be built. There is added this too: that the refractions of the heavenly bodies — by no means reaching to the zenith, but vanishing entirely within the half-quadrant [45°] and still lower — evidently testify that the celestial transparency does not consist in a solid matter far different from the fluid transparency of the air; so that those refractions which occur in the luminaries and stars in a lower position toward the Horizon insinuate themselves and draw their origin from the vapours alone (well-nigh) which surround the Earth nearby, even when the air is most serene — thus, by optical reasonings alone, which are affirmed by the strong defences of Geometry, openly eluding the Aristotelian opinion (too long inveterate) about the nature and matter of the heaven. Nay, this too — about which our oft-named Gellius reminded you, that Mars in the acronychal position is rendered nearer to the Earth than the Sun itself — has been continually and by subtle search investigated by us, and found to be so, especially about the end of the year 1582, when, lofty in Cancer [♋] — its very northern latitude exalting it the more — it was opposed to the Sun. For I then ascertained, from manifold and accurate observations obtained in various positions and diligently compared with one another, that it admitted a parallax a little greater than the solar — which could in no way have happened if its whole revolution were completed in orbs standing above the Sun's Sphere, as the Ptolemaic disposition holds. I attended also, very often, to its retrograde transits, when it was acronychal, and, after some days were interposed, I found them rendered somewhat swifter than the Ptolemaic reckoning bears — the Copernican numeration rather agreeing here, especially if the application be made to the truer Apogee and to the opposition with the Sun's mean motion. And hence it appeared not obscurely, and surely enough, that Mars, traversing the part of the heaven averted from the Sun, is brought nearer to the Earth than [the Sun] itself. Whence it necessarily follows, either that the Copernican hypotheses are true, or that the distribution of the heavenly [bodies] is to be ordered as has lately been established by us. But that Copernicus's threefold motion of the Earth — an ingenious speculation, and not much abhorrent from the celestial appearances — by no means holds in the truth of the matter (if there is anyone who still hesitates) will, in its place and time, be irrefragably proven by us, not merely Theologically and Physically, but also Mathematically: even though he hoped that he had proposed [things] Mathematical enough to Mathematicians, and irreprehensible by them. Nay, the very comets of which I spoke (to indicate this in passing now too), in so far as some of them advanced about the part opposite the Sun, gainsaid the annual motion of the Earth asserted by Copernicus; for nothing of [that motion] detracted from their appointed course, much less compelled them to be driven backward or retarded with a slower step, as we look on thus — about which [comets] I have resolved to treat more amply in a more opportune place. From all these [things] now recalled, therefore, it is given [us] to conclude, beyond all controversy, that the circuit of the Martian star — although it passes through the Solar [orb] — has been ordered by us not inelegantly, nor beyond possibility, on account of the most fluid and everywhere pervious substance of the heaven, the abolition of really-existing orbs, and the absurdity of the Copernican system.

Werner's little book on the motion of the eighth Sphere [De motu octavae sphaerae], from your gift, I have not yet received. I think it has fallen in somewhere, on account of the far-off distance of places between us and the difficulty of the journeys. I nonetheless say great thanks to you for this not-to-be-despised benevolence of yours toward me too. And, that you may be aware [of it], I obtained that Book some while ago from Master [Paul] Fabricius, the Imperial Mathematician, through the mediation of Master Doctor Thaddaeus Hagecius [Tadeáš Hájek], also notably skilled in Mathematics. I looked into it, examined it, [and] dissolved it: for, to expound in few [words] what the matter is, those three stars which he uses as a foundation for the rest, and on which he tries to build the intricate changes of the eighth Sphere, were not observed by him from the heaven diligently, as was fitting — although he so pretends (which, however, I would have said with the peace of the shade [Manes] of a man otherwise most erudite and splendidly deserving of Mathematics) — but rather he so fashioned and adapted them at [his own] pleasure, that they might agree with his intention. Which is clear enough from this: that he retains the latitudes of these same [stars] everywhere the same as the ancients presupposed, and nonetheless, his own declinations being assumed, forms differences of longitudes equal to the received notation. Which can by no means cohere with one another. For neither do those latitudes of these same stars — except Spica alone, in which only a single minute is wanting — stand so from the heaven, nor do the intervals of longitudes agree with the appearances. Hence it is clear how invalid and ruinous the rest become, which he labours to build up, not without acumen and subtlety of talent. I say nothing now of the fact that neither he, nor that great Copernicus, noticed that the stars themselves change their latitudes, in proportion to the altered obliquity of the Ecliptic (as has been undoubtedly ascertained by us); much less that they guarded this divagation by any hypothesis.

I understand that you are now publicly expounding your Theoricae [Planetary Theories] and illustrating [them] more copiously, which too I greatly praise. In those Theoricae you have approached the Copernican aim more nearly, by reducing his speculation to the Ptolemaic form, than was done before in the resolution of the Astronomical Tables from the Prutenic [Tables], where you admitted [things] too alien from his symmetry and reckoning — which I do not doubt you have yourself noticed and emended long since. But not even here do they square exactly; and the incongruity of the Equants from a circular and regular motion (which regards its own, not an alien, centre) could be more conveniently excused by certain small circles [circelli], lest it be a stumbling-block to Mathematical perfection and regularity. The reality, too, and the multitude of so many orbs — filling and drawing [the planets] whither and as it has pleased — is not necessary, nay altogether superfluous, since nothing of this kind really belongs to the heaven itself, as we hinted before. Yet you do rightly in imbuing students with at least some knowledge of the celestial [matters], and in rendering and preparing their talents fit for grasping more solid and fuller [things]. "It is something to advance thus far, if it is not granted [to go] beyond," as the Poet said. When our sketches [hypotyposes], and the demonstrations and numbers superinduced on them, shall have seen the public light, I will leave it free to you to discuss these too, to explain [them], and to propose and declare [them] to your hearers. Meanwhile, use those [things] which are ready to hand; and if any delay shall intervene before you have received ours (as happens in arduous matters), bear it patiently and interpret [it] candidly. I will add one [thing] or another still; afterward I will make an end of writing.

If some Eclipses — especially Lunar, or even Solar — have been carefully noted, as to the moments of time, at Rome, Bologna, or even in other more celebrated Cities of Italy, in some years most recently elapsed, bring it about that, as many as it shall be possible to have, a supply of them be made [available] to me; whereby it may be permitted to discern the difference of our Meridians [the longitude-difference], our own observation in the same [eclipses] being collated, more surely and precisely than has been done before. The last solar Eclipse occurred here at 6 hours 51 minutes elapsed from midnight, as to its first beginning. The last end was at the hour being exactly the ninth, [and] 0 minutes; nor were more than four digits of the Sun covered — that is, a third part of it — although the canons promised, in our clime, eight [digits], and a Moon's interposition twice as great. I should also wish to know what was observed by you in Italy, where the eclipse ought, according to the Canons, to have been almost total. I have written to the oft-said Gellius to procure that a certain instrument be made there at my expense, after the likeness of one of those which he saw and handled here, by means of which he may note from the heaven certain stars there climbing higher than here, and some besides which, on account of the great altitude of the Pole [here], do not rise [here]; and, at the same time, may scrupulously scrutinize and inquire into the Pole-elevations [latitudes] of certain selected towns of Italy, in the manner (as he knows) customary to me. In which matter, if he shall come to Bologna, I would have you kindly asked to be at hand to him, by the convenience of the place and the means of executing the whole matter safely and surely; and likewise, if he shall have taken upon himself any other [things] to be expedited and procured in my name, that you would be willing not to deny [him] your relieving help in all those [matters] too in which he seeks your aid with [your] counsel — which I promise myself you will by no means [fail to] do, from the integrity of your mind and your desire of deserving well of me.

It now occurs [to me] that I once read, in the Prolegomena of your Tables of the Second Movables, that you are of the same opinion with that Domenico Maria of Ferrara [Novara] of most laudable memory, Copernicus's Teacher — namely that the latitudes of places are successively, to some extent, changed; and you bring forward there, from the same [author], reasons by which you think it can be persuaded — which, however, I (I would say it with the peace of you both) judge not valid and ratified enough; and that this by no means happens, I shall evidently show elsewhere. Certainly Rome alone — which still retains nearly the same latitude, from the observation of Regiomontanus, as once in Pliny's time, an investigation having been made by the proportion of the gnomon and the shadow (provided account be had of the centre of the Sun, not of its uppermost surface) — proves that the polar altitudes do not vary. For Pliny, in the second Book of the Natural History, chapter 72, reports that in his time, on the day of the equinox, in the City of Rome, a ninth part of the gnomon was wanting to the shadow. There was therefore then, according to Geometric reckonings, the meridian altitude of the Sun = 48° 22′, and that as to its uppermost limb. Wherefore, the apparent semidiameter of 16 minutes being subtracted, the altitude of the centre comes out 48° 6′, representing at the same time the inclination of the Equator there. Nor do Refraction and solar Parallax here introduce any sensible difference, since they nearly eliminate one another. The latitude of Rome, therefore, comes out hence 41° 54′. With this we shall compare Regiomontanus's observation, made at Rome in the preceding age, in the following manner:

Since therefore the later observation of Regiomontanus — which seems more agreeable than the earlier — yields the latitude of the City of Rome, about these centuries, [at] 42 degrees very nearly, and in Pliny's age, from the gnomon's shadow, it was noted at least 6 minutes (almost imperceptible) smaller, it is surely clear enough that, in so great an interval of time elapsed, it admitted no perceptible alteration. So too the same Pliny, in the cited Chapter, asserts that at Venice [in Pliny: the region] the equinoctial shadow was equal to the gnomon. There was therefore the uppermost altitude of the Sun [at] precisely half a quadrant [45°]; but, account being had of the centre, 44 [degrees] and as many minutes [44° 44′] the same with the altitude of the Equator. Wherefore at Venice the Pole-elevation then came out 45° 16′, which agrees, to the very Minute, with the neoteric observation of Pitati [Pietro Pitati], which you too approve — a sufficiently evident sign that, with so many centuries elapsed, the inclination of the Horizon to the axis of the world has remained utterly unmoved. Nay, Ancona too, cited by Pliny in the same place — [in] that a thirty-fifth part of the gnomon was left over of the shadow — yields the latitude of that place [at] 44° 10′, although the neoterics establish it [at] 43° 40′ — not greater than of old, but rather (by some carelessness of observation, doubtless) a half-degree smaller. And from these three Cities of Italy, conserving their latitudes unvaried for so many centuries, it is clear more than sufficiently that the polar altitudes, even with very many years elapsing, are by no means increased or subject to any alteration. For a discrepancy of 1° 10′ (as much as Domenico Maria admits) could not slip through in this reckoning, since not even twice as great a difference of latitude is found between Rome and Ancona — which, however, Pliny very aptly distinguished by the proportions of the shadow and the gnomon; so that to rest in these proofs [tecmiria, τεκμήρια], until more and more exact [ones] be brought forward, is not superfluous. Which is what we intended to show.

I have resolved to see to it that the Pole-elevation of Alexandria too be precisely measured, on account of certain Ptolemaic observations instituted there. For whether he [Ptolemy] too deduced it scrupulously enough, I doubt not without cause: just as I have found out long since that Copernicus fell short by 2¾ minutes in designating the latitude of Frauenburg [Frombork], one of my [men] having been sent thither with [my] own and likewise exact instrument. When at Alexandria likewise an inquiry shall have been made (as I hope), it will surely be made manifest that the Pole's altitude there has not been sensibly altered in so many centuries elapsed, except in so far as it may perhaps have been erred by Ptolemy himself in a few minutes. And this will be a more certain occasion for resolving this knot — not, however, of itself so Gordian. I should nonetheless wish to inspect the books of that most excellent [Domenico] Maria, if any are extant (which I have never seen), or even are reserved in manuscript somewhere. In which matter, if you too, favouring our wishes, shall favour [them] with that helping hand, you will perform a most welcome thing, to be compensated with every kind of good offices, as far as lies in my power. I will add no more now, lest I exceed the bounds of a Letter too much and detain you longer.

Farewell, most distinguished Man, and continue constantly to love me in return — me, most loving of you — as you have begun; and persevere in continuing, by the same [means] (since it cannot be done otherwise), the friendship once begun by letter. The merchants of your country, setting out for the Frankfurt Fairs in Germany, will give a not-inconvenient opportunity of writing back and of conveying letters to me safely. Again, fare well long and happily, and do not cease to deserve well of Astronomical studies; and now a third time, and many times, farewell.

Given at Uraniborg, on the Kalends of December, of the old Julian Year 1590.

POSTSCRIPT, AS THEY CALL IT.

Lest I send my letter altogether empty of my observations and restitutions drawn down from the heavens, I have added the Ephemeris of the coming year, derived from our own observations and demonstrations reduced into numbers; the reasoning and foundations of which the first chapter of the former volume — once you have obtained it — will set forth. Meanwhile, weigh well this delineation of the Sun's course, and compare it with each of the two customary reckonings. You will certainly recognise how far the Sun himself, King and Guide of the other Planets, departs in the usual Tables from the celestial rule to which our own [tables] are most closely conformed; and from this you may in some measure reason concerning the rest, and perceive the deviations of the customary Canons.

Farewell.

TYCHO BRAHE

  • Sender / recipient. Tycho (at Uraniborg, in his last full decade on Hven). Giovanni Antonio Magini (1555–1617), Paduan-born professor of mathematics at Bologna (where he had beaten Galileo to the chair in 1588), astronomer, cartographer, and Ptolemaist; author of Ephemerides, Novae coelestium orbium theoricae, Tabulae secundorum mobilium, and the Tabulae primi mobilis. The correspondence is cordial but doctrinally fenced: Magini admired Tycho's data while remaining geocentric. (Cf. the later Magini exchanges 16000304001 / 16010124124 and the Baldi self-monument thread.)
  • The scientific spine.
  • The fixed-star catalogue, withheld. Tycho will not yet send his 1000-star catalogue (here promised at "500 or even 600" in the printed subset), fearing winter interception en route through "the domains of diverse lords"; but the Progymnasmata Tome I (on the New Star of 1572) is at his own Uraniborg press, "almost to the colophon," and will carry the restored star-places "next summer." He urges Magini to delay reissuing his Ephemerides until Tycho's restitutions are out, since the Alphonsine and Copernican fixed-star restitutions "deviate by almost the whole heaven" — even Spica (Copernicus's sole reference star) is misplaced.
  • The Mars "particular inequality." A new anomaly unknown to the ancients and to Copernicus, "analogous to neither of their hypotheses," most perceptible in Mars because the solar circuit is largest relative to the Martian orbit. Tycho denies Mars is "unobservable" (a Pliny error), claiming it gives him less trouble than Mercury or Venus. He wants two further acronychal (opposition) observations — the June-1591 opposition near solar perigee (all-night visibility) and the opposition two years later in Pisces near the Sun's mean elongations — where the Alphonsine–Copernican discrepancy "exceeds nine degrees." He doubts both Ptolemy's and Copernicus's Mars eccentricity/apogee, suspecting Copernicus fitted his determination "at pleasure" to his assumption (Mars errs 3–4° from the sky within Copernicus's own lifetime).
  • The world-system & fluid heavens (the doctrinal heart). The Tychonic system ("discovered from the heaven itself, not from books") eliminates Ptolemaic incongruities and Copernican absurdities; the Mars orb crossing the Sun's (bifariam transeat) is no objection, because the solid adamantine spheres are a groundless Aristotelian fiction. Proof: five comets observed in the "highest aether," moving freely, accompanying no solid orb; and refraction, which vanishes above c. 45° and arises from vapours near the Earth, shows the celestial transparency is not solid matter. The Mars-parallax result — Mars at opposition "nearer the Earth than the Sun itself," demonstrated about the end of 1582 with Mars lofty in Cancer, its parallax "a little greater than the solar" — forces the disjunction: either Copernicus or Tycho. But Copernicus's triple Earth-motion Tycho promises to refute "theologically, physically, and mathematically"; and the comets (some moving opposite the Sun, undisturbed) "gainsay the annual motion of the Earth." Hence the Mars-circuit crossing the solar orb is admissible.
  • The Werner critique. Tycho had already (via Paul Fabricius, the Imperial Mathematician, through Hagecius) examined Johann Werner's De motu octavae sphaerae (1522): Werner's three foundation stars were "fashioned at pleasure" not observed — he keeps the ancient latitudes yet assumes new declinations to force the received longitude-differences, which "cannot cohere." Only Spica is near right (off by 1′). Neither Werner nor Copernicus noticed that stellar latitudes change with the altered obliquity of the Ecliptic (a Tychonic finding).
  • Magini's Theoricae. Praised for reducing Copernicus "to the Ptolemaic form," but the equant's departure from uniform motion about its own centre would be "more conveniently excused by small circles" (epicyclets), and the reality of the orbs is "altogether superfluous." Horatian tag: "Est aliquid prodire tenus, si non datur ultra" (Horace, Epist. I.1.32).
  • The eclipse & longitude request. Tycho asks for Italian eclipse timings (Rome, Bologna) to fix the meridian/longitude difference. He reports the last solar eclipse at Uraniborg: first contact 6h51m after midnight, end at 9h00m, only 4 digits (⅓) covered though the canons promised 8 — and asks what Italy saw (where it "ought to have been nearly total"). He has commissioned, via Gellius, an instrument for Bologna to measure southern stars and Italian latitudes.
  • The latitude-constancy demonstration (against Domenico Maria Novara). Magini, following Domenico Maria Novara (Copernicus's Bologna teacher), held in his Tabulae secundorum mobilium prolegomena that terrestrial latitudes drift. Tycho refutes this with gnomon-shadow data from Pliny (Nat. Hist. II.72) reduced to the Sun's centre: Rome = 41°54′ (Pliny) vs c. 42° (Regiomontanus 1462) — change of only c. 6′; Venice = 45°16′ (matching Pietro Pitati); Ancona = 44°10′ (Pliny) vs the neoterics' 43°40′ ("a careless half-degree"). Domenico Maria's claimed 1°10′ drift cannot survive. He plans to measure Alexandria (to test Ptolemy) and notes Copernicus erred 2¾′ in the Frauenburg latitude (verified by Tycho's own man sent with an exact instrument). Greek tag: Tecmirijs = τεκμήρια, "proofs/sure signs."

A clean style code-switch: Magini (Catholic Bologna) wrote "Idibus Septembris anni recogniti" = 13 September, New Style ("the reformed year"); Tycho (Lutheran Denmark) received it "8 Calendarum Decembris anni veteris Iuliani" = 24 November, Old Style (ante diem VIII Kal.   • What it is. A postscript ("Postscriptum, ut vocant" — Tycho's own wry Latin for the genre) to a letter to Giovanni Antonio Magini of 1 December 1590, sent to accompany an Ephemeris for the coming year (1591) enclosed with the letter — the postscript explains the enclosure and how to read it.
  • The enclosure. The Ephemeris was derived from Tycho's own observations and "demonstrations reduced into numbers" — i.e. his own emerging solar theory, distinct from both the Alphonsine and Copernican/Prutenic reckonings then in scholarly use (the same two systems named a few lines earlier in this letter's published tail).
  • "The former volume." Tycho refers Magini to "the first chapter of the former Tome" for the underlying reasoning — plausibly his still-unfinished treatise on the new star of 1572 and the Tychonic solar and lunar theories, later completed by Tengnagel and Kepler's circle and published posthumously as the Astronomiae instauratae progymnasmata (1602/1610) [Christianson, On Tycho's Island (2000)]. At this date (1590) the work was still in progress, consistent with Tycho speaking of Magini's future possession of it ("ubi illum adeptus fueris").
  • Independent corroboration. This exact Latin postscript text is quoted, essentially verbatim, in Antonio Favaro's edition of Tycho's correspondence with Magini, Carteggio inedito di Ticone Brahe... con Giovanni Antonio Magini (Bologna, 1886) — the recovered text therefore matches an independently published nineteenth-century critical transcription rather than resting on the transcription alone [Favaro, Carteggio inedito (Bologna, 1886)].
