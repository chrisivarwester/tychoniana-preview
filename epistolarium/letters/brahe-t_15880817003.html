DOCTRINA ET VIRTVTE ORNATISSIMO VIRO D. CHRISTOPHORO ROTHMANNO, ILLVSTRISSIMI PRINCIPIS WILHELMI LANDTGRAVII HASSIÆ, ETC. MATHEMATICO EXIMIO, AMICO SUO MULTUM DILECTO. S. D.

ACVR serius Illustriss. Principis et tuis respondeam literis, Eruditissime exoptatissimeque mi ROTHMANNE, excusationem tibi perscripsi in ijs quas interea per GELLIVM SASCERIDEM meum ad te dedi; nec enim dubito eas vna cum libro nostro secundo, de Ætherei Mundi recentioribus Phænomenis, qui ijsdem adiunctus erat, dudum tibi esse traditas. Librum vero illum te etiam interim diligenter euoluisse existimo, vt itaque tuam mihi de ijs, quæ continet, sententiam aperias, et ingenue mentem tuam de his exponas, plurimum desidero. Ego vicissim tibi, quid veritati consentaneum videatur, tum in his, tum etiam alijs, libenter et liberaliter, quantum in me est, declarare non intermittam. Nunc itaque tuis literis erudita in Astronomicis rerum cognitione luculenter refertis, vt paulo etiam vberius respondeam, non diutius supersedendum duco. Percommodum vero accidit, quod posteriores tuas literas, quibus ipsemet nimiam vaporum terrestrium eleuationem, in prioribus per incuriam aliquam commissam, opportune emendas, satis mature acceperim. Alias enim hac de re te commonefacere in animum induxeram. Summi enim halitus iuxta Opticorum demonstrationem ad 12. Milliaria Germanica vix ascendunt, quæ Terræ Semidiameter (cui tu eorum remotionem antea adæquasti) plus septuagies continet. Verum hæc ab Opticorum traditionibus disconuenientia, facile inter alias occupationes per festinantiam obrepere potuit. Nunc itaque ad priores tuas literas, admissa sublimitatis vaporosæ in posterioribus emendatione, ordine respondebo. De Cometico vero negotio Anni 86. iam non plura addam, satis enim in hoc conuenimus, nec dimidij, vel integri Minuti, quoad Parallaxin, discrepantia alicuius est momenti, imo prorsus insensibilis; sic neque de Declinatione maxima vlterius verba faciam: Eam enim a vobis nunc per rectificatum Quadrantis perpendiculum, meis inuentis apprime consentientem, comprobatam esse perlibenter intelligo. De Materia Cæli et Refractionibus, quoniam hæc res nondum videtur satis inter nos decisa, operæpretium erit, vt vlterius tecum agam, tuæque hac in parte petitioni morem geram.

Materiam Sphærarum cælestium tu non aliam quam elementarem Aërem esse, et Refractiones nullas ob Diaphanorum Ætheris et Aëris diuersitatem fieri contendis, omnemque Refractionum occasionem in solos vapores Horizonti obuersantes reijcis; At ego Cælum quidem non e dura imperuiaque materia in formam membranarum pellucidarum extensa, constare, velut hactenus, etiam a præcipuis Philosophis, creditum est, vel ex ipsis Cometarum motibus edoctus sum; Aëreum tamen vel vllius elementaris naturæ particeps esse nequaquam admisero. Absit enim vt purissimæ perfectissimæque et nullis mutationibus obnoxiæ cælesti naturæ elementare, corruptibile variabile atque huic inferiori caducæ Regioni conforme aliquid assuatur; nam ne id vnquam vllus saniorum Physicorum admittere ausus est. Omnes siquidem vnanimiter Cælum e numero et natura quatuor Elementorum prorsus exemtum et præstantiorem quandam naturam præ se ferre, rectissime statuunt, ideoque illud quintam quandam essentiam a quatuor illis elementaribus prorsus diuersam longeque excellentiorem nuncupârunt. Quod igitur Physici abhorrent non admittatur, vt ipsi Mathematici tanta Cælum labe aspergant, vt ex Aëre elementari illud constare asseuerantes simplicissimæ et purissimæ adeoque perfectæ ipsius substantiæ et naturæ plurimum derogant. Et quæ, quæso, fieret Elementorum proportio, si solum Aërem a Terris vsque ad octauam Sphæram tam immensam vastitatem complere concesserimus? Sane ambo Elementa Terræ et Aquæ, nullam, quæ sensibus concipi posset, ad vnicum Aërem habebunt Symmetriam. Præterea si Aër elementaris orbibus cælestibus includeretur, consentaneum foret, e concitatissimo Syderum motu sonum ingentem cieri, qui proculdubio auditum etiam nostrum percelleret. Officeret etiam Aër perpetuitati Reuolutionis cælestium corporum, et puritati eorum longo vsu temporeque detraheret, imo quasi atterendo, constantissimam harmoniam labefactaret. Scio quidem Paracelsum totum Cælum, quartum et igneum Elementum appellare; quod si de viuifico calore Solis et Siderum intelligatur, locum fortasse mereri poterit; Nam Elementum Ignis infra Lunam esse, vt Peripatetici somniant, si nihil aliud, vel ipsa Refractionum subtilitas, quæ hac ratione per tot Diaphana maior redderetur, sufficienter refellit. Omnem itaque calorem in corporibus mixtis, quem Physici Elemento Ignis attribuunt, cælitus prouenire consentaneum est. Quod si aliter Paracelsus sentiebat, absurda est opinio. Neque etiam ignoro quosdam Theologos e Mose, proculdubio minus recte intellecto, asseuerare Cælum ex Aquea materia constare; quam tamen interpretationem Philo Iudæus non admittit, sed ipsum nullatenus ex vllis Elementis compositum contendit; videtur etiam hæc sententia omni naturali ratiocinationi plurimum aduersari. Qui vero Cælum aëreum statueret, neminem ante Iohannem Penam Gallum percepi, cuius tu sententiæ suffragari videris, et nuper etiam Iordanus Nullanus in quodam scripto de Mundo contra Peripateticos idem asserere ausus est, forte etiam quidam alij eâdem sunt imbuti opinione. Sin vero per Aërem, qui inter cælestes circuitus continetur, aliam et subtiliorem purioremque substantiam, quam est Aër hic elementaris, intelligere velitis, non vsque adeo refragabor, nisi quod nomen Elementi Aëris in tam sublimi negotio per se nimis vile et suspectum sit, si is Cælo assignatur; oportebitque hac ratione distinguere inter Aërem æthereum et elementarem, sicque nihilominus res eodem redibit, non esse videlicet eiusdem exquisite puritatis et perspicuitatis, multumque adhuc suberit dubij, quod ne hac quidem distinctione satis enodatum appareat. Verum licet nulli mortalium de ipsissima Cæli materia (si modo e materia aliqua constare proprie dici potest) certi quid proferre in promtu esse credam. Est enim Cælum abstractum quid, et immateriali simile, nostrum captum effugiens, nihilominus satis concludere licet, ipsum de nulla elementari natura participare. Cum hæc sit longe præstantius et diuinius quiddam adeoque incorruptibile et supra omnem elementarem qualitatem exaltatum e purissima, simplici et pellucida rarissimaque substantia, admiranda DEI sapientia et nobis imperscrutabili modo plasmatum. Vndecunque igitur Cæli substantia fuerit, quæ sane potius, quæ non sit, quam quæ sit, percipitur, eam ipso Aëre liquidiorem, puriorem et subtiliorem esse necesse est, vt ob id non immerito statuam Diaphanum Ætheris Diaphano Aëris perfectius puriusque esse. Licet enim non admodum sensibilis Siderum Refractio in aliquanta super Horizontem Altitudine ingeratur, neque tanta, quantam veteres optici, qui Cælum e duro et adamantino Diaphano constare opinabantur, suspicati sunt, tamen id non propterea accidit, quia nihil aliud sit inter octauam Sphæram et Terram quam Aër elementaris. Nam, vt in prioribus etiam literis testatus sum, non est admodum magna differentia inter Ætheris et Aëris transparentiam, neque sicut Aëris et Aquæ Diaphana sensibiliter discrepant, sed cum ambo tenuissima sint et liquidissima, plurimum habent affinitatis, imo aliqualis ea quæ intercedit diuersitas, successiue imminuitur, prout Aër a Terris sublimior et Lunæ circuitibus vicinior redditur; illic enim cum Æthere quasi in vnum coalescit, cælestemque naturam successiue imbuit, atque propterea Refractionem sensibilem non causatur. Non enim datur certus aliquis locus in definita a Terra distantia, in quo Refractio illa procreetur, sed paulatim Diaphanum aëreum in æthereum transformatur, ideoque non contingit aliqua repugnantia, qualem inter Aërem et Aquam fieri videmus, sed potius identitas quædam conciliatur, quæ Refractioni admodum perceptibili, præsertim in sublimiori Stellarum situ, quando magis directe earum Lumen ad nos vibratur, occasionem præcludit. Proptereaque ego minimam partem Refractionum diuersitati Diaphanorum Ætheris et Aëris attribuo, sed potissimam earum causam vapores, Terræ obuersantes esse statuo, ita vt duplici ratione Refractiones contingant, partim ob vaporosos halitus, Aërem Terris propinquum condensantes. Atque illæ sunt maximæ admodumque perceptibiles, ideoque earum animaduersio plurimis in propatulo est: Partim vero ob Diaphanorum Cæli et Aëris aliqualem varietatem. Hæc autem, quia in ipso etiam Horizonte non admodum magna est, neque etiam vltra 30. Gradum Altitudinis, in Stellis præsertim, percipitur, minus hactenus est considerata, ideoque de ea potissimum verba feci, alteram aliâs satis perspectam præsupponens. Neque etiam negârim hanc ipsam, de qua loquor, vsque ad verticem quodammodo prorogari, sed vltra 30. Gradum Eleuationis ita successiue magis atque magis attenuari iudico, vt nullatenus deinde in sensus cadat, ob Siderum radios directiores, neque adeo magnam aut repugnantem Diaphanorum diuersitatem.

Asseris ipsemet in tuo mihi transmisso scripto de Cometa vltimo, Refractiones non vltra illud spatium, quo vapores ascendentes multiplicari solent, perdurare, hocque ad summum esse 20. Partium, et ob id nullam aliam quam per solos vapores fieri Refractionem; at cum ego Refractiones in Sole, circa 30. Gradum perceptibiles esse animaduertissem, inferebam eas aliunde etiam, quam per vapores procreari, ideoque ex diuersitate Diaphanorum Ætheris et Aëris. Nec video quomodo hæc meam expugnent sententiam, et pro te faciant; neque enim dixi in 30. Gradu Altitudinis prorsus nullam fieri Refractionem, imo dum accuratius meas Obseruationes examino, adhibita Parallaxeos differentia, inuenio in Sole Refractionem fieri iuxta Altitudinem 30. Partium fere 1½. Min. quod vix per vapores solos excusari poterit, siquidem ipsorum impedimentum subito circa Horizontem alteretur, inque tanta Altitudine perexiguum sit, vt postea e tua ipsius demonstratione patebit. Ast illud, quod fit ratione Diaphanorum, non quidem ita magnum est, sed diutius durat, quamuis in Stellis fixis hactenus animaduertere non potuerim, Refractionem sensibilem tam alte, atque in Sole insinuari, vt in antecedentibus literis etiam indicaui, idque, vt existimo, ob luminis et quantitatis, atque etiam distantiæ maximam differentiam. Neque si Refractio circa Horizontem esset 3. Minut. statim consequitur, vt ob id ea in Altitudine 45. Graduum fieret 1½. Minut. vt tu vis. Non enim proportionabiliter et æqualiter decrescunt Refractionum quantitates, sed in decliuioribus Altitudinibus, multo plus illis decedit quam in sublimioribus, idque proculdubio vtraque de causa, tam ratione vaporum Terræ obuolitantium, quam etiam diuersitatis Diaphanorum Cæli et Aëris, principaliter tamen et maxime ob ipsos halitus terrestres, quando per obliquum Stellarum lumen recipiunt; id enim ipsa testatur experientia, subito circa Horizontem alterari Refractiones, in sublimiori vero situ sensim et tardius id fieri. Sic in Sole animaduerti a primo in secundum Gradum Altitudinis variari Refractionem sex Scrupulis primis, a secundo vero in tertium saltem quaternis, in sublimiori vero situ vltra 20. Gradum Altitudinis mutatio illa vix est dimidij Minuti, et licet hoc per vapores solos non inconuenienter excusari poterit, tamen aliquid etiam ex Diaphanorum discrepantia clam sibi habet admixtum. Quod autem non vsque ad verticem sensibiliter durare appareant, per Diaphanorum discrimen obortæ Refractiones, in causa est, quod non admodum magna sit eorundem discrepantia, et sensim, vti prius dixi, sibi inuicem assimilentur, adeo vt lumen Stellarum, quando sublimiores fuerint, facile irrefracte vtraque penetrare queat, siquidem tunc non adeo oblique, sed quasi per directum lumen ad nos vibrent, nam si concedi potest id quod tu infers, Refractiones non tam fieri ob vapores istos, quam propter eorum circa Horizontem multiplicationem, radios enim Stellarum et Solis vincere illam crassitiem, nec ob identitatem Diaphanorum in ea punctum aliquod Refractionis in paulo sublimiori situ inuenire, certe ratione Diaphanorum Ætheris et Aëris idem multo potius admittendum venit, vt in sublimiori situ, sine perceptibili Refractione Stellæ nobis illucescant: Longe enim maior hîc est Diaphanorum identitas et similitudo, quia non tantum inter se discrepare conuenit Æthera et Aërem superiorem, quantum Aërem purum et vaporosum.

Patet itaque quod vapores sint quidem sexduplo proxime densiores circa Horizontem, quam in Altitudine 30. partium; neque tamen ob id necessario consequitur, prorsus nullam, ratione vaporum in tali Altitudine fieri oportere Refractionem, siquidem interstitium vaporum 23. milliaria sua capacitate adæquans, non videtur prorsus insufficiens ad refringendum aliquantulum Stellarum radios, aut si id concedi poterit, quanto magis in diuersitate Diaphanorum Ætheris et Aëris non idem admittatur, ex quo hæc in tali Altitudine multo adhuc minorem ob causas supra iudicatas ingerat differentiam. Præterea etiamsi concedatur nullam prorsus fieri ex occasione diuersitatis Aëris et Ætheris Refractionem, sed omnem e solis vaporibus originem trahere, tamen non necessario ob id sequeretur, Cælum ipsum ex Aëre elementari et animali, vti tibi placet, constare; potest enim tanta esse Diaphanorum similitudo et vicinitas, mutuaque connexio, vt Refractionem nullam, quæ a nobis percipiatur, pariat, lumine Stellarum fortiter vtrumque Diaphanum penetrante. Neque enim statuimus Cælum e dura et imperuia perspicuitate constare, vt Alhasen et Vitellio, qui maximam ob id accidere Refractionem frustra crediderunt, sed liquidissimum esse ipsum Æthera et rarissimum, nullisque realibus orbibus contextum, quemadmodum tu etiam mecum optime sentis, et licet ipso Aëre perfectius quiddam sit, tamen circa Reuolutiones, præsertim lunares, Aër hic elementaris adeo exaltatur, vt quasi in Ætheream transmigret substantiam, ideoque Diaphanorum imperceptibilis efficitur diuersitas, vt prius etiam indicatum est. Quo vero Aër Terræ magis appropinquat, eo minus de Ætherea natura participat, plusque habet elementaris qualitatis, et nihilominus admodum subtilis et rarus est, vt lumen Stellarum facile et irrefracte transmittat, nisi Terræ vicinior fuerit, vbi per vapores condensatur, turbidiorque redditur. Sed de Refractionibus vnde eueniant, an saltem intermediantibus vaporibus, vt tibi videtur, an vero non solum ab his, sed etiam aliquatenus ex Diaphanorum Ætheris et Aëris discrepantia, mixta quadam ratione, vt mihi placet, frustra tantopere disputamus, siquidem de motu cælestium corporum restituendo, ipsaque Astronomia a mendis repurganda instaurandaque principalis agitur quæstio, et licet huic intentioni Refractionum cognitio necessarias adferat suppetias, quod sine harum præcautione, ipsissima Stellarum loca cælitus deduci nequeant, tamen quia sufficit Astronomum scire quam alte Refractionum impedimenta ascendant, et quanta in certis Altitudinibus fuerint, vbi præsertim Aëris similis et inconfusa affulserit serenitas, idcirco vnde Refractiones hæ proueniant, an duplici vel etiam vnica de causa, non multum interest subtiliter et ambiguose disceptare: Quemadmodum ipsa etiam de materia Cæli quæstio, non proprie ad Astronomi decisionem pertinet; Is enim non quid sit Cælum, et vnde ipsius splendida corpora constent, sed saltem quomodo omnia hæc moueantur, ex Obseruationibus certis peruestigare laborat. Alterum enim Theologis et Physicis discutiendum relinquit, apud quos etiam hactenus idipsum non satis explanatum permansit. Tanta enim est, vt opera vniuersa Creatoris, ne quidem terrestria et sensibus externis obuia, nobisque adeo vt palpentur propinqua, penitus haurire queat: nedum vt sublimem illam et remotissimam Ætheris naturam et substantiam satis peruestigare explicareque sustineat. De Cæli itaque materia, quæ est imperscrutabilis, non est vt anxie disceptemus, nisi quod id necessario statuendum censeam, eam qualiscunque sit, nihil cum elementari natura commune habere, ideoque nequaquam esse aëream, sed diuiniorem et longe perfectiorem in se continere naturam, quam quæ vllis elementaribus rebus assimilanda merito veniat. Sufficit autem in principaliori scopo inter nos conuenire, Cælum videlicet nullis realibus et duris atque imperuijs orbibus, quibus Sidera affixa circumgyrantur, esse contextum, sed e liquidissima, rarissima, et subtilissima substantia constare, quæ septem Planetarum cursus liberos, quocunque fert naturalis impetus et congenita scientia, absque vlla retardatione, admittat. Id inquam a Veteribus et maxima etiam parte Recentiorum hactenus non perspectum, neque pro indubitato concessum, satis est ad Astronomiæ redintegrationem pro rato compertoque admittere: Sic enim Hypothesibus conuenientius ordinandis, quemadmodum etiam a nobis iampridem factitatum est, compendiosior et planior sternetur via. Quare, vt dixi, circa Cæli materiam, et Refractionum occasionem, pluribus vt occupemur, non est operæpretium; saltem si quid in Refractionibus per certam experientiam vlterius innotuerit, vt inuicem communicemus, ad præcisionem Astronomicam constituendam conducere videtur.

Vnicum itaque solummodo de his addam, postea ad alia transiturus. Refractiones in Sole non inueniuntur hîc circa Horizontem multopere maiores quam apud vos; siquidem et ego iuxta Altitudinem trium Graduum deprehendi in Solstitio æstiuo, non contingere Refractionem maiorem 12. Scrupulis, nam quod me eam tunc 28. esse Scrupulorum, asserere existimas, videris non satis attendisse, quod de Sole in ipso occasu, sese abscondente locutus sim, quando videlicet eius dimidia pars solummodo supra Horizontem cernitur, quod in hac Insula satis animaduertitur, præsertim in loco meæ habitationis, qui adeo altus est, vt vndique liberum præbeat Finitorem visibilem. Plurimum etenim variantur circa ipsum Occasum Refractiones, adeo, vt trium Graduum differentia per quartam circiter vnius partem, illas alteret. Sic etiam in Altitudine vestra Brumali 15. partium, quando apud vos in Meridiano efficitur Refractio 7⅓. Minut. nobis in consimili Altitudine, Sole iuxta 5. Gradum ♒ vel ♏, versante, Refractionem non maiorem vnquam inuenire potui Scrupulis 7½. quod cum vestra satis concordat, cum tu tamen existimes Refractiones hîc sensibiliter maiores, quam illic insinuari. Et in Solstitio hyberno, quando Sol in Meridie nobis tantummodo Grad. 10⅔. attollitur, Refractio euadit 9½. saltem Scrupulorum, adhibita in omnibus Parallaxeos ratione. Vides itaque Refractiones Solares insensibiliter maiores hîc, quam apud vos, modo satis diligenter in consimili Altitudine expendantur, cælitus apparere. Suspicor itaque, in Stellis Fixis circa Refractionis inquisitionem, tuis inuentis aliquid subesse vitij, in eo, quod aliquantulum nostris minus habeant, vel forte, quia mare vastum e loco meæ Obseruationis versus Boream patens, Refractiones in Septentrionali Horizontis parte ob vapores inde continue magis exhalantes, paululum auctiores quam alibi efficiat, in cæteris Horizontis locis non video sensibilem a tua annotatione differentiam, ideoque nihil fere importat, quod iuxta Veterum sententiam Aër in his borealibus locis densior crassiorque statuitur, nam 4½. Gradus, quibus in Latitudine differimus, parum Aëris naturam immutant; quin et Augustæ Vindelicorum sub Eleuatione 48⅓. aliquot Hyemes expertus sum multo frigidiores ijs, quæ apud nos communiter fiunt, et Æstates minus etiam calidas. Quia vero per Crepusculorum obseruationem discerni posse existimas, vtrum Aëris crassi superficies altius hîc attollatur, quam in vestro Horizonte: Scias me diligenter animaduertisse, iuxta tempora Æquinoctialia, Crepusculum matutinum hîc incipere Sole saltem Grad. 16⅓. infra Horizontem depresso, adeo, vt arcus Crepusculi tunc sit tantummodo 25. præcise Graduum, quem tamen Nonniana et communiter recepta ratio Partium proxime 28. efficeret: Si quando idem illic diligenter exploraueris, non dubito quin inuenies arcum Crepusculi, nihilo apud nos maiorem esse, quam Cassellis, et ob id nostri Horizontis vapores non altius ascendere, quam vobis, vnde etiam consequitur Aërem inferiorem non esse sensibiliter crassiorem in hac boreali plaga, quam in locis paulo meridionalioribus. Ex his etiam omnibus satis colligitur, ea, quæ circa vaporum naturam, et hinc solummodo deductam Refractionum rationem, adeoque de totius Cæli postmodum ex his Aërea et Elementari pronuntiata materia a te in medium proferuntur, non ita certis niti fundamentis, atque ingeniose et luculenter persuadere conaris. His itaque relictis ad cætera pergamus.

Latitudinum in Fixis Stellis variationem, mutationi Declinationis Eclipticæ obnoxiam esse, satis inter nos conuenit, et abunde e quam plurimis Stellis, in posterioribus meis literis comprobatum est, nec solius Syrij dissentanea latitudo huic assertioni quidpiam derogat, vt tute etiam mihi recte suffragaris, causasque lapsûs Veterum in Declinatione et Latitudine eius annotanda, apprime verisimiles adducis, quare hac de re nihil attinet nunc plura dicere. Deliquium Lunæ Anni 84. equidem satis sciebam minus diligenter a Witichio obseruatum, neque ipsius proprijs annotationibus correspondere, siquidem eius in his tractandis minus peritam exercitationem, dudum cognitam habebam. To the most accomplished man in learning and virtue, Master Christoph Rothmann, distinguished mathematician of the Most Illustrious Prince Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, etc., his much-beloved friend — heartiest greeting.

That I should be answering rather late the letters of the Most Illustrious Prince and your own, my most learned and most longed-for Rothmann, I set out my excuse in those [letters] which in the meantime I sent to you by my [man] Gellius Sascerides; for I do not doubt that they, together with our second book, On the More Recent Phenomena of the Aethereal World, which was joined to them, were long since delivered to you. And I reckon that you have also in the meantime diligently worked through that book — so that you may disclose to me your opinion of the matters it contains, and frankly set out your mind upon these things, I very greatly desire. I in turn will not fail to declare to you, freely and openly, so far as in me lies, what seems consonant with the truth, both in these matters and in others. So now, since your letters are richly filled with learned knowledge of astronomical things, I judge it should be put off no longer that I answer somewhat more amply. And it fell out very conveniently that I received in good enough time your later letter, by which you yourself opportunely correct the excessive elevation of terrestrial vapours that had been committed in the earlier [letter] through some oversight. For otherwise I had resolved to remind you of this matter. For the highest exhalations, by the optical writers' demonstration, scarcely rise to 12 German miles, of which the Earth's semidiameter (to which you previously made their distance equal) contains more than seventy times over. But this discrepancy from the optical writers' teachings could easily have crept in through haste amid other occupations. So now I shall answer your earlier letter in order, the emendation of the vaporous elevation in the later one being granted. About the cometary business of the year [15]86 I shall add nothing more now, for we agree in this sufficiently, nor is a discrepancy of half a minute, or a whole one, as regards parallax, of any moment — indeed it is wholly imperceptible; nor likewise shall I say anything further about the greatest declination [of the ecliptic], for I learn most gladly that it has now been confirmed by you, by the rectified plumb-line of your quadrant, in close agreement with my own findings. Concerning the Matter of the Heaven and refractions, since this matter does not yet seem sufficiently settled between us, it will be worth the effort that I treat it further with you and comply with your request on this head.

You contend that the matter of the celestial spheres is none other than elemental air, and that no refractions arise on account of the difference of the transparency [diaphana] of aether and air, and you throw back the whole occasion of refractions onto the vapours alone that lie facing the horizon. But I — that the heaven is not composed of hard and impervious matter stretched into the form of transparent membranes, as has hitherto been believed even by the foremost philosophers — of this I have been taught even by the motions of comets themselves; yet that it is airy, or partakes of any elemental nature, I shall by no means admit. For far be it that anything elemental, corruptible, variable, and conformable to this lower perishable region should be stitched onto the most pure and most perfect celestial nature, which is subject to no changes; for not even has any of the sounder natural philosophers ever dared to admit that. For all unanimously, and most rightly, hold that the heaven is utterly exempt from the number and nature of the four elements and bears forth a certain more excellent nature, and therefore they have named it a certain fifth essence [quintessence], wholly different from those four elemental ones and far more excellent. Let it not then be admitted, what the natural philosophers abhor — that the mathematicians themselves should sprinkle the heaven with so great a blemish, so that, asserting it to be composed of elemental air, they greatly derogate from its most simple and most pure and so most perfect substance and nature. And what, I ask, would be the proportion of the elements, if we should grant air alone to fill so immense a vastness from the Earth all the way to the eighth sphere? Surely the two elements of earth and water will bear no proportion that could be conceived by the senses to the single [element of] air. Besides, if elemental air were enclosed within the celestial orbs, it would be consistent that a huge sound should be stirred up by the most rapid motion of the stars, which would doubtless strike even our hearing. Air would also impede the perpetuity of the revolution of the celestial bodies, and would detract from their purity over long use and time — indeed, as if by wearing them down, it would shake their most constant harmony. I know, indeed, that Paracelsus calls the whole heaven a fourth and fiery element; and if this be understood of the life-giving heat of the Sun and stars, it may perhaps deserve a place. For that the element of fire is below the Moon, as the Peripatetics dream, the very subtlety of refractions — which by this reasoning would be rendered greater [in passing] through so many transparent bodies — if nothing else, sufficiently refutes. And so it is consistent that all the heat in mixed bodies, which the natural philosophers attribute to the element of fire, comes from on high [from the heaven]. But if Paracelsus thought otherwise, the opinion is absurd. Nor am I unaware that certain theologians, [arguing] from Moses (doubtless less rightly understood), assert that the heaven is composed of watery matter — an interpretation which, however, Philo the Jew does not admit, but contends that it [the heaven] is in no way composed of any elements; and this opinion too seems much to oppose all natural reasoning. But of those who would hold the heaven to be airy, I have learned of no one before Jean Pena the Frenchman, whose opinion you seem to favour; and recently too Giordano of Nola has dared to assert the same in a certain writing On the World, against the Peripatetics — and perhaps some others as well are imbued with the same opinion. But if by "air," which is contained among the celestial circuits, you should wish to understand another and subtler and purer substance than this elemental air, I shall not so far oppose it — except that the name "element of air" in so sublime a business is in itself too cheap and suspect, if it be assigned to the heaven; and in this way one will have to distinguish between aethereal and elemental air, and so nonetheless the matter will come round to the same point — namely, that it is not of the same exquisite purity and transparency, and there will still remain much doubt, which would not appear sufficiently unravelled even by this distinction. But although I believe that nothing certain about the very matter of the heaven (if indeed it can properly be said to consist of any matter at all) is ready to hand for any mortal. For the heaven is something abstract, and like to the immaterial, escaping our grasp; nonetheless one may sufficiently conclude that it partakes of no elemental nature. Since this is something far more excellent and more divine, and so incorruptible and exalted above every elemental quality, fashioned out of a most pure, simple, and transparent and most rarefied substance, by the admirable wisdom of God and in a manner inscrutable to us. From whatever source, then, the substance of the heaven may be — which indeed is perceived rather as what it is not than as what it is — it must of necessity be more fluid, purer, and subtler than air itself; so that on this account I not undeservedly hold the transparency [diaphanum] of aether to be more perfect and purer than the transparency of air. For although a not very perceptible refraction of the stars is brought in at some altitude above the horizon, and not so great as the ancient optical writers — who supposed the heaven to consist of a hard and adamantine transparent body — suspected, yet this does not happen for the reason that there is nothing else between the eighth sphere and the Earth than elemental air. For, as I attested also in my earlier letter, there is no very great difference between the transparency of aether and of air, nor do the transparent bodies of air and water differ perceptibly; but since both [aether and air] are most tenuous and most fluid, they have very much affinity — indeed, what diversity there is between them is gradually diminished as the air is rendered loftier from the Earth and nearer to the Moon's circuits; for there it coalesces, as it were, into one with the aether, and gradually takes on the celestial nature, and on that account does not cause a perceptible refraction. For there is no fixed place at a definite distance from the Earth in which that refraction is generated, but the airy transparent body is gradually transformed into the aethereal, and therefore no contrariety occurs of the kind we see arising between air and water, but rather a certain identity is brought about, which forecloses the occasion for any very perceptible refraction — especially in the loftier position of the stars, when their light is darted more directly to us. And on that account I attribute the smallest part of refractions to the diversity of the transparency of aether and air, but I hold their chief cause to be the vapours that lie facing the Earth, so that refractions occur in a twofold way: partly on account of the vaporous exhalations that condense the air near the Earth — and these are the greatest and most perceptible, and therefore the noticing of them lies open to very many; but partly on account of some variety of the transparent bodies of the heaven and of air. This latter, however — because even at the horizon itself it is not very great, nor is it perceived (especially in the stars) beyond the 30th degree of altitude — has hitherto been less considered, and therefore it is chiefly of it that I have spoken, presupposing the other as elsewhere sufficiently understood. Nor would I deny that this very one of which I speak is in some measure prolonged right up to the zenith, but beyond the 30th degree of elevation I judge it to be so gradually more and more attenuated that thereafter it in no way falls upon the senses, on account of the more direct rays of the stars, and the not-so-great or contrary diversity of the transparent bodies.

You yourself assert, in your writing on the last comet which you sent me, that refractions do not endure beyond that space within which the ascending vapours are wont to be multiplied, and that this is at most [up to] 20 degrees, and that on this account no refraction arises other than through the vapours alone. But since I had observed refractions in the Sun to be perceptible around the 30th degree, I inferred that they are generated also from elsewhere than through the vapours — namely, from the diversity of the transparent bodies of aether and air. Nor do I see how these [observations] overthrow my opinion and make for your side; for I did not say that no refraction at all occurs at the 30th degree of altitude — indeed, when I examine my observations more accurately, with the difference of parallax applied, I find that in the Sun a refraction of nearly 1½ minutes occurs at an altitude of 30 degrees — which can scarcely be excused by the vapours alone, since their impediment is suddenly altered around the horizon and at so great an altitude is exceedingly small, as will afterward be evident from your own demonstration. But that [refraction] which arises by reason of the transparent bodies is not indeed so great, but lasts longer — although in the fixed stars I have not hitherto been able to observe a perceptible refraction insinuating itself so high as in the Sun, as I indicated also in the preceding letter; and this, as I reckon, on account of the very great difference of light and of magnitude, and also of distance. Nor, if the refraction around the horizon were 3 minutes, does it at once follow that on that account it would be 1½ minutes at an altitude of 45 degrees, as you would have it. For the quantities of refractions do not decrease proportionally and equally, but at the lower altitudes much more is lost from them than at the higher; and this doubtless from both causes, both by reason of the vapours flitting about the Earth, and also of the diversity of the transparent bodies of the heaven and of air — yet chiefly and most of all on account of the terrestrial exhalations themselves, when they receive the light of the stars obliquely; for experience itself attests this, that refractions are suddenly altered around the horizon, but in the loftier position this happens gradually and more slowly. Thus in the Sun I have observed the refraction to vary by six first-minutes [arc-minutes] from the first to the second degree of altitude, but from the second to the third by at least four; while in the loftier position, beyond the 20th degree of altitude, that change is scarcely half a minute — and although this can be not inconveniently excused by the vapours alone, yet something also has secretly got mingled into it from the discrepancy of the transparent bodies. But that the refractions arisen through the distinction of the transparent bodies do not appear to last perceptibly right up to the zenith, the cause is that the discrepancy of these [bodies] is not very great, and that gradually, as I said before, they are assimilated to one another — so much so that the light of the stars, when they are loftier, can easily penetrate both without refraction, since then they dart it to us not so obliquely but as it were by a direct light. For if what you infer can be granted — that refractions arise not so much on account of those vapours as on account of their multiplication around the horizon, since the rays of the stars and of the Sun overcome that thickness, and find no point of refraction in it at a slightly loftier position because of the identity of the transparent bodies — then surely, by reason of the transparency of aether and of air, the same is much rather to be admitted: that in the loftier position the stars shine forth to us without perceptible refraction. For far greater here is the identity and likeness of the transparent bodies, since the aether and the upper air do not differ among themselves as much as pure air and vaporous [air] do.

And since the angle AEX is right and EA is a given side, there comes out, by trigonometric operation, the side XE [as] 9 parts 55 minutes — of which, namely, EA is 60. And so great will be the density of the vapours next to the horizon, which will amount to 142 German miles, inasmuch as EA, the Earth's semidiameter, is 860 of the same [miles], very nearly. But in the other triangle AEO, from the two known sides AO (likewise 60 parts 48 minutes 50 seconds) and EA (as before, 60), and the included angle OEA of 120 parts — namely the angle OEX of 30 parts being added to the right angle XEA — there will come out also the third side OE, [of] nearly 1 part 36 minutes, measuring the extent of the vapours at an altitude of 30 degrees; and if the conversion be made into miles as before, 23 German [miles] will leap forth. It is therefore plain that the vapours are indeed very nearly six times denser around the horizon than at an altitude of 30 degrees; nor yet does it on this account necessarily follow that absolutely no refraction need occur by reason of the vapours at such an altitude, since the interval of vapours, amounting in its capacity to 23 miles, does not seem altogether insufficient to refract the rays of the stars a little. Or if that can be granted, how much more is the same not to be admitted in the diversity of the transparency of aether and air — from which this [diversity], at such an altitude, brings in a still much smaller difference, for the reasons judged above. Moreover, even if it be granted that no refraction at all arises on the occasion of the diversity of air and aether, but that the whole draws its origin from the vapours alone, yet it would not necessarily follow on that account that the heaven itself is composed of elemental and animal[ate] air, as is your pleasure; for the likeness and nearness of the transparent bodies, and their mutual connection, can be so great that it begets no refraction such as is perceived by us, the light of the stars strongly penetrating each transparent body. For we do not hold the heaven to consist of a hard and impervious transparency, as Alhazen and Witelo [held], who on that account vainly believed that the greatest refraction occurs — but that the aether itself is most fluid and most rarefied, and woven of no real orbs, just as you too most rightly agree with me; and although it is something more perfect than air itself, yet around the revolutions, especially the lunar ones, this elemental air is so exalted that it passes over, as it were, into aethereal substance, and therefore the diversity of the transparent bodies is rendered imperceptible, as was indicated before too. But the more the air approaches the Earth, the less it partakes of the aethereal nature, and the more it has of elemental quality; and nonetheless it is very subtle and rare, so that it transmits the light of the stars easily and without refraction, unless it be nearer the Earth, where it is condensed by vapours and rendered more turbid. But as to refractions — whence they come, whether merely with the vapours as intermediaries, as it seems to you, or rather not only from these but also in some measure from the discrepancy of the transparency of aether and air, by a certain mixed reasoning, as is my pleasure — we dispute over this so greatly in vain, since the principal question being treated is about restoring the motion of the celestial bodies and about astronomy itself being purged of faults and renewed; and although the knowledge of refractions brings necessary aid to this aim — because without taking precaution of these, the very places of the stars cannot be deduced from the heaven — yet because it suffices the astronomer to know how high the impediments of refractions ascend, and how great they are at certain altitudes (especially where a uniform and unconfused serenity of air has shone clear), therefore whence these refractions come, whether from a twofold or even a single cause, it does not much matter to dispute subtly and ambiguously. Just as the very question concerning the matter of the heaven does not properly pertain to the astronomer's decision; for he labours to investigate from sure observations, not what the heaven is and of what its splendid bodies consist, but only how all these things are moved. The other he leaves to be debated by the theologians and natural philosophers, among whom indeed this very thing has hitherto remained not sufficiently explained. For so great is [the matter] that one cannot fully drain even the whole works of the Creator — not even the terrestrial ones, lying open to the external senses and so near us that they may be touched — much less is one equal to investigating and explaining sufficiently that sublime and most remote nature and substance of the aether. About the matter of the heaven, therefore, which is inscrutable, there is no reason for us to dispute anxiously, except that I judge this must necessarily be laid down: that it, whatever it may be, has nothing in common with the elemental nature, and therefore is by no means airy, but contains within itself a more divine and far more perfect nature than may deservedly be likened to any elemental things. But it suffices that we agree on the more principal aim — namely, that the heaven is woven of no real and hard and impervious orbs, by which the stars are carried around fixed [to them], but consists of a most fluid, most rarefied, and most subtle substance, which admits the free courses of the seven planets, wherever their natural impulse and inborn knowledge carries them, without any retardation. This, I say, hitherto not perceived by the ancients and even by the greatest part of the moderns, nor granted as indubitable, is enough to admit as ratified and ascertained for the reintegration of astronomy; for thus, by ordering the hypotheses more suitably — as has also for some time been done by us — a more compendious and plainer way will be paved. Wherefore, as I said, it is not worth the effort that we busy ourselves at length about the matter of the heaven and the occasion of refractions; only, if anything further in refractions becomes known through sure experience, it seems conducive, to the establishing of astronomical precision, that we communicate it to one another.

So I shall add one single thing only about these matters, and afterward pass on to others. Refractions in the Sun are not found here around the horizon much greater than with you; for I too, at an altitude of three degrees, found at the summer solstice that no refraction greater than 12 minutes occurs — for as to your reckoning that I assert it then to be 28 minutes, you seem not to have attended sufficiently that I spoke of the Sun in the very setting, as it hides itself, when namely only half of it is discerned above the horizon — which is well enough observed on this island, especially in the place of my dwelling, which is so high that it affords a free visible horizon [finitor] on every side. For refractions vary very greatly around the very setting, so much so that a difference of three degrees alters them by about a quarter of one degree. So likewise at your winter altitude of 15 degrees, when with you a refraction of 7⅓ minutes is produced on the meridian, I, at a like altitude — the Sun being near the 5th degree of ♒ Aquarius or ♏ Scorpio — was never able to find a refraction greater than 7½ minutes, which agrees well enough with yours, although you nonetheless reckon that refractions here insinuate themselves perceptibly greater than there. And at the winter solstice, when the Sun at midday rises for us only 10⅔ degrees, the refraction turns out to be at least 9½ minutes, the reckoning of parallax being applied in all cases. You see, then, that solar refractions appear in the heaven imperceptibly greater here than with you, provided they be weighed diligently enough at a like altitude. I suspect, therefore, that in the fixed stars, in the inquiry concerning refraction, there is some fault underlying your findings, in that they have somewhat less than ours; or perhaps because the vast sea lying open from the place of my observation toward the north makes the refractions in the northern part of the horizon a little greater than elsewhere, on account of the vapours continually exhaling more from there. In the rest of the places of the horizon I see no perceptible difference from your annotation, and so it matters hardly at all that, according to the opinion of the ancients, the air in these northern places is held to be denser and thicker; for the 4½ degrees by which we differ in latitude change the nature of the air little. Indeed, at Augsburg too, under an elevation of 48⅓ [degrees], I experienced several winters much colder than those that commonly occur with us, and summers also less hot. But because you reckon it can be discerned, by the observation of twilights, whether the surface of the thick air is raised higher here than in your horizon: know that I have diligently observed, near the equinoctial times, that the morning twilight here begins when the Sun is depressed at least 16⅓ degrees below the horizon — so much so that the arc of twilight is then precisely only 25 degrees, which, however, the Nonnian and commonly received reckoning would make nearly 28 degrees. If you should at some time diligently explore the same there, I do not doubt that you will find the arc of twilight to be no greater with us than at Kassel, and on that account the vapours of our horizon to ascend no higher than with you — whence it also follows that the lower air is not perceptibly thicker in this northern region than in places somewhat more southerly. From all these things, too, it is sufficiently gathered that those things which you bring forward concerning the nature of the vapours, and the account of refractions deduced from these alone, and so concerning the matter of the whole heaven, afterward pronounced from these to be airy and elemental, do not rest on foundations so sure as you ingeniously and clearly endeavour to persuade. These things, then, being left, let us proceed to the rest.

That the variation of the latitudes in the fixed stars is subject to the change of the declination of the ecliptic, is sufficiently agreed between us, and has been abundantly confirmed from very many stars in my later letter; nor does the discordant latitude of Sirius alone derogate anything from this assertion, as you too rightly support me, and you adduce most plausible causes for the ancients' slip in noting its declination and latitude — wherefore there is no need to say more about this matter now. The eclipse of the Moon of the year [15]84 I knew well enough to have been observed less diligently by Wittich, and not to correspond with his own annotations, since I had long known his less skilled practice in handling these things. In geometrical matters and in the handling of triangles and numbers he was nimbler and more successful; I did not know, when I was writing the later [letter], that he had yielded to the fates, otherwise I should have dealt with him more sparingly. The eclipse of the Moon which fell in September of the preceding year I observed here after a fashion, so that I am sure enough of its time within 2 or 3 minutes; for although here, while it lasted, a certain serenity shone for us at least through momentary intervals among the recurring clouds, yet the sky here was not so dark and rainy as with you. In the more westerly tract of lands, throughout its whole duration, a constant and clear serenity shone. For it was reported to me that in England it was very well observed from beginning to end — indeed, even in the Cimbric Chersonese (which is the more westerly part of Denmark, distant at least 30 miles from here, and a little nearer the setting [west] than your meridian), throughout the whole time during which this eclipse lasted, the sky was thoroughly serene and on every side free of clouds, as one of my students, to whom leave had then been granted to go away thither into his homeland, reported on his return here. Hence it becomes manifest on how frivolous and uncertain a foundation the meteorological predictions of the common run of astrologers rest, since in horizons so near one another the constitution of the air often differs by the whole heaven [i.e. utterly] — but this is beside the point. The eclipse of the Moon of this passing year, which fell at the beginning of March, I observed more diligently, for there was a moderate serenity for as long as it laboured in the shadow; and I found that its times exceeded the Prutenic calculation by half an hour, but anticipated the Alphonsine by 1 hour and a third [1⅓ h], and the Peurbachian by five-twelfths of an hour [a quincunx]; and that, moreover, it fell short of Maestlin's newfangled restitution, which he set at the front in his Ephemerides, by a little more than a whole hour. So it is sufficiently apparent how far the reintegration established by him in these matters is hitherto to be trusted. Thus in that eclipse which occurred in the year [15]81, on 15 July in the morning at dawn, his correction overran the observation by one hour and a third; he reports too, in the same [eclipse], setting down his rectification, that the Moon ought to have been eclipsed by three digits — namely by a quarter of its body — when it set on the Tübingen horizon, whereas in this our [horizon], which is still more easterly by half an hour, the Moon entered the Earth's shadow half [eclipsed] before it sank beneath the horizon — so that on this account it turns out consistent that with them it lay hidden, as it were, wholly in the shadow before its setting. Thus also in certain other eclipses I have several times observed a notable difference of Maestlin's restitution from the heaven itself, so that hence it is sufficiently plain that the course of both luminaries has not yet been perceived by him with that precision which he supposes — indeed, even in the rectification of the fixed stars he has hitherto greatly deviated from the mark, as is clearly evident from his little book on the comet of the year [15]80. I see that he is not destitute of suitable instruments, [but] of native talent and knowledge. Let this much be enough to have said about eclipses.

That you observe the courses of the planets less diligently than the places of the fixed stars seems something alien to this whole undertaking; for the places of the fixed stars are verified chiefly for this cause, that through them the courses and appearances of the planets may be more rightly made known. In the three more distant planets, especially Saturn and Jupiter, I still require certain acronychal observations, so that I may at some time, with the favour of divine clemency, restore their circuits exactly congruent with the heaven; in the rest of the planets there is enough means at hand for accomplishing it. Take care that hereafter you bestow faithful labour also on the observations of the wandering stars [planets], so that, a collation being at some time made, the agreement between us may be tested in these too. That your Most Illustrious Prince ponders the publication of your observations and rectifications in the places of the affixed [fixed] stars, and urges that it be done through you, I judge very praiseworthy and perpetually honourable to His Highness's name; labour, then, that we may shortly have it; nor is there reason that the difference of five minutes between us in the longitudes of all the fixed stars should move you, for it is exceedingly small, and easily earns excuse among those who understand the intricate depth of the business. But that in my own determinations of these things those five minutes by no means fall short, and that in these I have worked everything out with that precision to which our senses can attain, I have set out clearly to your Most Illustrious Prince, and I have added reasons which confirm me in my position and which persuade evidently enough that I have admitted nothing more or less than is right in these matters. Venus's diameter, to be sure, although it be largish, will yet not excuse this discrepancy between us; for when I observe the star of Venus through the slits of the earlier sight [pinnule], somewhat narrower (as ought to be done with the larger stars), its distance from the fixed stars always fell to the same minute, even when repeated several times — so that there is hence no danger at all but that the places of the fixed stars have been restored by me to the hairline [exactly], once and again, both in the morning and in the evening, nor will anything of what was written to me in the Most Illustrious Prince's letter be able in any way to derogate from our findings. Nonetheless I judge that you do rightly, in that, trusting also to your own observations and restitutions, you have decided to change nothing in these; for thus posterity will less suspect that we, by deliberate effort and by sharing counsels, ordered these longitudes of the stars by unanimous agreement. Yet I do not doubt that at some time, when through renewed instruments, by the benefit of Venus not only as Evening- but also as Morning-[star], you have recalled this business to the anvil from the opposite side and with diligent consideration, you will yourselves most clearly detect that those five minutes in your notation are in excess of the celestial measuring-line, but in mine by no means fall short; and that then, taught by your own experience, you will, I hope, of your own accord stretch out your hands to the more exact verification. For until this matter has been most diligently examined through the reciprocal observation of Venus relative to the Sun, applied to the stars, anything certain is announced in vain; for that, through Venus observed by you as Evening-star only, a plausible agreement with the earlier findings appeared, was happening rather on account of the insinuation of refraction in Venus and in the setting Sun, than because it was really so consonant with the heaven — as I have set out more fully in writing to your Most Illustrious Prince. I marvel that you affirm the motion of the Sun transmitted by me to you, near the first degrees of ♒ Aquarius, to be somewhat slower than is right, since you yourself do not deny that it elsewhere agrees most exactly, in the whole northern semicircle, and near both equinoxes, with your observations themselves attesting the same. For one ought to have remembered that in my earlier letter I indicated openly enough that the observation of the Sun's motion agrees with the heaven itself only in the northern semicircle, but in the southern, on account of the impediments of refractions, not so. And the Sun, being on the meridian with you at the beginning of ♒ Aquarius, rises only 18½ degrees, where it induces a very perceptible refraction, and necessarily appears farther on by observation than it really is — which has often become known to me too as I tried this very thing. Remove, then, the impediments of refraction in the southern semicircle, and it will be plain that our restitution in the Sun's motion corresponds there too, no less than in the northern, very closely with the celestial standard. And I have sent to your Most Illustrious Prince in addition 5 years' Ephemerides of the Sun, deduced from our calculation, so that in the following years a trial of this matter may be made with your instruments, the just-mentioned caution being applied.

You pass afterward from the celestial to the poetical — or rather to certain hieroglyphic figures of the connection of the higher and lower things, which you chanced to behold in a certain poem composed by me four years ago for pleasure's sake, [addressed] to the most noble man, equally most learned in Latin and Greek letters, Falk Gøye, my friend and kinsman, beloved by me; and you anxiously ask of me why beside the depicted Philosopher who scrutinizes celestial things there is written on this side and that LOOKING UP, I LOOK DOWN [SUSPICIENDO DESPICIO], and about the other [Philosopher], who [scrutinizes] terrestrial things, LOOKING DOWN, I LOOK UP [DESPICIENDO SUSPICIO]. That I may not, in this part too, fail your wishes altogether, take it thus. You conjectured rightly that these are hieroglyphics; for they regard not only each Astronomy — that higher celestial one, and the lower terrestrial — but also the more divine and less commonly used Theology itself, and so the knowledge of all Ethics, namely the discernment of virtues and vices. But at first sight it bears forth the physical consideration of created things, in which the higher are so connected with the lower by an admirable analogy that neither can be rightly perceived nor thoroughly searched out without the other; for, as you rightly gather, that art which we call Spagyric [chymistry] contains the investigation of all nature, and so a certain inner handling [of it], and presents a pleasant and not useless exercise of a certain terrestrial Astronomy (as I called it before). And that you may be made conscious of some part of the things you seek, it is fitting to know that there are seven planets in the heaven, just as there are seven metals in the earth, and that in man — fashioned to the idea of both, who on that account is rightly called a Microcosm — there are seven principal members; and all these are bound together with one another by so beautiful and fitting a likeness that they seem to have well-nigh equal offices and the same properties and natures. Thus the two principal luminaries in the heaven, the Sun and the Moon, are matched with the two more excellent metals, Gold and Silver, and in man with the two chief vital members, the heart and the brain. The two beneficent planets, Jupiter and Venus, take to themselves a place among the earthly metals, Tin and Copper, of the second rank after the former; and in our bodies, the liver, the workshop of the blood, and the kidneys [Renes], the ministers of generation. But the two planets which they call maleficent, Saturn and Mars, correspond to the baser metals, namely Lead and Iron, and in the bodies of men are allotted two less necessary and lowly members, the Spleen and the Gall-bladder [Fel], which are the receptacles of the overflowing black and yellow bile. The celestial Mercury, as it is by its nature indifferent [neutral] and more changeable than Proteus, so it has analogous to itself the terrestrial Mercury, or quicksilver, which likewise is transmuted into various forms and emulates the nature of all the rest of the metals, and puts on marvellous metamorphoses; and in the microcosmic body, to this among the seven principal members the Lung is rightly likened, whose office is to draw breath [Spiritus] and to revive the heart and the rest of the members by its respiration; it serves speech, too, very much — just as that celestial Mercury also is believed to preside over this [speech]. There are, besides, many things in these seven, bound together with one another by an admirable likeness, which cannot be explained in few words. So too the rest of the earth's minerals — gems, marcasites, and salts — agree by a fixed law with the various planets, and are associated chiefly with the nature of the fixed stars, which contain the powers of the planets within themselves, though more obscurely; just as herbs and vegetables too, by a certain fixed order, are assigned in terrestrial Astronomy not only to the planets but also to the fixed stars, and emulate their nature so far as in them lies. These things and many more of the kind, to understand thoroughly, common Physics does not teach; nor have the Academies — maddened and encrusted by the sophisms and rouges of the Peripatetics — either learned or [do they] teach how to look into the real, very kernel of truth lying hidden in these matters. The Pyronomic [fire-working] School must be approached, which makes manifest not the things that are plausibly discoursed of, but the things that are truly handled with the hands, seen with the eyes, and perceived by the outer and inner senses. Antiquity had some Teachers in this Gymnasium, and our own Age too has not been wholly destitute of them, although the pompous and formal Professors of the Academies either neglect them through carelessness, or repudiate them through ignorance, or even detest them out of hatred and prejudiced judgement; for the more sincere truth, not only in divine matters but also in worldly Philosophy, has both been, and hereafter will be, hated as much by the common run of men (which were to be borne) as by those who most profess it (which is to be lamented) — unless God shall at some time institute a special and marvellous Metamorphosis of the world. The authors in this more secret Philosophy are very many, among whom the chief place is held, as for the ancients, by that Hermes Trismegistus, author of the Emerald Tablet; Geber; [John of] Rupescissa; Arnaldus of Villanova; Raymond Lull; Thomas Aquinas; Roger Bacon; the Turba Philosophorum; Albertus Magnus; and in the more recent Age, Isaac Hollandus and Theophrastus Paracelsus, whom more attack than understand. But although these, and certain others besides who are writers in this more occult art, supply great assistance to learners, and the occasion of weighing and investigating the whole matter through natural causes, yet unless there be added a diligent and long-continued handling through Pyronomic [fire-working] practice, one sweats over the bare pages in vain; for most rightly does Paracelsus say that no one knows more in this Art than he has himself experienced through fire. For a silence more than Pythagorean forbids these things to be wholly entrusted to disciples other than the worthy and fit. If you are at some time with me, perhaps you will behold in these matters certain things previously desired, and not everywhere to be met with. For I cultivate this terrestrial Astronomy with no less effort and expense than that celestial one, the suitable structures being arranged for treating it duly, and with a great variety and abundance of furnaces, all of which the pen does not undertake to relate.

And I should wish all these to be published by you, for they seem likely to bring much light to these Arts. But if it should not be convenient for you that this be done shortly, [then] at least, that the manuscript and transcribed copies be communicated to me, to be inspected for a short time and soon to be restored to you in good and inviolate faith — provided this were open [permissible] for you — I would have you requested. And although the Prutenic calculation often perceptibly deviates from the heaven itself, you can nonetheless apply the Copernican hypotheses, reduced to the immobility of the Earth, to it, and publish that most excellent labour, so that learners may more rightly, and with greater pleasure and profit, fundamentally perceive the congruence of the calculation and the hypotheses; for this has not been done faithfully enough by others who have undertaken to attempt it, while they violently thrust the Ptolemaic and Alphonsine hypotheses upon the Copernican numbers, short of exact correspondence. I shall work to adapt my own restitutions in the course of all the planets to a hypothesis of my own, not so long ago devised, and I have resolved to show the agreement of the calculation with these, and so with the heaven itself, and to set it forth myself in a particular work, the divine Power favouring. But of this hypothesis you have a more general delineation in our second Volume, On the New Generations of the Aethereal World, lately transmitted to you, which the more diligently you examine, the more it will please you; for it removes all the absurdities, both mathematical and physical, that lurk in the old Ptolemaic and in the recent Copernican assumption, and satisfies the more particular appearances most aptly on every side, which neither of the others does in every respect, as we shall demonstrate elsewhere from the observations themselves. Since among other things you make mention of the Equations of days, know that I lay down certain things in these differently from the opinions of my predecessors, and that it [the equation of days] is not so great as is assumed even by Copernicus himself — having been taught not only by sure reasonings, but also by experience itself — concerning which a more convenient occasion for discoursing will be given in its place. If you dedicate your writing on the comet to the most ample and most noble Master Niels Kaas, the Chancellor, a man brilliantly deserving of our Fatherland and of all the good arts — as you write you will do — I do not doubt that it will be most welcome to him, although I have hitherto said nothing privately to him about this matter. You add afterward that you would readily grant me that all comets are generated in the aether, were it not that the observations of Regiomontanus and of Vogelin stood in the way; but I shall show, in the Epilogue of our whole Work, in what place these are to be held, clearly and by reasonings in no way doubtful, concerning this whole business. Regiomontanus's annotations do not agree with themselves, nor could the parallax of so swift a comet at the Ear of Corn [Spica] of ♍ Virgo be traced out in any way; since, then, he trusted too much in these together with the assertions of Aristotle, if he obtained through some carelessness any conjecture about some parallax that would render it sublunar, he held to it tooth and nail as the truer; for he seems to have laboured much in this, to dig out as great a [parallax] as possible. Vogelin's observations are quite erroneous, nor do they agree, in the same comet, with those which are found published by Peter Apian and Fracastoro, as I shall show more fully elsewhere. And the daily motion besides, which is set by him at least at 2⅔ degrees, seems to refuse [the claim] that this comet was so near the Earth that it produced a parallax of 35 degrees — so much so that it was not distant from the Earth's surface by [more than] one and a half semidiameters of the Earth, as the same author does not hesitate incompetently to maintain. Although — to say the very thing as it is — the swiftness and slowness of motion do not sufficiently discern the remoteness or the approach of comets, whence nothing stands in the way of that which you seem to oppose to us: namely, that the swiftness of motion in the Regiomontanian comet, which is set at 40 degrees of a great circle in one day, argues that it was sublunar, and had as great a parallax as he attributes to it. For if it was permissible for the comet of the year 1585 to be situated in the highest orb of Saturn — even by your own judgement — and nonetheless from the beginning to complete a daily motion of three degrees, when the star of Saturn itself, even at its swiftest, accomplishes at least 8 minutes in one day (so much so that the comet was then more than twenty times swifter than Saturn itself, even at its fastest): who, I ask, will not also be able to grant that that comet described by Regiomontanus was above the Moon, although by its daily motion it was about three times swifter? For since the very reality of the orbs does not belong to the heaven (on which matter we agree with one another), these secondary and adventitious stars [comets] can in any case obtain whatever motion their inborn knowledge brings, in whatever place of the heaven, higher or lower, they are at last revolved; for they are not in every respect subject to the laws of the planets and of the stars coeval with the world. So too the magnitude of certain comets does not sufficiently prove their nearness; for they can, in the heaven itself and at whatever loftiness beyond the Moon, acquire as great a magnitude as it pleased the Fashioner [Creator]. Nor is the sublunar air, which you reckon the same as the celestial, apt for the generation of such and so excellent phenomena. Concerning these things and the like I am about to set out my opinion more fully in the general conclusion of our Work On the Extraordinary Generations of the Aethereal World, intending, as I hope, to satisfy you and others in this part. About the motion or rest of the Earth or of the heaven itself, as far as concerns the daily revolution, I shall disclose elsewhere what I think.

But I should very greatly wish that at the first opportunity, leave having been obtained from your Most Illustrious Prince, you would not fail to visit me here; for thus we shall confer in person more conveniently and fully, both about these things and about others pertaining to the divine art of Astronomy and to the Philosophy hidden from the common run of the learned, and I shall bring it about that you do not repent of that journey. In the meantime, and always, fare you most happily, my most beloved Rothmann, for it is time that I now close the letter, since your letters have now, in all the things they contained, been answered copiously, according to our strength and the occasion of the time. It will be your part to give labour with all diligence, that a reply from your Most Illustrious Prince and from you be prepared for me betimes, and that this be accomplished in the meantime, while the bearer of these [letters] is away at the Fair — so that, returning to you, he may find it certainly completed and written out at your house. For neither his travelling-companions nor the conveniences of the wagons will allow him to draw out any delay there. For I should very greatly wish to obtain a reply to this my letter from you before winter, so that it may be the more opportunely permitted to write back to the coming spring Fair afterward. Do you, therefore, move every stone, lest the business be procrastinated.

Again, fare you most happily, and continue to love in return me who most love you.

Given at Uraniborg, the 17th day of August, in the year 1588. TYCHO BRAHE.
