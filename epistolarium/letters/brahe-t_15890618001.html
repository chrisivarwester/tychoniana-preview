The original

IOANNES CRAIGUS AD TYCHONEM BRAHE.

DEDI ante ad te literas, nobilissime vir, quibus tuis respondi, sed in ea festinatione scripta sunt, dum alio avocarer, ut relegere attente, quae scripseram, non potuerim. Paucis exposui, quid de quaestione a te mota sentiam, non contradicendi animo, sed veritatis et amicitiae nostrae causa, quo modo ut accipias peto. His literis simili modo dignam tractarem quaestionem, sed communis laetitia ita animum meum occupavit, ut ab ea ad eiusmodi speculationes abstrahi nequeat. Alia erit occasio; nunc quod omnium apud nos iudicijs salutare huic regno videtur, et omnium votis vehementer optatur, id ut eveniat et feliciter et statim, Deum oramus. Nihil etenim aeque in votis omnium est, quam ut clementiss. Domino nostro coniux sit, quae hac illustri Legatione petetur, nam inde optimas Reginas semper experti sumus, et nunc fore speramus: mihi quidem dubium non est, cui progenitores illustrissimae Principis partim visi, partim aliter noti sunt; sed id omnes metuimus, cum brevi temporis spacio, longo locorum intervallo tanta res peragenda sit, ne tantum bonum nobis, quam statim optamus, non contingat; sed votis aëra fatigabimus, ut hincinde ventos aspiret secundos. Et Deum orabimus, ne ulla hominum consilijs sit mora. Ille igitur, in cuius manu corda Regum sunt, ita omnia prosperet, ut et hominum consilia et Principum in hoc uno proposito affectus uniat. Illustrem Dominum Legatum comitantur ex amicis nostris aliqui, Dominus SKYNENS, iurisprudentia et dignitate apud nos praestans, et doctissimis in Germania viris olim charissimus; nobilis iuvenis MARCUS SWUNTONIUS, propinqua affinitate mihi iunctus; adolescens egregiae indolis et expectationis, THOMAS NICOLSONIUS, Philosophiae magister. Hos tua benevolentia complectaris et amicitia etiam digneris peto; praesertim apud te meo sit loco affinis meus. Incessit me quidem hoc tempore cupiditas tui visendi, sed ab aegris nobis abesse vix licet; meo loco, ut dixi, hos habeas. Vides, quomodo familiariter tecum agam, ac si non recens, sed multis annis confirmata esset nostra amicitia; at ea tamen est, ut mihi persuadeam, ut id abs te petere et expectare audeam. Ad nos si vestratium aliquos venire contigerit, sunt illi omnes eo apud nos loco, ut amicitiae non sint futuri immemores.

Ne inanes omnino hae literae essent, eam tractarem quaestionem, an hypotheses esse possint, quibus statuatur, absque orbibus Planetas moveri. Mihi aliquando in mentem veniebat, unicum esse ab octavo ad concavum Lunae orbem, qui a primo motu deficeret, in quo Planetae suos peculiares haberent orbes, quasi epicyclos quosdam, quibus omnis apparens anomalia possit explicari, sed has imaginationes alia excusserunt. Et duntaxat adhuc arridet ARISTOTELIS sententia, Planetas orbium certorum esse partes, a qua tamen nostra speculatio non abhorret. Sed tubis ad naves convocatur, iamque illustriss. Dominus Comes ingressus est, ventis afflantibus secundis. Faxit Deus, ut secundissimi sint.

Bene et feliciter vale, et CRAIGUM tui amantissimum esse existimato.
Datae 18. Iunij Edenburgi.
Tuus
IOAN. CRAIGUS,
Medicus.

POST SCRIPTA.

IAM audio, plures adhuc ex amicis nostris unâ proficisci, et inter alios M.
GUILIELMUM FULLERUM; iuvenis est
egregius, nec tua amicitia indignus. Vide, quam ego tibi notos quasi
accumulem; amici omnes mei sunt, et tuarum excellentium virtutum erunt
admiratores.

Iterum vale. Hæc quoque raptim.

IOAN. CRAIGUS, D.

English translation machine translation, Secure

John Craig to Tycho Brahe.

I sent you a letter before, most noble Sir, in which I answered yours; but it was written in such haste, while I was being called away elsewhere, that I could not read over attentively what I had written. In few words I set out what I think about the question you had raised — not in a spirit of contradiction, but for the sake of truth and of our friendship, and I beg you to take it in that way. In the present letter I would treat a question worthy [of you] in like manner, but a common joy has so taken hold of my mind that it cannot be drawn away from it to speculations of that kind. There will be another occasion; for now, that what in the judgement of all of us here seems to be of benefit to this kingdom, and is fervently desired by the prayers of all — that this may come to pass, both happily and at once, we beseech God. For nothing is so much in the prayers of all as that there should be a consort for our most clement Lord, who is to be sought through this illustrious embassy; for from that quarter we have always found the best Queens, and we hope it will be so now too. I, indeed, have no doubt of it, who have seen the forebears of the most illustrious Princess in part, and in part know them otherwise; but this we all fear — since so great a matter must be carried through in a short space of time, over a long distance of places — lest so great a good not fall to us as soon as we desire it. But we will weary the air with prayers that it breathe favourable winds on this side and that. And we will pray God that there be no delay in men's counsels. May He, then, in whose hand are the hearts of Kings, so prosper all things that He may unite both the counsels of men and the dispositions of Princes in this one purpose. The illustrious Lord Ambassador is accompanied by some of our friends: Master Skene, eminent among us in jurisprudence and in dignity, and once most dear to the most learned men in Germany; the noble young man Marcus Swunton, joined to me by close kinship; [and] a youth of outstanding character and promise, Thomas Nicolson, master of philosophy. I beg you to embrace these men with your benevolence and to deem them worthy also of your friendship; and especially let my kinsman stand with you in my place. A desire of visiting you did indeed come over me at this time, but I can hardly be away from my patients; in my place, as I said, you are to have these men. You see how familiarly I deal with you, as though our friendship were not recent but confirmed by many years; yet it is nonetheless such that I persuade myself I may dare to ask and expect this of you. If any of your countrymen should chance to come to us, they all hold such a place with us that they will not be forgetful of friendship.

Lest this letter be altogether empty, I would treat the question whether there can be hypotheses by which it is established that the Planets move without orbs. It once came into my mind that there was a single orb from the eighth [sphere] to the concave [surface] of the Moon, which would lack the first motion, in which the Planets would have their own particular orbs, like certain epicycles, by which all apparent anomaly might be explained; but other [thoughts] shook these imaginings out. And so far only the opinion of Aristotle pleases me, that the Planets are parts of fixed orbs — from which, nevertheless, our speculation does not shrink away. But the call to the ships is sounding by trumpets, and now the most illustrious Lord Count has gone aboard, with favourable winds blowing. May God grant that they be most favourable.

Fare well and happily, and reckon that Craig loves you most dearly.
Given 18 June, at Edinburgh.
Yours,
John Craig,
Physician.

POSTSCRIPT.

I hear even now that several more of our friends are setting out together, and among others Master William Fuller — he is an outstanding young man, and not unworthy of your friendship. See how I heap up acquaintances upon you, as it were; they are all my friends, and will be admirers of your excellent virtues. Farewell again. This too, in haste. John Craig, Doctor [of Medicine].

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Ioannes Craigus = John Craig (d. 1620), the Scottish physician and mathematician, a graduate of Frankfurt an der Oder who taught logic and mathematics in Germany before returning to Scotland to become physician to King James VI. He carried on a sharp astronomical correspondence with Tycho over several years, in which (as here) he disputed Tycho's positions; he signs himself Medicus, "Physician."
  • The "question you had raised" / the orbs controversy. The technical paragraph engages Tycho's claim — central to the Tychonic system and developed from the path of the 1577 comet — that the heavens are fluid and the planets move absque orbibus, "without [solid] orbs." Craig defends instead the Aristotelian view that Planetas orbium certorum esse partes ("the Planets are parts of fixed orbs"), i.e. that the planets are carried by real solid spheres. This is the running scientific subject of the Craig–Tycho exchange. Orbis is rendered "orb" by the edition's convention; epicyclos "epicycles"; anomalia "anomaly".
  • ab octavo ad concavum Lunae orbem — "an orb from the eighth [sphere] to the concave [surface] of the Moon": the eighth sphere is the sphere of the fixed stars; the concavum Lunae is the inner (concave) surface of the Moon's sphere. Craig floats, then rejects, a single great orb filling the space between, lacking the primus motus (the diurnal "first motion" of the eighth sphere), within which the planets would carry their own epicycle-like orbs. The bracketed words [sphere], [surface] are editorial supplements for an elliptical Latin construction.
  • The "common joy" / the embassy. The illustris Legatio ("illustrious embassy") and the coniux … quae hac illustri Legatione petetur ("a consort … who is to be sought through this illustrious embassy") refer to the Scottish embassy of 1589 sent to negotiate the marriage of James VI of Scotland (the clementissimus Dominus noster, "our most clement Lord") to Anne of Denmark, daughter of the late Frederik II — the illustrissima Princeps whose progenitores ("forebears") Craig says he has in part seen and in part otherwise knows. The marriage by proxy took place at Kronborg in August 1589. The departure of the embassy's ships, described in the closing lines, dates the letter precisely.
  • illustriss. Dominus Comes — "the most illustrious Lord Count": the head of the embassy, George Keith, 5th Earl Marischal, who led the marriage embassy to Denmark; named here only by title (Comes, "Count/Earl"). Earlier in the letter the same man is the Illustris Dominus Legatus, "the illustrious Lord Ambassador."
  • The named companions. Dominus SKYNENS = Master [John] Skene (c. 1543–1617), the eminent Scottish jurist (later author of De Verborum Significatione and editor of the Scots statutes), who had indeed studied and been known in Germany — Craig's doctissimis in Germania viris olim charissimus. MARCUS SWUNTONIUS = Marcus Swinton (Swunton), described as Craig's kinsman by close affinitas (marriage-kinship); Craig asks Tycho to receive him "in my place" (meo loco). THOMAS NICOLSONIUS, Philosophiae magister = Thomas Nicolson, Master of Philosophy (M.A.), a promising young man. The vernacular forms of these surnames are given as identifications; the Latinised forms are kept in the source block.
  • affinis meus — "my kinsman" (by marriage): refers back to Marcus Swinton, whom Craig has just called propinqua affinitate mihi iunctus.
  • votis aëra fatigabimus, ut … ventos aspiret secundos — "we will weary the air with prayers, that it breathe favourable winds": the subject of aspiret is left grammatically open (the air, or God); rendered with "[the sea]" rejected in favour of keeping the air as antecedent — see crux note. ventos secundos "favourable winds," picked up at the close in ventis afflantibus secundis and the wordplay secundissimi.
  • in cuius manu corda Regum sunt — "in whose hand are the hearts of Kings": a Scriptural allusion to Proverbs 21:1 (cor regis in manu Domini), kept as allusion.
  • tubis ad naves convocatur — "the call to the ships is sounding by trumpets": impersonal passive (convocatur, "there is a summoning"), rendered "the call … is sounding." The scene is the embarkation of the embassy's fleet.
  • Faxit Deus, ut secundissimi sint — "May God grant that they be most favourable": faxit is the archaic/optative form of facere (= faciat), a deliberate solemn flourish; secundissimi ("most favourable") superlative of the secundi winds, retaining the wind/fortune double sense.
  • Greek / symbols. None present.
  • Edition & witness. Edition of record as given in the source: Commercium litterarium clarorum virorum. The MS witness recorded is a handwritten copy preserved in Berlin within a copy of Tycho's astronomical writings (shelfmark not specified in the source record). Dreyer prints this letter in the Epistolae (Opera Omnia VII); the present text follows the supplied transcription.
  • Calendar. Dateline Datae 18. Iunij Edenburgi = "Given 18 June, at Edinburgh." Scotland, like Denmark, was still on the Julian calendar (Old Style) in 1589; the date is therefore O.S., consistent with the edition label 1589. 18. juni. Edinburgh. No in-letter date discrepancy.
  • Back-translation fix. Removed the bracketed "[the sea]" from the body of the aspiret clause — it contradicted the apparatus note (which keeps aër the open antecedent and inserts none); the rendering now matches.
  • What this is. The letter's actual POST SCRIPTA, appended after the sign-off implied by the published tail's own valediction and dateline — a last-minute addition written, as Craig says, "in haste" while the fleet was already boarding (the published tail describes "the most illustrious Lord Count" going aboard ship with favourable winds).
  • The sender. John Craig (d. c.1620), Scottish physician who took his medical degree at Basel in 1580 and became personal physician to James VI of Scotland; he and Tycho carried on a protracted epistolary dispute over the nature of the 1577 comet (whether supralunary or meteorological), touched on more fully elsewhere in their correspondence [Christianson et al., Tycho Brahe and Prague: Crossroads of European Science (2002), ch. "Tycho Brahe and John Craig: The Dynamic of a Dispute"; Mosley, Bearing the Heavens].
  • William Fuller. Introduced here as "an outstanding young man," one of a further group of Craig's friends travelling together at this moment (June 1589) — plausibly in the train of the embassy then crossing to Denmark in connection with James VI's marriage to Anne of Denmark. Fuller recurs in Craig's later correspondence: in his next letter to Tycho (28 February 1590), amid a request to be recommended as personal physician to the newly-wed Anne of Denmark, Craig again mentions Fuller by name [Christianson et al., Tycho Brahe and Prague (2002), p.75] — though the exact nature of Fuller's business is not spelled out in the material consulted.
  • Context. "The most illustrious Lord Count" of the published tail is almost certainly the leader of the 1589 Scottish mission to Denmark for the marriage negotiations; Craig's postscript is social lubrication, vouching for a travelling companion to secure him a welcome from Tycho.
  • Correction. Removed the spurious 'fore' from 'salutare huic regno fore videtur' per Dreyer, TBOO VII pp.191-192, which prints simply 'salutare huic regno videtur'; English adjusted from 'seems likely to be of benefit' to 'seems to be of benefit'.
