To the Most Illustrious, High-born Prince and Lord Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, Count of Katzenelnbogen, Dietz, Ziegenhain, and Nidda, my gracious Lord.

Most Illustrious, High-born Prince, gracious Lord. Together with my ever-ready offer of service, I have not been able to refrain from writing to Your Princely Grace by the present messenger, who has come up for the impending Frankfurt Fair.

First, as concerns the elk [Elendtsthiere], for the procuring of which I promised my utmost diligence: I have them on order in several places in Norway. But since sailing has not yet begun, on account of the winter, I cannot so soon obtain any [definite] word about it. As soon, however, as one or more of such animals shall be made over to me — which I expect for the spring — I shall let Your Princely Grace know, so that they may be fetched away from here by Your Princely Grace's own dispatched messenger (in the event that no opportunity falls to me myself to accomplish the same), one who knows the paths and the ways.

I have addressed to Your Princely Grace's Mathematician Rothmann, for every event [in omnem eventum], in case he should have arrived back with Your Princely Grace, a letter enclosed herewith. But should he not yet have returned and installed himself [there], then Your Princely Grace can have the letter opened and read out by another, and take note of its contents therefrom. The chief matter in that same letter is: that if Rothmann should have noted down most diligently, by Your Princely Grace's instruments [Instrumenta], the eclipse of the ☽ Moon that lately occurred at the end of December [in fine Decembris], in all its moments and other requisites [per omnia sua momenta et alia Requisita], those observations [Observationes] might be communicated to me. For although it was here at that same time not at all clear [enough] that one could have observed the said eclipse, yet I have so often and so long inquired into the motion of the ☽ Moon [motum ☾ae] by manifold observations, and tracked it, that I am confident it will agree at all times with my numbers [numeris] in a just concordance; and that thus, by a collation of the observation made there with the computation from my own Tables in the Moon's motion [per collationem observationis istic factae, cum numeratione e propriis Tabulis in motu ☾ae], I may infer from it accurately enough the difference of meridians [Differentiam Meridianorum]. But in case Rothmann should not have been on the spot with Your Princely Grace at that time, I rather suppose that Your Princely Grace's clockmaker [Automatopaeus], or someone else, will not have neglected this, supposing it was otherwise clear there, so that it might nevertheless be communicated to me. And if there are still other eclipses on hand that have been diligently observed in various years — be it long ago or lately elapsed, it is all one — I would also gladly be made a sharer of their notation, [namely] how and from which stars they were taken, for the aforesaid reason.

Without doubt Maestlin [Mesthlinus] will have several of the above-mentioned [kind of] observation in store, carefully obtained by altitudes of the stars or by whatever other method [per altitudines stellarum vel quacunque alia ratione sedulo deprehensas]. If, therefore, Your Princely Grace would be willing, without trouble, to make the gracious provision of having him approached on this matter, he would not refuse them to Your Princely Grace, so that they might then come to me too. For I am not myself acquainted with him. I desire only the bare observations [nudas observationes], just as they were marked down, and I shall well know thereafter how to extract [extricirn] the times [tempora] from them by my own reasonings [ratiociniis]. If, too, that same Maestlin should have diligently noted down ♄ Saturn [Saturnum] about the years [15]76, 77, and 78, and likewise ♂ Mars [Martem] in the years [15]76 and 78, in the acronychal position [in situ acronychio], several times, I would gladly have such observations fall to me as well, so that I might collate [collationirn] them with my own (which I obtained about the same times, yet not so many and so complete as I would gladly have). I have otherwise a good part of the observations about the Sun's opposition [Observationum circa Solis oppositum] in store, almost throughout the whole circuit of the eccentric [ferme per totum Eccentrici ambitum]; nevertheless, for a particular reason, I would gladly have examined their acronychal appearances [Apparentias acronychas] in these two planets about the said years, and compared [conferirt] them with the others that I have brought forward for myself. He may have noted them down however he will — whether by the intervening cross-staff, by distances from certain fixed [stars], or by straight lines along the extension of a thread, by observation made crosswise in that customary manner [sive intermediante Radio per distantias a certis Fixis, sive per lineas rectas iuxta fili extensionem, modo illi usitato decussatim facta animadversione] — yet I shall all the same know how to set myself right from them, as far as is possible, provided only that the names of the stars and the very method of observation [Nomina stellarum, et ipsa observationis ratio] are indicated to me.

I have also written into Italy and Spain [Italiam vnd Hispaniam], as well as into Poland [Poloniam], that I may obtain certain lunar eclipses of past years [Eclipses Lunares praeteritorum annorum] (those, that is, that were somewhere accurately taken from the heavens [accurate caelitus genomen]), and that from these — as also from those I can procure out of Germany — I may investigate the differences of meridians [Meridianorum Differentias] more correctly than has hitherto been done, and thus restore [restituiren] geography in that respect, so far as is possible (since geography, as Your Princely Grace well knows, lends astronomy no small assistance).

I would also most dutifully have asked Your Princely Grace to give command to your clockmaker [Automatopaeo] or watchmaker, that if he should know anywhere a young, unmarried journeyman who could deal diligently with clocks and automata [Horologiis vnd Automatis] and attend to my astronomical instruments [Instrumenta Astronomica], he should arrange that the same betake himself in here to me at the first [opportunity]. So would I employ him here for such work, and give as good a wage as he could get elsewhere. For my goldsmith — who dwelt here with me in the country, attended to my instruments, made many of them with his own hand, and served me herein diligently and faithfully for many years — has (alas) died on me a short while ago, so that I therefore need another skilled journeyman in his stead, of whom there are scarcely any to be had here in this country. I trust Your Princely Grace's clockmaker will do his best in this.

I long to obtain Your Princely Grace's observations of the fixed stars [Observationes stellarum fixarum] and to compare them with my own. For I believe nothing other than that Rothmann must by now be finished with them. As concerns my first volume [ersten Tomum], in which I treat of this matter at length, Your Princely Grace will get to see it, with God's help, in this coming summer, for it, together with several other things, is soon to be completed in print.

I have also written to Your Princely Grace's Mathematician concerning 18 minutes that are everywhere lacking in the observations of the comet of the year [15]80 communicated to me [in Observationibus Cometae anni 80mi mihi communicatis]; for as often as the comet passed across the meridian [per Meridianum], just 18 minutes were taken there at Kassel too few, whereas my observations made at the same time [eodem tempore factae] always have just so many minutes more. Whence I reckon that such a defect was committed by a quadrant, through a fault of the plumb-line, not rightly set by so many scruples [durch einen Quadrantem vitio Perpendiculi, tot Scrupulis non rite directi, commissirt]. I spoke of this myself with Rothmann, when he was here, and demonstrated the same to him by a collation of your observations and mine [per collationem vestrarum atque mearum Observationum]; whereat he marvelled, and knew no proper cause for it. But he promised, as soon as he should arrive back with Your Princely Grace at Kassel, to investigate this more thoroughly. For he was not, at the time when this comet was observed, in Your Princely Grace's service. If, then, it was attended to by the clockmaker or by another, I would gladly have some report as to whereon this deficiency arose, before I put these observations to use in the description of that comet [in descriptione istius Cometae]. But that in my own meridian observations [Observationibus meridianis], made in the same [period], there is no error, the altitudes [Altitudines] taken likewise at Prague by Thaddaeus sufficiently show, which agree tolerably well with mine, at least when account is taken of the diversity of horizons [habita saltem ratione diuersitatis Horizontum]. I will not, however, trouble and detain Your Princely Grace any longer this time with such matters and others, but, awaiting Your gracious reply and report by the present messenger when he comes back from the Fair, herewith commend the same most diligently into the all-powerful, grace-rich protection of God, unto long bodily health and peaceful governance of the land.

Given at Uraniborg, in February, the year 1592.
Your Princely Grace's
ever willing-in-service
TYCHO BRAHE
with his own hand.

[Postscript.] Further I dutifully give Your Grace to understand that I have written to Your Grace's son, Landgrave Maurice, and have dutifully asked his Grace to be willing, without demur, to compose for me a Poem in commendation of my astronomical Works, since I am sufficiently informed that his Grace possesses a good poetic vein, and takes delight in it. I trust, therefore, that his Grace will condescend to inscribe such a Poem, with his own hand, upon the one side of the first leaf — where the title of my Volumes, which I now have in hand to prepare for the press, stands set down together with the Imperial Diploma, of which I have sent his Grace copies. This will be very welcome to me, and, as I judge, not without honour to his Grace, and will moreover serve to the advancement of these studies.

Apparatus

  • Sender / recipient. Tycho Brahe, writing from Uraniborg on Hven, to Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel (1532–1592), the astronomer-prince whose Kassel observatory was Tycho's chief institutional peer and rival. The full titulature in the address — Count of Katzenelnbogen, Dietz, Ziegenhain, and Nidda — is the standard Hessian style and is preserved. (Wilhelm died on 25 August 1592, a few months after this letter.)
  • Document type / register. A learned-court letter in chancery German, switching freely into Latin for every technical noun and verb (Observationes, in omnem eventum, per omnia sua momenta, motum ☾ae, Differentiam Meridianorum, Apparentias acronychas, restituiren, conferirt, etc.). The Latin code-switches are retained in the translation in [square brackets] after the English rendering, by the edition's convention; technical terms follow the editorial glossary–5.2.
  • Persons named.
  • Rothmannus = Christoph Rothmann, Wilhelm IV's mathematician; he had visited Tycho at Hven in 1590 and (as the letter notes) had by 1592 effectively left Kassel service. Tycho is unsure whether Rothmann has returned, hence the contingency instructions.
  • Mesthlinus = Michael Maestlin (1550–1631), the Tübingen astronomer (later Kepler's teacher). Tycho stresses "I am not myself acquainted with him" (ich bin selber nicht mit ihm bekandt) — a hedge preserved exactly.
  • Thaddaeus = Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku (Thaddaeus Hagecius, 1525–1600), the Prague imperial physician-astronomer, whose Prague altitudes of the comet Tycho cites to vindicate his own meridian observations.
  • Automatopaeus (Greek-derived Latin, "automaton-/clock-maker") = the Landgrave's clockmaker, the celebrated Jost Bürgi (not named in the letter); rendered "clockmaker," with the term flagged. The parallel German gloss oder Vhrmacher ("or watchmaker") confirms the sense.
  • Goldtschmidt — Tycho's resident goldsmith / instrument-maker at Uraniborg, recently deceased; not named here.
  • Astronomy — technical notes.
  • Eclipsin ☾ae … in fine Decembris — the lunar eclipse of late December 1591; cloud at Hven prevented Tycho observing it, hence his appeal for the Kassel timing as a check on his lunar tables and for deriving the difference of meridians (longitude difference Hven–Kassel). The glyph ☾ is kept in the source block and named "Moon" in the translation by the edition's convention; ☾ae is the Latin genitive Lunae abbreviated on the glyph.
  • in situ acronychio / Apparentias acronychas — acronychal position / appearances: a planet observed at opposition to the Sun (rising at sunset). Kept in Latin with the English; acronychal retained as the period technical term.
  • circa Solis oppositum … per totum Eccentrici ambitum — Tycho's program of solar-opposition observations of the superior planets distributed around the whole circuit of the eccentric (orb), the method of determining a planet's orbital elements. Eccentric / opposition per glossary.
  • ♄ (year markers 76/77/78) = Saturn; ♂ (76/78) = Mars; named in English, glyphs kept in source. ♄num / ♂tem = the Latin accusatives Saturnum / Martem abbreviated on the glyph.
  • Radio … per lineas rectas iuxta fili extensionem … decussatim — the cross-staff (radius astronomicus, glossary) and alternative thread-/sighting methods of taking angular distances; rendered descriptively.
  • Cometae anni 80mi — the great comet of 1580, observed at both Kassel and Hven; the 18-minute systematic discrepancy in the Kassel meridian transits is the crux Tycho attributes vitio Perpendiculi — a fault of the [quadrant's] plumb-line mis-set by "so many scruples" (Scrupuli = arc-minutes here). This is the clock-vs-instrument dispute documented in Dreyer (Tycho Brahe, on the Kassel observations) and Thoren (Lord of Uraniborg: "eighteen minutes in their timings of the comet of 1580").
  • ersten Tomum — Tycho's first volume, i.e. the Astronomiae instauratae progymnasmata (Vol. I of his projected great work, which would carry the comet and fixed-star material); "soon to be completed in print" is the author's own optimistic forecast (it did not in fact appear until 1602, posthumously) — preserved as written, not corrected.
  • Greek-derived term. Automatopaeus (ἀυτοματοποιός, "maker of self-moving things") — the court clockmaker; flagged rather than silently Englished, with the term shown.
  • Calendar / dateline — flagged crux. The subscription reads "Dat. Vraniburg den Februarii Anno 1592" — the day-of-month is omitted in the source text (a blank or lost numeral between den and Februarii). The edition label supplies "22 februar"; the identifier encodes …0220…. I have therefore led the header with the as-written (incomplete) dateline — "[—] February 1592" — and appended "(catalogued 22 February 1592)," by the edition's convention: the catalogued 22 February is not substantiated by the source's own dateline and is recorded here, not asserted in the header. The date is Old Style (Denmark kept Julian until 1700; Hesse-Kassel likewise Julian in this period). The companion Magini letter adjacent in the edition (1 Feb 1592 n.s., Bologna) is unrelated.
  • Formulae (chancery German,). Address Mein gnediger Herr → "my gracious Lord"; Ew. F. G. (Ewer Fürstlichen Gnaden) → "Your Princely Grace"; subscription Alzeit dienstewilliger → "ever willing-in-service"; manu propria → "with his own hand." Franckfurter Messe = the Frankfurt Book Fair, the channel by which Tycho's messenger travelled and by which the reply is to come back.
  • §1 Dreyer collation: the German original leaves the day blank, but Dreyer's facing Latin (Opera Omnia VI, p. 298) dates it 20. Februarii, and Wilhelm's reply (VI, p. 300) quotes "ewer Schreiben sub dato Uraniburg, den 20. Februarij." The day is therefore 20 February (not the catalogue's 22); supplied editorially as [20.] in the Source and fixed in the header and translation.

Dreyer prints this Feb-1592 letter only in Latin (no German facing for the postscript), so the Latin is the source form. "C. E." = Celsitudo Eius (His Highness, sc. Maurice); "C. T." = Celsitudo Tua (Wilhelm).
  • What the recovered text is. The postscript to Tycho's letter of 20 February 1592 to Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel — not a separate enclosure but the letter's own closing paragraph, written directly after the signature, which the defective transcription had previously truncated at "TYCHO BRAHE manu propria."
  • Landgrave Maurice ('Landgraff Moritz'). Wilhelm IV's son and heir; he succeeded his father later in 1592 (Wilhelm died that summer/autumn) and became known to posterity as Moritz der Gelehrte ("the Learned"), a patron and practitioner of poetry and music in his own right — matching Tycho's flattering claim that he possessed "ein gute Venam poëticam."
  • The volume this concerns. Corpus evidence (Mosley, Bearing the Heavens, Cambridge 2007, discussing the dedication leaf) confirms Tycho's Epistolarum astronomicarum liber primus (Uraniborg, 1596) was in fact dedicated to Landgrave Maurice. This 1592 postscript shows Tycho cultivating that dedication four years in advance, inviting Maurice to autograph a commendatory poem onto the title-leaf of the very 'Tomorum' he was then preparing for the press, alongside the printed Imperial Diploma (Rudolf II's printing privilege).
  • Errors corrected in the prior English of this passage. (1) "I shall humbly entreat" wrongly casts as future what the German states as already done — 'gebetten' is past tense: Tycho reports he has already asked Maurice, in the letter to him he mentions sending. (2) "I have long enough understood" mistranslates 'gnugksamb berichtet' ("sufficiently informed," not "for a long time"). (3) "three copies" of the Imperial Diploma is invented — the German gives only 'Exemplaria' (copies), no number. (4) The closing sentence "Given as above in the letter" has no counterpart anywhere in the German and has been removed as a fabrication. (5) "upon the other part of the first page" mistranslates 'auff die eine seidt' ("on the one side," not "the other").
  • Crux. 'ihrer F. G.' — the pronoun governing whom Tycho sent the title-leaf/Diploma copies to — differs from the 'Ew./Eure F. G.' used for the addressee Wilhelm throughout the rest of the letter. Read here as continuing to refer to Maurice in the third person (parallel to 'seine F. G.' earlier in the same sentence), i.e. the copies went to Maurice, not to Wilhelm; but an ENHG scribal variant of 'seiner' cannot be wholly excluded.
  • Correction. Removed a fabricated day-number ('the 20th') from the dateline; the source gives only 'den Februarii Anno 1592', with no day stated.

The translation and its checking

Grade Probable · not collated against Dreyer's pages; defects the audit noted: fabrication (2).

Not collated against Dreyer — no page reference for this letter, so the grade is capped at Probable however sound the translation looks. the edition gives no page reference for this letter (not printed in Dreyer's TBOO), so by the audit's rule this was audited against the transcribed original alone and would normally be capped at Probable — but a fabrication was found even without Dreyer access, which per the grading rule forces C regardless of that cap. Checked every proper name, number, date and place in the German against the English paragraph by paragraph: the elk order in Norway, the Rothmann letter and the late-December lunar eclipse, Maestlin's Saturn 1576/77/78 and Mars 1576/78 acronychal observations, the Italy/Spain/Poland lunar-eclipse requests, the deceased goldsmith and the clockmaker request, the 18-minute comet-of-1580 discrepancy at Kassel and Thaddaeus's corroborating Prague altitudes, and the postscript naming Landgrave Moritz and the Imperial Diploma — all faithfully rendered except the fabricated day-number in the dateline.

1 defect found:
- fabrication (high). The German dateline carries no day-number at all ('den Februarii', not 'den 20. Februarii') — the English inserts a specific '20th' with no counterpart in the transcribed original. This is exactly the invented-day-number failure mode this audit was briefed to watch for; the '20' appears to have been pulled from the catalogue metadata (date field / identifier) rather than from the letter's own text. At: “Given at Uraniborg, the 20th of February, the year 1592.”.

Repaired. No Dreyer page images were available for this letter (no page reference), so the fix rests entirely on the letter's own internal evidence, which is conclusive on this point: the German dateline as given in the transcribed original literally has no day-number between 'den' and 'Februarii'. Only the fabricated '20th' was removed; nothing else in the letter was checked or touched, since no edition-of-record collation was possible.
