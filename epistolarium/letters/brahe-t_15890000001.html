CLARISSIMO DN. DOCTORI BRUCÆOTYCHO BRAHE.
S. P.

RECTE et prudenter asseris, in qua etiam sententia ego semper fui, esse levitatis magnæ, ingenij parvi, imbecillis iudicij, imo inconstantis animi, non recte expensis adversariorum opinionibus, quibus calumniandi quam recte sentiendi maior est cura, subito assentiri. Verum dum te id in usu habere iactas, et diu multumque premere, quæ in motis quæstionibus contradicuntur, et quomodo refelli queant, sicque vel in priore sententia persistere, aut etiam rectius sentientibus adhærere, id (si pace tua verum dicere liceat) an ubique citra omne præiudicium adeo integre observes, quemadmodum tibiipsi forte abblandiendo polliceris, equidem non minimam mihi dubitationem movet. Enimvero plurima eorum, quæ ex familiari inter nos colloquio olim perspecta habeo, tum etiam literæ tuæ aliquoties de rebus Philosophicis ad me perscriptæ contrarium quiddam rem omnem enucleatius discutienti insinuant. Et ne longe exempla petamus, certe hæ ipsæ (ut de cæteris nunc non dicam) quibus te ita purgas et æquitatem in iudicando eximiam prædicas, tibi hac in parte adversantur. Quorsum enim tenebriones vocas eos omnes, qui veteres quosdam Medicos et Philosophos, sicubi absona et minus comperta pro vero venditârunt, non saltem iustis rationibus, sed evidenti experientia et sensibus ipsis indies refellunt, curque eos turbæ indoctæ fucum facere et convitiari ais? Anne hoc potius est convitijs rem agere et quasi cum præiudicio quodam cæcoque affectu eos insectari, quorum nec scripta nec ratiocinationes nec etiam operationes mechanicas unquam rite exploratas habuisti, tenebriones vocitare? Dubius aliquandiu hæc legens substiti, quosnam tam egregio epitheto ornare volueris, vixque capiebam, antequam ulterius progrediens intellexi, te de magno illo THEOPHRASTO PARACELSO Germanorum incomparabili Philosopho et Medico, vobis Galenistis non minus quam noctuæ lumen diei inviso et odioso, verba facere. Scripseram siquidem in ijs ipsis, quas adeo invite accipis literas, pauca quædam de hoc viro. Ad id enim respondens, quod in prioribus tuis adduxeras, veteres stellas humore vesci arbitratus esse, addideram, te brevi in Paracelsistam transformatum iri, si talibus opinionibus aurem præbere velles. Nam et is astra nutriri voluit. Hinc occasionem nactus infers, quod in tenebrionis istius sententiam te trahere velim, cum alio spectet tua oratio. Hîc primum cum eximium illum superioris ævi Philosophum, qui doctrinam et placita sua ex ipso naturæ thesauro indesinenter depromsit, et reipsa quamplurima ita se habere contra veterum quorundam et neotericorum Sophisticas argutias vel ipsis sensibus demonstravit, tenebrionem appellas, cuius tamen scripta et traditiones vel nunquam legisti, vel si aliquando per transennam a te forte inspecta sunt, nondum rite percepisti, nedum ut usu, industria atque indefesso labore eorum intellectum et exercitationem tibi familiarem reddideris. Quorsum igitur de re ignota et tibi nunquam satis vel grossiori indagine perspecta iudicium fers? Sicne decet de tota posteritate præclare meritum virum tenebrionis titulo infamare? An hoc est diu multumque premere, quæ contradicuntur, et æquali lance omnia ponderare? Immo hoc est de re ignota iudicium non saltem præcipitanter et inconsiderate ferre, sed etiam convitijs et dicterijs virum laude potius quam vituperio dignum immerito proscindere et in defunctum sævire. Sic et Ardea aquas, quas tranare nequit, culpat. Anne satis tibi conscius es, quoties, dum Rostochij vobiscum eram, me conveneris, ubi solum nactus esses, nemine præsente clam a me Paracelsicorum medicamentorum præparationem sciscitabaris, admodum solicite nunc de his, nunc de illis, ut te eorum confectionem edocerem, me rogitans, utut in præsentia aliorum, si quando de his mentio fiebat, scommatis et reprehensionibus atque contemtu illa præ Galenicis etiam me audiente exciperes; scilicet ne videreris tot annos in fucis verborum, neglectis rerum penetralibus, frustra insumsisse et optima quæque neglexisse. At si horum recordatio tibi minus grata aut invisa est, quoties me per literas, quas adhuc in promtu habeo, rogasti, ut tibi Paracelsicorum remediorum nonnulla communicarem, quæ morbos illos difficilimæ curationis, utpote Epilepsiam Apoplexiam, Paralysin, Hydropem, dolores podagricos et similia Galenistarum syrupos, decoctione et clysteres respuentia mala, præsenter tollerent, ut tibi tuæque praxi per ea honorem et lucrum conciliares? Et certe contra Epilepsiam efficax remedium e PARACELSI fontibus derivatum iamdudum a me impetrasti, quod per vitrioli exaltatum et depuratum spiritum, intervenientibus quibusdam appropriatis specificis, pertinax illud et horrendum malum generaliter profligat. Idne inficiaris? Nonne eius præparationis processum D. LEVINO BATTO communicasti, qui eum ijsdem verbis, quibus ad te perscriptus erat, postea in quibusdam thematibus de hoc morbo agens propalavit, citra tamen autoris et instructoris mentionem, quasi vobis ipsis hæc asscribere, quæ aliorum non parvo labore et sumtu comparata erant, nihil referret, sed honori daretur? Verum et plura in his tibi iamdudum iuxta tua avida desideria concessissem, ni scirem vos, qui PARACELSUM odistis, non satis sincere et integre debitaque gratitudine hæc affectare, sed saltem ut vobis quæstum et famam comparetis, alijs vero labores exantlandos et sumtus erogandos relinquatis, et unâ, quod pessimum est, præceptoribus vestris et vobisipsis asscribatis, quæ feliciter per hæc arcana remedia præstari queant, ipsum THEOPHRASTUM unâ cum his, qui longo usu et labore hæc per Pyronomica exercitia excoluerunt, scommatis et cavillis interim excipere non verentes, ut soli sapere videamini. Atque hic plerumque mos est eorum, qui malunt docti et sapientiores haberi quam esse. Quod si itaque adeo tibi invisus est PARACELSUS, ut ipsum omnesque eius sectatores tenebriones appellare non dubites, cur clam medicamenta ex eius schola prodeuntia expetis, palam vero irrides et improbas? Cur viri doctrinam nunquam a te intellectam, multo minus solide exploratam convicijs et maledictis infestas? aut cur non iudicium suspendis, donec rationes et quæ utrinque contradicuntur perspecta habueris, si id evitare, quod levitatis est magnæ, ingenij parvi, et imbecillis iudicij, ut ipsemet scite mones, adeo in votis habes? Verumenimvero hæc non propterea refero, quod GALENUM et veteres Philosophos, quos semper magnifeci et adhuc magnifacio, in omnibus culpare, atque ijs detrahere, et PARACELSUM undiquaque erroribus immunem pronunciare velim (omnes enim homines fuerunt, et tam falsa, quam vera ex hoc inexhausto naturæ thesauro, partim aliquatenus introspiciendo, partim cæcutiendo haurire potuerunt) sed saltem ut admoneam te, dum virum illum inauditum et non intellectum damnas, contra propria axiomata, quæ in usu te habere refers, peccare. Sic non minorem mihi infers iniuriam, dum te in eius sententiam me trahere asseveras, atque sic amicis irridere, cum ex meis ad te datis literis nihil eiuscemodi, sincere omnia dijudicando, colligi queat. Illic enim potius PARACELSI opinionem circa siderum alimenta improbavi, quam quod tibi irriserim; nisi ridiculum duxeris, te tanto et tam excellenti in naturæ pervestigatione adeoque pertractatione Artifici quadam in parte assimilare, cum hoc laudi potius tibi ducendum, quam vituperio vertendum censeres, modo usque adeo veritatis latentis penetralia per iustas ratiocinationes discernere cuperes, velut te perpetuo in votis habere persuadere mihi conaris. Ride, quantum voles, cachinnis non eluditur veritas. Memini, me inscriptionem quarundam literarum aliquando ad te dedisse, qua te Philosophiæ Aristotelicæ et Medicinæ Galenicæ Doctorem et promotorem eximium prædicavi. Hanc intitulationem tali interpretatione excepisti, quod epistolæ istius inscriptio, nisi teipsum novisses, elatioris te animi reddidisset, addens: Vah? quantus ego futurus sum Thraso, si talem inveniam Gnathonem? At sane ego non Gnathonice et adulatorie tee isto titulo ornavi. Nec enim vel ipsis Regibus aut Principibus virisque summis adulari unquam didici. Neque præcipuam laudem in his autoribus evolvendis et reputandis proclamandisque sitam esse iudico, ex quo in præcipuis quibusdam quæstionibus longissime a veritatis scopo recesserint, ut in plerisque tum ab alijs tum etiam a meipso satis evidenter ostensum est, et inposterum, favente Numine, luculentius manifestabitur. Hîc igitur potius stomachari debuisses, meque, quod ironijs ludam, accusare. Sed ita mundo acceptum est, ut tenebras lucem, hanc vero noctem reputet, ita seipsum et alios non sine gravi veritatis iactura deludere solet. At si forte hæc tibi obscuriora sunt, et data opera magis incredibilia, quam quod probare possint, te non semper æquali lance diversorum Philosophorum placita, antequam ratam de his tuleris sententiam, librare, addam unum vel alterum exemplum ex ea ipsa epistola. Ais me asserere, Lunæ latitudinem maximam, quæ mea inventione Ptolemaicam quarta gradus parte excedit, non facere magnum in eclipsibus discrimen, quæ circa nodos incidunt, idque illic verum esse, sed tamen in maxima a nodis remotione, iuxta quam eclipses contingere possunt, aliquid discrepantiæ daturam credis. Utrum et hîc mea ratiocinia iusta trutina expenderis, antequam de his iudicium iam secundo protuleris, videbimus. Ego in literis illis, quibus meam sententiam inventaque tutatus, hæc ipsa, quæ nunc infers, satis diligenter examinavi, et Mathematice demonstravi, perque numeros comperta reddidi, et tam in ipsis intersectionibus quam remotissimo limite adeoque his intermedio differentiam, quæ contingit inter latitudinem Lunæ Ptolemaicam et nostram, oculis subieci, eamque asserui iuxta distantiam a nodis, ubi limes eclipsium est remotissimus, 12 graduum non maiorem evadere 3 scr. quæ unum saltem digitum in obscuratione Lunæ causari ad summum possint, cuius tamen perceptio quamobrem sensibus minus pateat, rationes insuper addidi. Cum igitur hæc sint satis evidenter a me demonstrata et scrupulose numerata, ut nulla restet contradicendi iusta occasio (taceo, quod etiam Mathematici Landgravij experimento et testimonio, quod unâ adiunxi, id muniveram) cur, quæso, ea nunc denuo in dubium vocas, et cramben semel coctam ad fastidium recoquis? Cur non manifestæ experientiæ et observationum certitudini eorumque, quæ contradici possint, iustæ dilutioni non acquiescis? An hoc est iuxta tua veritatis indagandæ principia, diu videlicet multumque pensitanda esse ea, quæ in controversiam vocantur, et rationes utrinque ponderandas, antequam sententia de talibus minime dubia feratur? Sic neque de ijs, quæ ego circa cœli materiam non, ut quidam neotericorum sentiunt, prorsus aëriam (secuti in hoc IOANNIS PÆNÆ Galli sententiam) in contrarium adducturus sum, dubitare poteris, priusquam meas rationes, quas forte iam antea suspectas habes, penitius cognoveris. Si tibi neotericorum quorundam totum mundum visibilem aëre elementari replentium arridet opinio, quam etiam sententiam mordicus tuetur antedictus Landgravij Mathematicus, et multis modis a me id obtinere, sed frustra elaborat, equidem per me id tibi et alijs facile licebit. Sed vide, quanta nota vestro ARISTOTELI sic inuratur. Is enim elementorum proportiones et mensurationes hac ratione ignorasse, ut de neotericis nunc non dicam, et cœli naturam frustra ab his exemisse, atque quintum quoddam (idque mea sententia non inepte) immerito appellasse videbitur. Et quam parva ad GALENUM vestrum hinc redundabit gloria, utpote eiusdem Philosophiæ consortem? Præterea quamvis cuiuslibet Cometæ consideratio per se circa individua saltem versetur, nec generalem aliquam de omnibus doctrinam extruat quoad singulas particularitates, quæ diversis incidere possunt, tamen multa habent communia, et unius saltem diligens pervestigatio ad reliquorum cognitionem uberiorem viam sternit; ut ob id non ratum sit, hanc cognitionem cum ipsis Cometis intercidere, velut tu adhuc nostros conatus et indefessos circa hoc negocium labores nimium (ut videtur) extenuando obijcere perstas. Manet enim scientia et veri inquisitio in animis eruditorum, qui rite candideque philosophantur, et hæc exempla ad uberiorem in his perfectioremque, quando plures exoriuntur Cometæ, veritatis inquisitionem, non infrugifere conducunt; quin et de multis alijs ad Astronomicum negotium pertinentibus (de quibus tum in opere nostro subinde ago, tum etiam in literis istis te commonefeci) disserendi ansam non postremam, neque usque adeo contemnendam præbent. Quod si tibi hæc ita non arriserint, frustraneamque, quæ his impendatur, diligentiam duxeris, ista non legas, nec cures quicquam, imo contemnas, abijcias et oppugnes, si lubet: me non habebis antagonistam. Inveniet forte noster conatus aliquos, qui tot vigilatas noctes, tot indefatigatos labores, tot meditationes et veri perquisitiones, tantosque in hæc erogatos sumtus non ingratis animis excipient. Imo si vel nulli quam maxime unquam candida mente hæc agnoscere velint, ego tamen a veri rectique inquisitione et promulgatione animum nequaquam abiecturus sum, cum virtus et veritas sibijpsis precium sint, nec extraneis indigeant adminiculis aut suffragijs. Si id te ita movet, quod dixerim, nostros labores forte tamdiu inter eruditos locum aliquem inventuros atque ea, quæ de primo mobili et vulgaribus Planetarum theorijs toties recantantur, in quibus nihil novi aut diu desiderati, sed saltem aliquoties actum denuo agitur, ut revera in his compleatur Comici sententia: Nihil dici, quod non dictum sit prius: si, inquam, idipsum ita te exagitat, ut de tuo saltem sphærico libello (quem nonnullas præclaras demonstrationes non ubique obvias continere scio) prolatum suspiceris, cum tamen theoriarum unâ mentionem fecerim, quas te nullas edidisse compertum habeo, ut ob id in tot scommata prorumpendum duxeris, Bosphoros, tineas, blattas, cauponarum et pharmacopœorum avaras technas nostris laboribus oblique ominando, nihil ista me movent, sed tibi potius bilem excitasse videntur. Scio equidem, qualia sint ea, quibus utplurimum nostri ævi scriptores, vel potius transcriptores in singulis facultatibus et artibus chartas onerant, quodque ea vix diutinam ætatem sustinebunt: et experientia testatur, superioris seculi eiuscemodi non magni momenti scripta iam exolevisse. Nec ego animum induxi, quidpiam in medium proferre, quod ab alijs satis elaboratum anteaque promulgatum est, ne videar actum agere, sed ea potissimum, quæ tot iam seculis diu multumque frustra expetita et desiderata sunt, quorum etiam pervestigationem solidam sumtuum penuria et adminiculorum defectus alias non ubique admittit. Si ista nihilominus aliquando superesse nequeant et a posteris conservari augerique, utique nihil aliud eveniet, quam quod iamdiu accidit, ea ignoratione opprimi oblivioneque, quæ magno labore e tenebris in veritatis lucem deducta sunt; et nihil ad me pertinebit, si posteri ea tueri conservareque nequeant aut nolint. Sufficit, me DEI aliquale donum non humi abscondisse, nec tempus inutiliter consumsisse, de cætero non solcitus. Ego nihil eorum ago, quod immortalitatem ullam, quam in solius DEI gratia positam scio, inde consequi sperem, et quemadmodum (ut CICERO ait) ea, quæ de nobis, antequam nati sumus, loquebantur homines, sive non, nihil nos afficiunt, sic etiam post fata aliorum tunc viventium nostri recordatio nihil nobis boni damnive adfert, sola ea, quæ apud DEUM est, immortalitate perpetuitatem æviternam largiente. Cætera omnia cum caduco mundo interitui et dissolutioni obnoxia sunt. Qui igitur in his sempiternum aliquid ponit, seipsum decipit. Habes, quid ego etiam de his sentiam, ob id inculcanda, ne me aliter opinari frustra existimes, et contra iudicij æquitatem, quam præ te fers, nescius et hac in parte pecces. Istam non levem iniuriam, quam in meam Cometæ Anni 77 theoriam, asserens talem Cometæ ductum aliquando terris uniri, commisisti, vix uno vel altero verbo excusas, ne forte errorem agnoscere videaris. At quî Mathematicus ignorare debeat, digressionem maximam a Sole eorum Phaenomenωn, quæ Terram non simul ambiunt, in loco contactus, qui sit per lineam visus et circulum sui ductus, capiendam esse, quemadmodum in ♀ et ☿ ab omnibus Astronomis hactenus quam proxime fieri consuevit, et ista per se in libro meo satis fideliter explicata sunt, ut nullam hæsitationem vel obiectionem iure mereri possint?

Ea, quæ ad ultimas literas iocose saltem et obiter scriptas copiosius fere, quam ad ullas maiores et ad rem Astronomicam non leviter, ut puto, facientia tractantes respondes, nolo multis replicare. Scimus, quales sint incolæ nostro ævo temperatissimam mundi regionem inhabitantes, quales etiam Africam incolentes, ut de cæteris non dicam. Id tantum addam, in extrema Norwegia et Suecia rusticos reperiri non paucos, mansuetiores, humaniores et hospitaliores, quam plærique rus incolentium inter eos, qui in septentrionali Germaniæ parte degunt. Nam isthic versus Boream non solum sunt omnes etiam agricolæ hospitales, sed bene secum actum putant, si a peregrinis visitentur, ijs cibum et potum in copia largiuntur gratis, nihil pecuniarum recipientes, cum ijs blande colloquuntur, congaudent, cantant, et similia humanitatis officia lubenter præstant, promovent postea equis, curribus, cymbis, quorsum ulterius tendere cupiunt, atque hæc obvia vel nullâ vel minimâ mercede. Hæc ita se habere, norunt ij, qui istas regiones peragrârunt. Cum itaque utriusque plagæ homines, de quibus loquor, sint rustici, nec literis instituti, nec peregrinatione aut conversatione mansuetiores redditi, nec delicatius educati, quî fit, ut Boreas Austrum in his humanitatis officis vincat? Vide, qualia ingenia ultima Islandia producat, quod procul dubio expertus es in ijs, qui subinde vestram Academiam invisunt. Imo vix isthic reperitur rusticus aliquis, qui non ludum Scacchorum, qui omnium est ingeniosissimus, expedite et scite admodum calleat, qui tamen tantum habet difficultatis, ut multi sint alibi in civitatibus, qui eum vix assequuntur. Sed nolo ad comparationem, quæ ut recte ais, odiosa est, diverti. Latet quiddam in terrarum Naturis et Genijs abstrusi, quod nec aëris nec cœli influxu totaliter excusari potest, imo idipsum non perpetuo in ijsdem locis durat, sed habet suas quasdam metamorphoses et transplantationes, vulgaribus Philosophis nondum perspectas.

Hæc literis tuis respondere libuit, nullo te tuave carpendi studio (neque enim amicitia nostra diutina patitur) sed ut ingenue te admonerem, ne nimium tuo ipsius iudicio semper tribueres, aliorum laboribus et inventis detrahendo eaque elevando, et ne in tantum veterum Philosophorum et Medicorum sententijs te mancipares, ut neotericos veritatis latebras enodare posse diffideres. Vale et hæc subito exarata boni consule atque candide interpretare, profecta ab homine tui amante et libertate utente Philosophica. Iterum quam feliciss. vale.

To the most renowned Master Doctor Brucaeus — Tycho Brahe.
Heartiest greeting.

You assert rightly and prudently — and it is a view in which I too have always stood — that it is a mark of great frivolity, of small wit, of feeble judgment, indeed of an inconstant mind, to assent suddenly [to a thing] when one's adversaries' opinions have not been rightly weighed — opinions whose authors care more for slander than for thinking rightly. But while you boast that you make a practice of this, and that you press long and hard upon the points that are contradicted in disputed questions, and on how they may be refuted, and so either persist in your earlier opinion or else attach yourself to those who think more rightly — whether (if I may, with your leave, speak the truth) you everywhere observe this so wholly, free of all prejudice, as you perhaps promise yourself in flattering yourself, that, I confess, raises no slight doubt in me. For very many of the things I once came to perceive clearly from our familiar conversation together, and likewise your letters written to me on several occasions about philosophical matters, suggest something quite to the contrary to one who sifts the whole matter more thoroughly. And not to fetch examples from afar: surely these very [letters of yours] (to say nothing now of the rest), in which you so clear yourself and proclaim your singular fairness in judging, run counter to you on this very point. For to what end do you call all those men "men of darkness" [tenebriones] who, wherever certain ancient physicians and philosophers have palmed off as truth things discordant and ill-attested, refute them daily, not merely by sound reasoning but by manifest experience and by the senses themselves; and why do you say that they deceive and rail at the unlearned crowd? Is it not rather to handle the matter with railing — and to assail with a kind of prejudice and blind passion men whose writings, reasonings, and even mechanical operations you have never duly examined — to keep calling them "men of darkness"? Reading this, I halted for a while in doubt as to whom you wished to adorn with so choice an epithet, and I scarcely grasped it, until, going on further, I understood that you were speaking of that great Theophrastus Paracelsus, the incomparable philosopher and physician of the Germans — to you Galenists no less hateful and odious than the light of day is to the owl. For in those very letters that you take so unwillingly I had written a few words about this man. Replying to what you had brought forward in your earlier [letter] — that the ancients held the stars to be fed on moisture — I had added that you would shortly be transformed into a Paracelsist if you were willing to lend an ear to such opinions; for he too held that the stars are nourished. From this you have seized an occasion to infer that I wish to draw you into the opinion of that "man of darkness," whereas my discourse looks elsewhere. Here, first: when you call a "man of darkness" that outstanding philosopher of the former age, who drew his doctrine and tenets unceasingly from the very treasury of nature, and who demonstrated, against the sophistical quibbles of certain ancients and of the moderns, by the very senses, that very many things are in fact so — whose writings and teachings, nonetheless, you have either never read, or, if they were perhaps at some time glanced at by you through a lattice, you have not yet duly perceived, much less made their understanding and exercise familiar to yourself by use, diligence, and tireless labour — to what end, then, do you pass judgment on a matter unknown, and never sufficiently examined by you even with the coarser sort of inquiry? Is it fitting thus to defame with the title "man of darkness" one who has deserved illustriously of all posterity? Is this to press long and hard upon the things that are contradicted, and to weigh everything in an even balance? Nay, this is not merely to pass judgment on an unknown matter rashly and inconsiderately, but also with railings and gibes to undeservedly tear to pieces a man worthy of praise rather than of reproach, and to vent one's fury on the dead. So too the heron blames the waters it cannot swim across. Are you sufficiently aware to yourself how often, while I was with you [pl.] at Rostock, you came to me — wherever you had found me alone, with no one present — secretly inquiring of me about the preparation of Paracelsian medicines, asking me most anxiously, now about these, now about those, to teach you their making — even though, in the presence of others, whenever mention of these was made, you would receive them with gibes, reproaches, and contempt, set against the Galenic [remedies], even in my hearing? — namely, lest you should seem to have wasted so many years in the husks of words, neglecting the inner sanctuaries of things, and to have neglected all the best [things]. But if the recollection of these things is unwelcome or hateful to you, [consider] how often you asked me by letter — letters which I still have ready to hand — to share with you some of the Paracelsian remedies, which would presently remove those diseases of the most difficult cure — to wit epilepsy, apoplexy, paralysis, dropsy, gouty pains, and the like ills that spurn the Galenists' syrups, decoctions, and clysters — so that through them you might procure honour and gain for yourself and your practice? And surely you long ago obtained from me an effective remedy against epilepsy, drawn from the springs of Paracelsus, which, by the exalted and purified spirit of vitriol, with certain appropriate specifics intervening, generally routs that stubborn and dreadful malady. Do you deny it? Did you not communicate the process of its preparation to Master Levinus Battus, who afterwards, in certain theses treating of this disease, divulged it in the very words in which it had been written out to you — yet without mention of the author and instructor, as though it were no matter to ascribe to your own selves these things which had been procured by no small labour and expense of others, but [as though] it were [thereby] credited to your honour? Indeed, I would long ago have granted you more of these things, in accordance with your eager desires, did I not know that you who hate Paracelsus seek these things not sincerely and wholly and with due gratitude, but only that you may procure gain and fame for yourselves, while leaving the labours to be drained out and the expenses to be paid by others; and, at one stroke — which is the worst of it — that you ascribe to your own teachers and to yourselves what may successfully be accomplished through these arcane remedies, not fearing meanwhile to receive Theophrastus himself, together with those who by long use and labour have cultivated these things through pyronomic [fire-] exercises, with gibes and cavils, so that you alone may seem to be wise. And this is generally the way of those who prefer to be reckoned learned and wiser than to be so. If, then, Paracelsus is so hateful to you that you do not hesitate to call him and all his followers "men of darkness," why do you secretly seek out medicines issuing from his school, while openly you mock and condemn them? Why do you harry with reproaches and curses the doctrine of a man you have never understood, much less soundly examined? Or why do you not suspend judgment until you have examined the reasons and what is contradicted on both sides — if you have so much at heart to avoid that which is a mark of great frivolity, of small wit, and of feeble judgment, as you yourself shrewdly advise? Yet I do not bring these things forward because I wish to blame Galen and the ancient philosophers — whom I have always esteemed greatly and still esteem greatly — in all things, and to disparage them, and to pronounce Paracelsus everywhere free of errors (for they were all men, and could draw from this inexhaustible treasury of nature things false as well as true, partly by looking in to some degree, partly by groping blindly), but only that I may admonish you that, while you condemn that man unheard and not understood, you sin against your own axioms, which you report yourself to keep in practice. So too you do me no less an injury when you affirm that I am trying to draw you into his opinion, and so [that I am] mocking my friends — when from my letters given to you nothing of the sort can be gathered, if all is judged sincerely. For there I rather disapproved of Paracelsus's opinion concerning the nourishment of the stars than mocked you — unless you reckoned it ridiculous that you should in some part be likened to so great and so excellent an Artificer in the investigation, and indeed the handling, of nature: whereas you ought to count this rather to your praise than turn it to your reproach, provided you so desired to discern the inner sanctuaries of hidden truth by sound reasonings, as you try to persuade me you perpetually have at heart. Laugh as much as you will: truth is not foiled by guffaws. I remember that I once gave a certain letter of mine an address [intitulatio] in which I proclaimed you an outstanding Doctor and promoter of Aristotelian philosophy and Galenic medicine. This styling you received with such an interpretation [as to say] that the address of that letter, had you not known yourself, would have made you more puffed up in mind, adding: "Bah! what a great Thraso shall I become, if I find such a Gnatho?" But assuredly I did not adorn you with that title in the manner of Gnatho and by way of flattery. For I have never learned to flatter even kings or princes or the highest men. Nor do I judge that the chief praise lies in unrolling and pondering and proclaiming these authors, seeing that in certain principal questions they have departed very far from the mark of truth — as has been shown plainly enough in most matters both by others and by myself, and hereafter, with the favour of the Deity, will be made manifest more brilliantly. Here, then, you ought rather to have grown angry, and to have accused me of playing with ironies. But it is so received in the world that it reckons darkness to be light, and this [light] to be night; thus it is wont to delude itself and others, not without grave loss to truth. But if perhaps these things are rather obscure to you, and made of set purpose more incredible — than that they can prove that you do not always weigh in an even balance the tenets of the various philosophers before you have passed a settled judgment on them — I shall add one or two examples out of that very letter. You say that I assert that the Moon's maximum latitude, which by my finding exceeds the Ptolemaic by a quarter of a degree, makes no great difference in the eclipses that fall near the nodes — and that there it is true, but that nonetheless, at the greatest distance from the nodes at which eclipses can occur, you believe it will yield some discrepancy. Whether here too you have weighed my reasonings in a just balance before now pronouncing judgment on these matters for the second time, we shall see. In those letters in which I defended my opinion and findings, I examined these very points which you now bring forward diligently enough, and I demonstrated them mathematically, and rendered them proven by numbers, and I set before [your] eyes the difference, which occurs between the Ptolemaic latitude of the Moon and ours, both at the intersections themselves and at the most remote limit and indeed midway between these; and I asserted that, at the distance from the nodes where the limit of eclipses is most remote, [namely] 12 degrees, [the difference] turns out no greater than 3 minutes [3 scr.] — which at most could cause but a single digit in the obscuration of the Moon; and I added besides the reasons why the perception of this is nonetheless less open to the senses. Since, therefore, these things have been demonstrated by me evidently enough and reckoned out scrupulously, so that no just occasion for contradiction remains (I say nothing of the fact that I had also fortified it by the experiment and testimony of the Landgrave's Mathematician, which I appended at the same time), why, pray, do you now once more call them into doubt, and re-cook to nausea cabbage once cooked? Why do you not rest content with manifest experience and the certainty of the observations, and with the just dissolving of what can be objected? Is this, in accordance with your principles of investigating the truth, that the things called into controversy must be pondered long and much, and the reasons on both sides weighed, before a judgment by no means doubtful is passed on such matters? In the same way, neither will you be able to doubt those things which I shall adduce to the contrary concerning the matter of the heaven — [holding it] not, as certain of the moderns think, wholly airy (following in this the opinion of the Frenchman Jean Pena) — before you have come to know my reasons, which perhaps you already hold suspect, more inwardly. If the opinion of certain moderns, who fill the whole visible world with elemental air, pleases you — an opinion which the aforesaid Mathematician of the Landgrave also defends tooth and nail, and labours in many ways to obtain this [assent] from me, but in vain — then for my part you and others will be quite free to hold it. But see how great a brand is thereby burned upon your Aristotle. For he will seem, on this reckoning, to have been ignorant of the proportions and measurements of the elements (to say nothing now of the moderns), and to have exempted the nature of the heaven from these in vain, and to have wrongly named [it] a certain fifth [substance] — and that (in my opinion) not ineptly. And how little glory will redound hence to your Galen, as being a partner of the same philosophy? Moreover, although the consideration of any given comet is in itself concerned only with individual cases, and builds no general doctrine about all [comets] as regards the several particularities that can occur to different ones, nevertheless they have much in common, and the diligent investigation of one alone paves a richer way to the knowledge of the rest; so that on this account it is not established that this knowledge perishes along with the comets themselves — as you still persist in objecting, by belittling too much (as it seems) our endeavours and our tireless labours about this business. For knowledge and the inquiry after truth abide in the minds of the learned who philosophize duly and candidly, and these instances contribute not unfruitfully to a richer and more perfect inquiry into the truth in these matters, when more comets arise; nay, they afford no last nor by any means contemptible handle for discoursing of many other things pertaining to the astronomical business (of which I treat from time to time in our work, and have likewise reminded you in those letters). But if these things do not please you so, and you have judged the diligence spent on them to be in vain, [then] do not read them, and care nothing about them — nay, despise them, cast them away, and attack them, if you like: you will not have me for an antagonist. Perhaps our endeavour will find some who will receive with no ungrateful minds so many sleepless nights, so many unwearied labours, so many meditations and searchings after truth, and so great expenses laid out on these things. Nay, even if absolutely no one should ever be willing to acknowledge these things with a candid mind, I shall nonetheless by no means cast down my spirit from the inquiry into, and the publication of, the true and the right — since virtue and truth are a price unto themselves, and need no external props or votes. If what I said moves you so — that our labours will perhaps for so long find some place among the learned, while the things so often re-chanted about the primum mobile and the common theories of the planets, in which there is nothing new or long desired, but only what has already been done is done over again several times, so that in these matters in truth the Comic poet's saying is fulfilled: "Nothing is said that has not been said before" — if, I say, this very thing so vexes you that you suspect it to have been uttered with regard at least to your little book on the sphere (which, I know, contains some excellent demonstrations, not everywhere obvious), although I made mention of theories alongside, of which I find that you have published none — so that on this account you have thought you must burst out into so many gibes, darkly hinting, against our labours, at "Bosphoruses" [Bospori], moths, bookworms, and the greedy tricks of tavern-keepers and apothecaries: those things move me not at all, but seem rather to have stirred up bile in you. I know indeed what sort of things they are with which most of the writers — or rather copyists — of our age load their papers in the several faculties and arts, and that these things will scarcely endure a long age; and experience attests that writings of that sort, of no great moment, of the former century have already gone out of use. Nor have I set my mind to bring forward into the open anything that has been sufficiently elaborated by others and previously published, lest I should seem to do what has [already] been done; but rather, above all, those things which for so many ages now have been long and much sought and desired in vain — things whose solid investigation, moreover, the scarcity of expense and the lack of aids does not everywhere otherwise permit. If these things should nonetheless one day prove unable to survive and to be preserved and increased by posterity, assuredly nothing else will come to pass than what has long since happened: that those things which by great labour have been led out of darkness into the light of truth are crushed by ignorance and oblivion; and it will be no concern of mine if posterity cannot or will not guard and preserve them. It is enough that I have not hidden some gift of God in the ground, nor consumed my time uselessly — for the rest I am not anxious. I do none of these things in the hope of attaining thereby any immortality — which I know is placed in the grace of God alone — and just as (as Cicero says) the things which men spoke about us before we were born, or did not speak, affect us not at all, so likewise after death the remembrance of us by others then living brings us neither good nor harm, since that immortality alone which is with God bestows everlasting perpetuity. All other things, together with the perishable world, are liable to destruction and dissolution. Whoever, therefore, sets anything everlasting in these [things], deceives himself. You have [now] what I too think about these matters — to be impressed upon you for this reason, lest you should idly suppose me to be of another opinion, and, against the fairness of judgment which you make profession of, should unwittingly sin on this point too. That not slight injury which you committed against my theory of the comet of the year [15]77, asserting that such a track of the comet is at some point joined to the earth, you scarcely excuse in a word or two, lest you should perchance seem to acknowledge an error. But how ought a Mathematician to be ignorant that the greatest digression from the Sun of those phenomena [Phaenomenōn] which do not revolve around the Earth at the same time [as the Sun] is to be taken at the point of contact, which is [defined] by the line of sight and the circle of its own track — just as in ♀ Venus and ☿ Mercury it has been the custom of all Astronomers hitherto to do as closely as possible — and that these things are explained faithfully enough of themselves in my book, so that they can by right deserve no hesitation or objection?

The things you treat of in reply to my last letter — written only jestingly and in passing — at almost greater length, as I think, than [you treat] of any weightier matters bearing not lightly on the astronomical subject, I do not wish to rejoin to at length. We know what sort the inhabitants are who in our age dwell in the most temperate region of the world, and what sort, too, those who inhabit Africa, to say nothing of the rest. This only will I add: that in farthest Norway and Sweden not a few peasants are to be found gentler, more humane, and more hospitable than most of the country-dwellers among those who live in the northern part of Germany. For there, toward the North, not only are even all the husbandmen hospitable, but they think it has gone well with them if they are visited by strangers; they bestow food and drink on them in abundance, free of charge, taking no money; they converse pleasantly with them, rejoice with them, sing, and gladly perform like offices of humanity; afterwards they help them on, with horses, carriages, and boats, to wherever they wish to travel further — and all this freely offered for no fee, or the smallest. That these things are so, those know who have travelled through those regions. Since, therefore, the men of either zone of whom I speak are peasants, neither instructed in letters, nor made gentler by travel or society, nor more delicately reared, how comes it that the North outdoes the South in these offices of humanity? See what wits farthest Iceland produces — which without doubt you have experienced in those who from time to time visit your Academy. Nay, scarcely is any peasant to be found there who is not nimbly and very skilfully versed in the game of chess, the most ingenious of all games — which nonetheless has so much difficulty that there are many elsewhere in the cities who scarcely master it. But I do not wish to turn aside to comparison, which, as you rightly say, is odious. There lies hidden something abstruse in the natures and genii of lands, which can be wholly accounted for neither by the influence of the air nor of the heaven; nay, that very thing does not endure perpetually in the same places, but has certain metamorphoses and transplantations of its own, not yet discerned by the common run of philosophers.

It has pleased me to answer your letter thus, with no zeal of carping at you or yours (for our long friendship does not allow it), but that I might frankly admonish you not always to attribute too much to your own judgment, by disparaging and belittling the labours and discoveries of others, and not to enslave yourself so far to the opinions of the ancient philosophers and physicians as to despair that the moderns can unravel the hiding-places of truth. Farewell, and take in good part these things hastily penned, and interpret them candidly, as proceeding from a man who loves you and who uses philosophical freedom. Again, may you fare most happily — farewell.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Dn. Doctori Brucaeo = Heinrich Brucaeus (Heinrich Brucäus / Hendrik van den Broeck, 1530/31–1593), professor of mathematics and then of medicine at the University of Rostock, where Tycho had studied (1566–68); a Galenist physician and an old friend with whom Tycho corresponded on natural-philosophical and medical matters. The letter is Tycho's reply in an ongoing exchange in which Brucaeus had derided Paracelsus.
  • Subject, person. Theophrastus Paracelsus = Paracelsus (Theophrastus Bombastus von Hohenheim, 1493/94–1541), the iatrochemical physician; Tycho — himself a practising Paracelsian/spagyric chemist at Uraniborg — defends him against Brucaeus's slur tenebrio.
  • tenebrio / tenebriones — literally "a creature/man of the dark, a skulker"; here Brucaeus's contemptuous label for Paracelsus and his followers. Rendered consistently "man of darkness." The polemic turns on this word, so it is kept verbatim and glossed rather than paraphrased.
  • Galenistae / Galenici — the Galenists, orthodox academic physicians (followers of Galen, in opposition to the Paracelsians). vobis Galenistis … noctuae lumen diei — "to you Galenists [Paracelsus is hateful] as daylight to the owl," a proverbial image kept literal.
  • D. Levinus Battus = Levinus Battus (Lieven Bats, d. 1591), physician (associated with Rostock); Tycho charges that Brucaeus passed Tycho's Paracelsian epilepsy recipe to Battus, who published it in disease-themata (academic theses/disputations) without crediting the author.
  • Mathematicus Landgravii / antedictus Landgravii Mathematicus = Christoph Rothmann, the mathematician of Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel; Tycho cites Rothmann's corroborating "experiment and testimony" on the lunar-latitude question, while opposing Rothmann's view that the heavens are filled with elemental air.
  • Ioannes Paena Gallus = Jean Pena (Joannes Pena, c. 1528–1558), French royal professor of mathematics, whose preface to his edition of Euclid's Optics/Catoptrics (Paris, 1557) argued for a fluid (airy) celestial region; Tycho names Pena as the source of the "moderns'" airy-heaven doctrine he is here contesting.
  • Cyprianus Leovitius does not appear in this letter (he does in the 1571 Wolf letter); not glossed here.
  • Astronomy — lunar latitude. Lunae latitudinem maximam … Ptolemaicam quarta gradus parte excedit — Tycho's measured maximum (celestial) latitude of the Moon exceeds Ptolemy's value by a quarter of a degree (≈15′). nodos / a nodis remotione — the nodes of the lunar orbit and distance therefrom; limes eclipsium = the eclipse-limit. Glossary: latitudo → latitude, nodus → node.
  • 3 scr. — scrupula = arc-minutes (the scrupulum / scriptulum as 1/60 of a degree); rendered "3 minutes." digitum = a digit (1/12 of the Moon's diameter), the standard unit of eclipse-magnitude; "a single digit in the obscuration of the Moon." Both are technical period units, kept and glossed.
  • cometae … coeli materiam … quintum quoddam — the comet/heavens passage states Tycho's mature cosmology: the heaven is not (as Aristotle's defenders hold) a solid fifth element/quintessence (quintum quoddam), and the demolition of the comet-spheres bears on this; Tycho notes that Aristotle "not ineptly" called the heaven a fifth substance even so. primum mobile = the primum mobile; vulgares Planetarum theoriae = "the common planetary theories" — kept.
  • Comet of 1577. meam Cometae Anni 77 theoriam — Tycho's theory of the comet of 1577 (published in De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis, 1588). The astronomical core of the rebuttal: that the greatest elongation (digressio maxima a Sole) of bodies not concentric with the Earth is to be measured at the point of contact of the line of sight with the circle of the body's own path — quemadmodum in ♀ et ☿ "as in Venus and Mercury." Glyphs ♀ (Venus) and ☿ (Mercury) are kept in the Source block and named in English by the edition's convention
  • Greek. The text is overwhelmingly Latin. The single Greek element is the orthographic flourish Phaenomenωn — the Latin word phaenomenon given a Greek omega (ω) in its genitive-plural ending (printed -ωn); kept as printed and rendered "phenomena."
  • Medicine / alchemy. Epilepsia, Apoplexia, Paralysis, Hydropem [hydrops → dropsy], dolores podagrici — the diseases Brucaeus sought Paracelsian cures for; Hydrops glossed dropsy. syrupos, decoctione [decoction], clysteres — Galenic syrups, decoctions, clysters (enemas). vitrioli exaltatum et depuratum spiritum … appropriatis specificis — the exalted and purified spirit of vitriol with specifics: a spagyric (Paracelsian) anti-epileptic preparation. Pyronomica exercitia — "pyronomic exercises," i.e. operations governed by [the regulation of] fire — laboratory/alchemical fire-work; kept and glossed. arcana remedia = "arcane remedies." Period terms not modernised.
  • Literary allusions. Ardea aquas … culpat — the proverbial heron that blames the water it cannot cross (cf. the rooster/pearl type of fable-proverb); kept literal. Thraso … Gnatho — the braggart soldier Thraso and the parasite-flatterer Gnatho of Terence's Eunuchus; Brucaeus's self-deprecating jest that Tycho's flattering address would make him a Thraso to Tycho's Gnatho. Comici sententia: "Nihil dici, quod non dictum sit prius" — the Comic poet's maxim "Nothing is said that has not been said before," from the prologue to Terence's Eunuchus (lines 41: nullumst iam dictum quod non dictum sit prius); kept as the quoted sententia. Cicero ait — the consolatory commonplace that what men said of us before we were born, or after we die, does not touch us, echoing Cicero (Tusculan Disputations / De senectute register); attributed as Tycho gives it. cramben semel coctam ad fastidium recoquis — "you re-cook cabbage once cooked, to nausea," the crambe repetita proverb (Juvenal); kept.
  • Bospori / Bosphoros, tineas, blattas — "Bosphoruses, moths, bookworms": tineae and blattae are clothes-/book-moths and cockroaches (devourers of paper), Brucaeus's sneer that Tycho's labours are bookworm-fodder. Bospori is a crux: it is not the standard idiom here. It is most plausibly a learned/contemptuous coinage or a corrupt reading (possibly for Bombyces "silkworms," another paper/cloth-pest, or an allusion via "Bosporus"); the printed form is retained in the Source block and rendered literally as "Bosphoruses" with the metaphor (worthless, devouring pests) preserved. Not smoothed — see confidence.
  • sphaerico libello tuo — Brucaeus's "little book on the sphere," i.e. an elementary De sphaera (Sacrobosco-type) textbook; Tycho concedes it contains some good demonstrations but notes Brucaeus published no planetary theoricae.
  • Geography / ethnography. The second paragraph answers a jesting aside of Brucaeus's about northern peoples: Tycho praises the hospitality of peasants in farthest Norway and Sweden over north-German country-folk, and the wit of Icelanders (their skill at chess, ludus Scacchorum) who visit the Rostock Academia (university). temperatissima mundi regio = the most temperate zone. Genii … terrarum — the "genii (tutelary spirits/characters) of lands," a natural-philosophical not literal sense; kept.
  • Epistolary formulae. S. P. = Salutem plurimam (dicit) → "heartiest greeting". Vale … Iterum quam feliciss. vale → "Farewell … Again, may you fare most happily, farewell." libertate utente Philosophica = "using philosophical freedom," Tycho's sign-off justification for his frankness.
  • Negation note. The argument is built on a dense lattice of negations and concessive clauses (non … sed, nec … nec, non … nisi, ne … videaris). Each has been counted and reproduced one-for-one; in particular Tycho's disclaimer "haec non propterea refero, quod … in omnibus culpare … et Paracelsum undiquaque erroribus immunem pronunciare velim … sed saltem ut admoneam te" is rendered with all four of its negating/limiting members intact (he does not mean to blame Galen in all things, nor to declare Paracelsus everywhere error-free, but only to admonish Brucaeus). No negation has been added or dropped.
  • Calendar / dateline. The source carries no dateline. The edition catalogues it as "1589. empty" (year only, no day/place), and the MS witness (Vienna cod. Vindob. lat. 10686.66) is a copy (Afskrift). The header therefore gives 1589 [O.S.] with "(catalogued 1589)" and the place [Uraniborg] in brackets as the inferred but un-stated place of writing (Tycho was at Uraniborg in 1589); no day-date is asserted because the text substantiates none. Tycho and the Danish/Rostock orbit used the Julian (Old Style) calendar. A separate, dated Tycho→Brucaeus letter (21 February 1589, identifier brahe-t_15890221001) exists; this undated one is a distinct piece and should not be conflated with it.
  • Edition of record. As printed in the 1596 Epistolae tradition (Tychonis Brahei et ad eum doctorum virorum epistolae ab anno 1588) and re-edited by Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII (the Epistolae volume).
