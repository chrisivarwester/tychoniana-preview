The original

TYCHO BRAHE AD DAVIDEM CHYTRÆUM.

S. P.

REVERENDE et Clarissime vir, Amice inprimis honorande. Literas tuas iuxta initia Novembris superioris Anni Rostochio ad me datas paulo post huius primordia per filium huc revertentem accepi, proque singulari tua, quam isthic affatim declaras, erga me affectione et benevolentiâ gratias habeo plurimas. Respondissem citius et quæ in mei gratiam annuis tuis Historijs inserere decrevisti paulo aliter formata misissem, nî mihi tuæ tardius quam existimabas fuissent redditæ; defectus etiam tabellariorum effecit, ut id prius exequi non licuerit. Remitto autem nunc chartam illam, iuxta quam ea, quæ de Cæsa Mate ob me huc vocatum et benignissime exceptum memorare constituisti, in quibusdam, uti vides, certis de caussis mutata consignavi, omnia tamen tuo arbitrio, an sic vel aliter scribi velis, relinquens; acceptumque mihi est, quod hoc officium in mei gratiam non detrectes, et spero id Cæsæ Mati placiturum. Quia vero relationi de hoc præterito Anno illa immisceri non poterant, haud incongruum duxerim ad huius 16 Centenarij, quem Iubilæum vocant, eadem applicare, sumptâ occasione, quod hoc anno Progymnasmata mea Instaurationis Astronomicæ et nonnulla alia Opera, in Daniâ prius inchoata, sub auspicijs Augustissimi Imperatoris RUDOLPHI Secundi, Domini nostri Clementissimi, eius præsidio et liberalitate fultus, in publicum ediderim; sic enim, Deo conatibus nostris aspirante, me effecturum spero.

Habeo insuper gratias, quod ea, quæ isthic in Orbe Arctoo proximis temporibus gesta sunt, communicaveris. Inaudivi CAROLUM Ducem totâ Sueciâ iam potitum; utinam victoriâ illâ moderate uti vellet, nec nimis in eos, qui illi restiterunt, sævire, et Regni Senatoribus (inter quos est optimus ille ERICUS SPARRÆUS noster) parcere, ut pacatius et firmius eius sit in posterum Regimen, ne exitum infelicem sortiatur effera vindictæ cupiditas. Exemplum habet domesticum in ERICO fratre.

Quid in Dania Patria mea geratur, et an novas res isthic moliantur, qui fatis præsunt publicis, necdum mihi liquet. Optandum, ut pace hac, quâ multis iam Annis gaudent, diu frui possint, nec internis aut externis irrequietudinibus turbentur. Batavos pacis conditiones quascunque suspectas habere et renuere, accepi, adeo ut quidam apud eos Primarius nuper Hamburgum in hunc modum (prout eius formalia verba ad me relata sunt) perscripserit: Si concederetur Statibus Hollandiæ in membrana, quæ æquaret magnitudine bovinum tergus, quascunque ipsimet vellent pacis conditiones, consignare, easdemque ratas se habituros addicerent adversarij, tamen pacificationem omnem aversaturos, cum toties sub tali prætextu delusi sint.

De Ungaricis rebus nihil hîc, relatu dignum, scitur; per hyemem transactam parum a Cæsareanis effectum est. Dicitur Turca maiorem parare exercitum quam unquam antea. Cæsar noster vicissim, quantum potest, se contra ipsum armat, utut Imperij Principes pro maiore parte propter Hispanorum incursiones subsidia denegent et quædam alia agant. WAIUODA pro se recuperatam Transylvaniam feudi loco retinere laborat, cuius Legati admodum insolenter et tumultuarie nuper Pilsnæ se gesserunt, idque Imperator indigne ferens misit quendam ex suis, qui ea de re apud WAIUODAM conquereretur. Dicunt aliquot millia Gallicorum equitum et vicinarum Regionum adventare contra Turcas in gratiam Cæsaris belligeraturi. Quantam stragem ediderint superiori Anno Tartaricæ incursiones in superiori Ungaria et parte aliqua Moraviæ, existimo te iam antea percepisse. Deus tam eorum quam Turcarum furores et Tyrannidem reprimere atque a cervicibus Christianorum avertere dignetur. Ubi plura de rebus, quæ ibi atque in viciniâ gesta sunt, intellexero, te eorum reddere conscium non intermittam.

Cæsar adhuc Pilsnæ abest, ubi ultra semestre proxime elapsum mansit, ob luem Epidemicam, quæ gravissime antecedente anno totam Bohemiam et propinquas regiones infestavit, paulo post Pascha, uti existimant, Pragam rediturus. Deus ipsius Matem nobis quam diutissime florentem atque incolumem conservet. Cui etiam Divino Numini te tuosque commendo. Vale et generum tuum Dominum Doctorem IOHANNEM FREDERUM, cui pro carminibus missis gratias habeo, ex me peramanter saluta; scripsissem ei, si otium suppetijsset.

TYCHO BRAHE TO DAVID CHYTRAEUS.

Heartiest greeting.

Reverend and most renowned man, friend especially to be honoured. Your letter, sent me from Rostock about the beginning of November of last year, I received a little after the beginning of this [year], through my son returning hither; and for your singular affection and benevolence toward me, which you abundantly declare therein, I give very many thanks. I would have replied sooner, and would have sent — formed a little otherwise — those things you resolved to insert in my honour into your annual Histories, had yours not reached me later than you thought; the lack of couriers, too, prevented my doing it earlier. But I now return that sheet, in which I have set down — in certain places changed, as you see, for sure reasons — those things you resolved to record about the Imperial Majesty, on account of my being called hither and most kindly received, leaving all, however, to your judgement, whether you wish it written so or otherwise; and it is welcome to me that you do not decline this service in my honour, and I hope it will please the Imperial Majesty. But since those things could not be inserted into the account of this past Year, I should think it not incongruous to apply the same to [the account of] this 16th-century [year], which they call the Jubilee — taking the occasion that this year I have published the Progymnasmata of my Astronomical Restoration, and some other Works begun earlier in Denmark, under the auspices of the most august Emperor Rudolf II, our most clement Lord, supported by his protection and liberality; for so, God favouring our endeavours, I hope to bring it about.

I have thanks besides, that you have communicated what has lately been done there, in the Northern world. I have heard that Duke Charles has now gained all Sweden; would that he would use that victory moderately, not rage too much against those who resisted him, and spare the Senators of the Realm (among whom is that best man, our Erik Sparre), that his rule may be more peaceful and firmer hereafter, lest a savage desire of vengeance meet an unhappy outcome. He has a domestic example in [his] brother Erik.

What is happening in Denmark, my fatherland — and whether those who preside over public destinies are contriving new things there — is not yet clear to me. It is to be wished they may long enjoy this peace, which they have had now for many years, and not be disturbed by inner or outer unrests. I have heard that the Dutch hold suspect and refuse any peace conditions whatever — so much so that a certain leading man among them lately wrote to Hamburg in this manner (as his exact words were reported to me): that if it were granted to the States of Holland to set down, on a parchment that would equal an ox-hide in size, whatever conditions of peace they themselves wished — and the adversaries pledged to hold the same ratified — yet they would reject all pacification, since they have so often been deceived under such pretext.

Of Hungarian affairs nothing worth relating is known here; during the past winter little was accomplished by the imperial [forces]. The Turk is said to be preparing a greater army than ever; our Emperor in turn arms himself against him as much as he can, although the Princes of the Empire for the most part deny subsidies on account of the Spaniards' incursions and do certain other things. The Voivode strives to retain Transylvania, recovered for himself, in the manner of a fief; his envoys lately behaved quite insolently and tumultuously at Pilsen, and the Emperor, taking it ill, sent one of his men to complain of the matter to the Voivode. They say some thousands of French cavalry, and [those] of neighbouring regions, are coming to wage war against the Turks for the Emperor's sake. How great a slaughter the Tartar incursions wrought last year in Upper Hungary and some part of Moravia, I think you have already perceived. May God deign to repress the furies and tyranny both of them and of the Turks, and to turn [them] away from the necks of Christians. When I learn more of what is done there and in the neighbourhood, I will not fail to make you aware of it.

The Emperor is still away at Pilsen, where he has stayed beyond the past half-year on account of the epidemic plague which last year most grievously infested all Bohemia and the neighbouring regions — about to return to Prague, as they think, a little after Easter. May God preserve His Majesty for us, flourishing and unharmed, as long as possible; to which Divine Power I also commend you and yours. Farewell; and most lovingly greet from me your son-in-law Lord Doctor Johann Freder, to whom I give thanks for the poems sent; I would have written to him, had leisure sufficed.

Given from the imperial castle of Benátky, the 28th day of March, the year 1600, New Style.

About this time there rose to renown, at the court of the most august and most powerful Emperor Rudolph, the second of that name, in Bohemia and the surrounding parts of Germany and the neighbouring kingdoms, Tycho Brahe — sprung from an illustrious and most ancient line of Danish nobility, and, among his brothers, men splendid and pre-eminent in rank within that kingdom, the eldest by birth. For he, having in very many years past expended incredible labours and expense in purging and restoring divine Astronomy from its errors, in this year, under the auspices of the said most excellent Emperor, made public his Progymnasmata of the Restoration of Astronomy and certain other works in this sublime kind of study, so that Astronomy too might thus find, as it were, its own Jubilee.

Now the occasion of his migrating, beyond everyone's expectation, from Denmark his native land, first into Germany and then into Bohemia, was this. Twenty-four years ago, by the most gracious provision of King Frederick the Second of Denmark, of most praised memory, Tycho built handsomely, and at great expense, on the island of Hven in the Danish Sound, the castle of Uraniborg, for the convenient carrying-out there of the exercises of the science of the stars; and in it he set up very many astronomical instruments, wrought so magnificently and solidly that the world has scarcely seen the like elsewhere, invented and made by himself — just as we too, in our Historia Arctoa, have in its proper place made some mention of this Uraniborg. All of this, for as long as the said Most Serene King lived, was there generously maintained in its due use, just as also, after his lamentable death, throughout the whole eight years of the Interregnum, hope was moreover given — [earlier] by that most gracious King Frederick himself, and afterward by all the Senators of his Kingdom — that all these things would be suitably supported and perpetuated there, in honour of the Kingdom and of the King.

But since afterward, contrary to the expectation not only of Tycho himself but of many others, both within and outside that Kingdom, this turned out otherwise, he — lest so great an astronomical apparatus, and labours of such magnitude expended by him there over twenty-one years, should gradually collapse and at last utterly perish — chose rather, having left behind his most beloved native land, very many kinsmen of the foremost nobility joined to him by blood, and his other dearest friends, of his own accord to pass into foreign parts together with his whole household, than to be a spectator of this so unhoped-for and lamentable ruin. And so, three years ago now, namely in the year 1597, about the summer solstice, having taken with him, as has been said, his whole household, he first sailed hence to Rostock, where he remained for a quarter of a year, so as to leave time, in that neighbourhood, for his fellow Danes to look more rightly into the matter — he being still solicitous for his native land.

For that reason too, with winter coming on, he withdrew from there into Holstein, likewise bordering on Denmark, invited thither by letters of the most noble Lord Heinrich Rantzau, hero of most excellent memory, the Royal Viceroy there, his kinsman; from him he received the castle of Wandsbek, near the city of Hamburg, to inhabit with his household, and there he stayed a year's time — for, the reason having already been indicated, he was unwilling to settle any further off, however not inconvenient the terms meanwhile offered to him might have been.

While, then, he was staying there, a report of Tycho's departure from his native land was carried to the never sufficiently praised Emperor Rudolph. Wherefore His Imperial Majesty — being disposed, as he is, toward all the liberal disciplines, but chiefly toward this sublime study of Astronomy, which in every age has moved royal minds and hearts born for heaven to hold it in high esteem — with a favourable and most benign spirit, having heard beforehand of Tycho and that he was earnestly devoted to these pursuits and had achieved much of worth, summoned him to himself and brought him across into Bohemia with his whole household. Having received him most graciously, he settled him in a certain one of his castles, five miles distant from Prague, the capital of the Kingdom, called Benátky, so that there, enjoying philosophical leisure, he might weave to completion the web of the restoration of Astronomy which he had begun, and might have leisure for other pursuits worthy of a man of liberal condition; and he provided for him, liberally and most kindly, the expenses required for labours so arduous — even though, on account of the length of the Turkish war, the times were then more difficult than they would otherwise have been. A thing indeed most worthy of the liberality and munificence of the greatest Monarch of the world, and to be proclaimed to all posterity.

Nay more, [the Emperor] pledged, with most gracious will, that as to a new Uraniborg, which should not yield to the Danish one, he would himself provide, at a fitting place and time, for the placing and proper use there of Tycho's Instruments; and that he would most kindly provide him and his household with a suitable fief, lest, once rendered foreigners, they should in future be left destitute of what is necessary for an honest maintenance — even as we ourselves have received these and similar things, related and set down by trustworthy men, not without delight to us all, [addressed] to us and to other excellent and learned men here and there.

Thus that noble Tycho Brahe, standard-bearer among the votaries of Astronomy in our century — whose sublime genius and untiring zeal for illustrating these foremost disciplines concerned with the motions of the stars had in former years been a singular ornament and honour to all Denmark — having left that land behind, passed over into the Emperor's dominions, and there, not without God's own particular providence, obtained from the most powerful Emperor a quiet harbour for his studies. May the Author of the Universe, whose glory the heavens declare and whose handiwork the sky displays, grant that all things may incline toward, and be established in, the never-dying glory, honour, and profit — for as long as the stars endure — of the most august Emperor Rudolph the Second, who has advanced this business with his most gracious favour and liberality, and of the whole German Empire.

Apparatus

  • Recipient & occasion. David Chytraeus, the Rostock historian — Tycho's reply to a Chytraeus letter of "about the beginning of November [1599]" (a later one than brahe-t_15981007001, the 7 Oct 1598 letter), continuing the annual-history thread.
  • The migration-narrative for the Saxonia (cross-ref). Tycho returns the sheet on which Chytraeus had drafted his account "about the Imperial Majesty … on account of my being called hither," with Tycho's emendations "for sure reasons," all left to Chytraeus's judgement. Since it could not fit the prior year's account, Tycho proposes applying it to the Jubilee year 1600, on the occasion that "this year I have published the Progymnasmata of my Astronomical Restoration" under Rudolf II's auspices. (The same narrative Sturtz and Brasch also pursued; cf. brahe-t_16010415002, brahe-t_16000920002, brahe-t_16000916002. Chytraeus would die in 1600, leaving the work unfinished.)
  • The Scandinavian news. Duke Charles of Sweden "has now gained all Sweden"; Tycho hopes he will be moderate and spare the Senators — "that best man, our Erik Sparre" (executed at the Linköping Bloodbath, 1600) — warning by the "domestic example" of Charles's deposed brother Erik XIV. (Cf. brahe-t_15991203001, brahe-t_16010418001.)
  • The Dutch "ox-hide parchment" anecdote (a vivid set-piece). Tycho relays a striking figure of Dutch distrust of Spanish peace overtures: a Holland leader wrote that even if the States of Holland were allowed to write their own peace terms "on a parchment as large as an ox-hide," and the enemy pledged to ratify them, they would still refuse all pacification — "so often have they been deceived under such pretext." A memorable contemporary image of the Eighty Years' War deadlock.
  • The Hungarian war. The Turk preparing a great army; the Emperor under-supported (the German princes denying subsidies, citing Spanish incursions); the Voivode (Michael the Brave) seeking Transylvania as a fief, his envoys "insolent" at Pilsen; French cavalry expected; the Tartar incursions into Upper Hungary and Moravia. (Cf. brahe-t_16000926002, brahe-t_16010415002.)
  • Persons named. David Chytraeus (and his son-in-law Johann Freder, Frederus); Charles of Sweden and his brother Erik XIV; Erik Sparre; the Voivode (Michael the Brave); Rudolf II.
  • Calendar. Dateline Die 28 Martij Anno 1600 stylo novo; New Style (imperial). Header agrees with the label. (Same date as the Ursus letter, brahe-t_16000328001.)
  • What the recovered text is. A third-person Latin biographical notice of Tycho ("CIRCA hoc tempus inclaruit...") that forms the missing head of the letter catalogued as Tycho Brahe → David Chytraeus, Benátky, 28 March 1600; the published tail ("non intermittam... Vale...") is Tycho's own first-person close. Since the recovered text never shifts into Tycho's own voice, it reads as an enclosure carried within his covering letter rather than as part of the letter proper — and the exact seam between the two is itself unrecovered.
  • Not a private scrap. The identical incipit ("CIRCA hoc tempus inclaruit apud Augustissimum...") recurs in Tychonis Brahe Opera Omnia (vol. VIII, among the documents around Tycho's letter to Duke Ferdinand of 18/28 January 1601), confirming this text had an established place in the printed correspondence record and is not merely a manuscript fragment lost to the transcription defect.
  • The "Historia Arctoa" authorship crux. The aside "as we too, in our Historia Arctoa, have in its proper place made some mention of this Uraniborg" is illuminated by a separate, later Brahe letter on record in the register of Tycho's correspondence, in which Tycho refers explicitly to "Historia Arctoa ab optimo illo CHYTRAEO inde ab Anno millesimo quingentesimo inchoata" — "the Historia Arctoa, begun by that excellent Chytraeus from the year 1500" — almost certainly Chytraeus's own Chronicon Saxoniae (Rostock 1590; Leipzig 1593), which Tycho hoped to see extended to a full century by 1600. This makes it likely the "we" of the enclosure speaks in Chytraeus's own authorial voice — i.e. that this is, or closely follows, Chytraeus's own draft chronicle entry on Tycho for the year 1600, which Tycho is here returning (corrected or endorsed) under cover of his letter. FLAGGED AS CRUX: the precise authorship and transmission chain — Chytraeus drafting it and sending it to Tycho for correction, versus Tycho supplying a third-person self-notice (a known contemporary convention) for Chytraeus to copy verbatim — cannot be settled from this fragment alone.
  • Internal chronology checks out exactly. Uraniborg "twenty-four years ago" (built from 1576, correct for a document of March 1600), "twenty-one years" of labour there (1576–1597), departure "three years ago... 1597" via a quarter-year at Rostock then a year at Rantzau's Wandsbek, and the Danish Interregnum given as "eight years" (Frederick II's death 1588 to Christian IV's 1596 majority) — all match the documented record (Dreyer 1890; Christianson, On Tycho's Island / The Measure of the Heavens).
  • The Emperor's unfulfilled promises. The notice records, as of March 1600, Rudolf II's pledge to build Tycho a Bohemian "new Uraniborg" and to grant him and his family a fief so that, as émigrés, they would not be left without honest means of support — a specific, datable undertaking that can be set against what was actually delivered before Tycho's death in October 1601 (Benátky, then a Prague town residence, no purpose-built observatory, and only partial family provision).
  • Heinrich Rantzau appears as the Royal Viceroy in Holstein and Tycho's kinsman who hosted him at Wandsbek Castle in 1597–98, consistent with Lohmeier (2000) on Rantzau.
