The original

TYCHONI BRAHE IOAN: GEORG HERWART AB HOHENBURG.

LITERAS tuas, vir nobiliss. atque mathematicorum omnium facile princeps, Benachiæ ultima mensis novissime elapsi ad me datas, unâ cum adiuncto deliquij lunaris calculo et systemate probe accepi, et in primis magnopere sum lætatus, sacram Cæs. Maiest. id quod fore animus mihi iam antea præsagiebat, tibi tuisque studijs pro dignitate et meritis prospexisse, atque eius nomine mihi quoque subinde gratulor, quod hac ratione et amicitia tua, uti plane consido, frui et forte aliquando te quoque convenire et coram aliqua tecum conferre queam. De patria vero tua quod te sic amiserit, non miror. Quis enim unquam vel Propheta acceptus in patria?

Quæ scribis de Veneris stella, quæ aliquando præter solitum non comparuisse traditur, perplacuere, et gratias ago tibi magnas, quod magno me dubio liberaveris. Cum enim mihi persuaderem, posse Veneris stellam, subeunte stella Mercurij, Eclipsin pati, atque etiam ex calculo Prutenico eo ipso tempore ☌ ♀ et ☿ matutinam, paulo ante ortum Solis talem, in qua ☿ infra ♀ esset, animadvertissem, incertus animi eram, quid hac de re foret statuendum. Cæterum rationibus tuis prægnantibus omne mihi dubium ex animo exemisti.

Illa porro, quæ de motibus luminarium et eorundem defectibus subiunxisti, fuerunt mihi lectu iucundissima. Immensum vero quantum gavisus fuerim, cum ex hypothesi tua Lunæ, adeoque tota illius theoria et systemate animadverterem duplicem illam variationem, tam semimenstruam ex duplici motu ☾ a ☉, quam annuam illam ex distantia ☉ aut ☊ dependentem atque nodorum quam innuis reciprocationem tandem aliquando tuo Marte innotuisse, quæ simul unâ pariterque et veteribus et recentioribus, imo vero vetustissimis etiam et novissimis quibusdam authoribus de ipsa Lunâ, sidere infimo et cuius observatio omnium facillima videri queat, incognita fuerant. Neque iam dubito, quin ex his tuis fundamentis, non modo recentiora, verum etiam vetustissima quæque φαινόμενα exactius haberi queant. Eandem enim utique, vel saltem mutatis mutandis consimilem olim habuit rationem motus Lunæ, quam qualemque tu modo accuratissimis tuis rectificatissimorum, quoad eius fieri potest, instrumentorum observationibus eruisti.

Nec me movent observationes illæ a PTOLOMÆO et veteribus habitæ, cum plus satis constet, quam crassa Minerva veteres illi artifices astronomicas observationes tractarint, destituti non modo idoneis instrumentis, verum etiam ut plurimum calculi quoque præcisa ratione, id quod ARISTARCHI Samij, AUTOLYCI et HIPPARCHI Bithynij libelli, qui ad nos pervenere, cuivis facile fidem facient, plerumque enim ἐν πλάτει proportionem aliquam forte harmonicam, vel numerum aliquem rotundum, qui non multum a vero discrepare videbatur, quæsiverunt, eoque contenti pauca quædam minuta nihili æstimarunt, imo vel unius gradus quadrantem non magni fecerunt. Ipse quoque PTOLOMÆUS quoad latitudinem Lunæ, de qua scribis, cum acquieverit ERATOSTHENIS invento, quo is solstitialium punctorum distantiam ad integrum circulum se habere (ἔγγιστα) ut 11 ad 83 statuerat, consequenter necessario observationis ea ratione, qua usus est, ob assumptam a se nimiam istam Zodiaci obliquitatem, minorem iusto latitudinem Lunæ inferre debuit. Quodsi, ut credi par est, PTOLOMÆI temporis obliquitas Zodiaci tanta non omnino fuerat, quantam ex præfata ERATOSTHENIS opinione et HIPPARCHUS et PTOLOMÆUS eam admiserunt, ex ipsissima PTOLOMÆI observatione inferri potest, maiorem etiam tum fuisse ☾ in quadraturis latitudinem, quam eam quæ numero rotundo quinque graduum a PTOLOMÆO proditur.

Inaudivi quoque libenter, te tabulas tuas de motu luminarium, seorsim typis excusas, cæteris Planetis præmittere statuisse. Spero enim hac ratione futurum, ut tanto citius ad nos perveniant. Et forte longe omnium circumspectissime ageres, vel saltem desiderijs plurimorum affatim satisfaceres, sitimque nostram aliqua ex parte restingueres, si solas, nudas, puras, putas tabulas de motibus luminarium, instar Prutenicarum, sine omni demonstratione, præmitteres atque maturares. Musæ profecto meæ te supplices orant, obsecrant, obtestantur, ut quanto citius tuo beneficio Eclipses luminarium exactius supputari possint, id quod ex nudis tabulis fieret quam commodissime, atque interea temporis exemplo discipulorum magni illius PYTHAGORÆ merito atque iure optimo, τὸ αὐτός ἔφα rationis loco allegaremus, donec apparatum istum observationum atque demonstrationum tuarum concinnares.

Communicasti iam mihi, quod cum maxima gratiarum actione refero, totam rationem Systematis tui Lunaris, adeo ut præter epochas mediorum motuum atque diurnum cuiusvis medium motum quoad Lunam vix aliquid in residuo esse videatur. Quod si tantundem in Sole, æquatione temporis etc. fieret, posset iam quis per multiplicationes et resolutiones triangulorum absque tabulis ulterioribus calculum Eclipsium absolvere. Id propterea refero, quia multiplicationes et triangulorum resolutiones, quæ vicem tabularum mediorum motuum et æquationum supplere possunt, ego parum moror. Soleo enim sine multiplicationis ambagibus, solius additionis ope, numerum ex multiplicatione producendum facillimo tramite invenire, et consimiliter sine omni divisionis labore atque tædio sola subtractionis ope quotientem in quovis genere calculi, tam vulgari, quam sexagenario, reperire, atque ob id sine fastidio et temporis tractu triangula quæque, etsi radius instar VALENTINI OTHONIS sit maximus, facile resolvere. Hoc autem unicum unice exopto, ut Eclipses aliquas luminarium vetustas ex datis et placitis magni illius TYCHONIS BRAHE supputare queam. Quod si ulla ratione vel lege vel conditione hoc ipsum impetrare potero, ut Eclipses aliquas veteres, quas ab alijs hactenus nec animadversas nec supputatas collegi, calculo tuo exactissimo subijcere, et ad te Matheseos ARISTARCHUM deferre valeam, per Deum summum Opt. Max. in omnem eventum, hoc chyrographo meo teste καρτερὸν ὅρκον sanctissime iuro, nemini me unquam quicquam de ijs communicaturum, quæ ad huiusmodi calculum instituendum requiruntur. Atque utinam aliquo genere officiorum beneficium huiusmodi in me collatum rursus exæquare possem, pro viribus certe gratias referre conarer. Sed ignosce quæso, si flagrans desiderij ardor, quo hæc exopto, importuniora vota volenti nolenti exprimat. Quod si epidemica ista lues, quæ loca inter nos intermedia et hospitia præsertim publica invasisse traditur, non me absterreret, ipse ad te iter instituerem, anne forte hoc pacto voti compos fieri possem.

Principes mei clementissimi, id enim scire desideras, studijs quidem hisce non admodum afficiuntur, attamen literas et literatos amant, ita ut sperem, munus illud tuum, de quo scribis, futurum illis esse gratum atque acceptum, mihi certe erit quam gratissimum. Habet serenissimus Princeps meus Bibliothecam instructissimam, Regijs sumptibus comparatam, et libris manuscriptis Hebraicis, Græcis etc. refertam. Mitto his iunctum indicem librorum mathematicorum, quem velim apud te retineas, ut si forte occurrat liber aliquis, quem ex ijs desideres, eius me certiorem reddere possis. Hæc raptim, urgente tabellario. Vale feliciter, et me favore tuo prosequi perge. Quod si ulla in re tibi commodo vel usui esse queam, vel in his partibus, vel in ijs rebus, quæ per has regiones ad te devehi possunt, iube et impera.

Data Monachij die 17. Septembris Anno 99.
Nobilissimæ Dis Tuæ studiosissimus
IOAN. GEORG. HERWART ab Hohenburg etc. manu propria.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To Tycho Brahe, Johann Georg Herwart von Hohenburg.

Your letter, most noble man and easily prince of all mathematicians, sent me from Benátky on the last [day] of the month just past, together with the appended calculation and system of the lunar eclipse, I duly received; and first of all I greatly rejoiced that the Sacred Imperial Majesty has provided for you and your studies according to your dignity and merits — which my mind already presaged would be; and on that account I congratulate myself too, that in this way I may, as I fully trust, enjoy your friendship, and perhaps sometime meet you too, and confer some things with you in person. That your fatherland has so lost you, I do not wonder. For when was ever even a Prophet accepted in his fatherland?

What you write about the star of Venus, which is said sometime, beyond the usual, not to have appeared, much pleased me; and I give you great thanks for freeing me from a great doubt. For since I had persuaded myself that the star of Venus could suffer an eclipse when the star of Mercury passes beneath it — and had noticed, too, from the Prutenic calculation, at that very time a morning conjunction [☌] of Venus [♀] and Mercury [☿], a little before sunrise, such that Mercury was below Venus — I was uncertain in mind what was to be concluded about this. But by your weighty reasons you have taken every doubt from my mind.

Those things, further, which you subjoined about the motions of the luminaries and their eclipses, were most delightful to me to read. But beyond measure did I rejoice when, from your hypothesis of the Moon — and indeed from its whole theory and system — I noticed that double variation: both the semi-menstrual [twice-monthly], from the double motion of the Moon [☾] from the Sun [☉], and that annual one, depending on the distance of the Sun or of the node [☊]; and the reciprocation of the nodes which you hint — at last discovered by your own effort [tuo Marte] — which had been unknown alike to ancients and to moderns, nay even to certain most ancient and most recent authors, about the Moon itself, the lowest star, and whose observation might seem the easiest of all. Nor do I now doubt that, from these foundations of yours, not only the more recent, but even the most ancient φαινόμενα [phenomena] can be had more exactly. For the motion of the Moon once had altogether the same reckoning — or at least a closely similar one, mutatis mutandis — as that which you have just now drawn out by your most accurate observations with [your] most exactly-rectified instruments.

Nor do those observations had by Ptolemy and the ancients move me, since it is more than established how crudely [crassa Minerva] those ancient artificers handled astronomical observations — destitute not only of suitable instruments, but for the most part also of a precise method of calculation; which the booklets of Aristarchus of Samos, Autolycus, and Hipparchus of Bithynia that have reached us will easily make credible to anyone. For mostly they sought, broadly [ἐν πλάτει], some perhaps harmonic proportion, or some round number which seemed not to differ much from the truth, and content with that, valued certain few minutes as nothing — nay, made not much even of a quarter of one degree. Ptolemy himself, too, as to the Moon's latitude of which you write — since he acquiesced in Eratosthenes's finding, by which he had set the distance of the solstitial points to the whole circle to be (approximately [ἔγγιστα]) as 11 to 83 — consequently, by necessity, by the observation-method he used, on account of that excessive obliquity of the Zodiac which he assumed, had to infer a smaller-than-just latitude of the Moon. But if — as is fit to be believed — the obliquity of the Zodiac in Ptolemy's time was not altogether as great as Hipparchus and Ptolemy admitted it (from the aforesaid opinion of Eratosthenes), then from Ptolemy's very own observation it can be inferred that the Moon's latitude in the quadratures was then greater, too, than the round 5 degrees reported by Ptolemy.

I heard also with pleasure that you have resolved to publish your tables of the motion of the luminaries separately, printed before the other Planets. For I hope that in this way they will reach us so much the sooner. And perhaps you would act far the most circumspectly of all — or at least amply satisfy the desires of very many, and in some part slake our thirst — if you would publish, and hasten, the tables of the motions of the luminaries alone, bare, pure, and unadorned, like the Prutenic [Tables], without all demonstration. My Muses indeed, as suppliants, beg, beseech, and implore you, that the eclipses of the luminaries may, by your benefit, be calculated more exactly so much the sooner — which would be done most conveniently from the bare tables — and meanwhile, by the example of the disciples of that great Pythagoras, we may, deservedly and by best right, allege "τὸ αὐτός ἔφα" [the "he himself said it"] in place of reason, until you arrange that apparatus of your observations and demonstrations.

You have already communicated to me — which I report with the greatest giving of thanks — the whole reckoning of your Lunar System, so that, besides the epochs of the mean motions and the daily mean motion of any [body], hardly anything seems left over as to the Moon. And if as much were done for the Sun, the equation of time, etc., one could already, by multiplications and resolutions of triangles, complete the calculation of Eclipses without further tables. I report this because I care little for the multiplications and triangle-resolutions that can supply the place of tables of mean motions and equations. For I am wont, without the circuits of multiplication, by the help of addition alone, to find by an easiest path the number to be produced by multiplication; and likewise, without all the labour and tedium of division, by the help of subtraction alone, to find the quotient — in any kind of calculus, decimal or sexagesimal — and on that account, without distaste or expenditure of time, to resolve any triangles easily, even if the radius be as great as Valentin Otho's. But this one thing I singularly long for: that I may calculate some ancient eclipses of the luminaries from the data and principles of that great Tycho Brahe. And if by any means, law, or condition I can obtain this very thing — to subject some ancient eclipses (which I have collected, hitherto neither noticed nor calculated by others) to your most exact calculation, and to bring to you the Aristarchus of Mathematics — I swear most sacredly, by God the supreme, Best and Greatest, on every event, this my handwriting witness, a strong oath [καρτερὸν ὅρκον], that I will never communicate to anyone anything of what is required to institute such a calculation. And would that I could by some kind of service requite a benefit of this sort conferred on me; I would certainly try, to my strength, to render thanks. But pardon me, I pray, if the burning ardour of desire with which I long for these things wrings out, willy-nilly, importunate wishes. And if that epidemic plague — which is said to have invaded the places between us, and especially the public lodgings — did not deter me, I would myself set out on a journey to you, [to see] whether perhaps in this way I might attain my wish.

My most clement Princes — for you desire to know this — are not much affected by these studies; yet they love letters and the learned, so that I hope that gift of yours, of which you write, will be welcome and acceptable to them; to me, certainly, it will be most welcome. My most serene Prince has a most well-equipped Library, procured at royal expense, and filled with manuscript books — Hebrew, Greek, etc. I send, joined to this, an index of the mathematical books, which I would have you keep with you, that if perchance some book occur which you desire from them, you may inform me of it. These [things] in haste, the courier urging. Fare happily, and continue to pursue me with your favour. And if in any matter I can be of advantage or use to you — whether in these parts, or in those things that can be conveyed to you through these regions — command and order.

Given at Munich, the 17th day of September, the year [15]99.
Most zealous of Your most noble Lordship,
Johann Georg Herwart von Hohenburg etc., in his own hand.

Apparatus

  • Direction & correspondent. Incoming, Hans Georg Herwart von Hohenburg (Bavarian chancellor, Munich) → Tycho. The earlier Herwart letter (cf. the Kepler-on-Ursus transcript, brahe-t_16000911001, Sept 1600).
  • Significance — the reception of Tycho's lunar theory, and early prosthaphaeretic computation.
  • Tycho's lunar discoveries celebrated. Herwart responds with delight to Tycho's lunar hypothesis (sent with a worked eclipse-calculation), naming Tycho's genuine novelties: the "variation" (semi-menstrual, from the double Moon–Sun motion); the annual equation (depending on the Sun's/node's distance); and the reciprocation of the nodes — "discovered by your own effort," unknown to ancients and moderns alike. (The same lunar discoveries Tycho announced to Scaliger, brahe-t_16000723001.)
  • Critique of ancient data. Herwart argues the ancients (Ptolemy, Aristarchus, Autolycus, Hipparchus) worked "with crude Minerva," seeking round numbers "broadly [ἐν πλάτει]"; and gives a sharp technical argument that Ptolemy's assumed (too-large) zodiac obliquity — via Eratosthenes's 11:83 ratio — forced too small a lunar latitude, so the true quadrature-latitude exceeded Ptolemy's round 5°.
  • The plea for bare tables. Herwart begs Tycho to publish the solar/lunar tables alone — "bare, pure, unadorned, like the Prutenic Tables, without demonstration" — first, so eclipses can be computed sooner; meanwhile he'll trust Tycho on "ipse dixit" (τὸ αὐτός ἔφα) authority. (A pointed contrast with Scaliger's rejection of αὐτός ἔφα in brahe-t_16000310001.)
  • Proto-logarithmic computation. Herwart describes his computational shortcuts: finding products "by addition alone" and quotients "by subtraction alone," in decimal or sexagesimal — i.e. prosthaphaeresis (the trigonometric product-to-sum method that preceded logarithms) — enabling him to resolve triangles even with Valentin Otho's very large radius (the Opus Palatinum). A notable datum in the pre-history of logarithms.
  • The ancient-eclipse project & oath. Herwart's "one wish": to compute ancient eclipses he has collected (unnoticed by others) from Tycho's data — calling Tycho "the Aristarchus of Mathematics" — and swears a sacred oath (καρτερὸν ὅρκον) of total secrecy about Tycho's methods. He would travel to Tycho but for the plague on the roads.
  • The Bavarian ducal library. His Princes (the Dukes of Bavaria) love "letters and the learned"; Tycho's intended gift (the Mechanica) will be welcome; the ducal Library (Hebrew/Greek manuscripts) is offered — Herwart sends an index of its mathematical books for Tycho to mark desiderata.
  • The Venus-star thread. Herwart had theorised that a reported disappearance of Venus was a Mercury-occultation (a Venus–Mercury morning conjunction with Mercury below, per the Prutenic Tables); Tycho's "weighty reasons" resolved his doubt. (Connects to Scaliger's debunking of the Venus-star chronology, brahe-t_16000310001, and the chronology disputes.)
  • Cross-references. Route with the lunar-theory thread (brahe-t_16000723001/16000623001) and the Venus-star/chronology thread (brahe-t_16000310001, brahe-t_16000911001); a key document for the reception of Tycho's lunar work and early prosthaphaeresis.
  • Persons named. Herwart von Hohenburg; Rudolf II; Ptolemy, Aristarchus of Samos, Autolycus, Hipparchus of Bithynia, Eratosthenes, Pythagoras; Valentin Otho; the Dukes of Bavaria (Maximilian I / Wilhelm V). Places: Munich, Benátky.
  • Greek/terms reproduced. φαινόμενα, ἐν πλάτει, ἔγγιστα, τὸ αὐτός ἔφα, καρτερὸν ὅρκον; tuo Marte ("by your own effort"), crassa Minerva ("crudely"). Glyphs ☌ ♀ ☿ ☉ ☾ ☊ reproduced.
