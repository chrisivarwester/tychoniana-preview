ILLUSTRI ET EXCELLENTISSIMO VIRO DOMINO IOSEPHO SCALIGERO, IUL. CÆS. FILIO, MAGNO ACADEMIAe LUGDUNENSIS DECORI, AMICO SUO INPRIMIS HONORANDO.
S. P.

PERMAGNO equidem gaudio me tuis cultissimis et humanissimis literis affecisti, Vir Illustris, Amice honorande, partim quod de statu mearum rerum, postquam Dania excessi, te sincere sollicitum probent, partim quod de recondita circa Anni et siderum rationes contemplatione mecum solerter agant. Pro qua tua in nos benevola affectione, quodque meum TENGNAGLIUM adeo comiter et humaniter exceperis, et habeo et semper habiturus sum gratias. De abitus vero mei occasionibus e natali solo, quae non leves aut exiguae erant, deque locis et conditionibus, quibus postea usus sum, nihil hîc referam, indignum reputans, tuis auribus talia occinere. Nec ego hac sorte quidpiam moveor, cum oporteat Philosophiae studijs addictum κοσμοπολίτην esse, nec ullis mutationibus locorum temporum aut hominum affici, sed eodem in quavis rerum et eventuum vicissitudine sibi constare animo, ac fluctuantis insipientisque Mundi perversa iudicia ex alto negligere. De ijs etiam, quae tibi ob eximiam virtutem et doctrinae praecellentiam ignavorum et malevolorum peperit invidia, nihil addam, cum et hanc superare magnanimi sit ingenij. Tales aemuli et Zoili non alijs, sed sibiipsis detrahunt, suamque fatuitatem cordatioribus ludibrio exponunt, ut graviore opus non habeant vindicta quam propria. His itaque praetermissis et sublimi firmoque animo posthabitis, ad ea quae cognitionem non postremam sapiunt, et a te mota sunt, accedamus.

Sphalmata, quae Eruditissimo tuo volumini, arduo summoque labore comparato de Emendatione temporum per incuriam aliquam erepserunt, nemo intelligens et sani iudicij tibi imputârit; sed aut Typographo aut occupationum tuarum moli. Talia etiam mihi (si parva licet componere magnis) in meis quoque editis scriptis, ubi ea denuo inspicio, subinde contigisse animadverto, quae tamen nemo nisi malus et verborum potius quam rerum otiosus aestimator cavillatus fuerit. Nostras aequinoctiorum observationes tantopere a te probari plurimum mihi congratulor. Et si liceret mihi de meis inventis id, quod res est, testari, multo sane auro redimerem, Antecessorum nostrorum sive veterum sive recentium tam exquisita diligentia administratas fuisse animadversiones. Certe anni Coelestis subtilissima dimensio, a qua plurima alia dependent, nos non ita fluctuantes et incertos redderet. Solis in aequinoctialia puncta ingressum dimetiri, nedum alia, ubi declinatio eius minus variat, altiori indiget molimine quam putatur, alijsque medijs et Organis, quam vel Antiquos in usu habuisse, aut etiam REGIOMONTANUM et COPERNICUM eorumque coaetaneos adhibuisse legimus, indiget. Oportet enim ea tanta tamque solide et exquisite elaborata esse, ut intra sextam Minuti unius partem Altitudinem Solis meridianam discernere queant. Nec uni sed pluribus potius standum, collatione accurata, prout a nobis non saltem illo quod produxisti quinquennio, sed et plurimis alijs Annis pari praecisione factitatum est. Habenda etiam ratio Parallaxeos Solis, tum quoque refractionis in decliviore praesertim situ. Quae omnia cum vetustus ille et in scrutandis Luminarium motibus laboriosissimus HIPPARCHUS vix praecaverit, non mirum est, ipsius Aequinoctiorum observata tantopere claudicare, ut intra diei quadrantem sibiipsis non consentiant. Quae tamen aliquatenus inter se ob refractionum et Parallαξεων neglectas implicationes conciliare tentavimus. At vix intra duas vel tres horas id praestare licuit, cum instrumentum, quo usus est, singula Minuta non exhibuerit, sed solummodo duodecimam (uti opinor) gradus partem. Quod vel ipse prodit, dum nimis lato modo Aequinoctia aut in Horizonte aut in Meridiano, neglectâ ubique sex horarum differentia, contigisse assumat potius quam persuadeat, suis nimirum intra has metas diffisus Organis. PTOLEMÆUM quidem adhuc minori certitudine haec et alia scrutatum, multa ab ipso ea, qua opus fuit, praecisione non enucleata patefaciunt. Attamen illum totum diem in ingressibus Aequinoctiorum hallucinatum fuisse non dixerim. Oportuisset enim eum sic per quincuncem fere gradus Solis declinationem admittere erroneam, quod ea instrumentorum magnitudo et elaboratio, qua Tropicorum intercapedinem sive Eclipticae obliquationem intra tertiam minuti partem rimatus est (nisi et hanc ab HIPPARCHO mutuabatur) non patitur. Sic et Lunae latitudo maxima ab illo per Parallaticum deprehensa adeo magnam Instrumenti fallaciam non arguit. Si omnia loca, quae in eo suspecta habes, denotaveris et mecum unâ cum observationibus HIPPARCHI ad annos et dies Iulianos retro subactos, addita indicatione bissextilium communicaveris, inprimis gratum, nec inutile (uti spero) praestiteris officium.

Scripsit ad me studiosus ille Danus, olim mihi domesticus, qui iam apud vos est, CHRISTIANUS IOHANNIS, penes te esse librum de HIPPARCHI observatis, in Italia ante aliquot annos Graece editum, cuius exemplar impressum, aut meis sumtibus descriptum unâ cum tuis annotationibus marginalibus, ubi potissimum quid erroris aut dubij subesse putaveris, si impertieris, non modico me affeceris beneficio. ALBATEGNIJ quoque, quem et MAHOMETEM Aratensem (uti scis) vocant, opusculum castigatum (plurimis enim id quod extat scatet mendis) unâ cum ijs quas author ipse subiunxerat Tabulis, obtinere averem, tum quoque si quid praeterea apud te, antiquitatum solertissimum excultorem, similium librorum repertum fuerit, quod Astronomiam in ipso Coelo, non solis chartis quaesitam illustret. Optime sane feceris, si me de ijs reddideris certiorem, et conatus nostros ita non parum iuveris.

Quae de Aequinoctiali eiusque polis mobilibus, quodque differant a polo Mundi disseris, in ijs tibi adstipulari non possum, aliter me instruente ipsa experientia. Deprehendi enim ex mutata latitudine fixarum iuxta proportionem mutatae obliquitatis Signiferi, a temporibus TIMOCHARIS, HIPPARCHI et PTOLEMÆI, hucusque (si modo eorum observata in angulo obliquationis maximae et caeteris omni vitio careant, qua de re non citra rationes dubito) Eclipticam potius quam Aequatorem cum suis polis instabilem esse, Sole non semper eandem multo seculorum interiectu describente, et una ea loca, quibus aequinoctialem transit, successive anticipante. Unde fit ut stellae fixae tantundem progredi videantur, quantum Sol haec puncta anteriora reddit. Cumque id illi motui, qui convenit defectui Anni Coelestis ab Anno Iuliano, non plane coaequetur, fieri nequit, ut stellae fixae ijsdem Anni Iuliani diebus per aliquot seculorum intervalla cum Sole oriantur vel occidant, alijs etiam concurrentibus causis, quae discrepantiam pariant. Et licet id paucis quibusdam stellis utcunque per longum Annorum intervallum competere possit, tamen non semper aut omnibus. Nec etiam ulla est (uti tam in Opere tuo quam hîc infers) inter polum Aequinoctialis et Mundi discrepantia; sunt enim unum et idem. Neque extrema in cauda Ursae Minoris, quam Polarem vocant, quod prope polum sit, Mundi polus est, aut proxima huic, nisi aequinoctialis polum intellexeris, qui a Mundano (uti dixi) non differt. Distitit quidem haec stella temporibus HIPPARCHI a polo Aequatoris 12⅖ partibus. At hoc anno iuxta nostra Inventa appropinquavit illi intra G. 2 M. 51½, quam ante annos 25 praecise tribus gradibus ab eodem removeri, Augustae Vindelicorum per quadrantem 14 cubitorum in suo Radio, illic a nobis in horto prope praedium Consulis HEINZELIJ extructum deprehensum est. Non tamen ob id haec stella ullius orbis polus est, aut prope, sed hic accessus ad polum Aequatoris sive Mundi fit per mutatam eius longitudinem circa polos Eclipticae, quâ illius declinatio augetur, cum sit nunc circa finem signi ♊; tempore autem HIPPARCHI circa finem ♉ versabatur, confectis interea secundum longitudinem paulo magis 24 gradibus, latitudine autem non plus alterata, quam fert obliquitatis Eclipticae (modo sic se haberet) per tertiam gradus partem decrementum. Nam ea quam PTOLEMÆI Abacus in 66 exacte ponit gradibus, lato modo sumta est. Et si haec stella nostro Aevo ad Aequatorem referatur, non cadet in Colurum aequinoctialem, ut tu forte existimas, sed distabit ab hoc in partibus Aequatoris G. 5¾, testante idipsum ratâ experientiâ. Coniungi autem ipsa cum polo Aequatoris exacte nunquam poterit. Nam post annos proxime 500, ubi initium ♋ simulque Colurum Solstitialem assecuta fuerit, distabit a polo versus Eclipticam sive Aequatorem 27½ minutis. Etsi enim Eclipticae inclinatio paulo aliter tunc forte aucta fuerit (quod tamen vix futurum existimarim) tamen haec latitudinem saltem stellae alterabit, nec eam polo ob id propius admovebit, stellis nimirum fixos Eclipticae polos respicientibus, Sole autem nonnihil mobiles per secula describente, quatenus antiquorum traditis acquiescendum erit. Quare haec quae de diversitate polorum Mundi et Aequatoris deque stella polari quacunque tandem occasione concepisti, penitius tibi expendenda relinquo.

De libello PTOLEMÆI, in quo stellarum ortus et occasus ad singulos dies Anni Actiaci et Iuliani per aliquot Climata designantur, libenter audio, quae adducis. Da operam, ut eum mature obtineamus. Circa Asterismorum ordinationes etiamsi aliqua cum Recentioribus incidat nonnunquam differentia, nihil turbabit negotium, modo constet, cuius stellae fiat mentio. Anni Syderei ab Aegyptijs et Babilonijs introducta, posteaque a THEBITIO exculta, a COPERNICO vero nostro aevo renovata ratiocinatio, non ita vana atque inutilis est, prout existimas, si modo exacte constaret. Nos eam in Progymnasmatis Astronomicis adhuc accuratius castigavimus, quatenus Ptolemaicae cum nostris collatae ferunt Observationes: Sed ubi Hipparcheas in aliqua copia per te nactus fuero, hanc ipsam subtilius limitabo. Nam et ego potius cum HIPPARCHO quam PTOLEMÆO standum tecum censeo. Existimo autem non minus quam COPERNICUS stellas fixas quoad se immotas perpetuo manere. Progressionem vero illam, quam efficere videntur, per Axem Terrae in sua revolutione anticipatum libratumque, uti is voluit, non admitto, cum nihil eiuscemodi revera Terrae competat, at si veterum tradita omnimode rite constare credendum, verisimilius erit, ipsum Solem aliam atque aliam diversis seculis describere Eclipticam. Et quantulacunque sic subesse possit inaequalitas, eam per Solis universalem Hypothesin, prout e praeteritis Artificum observationibus enucleare licuerit, favente Numine salvabimus. Atque huc conducet Anni Syderei ratio, ut et in Planetarum simplici motu a certo et immobili puncto deducendo, et accuratius quam hactenus stabiliendo. Neque quidpiam infringit huius anni commoditatem, quod Caniculam seu Syrij lucidam, ut veteres hanc vocarunt, per 15 secula eodem proxime die Iuliano exortam esse asseveres. Id quod in hac stella quodammodo fieri potuisse tibi concesserim, ut ob id hac experientia edocti, antiquitus Annum Iulianum Cinicum appellarint. Nam et ego Geometrice ad motiones huius stellae interea factas adoptato calculo, eam in Horizonte Graecorum, ubi polus 40 plus minus gradibus attollitur, ortum suum cum Sole a PTOLEMÆI temporibus per 1500 Annos retro numeratos non nisi unico die Anni Iuliani anticipasse deprehendo. Quae differentia illis pene insensibilis, siquidem non satis scrupulose haec comperta habebant. Fit tamen ea convenientia per accidens in hac stella ob declinationem intervallo tot seculorum ita variatam (nimirum tribus gradibus, dempta una tertia) ut haec eam alterationem, quae ex Anni Iuliani ac Syderei disconvenientia incidere potuit, emendarit et quasi eliserit. Quod nihilominus in alijs stellis pariter non contingit ob aliam atque aliam declinationum alteratarum vicissitudinem, ut de curriculi Solis inaequali ad eosdem dies revolutione per longum temporis tractum ob Apogaei eiusdem mutationem nihil nunc dicam. Imo ne quidem in hac ipsa stella, si a PTOLEMÆO hucusque pari modo fiat periculum, adeo constanter diem Iulianum observari comprobabitur; differentia nunc usque in tertium diem sese extendente, eo quod tardior fuerit posterioribus seculis declinationis variatio, quam illis antecedentibus, nec reliquo motui adeo analoga. Et si quis post totidem secula in futurum idem tentet, fiet adhuc maior differentia, utpote quae in quintum diem excrescet. Quin et de Arcturo, quod ais, eum tempore HESIODI eadem die Anni Iuliani nempe VIII Martij ortum fuisse, qua idipsum prodiderunt in eo tractu METON et EUTEMON, numerationi diligentiori non congruit, si modo recte temporum intervalla nobis constiterint. PTOLEMÆI quidem aevo nona die Martij unico saltem posterius oriri potuit. HESIODI autem, quod noningentis Annis anterius statuimus, secunda die Martij per 7 nimirum dies prius exoriebatur. At tempore METONIS, qui vix 150 Annis post HESIODUM floruit, idem ortus in tertium diem Martij cadebat. Hisce autem proximis annis decimum nonum ferme diem attingit, ut ob id, quomodo Arcturi exortus ad certum diem Anni Iuliani multis seculis alligari queat, prout tua insinuat annotatio, non videam. Sic aliae et diversae stellae diversimodam quoque in hac pragmatεία ingerunt variationem, ut hinc nihil certi de Annorum rationibus mutuari liceat. Anni coelestis periodus in ijs quidem, quas accepisti, impressis chartis a nobis GELALAeo paulo minor adhibita est. Hanc tamen non tanquam universalem illic adduximus, sed solummodo ab Observationibus REGIOMONTANI per unicum Annorum centenarium huc translatam; quod certo consilio fecimus, ut Apogaei et Eccentricitatis, si qua subreperet incomperta mutatio, minus officeret, praesertim cum ad tria vel quatuor duntaxat secula motum Solis inibi emendatiorem tradere intenderimus. At si altius (quod praestat) id petatur, interlapsis plurimis seculis proveniet hic Annus paulo maior GELALAeo, nec tamen in tantum, ut Alphonsinum modum, cui tu nunc subscribis, assequatur. Qua de re praecisius aliquid, quam antehac statuere licebit, ubi omnibus HIPPARCHI observationibus me instructiorem reddideris, tum quoque si quas alias adhuc vetustiores aut intermedias in promtu habueris. Cuperem etiam antiquissimi illius Iudaici Aequinoctij, cuius meministi, exactam a te cognitionem obtinere, quanto nimirum tempore, quoad annos Iulianos a nostris observatis retro distiterit; ita tamen, ut iste annus, quo hoc Aequinoctium celebratum creditur, pariter a bissextili removeatur cum aliquo e nostris, in quibus ob id quinquennium apponere libuit, quo semper aliquis in promptu esset, qui sic cum vetustis inventis conferri commodius posset. Oportet enim ubique semper hanc collationem fieri in ijs Annis, qui vel pariter bissextiles sunt, vel a bissextili aequaliter distant, quod et in prioribus literis me monuisse recordor. Petis, ut stellarum Eclipticae vicinarum, praesertim Reguli et πρόποδος ♊ communicem loca atque exortus cum diebus Iulianis. Scias itaque cor ♌ Anno CHRISTI 1600 Dionysiaco completo (quo vulgariter, licet inepte, uti recte nos edocuisti, utimur) incidere, quoad longitudinem in G. 24 M. 17 ♌ cum latitudine G. 0 M. 27 Bor. tuncque obtinere Ascensionem Rectam p. 146 M. 46 ac declinationem p. 13 M. 54, ideoque oriri in Horizonte illo Graecorum cum 24 g. ♌ si completo, quod cosmice contingit 7. Augusti, Acronyce vero 2. Februarij. Et in nostra quoque latitudine intra eosdem dies tam cosmice quam Acronyce emergit. Propus ♊ longitudinem ad dictum tempus habet p. 27 M. 53, latitudinem vero p. 0 M. 58 Merid., Ascensionem Rectam p. 87 M. 42, declinationem p. 22 M. 33 Septentrionalem. Unde in eodem Boeotico tractu nunc oritur cum 29no ferme completo ♊, id est 10. Iunij cosmice, inter 9um et 10mum Decembris Acronyce.

Ut vero plurium stellarum, imo quotcunque et qualiumcunque volueris, loca ipsi coelo adamussim congrua in promptu habeas, atque hinc quicquid placuerit, circa earum habitudines sive veterum sive nostro aevo rimari queas, en hîc peculiari libro mille stellarum (plures enim in nostro climate vix observationi patent) exactissimam in ipso minuto, adeoque interdum semisse, quoad longum et latum restitutionem intra 20 elapsos annos magno labore et diligentia a nobis exantlatam, qualem (absit gloriatio) nulla viderunt priora secula, tecum secreto communico. Redegimus autem omnium longitudines consulto ad Annum vulgarem 1600 completum, quibus ALBATEGNIJ aetas G. 10 exacte, PTOLEMÆI 21⅖, HIPPARCHI 24¼, TIMOCHARIS 25¾ subtrahunt; latitudine pro ratione variatae inclinationis Eclipticae ad Aequatorem in singulis secundum earum peculiarem dispositionem nonnihil hinc inde crescente vel decrescente. Quae tamen differentia, ultra tertiam Gradus partem, ubi vel maxima est, hactenus alterata esse non invenitur, imo et in hac tantillula mutatione non parum subest dubij. Atque haec prout ipsi Artifices loca praecipuarum aliquot quarundam fixarum suo aevo prodiderunt, in quibus tamen, PTOLEMÆO praesertim et TIMOCHARI aliquem subrepsisse errorem, quo minus ea satis subtiliter scrutati sint, ob nimiam inaequalitatem suspicor, ALBATEGNIJ et HIPPARCHI denotationibus magis ratis exsistentibus. Ex quibus etiam praecipue collegi, inerrantes stellas singulis annis 51" conficere; quantum videlicet motui Solis simplici competit, in ea temporis differentiolâ, quae est inter annum Aequinoctialem et Sydereum, quam nos statuimus M. 20⅔ quamproxime. Quare in Annis Iulianis 70 Mensibus 7 unum Gradum absolvunt stellae, non autem centenis 30 (uti voluit PTOLEMÆUS) longe minus pluribus, prout tu existimare videris. Oporteret enim sic supra modum erroneas fuisse antecessorum animadversiones, quod nimis licentiose asseverare nemini fas est. Additus est iuxta calcem eiusdem libri catalogus 100 insigniorum stellarum Declinationes et Ascensiones Rectas ad annum 1600 et 1700 exhibens unâ cum suis differentijs, per quas non saltem ad intermedium seculum, sed et proxime antecedens ac subsequens hae utrinque satis commode adaptari queant. Si vero ortus et occasus earum ad certum Horizontem ratione Solis scire aves, id REGIOMONTANI Tabulae ex data declinatione et Ascensione Recta non difficulter suppeditabunt, aut, si mavis, per Triangula. Ut autem locus Solis e nostra emendatione etiam tibi verius quam alias constet, addidi Ephemeridem motum eius ad Meridiem singulorum dierum huius labentis Anni in longitudine locorum 37 partium expedientem. Inde ad plures antecedentes et sequentes eundem intra unum vel alterum Minutum derivare, exigui est laboris. At si praecisus hunc rimari lubuerit, utere meis Tabulis in motu Solis, quas puto te in capite illo primo de curriculo illius a nobis restituto (undecumque tandem tecum communicato) accepisse. Sin minus, habes hîc in omnem eventum quaternionem eius calculo ad proxima aliquot secula sufficientem. Mitto insuper alium librum, Mechanica Astronomiae nostra complectentem, quem tamen ordine primum statuo. Eo inspecto non obscure aestimabis, quanto molimine, quantaque diligentia talia tractârim. Et si lubuerit unum vel alterum Instrumentum ad instar eorum confici curare, habes inibi Idaeam et usum. Nec enim inutile foret, si Sextantem quempiam pro Syderum distantijs et Quadrantem iustae magnitudinis pro eorundem altitudinibus dimetiendis tibi paraveris. Qua in re praenominatus ille studiosus Danus tam quoad divisiones perficiendas quam caetera ordinanda tibi usui esse poterit; unâque in Praxin observationum opus diducere, prout apud me didicit; optarem enim, multis in locis coelestibus diligenter attendi, ne idem fiat nostro, quod de ROMULI aevo conqueritur NASO:
Et inobservata per annum
Sydera currebant.
Uti etiam ferme (quod dolendum) nunc accidit.

Facis quoque sub finem mentionem anni Liliani sive Pontificij, tam inepte constituti quam approbati. Quem licet omnibus suis numeris non exactissime constantem (quis enim hoc inconsulto coelo praestiterit?) convenientiorem tamen priore illo omnino censeo, qui tot dierum ob anni Aequinoctialis et Iuliani aliquale discrimen anticipationem commisit, inde ab initio annorum CHRISTI, vel (ut illi maluerunt) a concilio Niceno. Neque tam facile posthac iuxta modum illum Gregorianum adeo enormis divagatio subrepere potest; sed (ut dicam quod res est) haec summa praecisione (si qua foret necessaria) extricare nullis eiuscemodi compendijs et diverticulis licet. Verum habenda est ipsissima motus utriusque luminaris ad quaevis secula subtilis notitia; qualem ne hoc quidem tempore ex usitatis Tabulis citra omne erroris vestigium compertam habemus. Ut primi Mensis huius Anni in Sole et Luna ad longitudinem loci 35 partium e nostris animadversionibus et Tabulis subductus calculus, atque cum Alphonsinis et Coperniceis numeris collatus (quem etiam in quadam charta, ut hinc caetera ratiocinari queas, adiunxi) te edocebit. Haec igitur, quae nunc mittere commodum fuit, boni consule. Subiungam suo tempore, favente coelestium Opifice, de coelestibus plura et maiora. Et quamvis hisce serioribus nimium tibi faciam nunc negotij, lusus nihilominus insuper quosdam poëticos e multis quos habeo, in mei gratiam, postquam Dania excessi, hinc inde a diversis concinnatos, mihique missos unâ impertior, non quod tua Heroica vena quid dignum redoleant, sed tantum ut videas, alijs quoque meam inde emigrationem cordi esse. Selegi autem potissimum haec, in quibus discessus nostri causae utcunque insinuantur. To the illustrious and most excellent man, Lord Joseph Scaliger, son of Julius Caesar [Scaliger], the great ornament of the Academy of Leiden, his especially-to-be-honoured friend.
Heartiest greeting.

You have indeed affected me with very great joy by your most cultivated and most kind letter, illustrious Man, honoured Friend — partly because it proves you sincerely solicitous about the state of my affairs since I left Denmark, partly because it deals skilfully with me about the recondite contemplation of the reckonings of the Year and of the stars. For this benevolent affection of yours toward us, and because you received my Tengnagel so courteously and kindly, I both give and shall always give thanks. But about the occasions of my departure from my native soil — which were neither slight nor small — and about the places and conditions I have since made use of, I shall report nothing here, deeming it unworthy to drone such things in your ears. Nor am I moved at all by this lot, since one devoted to the studies of Philosophy ought to be a cosmopolite [κοσμοπολίτην, a citizen of the world], affected by no changes of places, times, or men, but remaining constant to himself, with the same mind, in every vicissitude of affairs and events, and disregarding from on high the perverse judgments of a fluctuating and foolish World. About those things too which the envy of the slothful and malevolent has bred for you, on account of your outstanding virtue and excellence of learning, I shall add nothing, since to overcome this also belongs to a magnanimous nature. Such rivals and Zoili [carping critics] detract not from others but from themselves, and expose their own foolishness to the mockery of the more sensible, so that they need no graver vengeance than their own. So, these things being passed over and set aside with a lofty and firm mind, let us proceed to those matters which savour of no mean knowledge, and have been raised by you.

The slips [sphalmata] which, by some carelessness, have crept into your most erudite volume On the Emendation of Times [De emendatione temporum], compiled with arduous and supreme labour, no intelligent person of sound judgment would impute to you, but either to the Typographer or to the mass of your occupations. Such things have happened to me too (if one may compare small things with great), in my own published writings, when I inspect them again — as I notice from time to time — which, however, no one but a malicious and idle appraiser of words rather than of things would cavil at. That our observations of the equinoxes are so greatly approved by you, I most heartily congratulate myself. And if it were permitted me to testify about my findings what the truth is, I should surely redeem with much gold [the wish] that the observations of our predecessors, whether ancient or recent, had been administered with such exquisite diligence [as they were not]. Certainly the most subtle measurement of the celestial year, on which very many other things depend, would not then render us so fluctuating and uncertain. To measure the Sun's ingress into the equinoctial points — let alone the other [points], where its declination varies less — needs a loftier effort than is thought, and needs other means and Instruments than we read that either the Ancients had in use, or even Regiomontanus and Copernicus and their contemporaries employed. For the instruments must be so great, and so solidly and exquisitely elaborated, that they can discern the Sun's meridian altitude within a sixth part of one minute [10″]. And one must rely not on one but rather on several [observations], with accurate comparison, as has been done by us with equal precision, not only in that quinquennium which you have adduced, but in very many other Years as well. Account must also be taken of the Sun's parallax, and likewise of refraction, especially in a lower [declining] position. Since that ancient Hipparchus — most laborious in scrutinizing the motions of the Luminaries — scarcely guarded against all these, it is no wonder that his observations of the equinoxes limp so greatly that they do not agree among themselves within a quarter of a day. Which, however, we have attempted to reconcile to some extent, on account of the neglected complications of refractions and parallaxes [Παραλλάξεων]. But scarcely within two or three hours was it possible to achieve this, since the instrument he used did not exhibit single minutes, but only (as I think) a twelfth part of a degree [5′]. Which he himself even betrays, when he assumes — rather than persuades us — that the Equinoxes occurred, in too broad a manner, either on the Horizon or on the Meridian, the six-hour difference being everywhere neglected, distrusting (within these limits) his own Instruments. That Ptolemy indeed scrutinized these and other things with still less certainty, many things not unfolded by him with the needful precision make plain. Yet I would not say that he was deceived by a whole day in the ingresses of the Equinoxes. For he would thus have had to admit the Sun's declination erroneous by nearly a quincunx of a degree [5⁄12° = 25′] — which the magnitude and elaboration of those instruments (by which he investigated the interval of the Tropics, or the obliquity of the Ecliptic, within a third part of a minute [20″] — unless he borrowed this too from Hipparchus) does not allow. So too the Moon's maximum latitude, ascertained by him by means of the Parallactic [the triquetrum], does not argue so great a fallacy of the Instrument. If you mark all the places which you hold suspect in it [Ptolemy], and share them with me, together with Hipparchus's observations reduced backward to Julian years and days (with an indication of the leap-years added), you will perform a service especially welcome, and (as I hope) not useless.

That Danish student, once a member of my household, who is now with you, Christian Johnson [Christianus Iohannis], wrote me that you have in your keeping a book on Hipparchus's observations, published in Greek in Italy some years ago — of which, if you would impart a printed copy, or one transcribed at my expense, together with your marginal annotations (where you especially think some error or doubt to lie beneath), you would affect me with no small benefit. I should also be eager to obtain the corrected little work of Albategnius — whom they also call Mahomet of al-Raqqa [Muḥammad al-Battānī], as you know — (for what is extant teems with very many errors), together with the Tables the author himself had subjoined; and likewise, if anything else of similar books be found with you — most skilful cultivator of antiquities that you are — which may illustrate Astronomy sought in the Heaven itself, not in papers alone. You will surely do excellently if you inform me about these, and so help our endeavours not a little.

The things you discuss about the Equator and its mobile poles, and that they differ from the pole of the World, in these I cannot agree with you, experience itself instructing me otherwise. For I have ascertained, from the changed latitude of the fixed [stars] (according to the proportion of the changed obliquity of the Zodiac), from the times of Timocharis, Hipparchus, and Ptolemy down to now — provided their observations on the angle of maximum obliquity, and the rest, lack all fault (about which I doubt not without reason) — that it is the Ecliptic, rather than the Equator, that is unstable with its poles: the Sun not always describing the same [ecliptic] over the great interposition of centuries, and at the same time successively anticipating those places at which it crosses the equator. Whence it comes about that the fixed stars seem to advance just as much as the Sun renders these points earlier. And since that [precession] is not exactly equal to the motion which corresponds to the deficit of the Celestial Year from the Julian Year, it cannot come about that the fixed stars rise or set with the Sun on the same days of the Julian Year over intervals of several centuries — other causes also concurring, which beget the discrepancy. And although that may somehow be the case for a few certain stars over a long interval of Years, yet not always, nor for all. Nor is there any discrepancy (as you infer, both in your Work and here) between the pole of the Equator and [the pole] of the World; for they are one and the same. Nor is the outermost [star] in the tail of Ursa Minor — which they call the Pole-star, because it is near the pole — the pole of the World, or the nearest [point] to this, unless you understand the pole of the equator, which (as I said) does not differ from the World's. This star, indeed, in Hipparchus's times, was distant from the pole of the Equator by 12⅖ degrees. But this year, according to our findings, it has approached it to within 2° 51½′; whereas 25 years ago it was found to be removed precisely 3 degrees from the same — [as] ascertained by us at Augsburg, by a quadrant of 14 cubits in its Radius, erected there in the garden near the estate of Consul Hainzel. Yet not for that reason is this star the pole of any orb, or near [it]; rather, this approach to the pole of the Equator (or of the World) happens through its changed longitude about the poles of the Ecliptic, by which its declination is increased — since it is now about the end of the sign Gemini [♊], whereas in Hipparchus's time it was about the end of Taurus [♉], a little more than 24 degrees having been completed meanwhile according to longitude, while the latitude is altered no more than the decrement of a third part of a degree [20′] which the Ecliptic's obliquity carries (provided it be so). For the [latitude] which Ptolemy's Table puts at exactly 66 degrees is taken in a broad manner. And if this star be referred to the Equator in our age, it will not fall on the equinoctial Colure, as you perhaps think, but will be distant from it, in degrees of the Equator, by 5¾°, established experience testifying the same. But it can never be conjoined exactly with the pole of the Equator. For after nearly 500 years, when it shall have attained the beginning of Cancer [♋] and at the same time the Solstitial Colure, it will be distant from the pole, toward the Ecliptic (or Equator), by 27½ minutes. For even if the inclination of the Ecliptic shall then perhaps have been increased somewhat otherwise (which, however, I should scarcely think will happen), yet this will alter only the star's latitude, and will not for that reason move it nearer to the pole — the stars, that is, regarding the fixed poles of the Ecliptic, while the Sun describes [poles] somewhat mobile through the centuries, insofar as one must rest in the traditions of the ancients. Wherefore these things which you have conceived — on whatever occasion — about the diversity of the poles of the World and of the Equator, and about the pole-star, I leave to you to weigh more deeply.

About the little book of Ptolemy [the Phaseis], in which the risings and settings of the stars are designated for the single days of the Actian and Julian Year through several Climes [Climata], I gladly hear what you adduce. Take pains that we may obtain it in good time. Concerning the arrangements of the Asterisms [constellations], even if some difference with the More-recent [astronomers] sometimes occurs, it will not disturb the business at all, provided it be clear of which star mention is made. The reckoning of the Sidereal Year — introduced by the Egyptians and Babylonians, afterwards cultivated by Thebit [Thābit ibn Qurra], and renewed in our age by Copernicus — is not so vain and useless as you think, if only it were exactly established. We have corrected it still more accurately in the Astronomical Progymnasmata, insofar as the Ptolemaic Observations, compared with ours, allow; but when I have obtained the Hipparchan [observations] in some quantity through you, I shall define this very thing more subtly. For I too judge, with you, that one must rather stand with Hipparchus than with Ptolemy. But I think, no less than Copernicus, that the fixed stars remain perpetually immobile in respect of themselves. That progression, however, which they seem to effect, I do not admit to come about through the Earth's Axis being anticipated and librated in its revolution, as he [Copernicus] held, since nothing of this kind in reality belongs to the Earth; but if the traditions of the ancients are to be believed entirely correct, it will be more probable that the Sun itself describes a different and different Ecliptic in different centuries. And however small an inequality may thus lie beneath, we shall save it by the universal Hypothesis of the Sun, as far as it has been possible to unfold from the past observations of the Artificers [astronomers], God favouring. And to this the reckoning of the Sidereal Year will conduce, both in deducing the Planets' simple motion from a certain and immobile point, and in establishing it more accurately than hitherto. Nor does it in any way impair the convenience of this [sidereal] year that you assert the Dog-star [Canicula] — or "the bright [star] of Sirius," as the ancients called it — to have risen on nearly the same Julian day through 15 centuries. Which I should grant you could in some manner have happened in this star, so that, taught by this experience, the ancients of old called the Julian Year "Canine" [Canicular]. For I too, by a calculation adopted geometrically to the motions of this star made meanwhile, find that, in the Horizon of the Greeks (where the pole is elevated about 40 degrees), its rising with the Sun, from Ptolemy's times reckoned back through 1500 years, has anticipated [the calendar] by only a single day of the Julian Year. Which difference was almost imperceptible to them, since they did not have these things ascertained scrupulously enough. Yet that agreement happens by accident in this star, because the declination has been so varied over the interval of so many centuries (namely by three degrees, less one third [= 2°40′]) that this [change] has corrected, and as it were cancelled out, that alteration which could have occurred from the disagreement of the Julian and the Sidereal Year. Which nonetheless does not equally happen in other stars, on account of the different and different vicissitude of the altered declinations — to say nothing now of the unequal revolution of the Sun's course to the same days over a long tract of time, on account of the change of its Apogee. Nay, not even in this very star, if the test be made in like manner from Ptolemy down to now, will it be proved that the Julian day is observed so constantly — the difference now extending up to the third day, because the variation of declination has been slower in the later centuries than in those preceding, nor so analogous to the rest of the motion. And if anyone should attempt the same as many centuries into the future, there will be a still greater difference, namely one that will grow to the fifth day. Moreover, as to Arcturus — what you say, that in Hesiod's time it rose on the same day of the Julian Year, namely 8 March, on which Meton and Euctemon reported the same in that region — does not agree with a more diligent numeration, if only the intervals of time have been correctly established for us. In Ptolemy's age, indeed, it could rise on 9 March, only one [day] later. But in Hesiod's [time], which we place 900 years earlier, it rose on 2 March, that is, 7 days earlier. But in Meton's time — who flourished scarcely 150 years after Hesiod — the same rising fell on the third day of March. Yet in these most recent years it reaches nearly the nineteenth day [of March], so that on this account I do not see how the rising of Arcturus can be bound to a fixed day of the Julian Year over many centuries, as your annotation insinuates. So other and diverse stars likewise introduce a variously-modified variation in this practical matter [pragmateia], so that from it nothing certain may be borrowed concerning the reckonings of the Years. The period of the celestial year, in those printed sheets which you have received, was employed by us [as] a little smaller than the Gelalaean [Jalālī] year. This, however, we adduced there not as universal, but only as transferred hither from the Observations of Regiomontanus over a single century of Years; which we did by a certain design, so that, if any unknown change of the Apogee and Eccentricity should creep in, it might hinder less — especially since we intended there to hand down the Sun's motion more correctly for only three or four centuries. But if it be sought higher (which is preferable), many centuries having intervened, this Year will come out a little greater than the Gelalaean, yet not so much as to attain the Alphonsine measure, to which you now subscribe. About which it will be possible to establish something more precise than heretofore, when you have rendered me better instructed with all of Hipparchus's observations, and likewise if you have at hand any others still more ancient, or intermediate. I should also wish to obtain from you exact knowledge of that most ancient Jewish Equinox which you mentioned — namely by how much time, as regards Julian years, it stood distant backward from our observations; yet in such a way that the year in which this Equinox is believed to have been celebrated be equally removed from a leap-year as some one of ours (in which, for this reason, it pleased [us] to set down a quinquennium, so that there might always be some [year] at hand which could thus be more conveniently compared with the ancient findings). For this comparison must everywhere and always be made in those Years which are either equally leap-years, or equally distant from a leap-year — which I recall having advised in my earlier letter too. You ask that I communicate the places and risings, with the Julian days, of the stars near the Ecliptic — especially of Regulus and of Propus [πρόποδος] of Gemini [♊]. Know, therefore, that the Heart of Leo [Cor Leonis = Regulus, ♌], in the Year of Christ 1600 of Dionysius completed (which we use commonly, though ineptly — as you rightly taught us), falls, as to longitude, at 24° 17′ of Leo, with a latitude of 0° 27′ North; and then has a Right Ascension of 146° 46′ and a declination of 13° 54′; and therefore rises, in that Horizon of the Greeks, with 24° of Leo completed — which happens cosmically [with the Sun] on 7 August, but acronychally [at sunset] on 2 February. And in our latitude too, within the same days, it emerges both cosmically and acronychally. Propus of Gemini has, at the said time, a longitude of 27° 53′ [of Gemini], a latitude of 0° 58′ South, a Right Ascension of 87° 42′, and a declination of 22° 33′ North. Whence in the same Boeotian region it now rises with nearly the 29th [degree] of Gemini completed — that is, on 10 June cosmically, between the 9th and 10th of December acronychally.

But so that you may have at hand the places of more stars — nay, of however many, and of whatever kind, you wish — exactly congruent to the heaven itself, and may hence investigate whatever you please concerning their relations, whether of the ancients or of our age: behold, here, in a special book of a thousand stars (for more scarcely lie open to observation in our clime), I communicate to you in secret the most exact restitution — to the very minute, and so sometimes to the half [30″] — as regards longitude and latitude, drawn out by us, with great labour and diligence, within the 20 elapsed years: such as (let boasting be absent) no prior centuries have seen. We have reduced the longitudes of all, by design, to the common Year 1600 completed; from which the age of Albategnius subtracts exactly 10°, that of Ptolemy 21⅖°, of Hipparchus 24¼°, of Timocharis 25¾°; the latitude, according to the proportion of the changed inclination of the Ecliptic to the Equator, increasing or decreasing somewhat, in each [star] according to its particular disposition, this way or that. Which difference [in latitude], however, is not found to have been altered, even where it is greatest, beyond a third part of a degree [20′]; nay, in this tiny change too no small doubt lies beneath. And these [places are given] as the Artificers themselves reported the places of some few of the principal fixed stars in their age — in which, however, I suspect that some error crept in, especially for Ptolemy and Timocharis (whereby they did not scrutinize them subtly enough, on account of the excessive inequality), the notations of Albategnius and Hipparchus existing as more reliable. From which I have also chiefly gathered that the fixed stars accomplish 51″ each year — as much, namely, as belongs to the Sun's simple motion in that little difference of time which is between the Equinoctial [tropical] year and the Sidereal, which we establish at very nearly 20⅔ minutes [of time]. Wherefore, in 70 Julian Years and 7 Months, the stars complete one Degree — not in a hundred [years and] 30 (as Ptolemy held), [and] far [from being completed] in many more, as you seem to think. For [the predecessors'] observations would thus have had to have been erroneous beyond measure — which it is lawful for no one to assert too licentiously. There is added, at the foot of the same book, a catalogue of 100 of the more notable stars, exhibiting [their] Declinations and Right Ascensions for the year 1600 and 1700, together with their differences, by which these can be conveniently enough adapted, not only for the intermediate [half-]century, but also for the immediately preceding and following [period], on either side. But if you are eager to know their risings and settings, with respect to a fixed Horizon, by reason of the Sun, the Tables of Regiomontanus will supply that without difficulty from the given declination and Right Ascension — or, if you prefer, by Triangles. And so that the Sun's place, from our emendation, may also be established for you more truly than otherwise, I have added an Ephemeris dispatching its motion at the Meridian, for the single days of this passing Year, in the longitude of places [of] 37 degrees. To derive the same for several preceding and following [years], within one or two Minutes, is thence of little labour. But if it shall please [you] to investigate this more precisely, use my Tables on the Sun's motion, which I think you received in that first chapter on its course restored by us (communicated to you from wherever, at last). If not, you have here, for every event, a gathering [quaternio] sufficient for its calculation for the next several centuries. I send, moreover, another book, comprising our Mechanics of Astronomy [Astronomiae Instauratae Mechanica], which, however, I place first in order. Having inspected it, you will estimate, not obscurely, with how great effort and diligence I have handled such things. And if it shall please [you] to have one or another Instrument made after their likeness, you have therein the Design [Idea] and the use. For it would not be useless if you should provide for yourself some Sextant for the distances of the stars, and a Quadrant of just magnitude for measuring their altitudes. In which matter that aforenamed Danish student can be of use to you, both as regards perfecting the graduations and arranging the rest; and, together [with you], to extend the work into the Practice of observations, as he learned with me. For I should wish that careful attention be paid in many celestial places, lest the same happen in our [age] which Naso [Ovid] complains of about the age of Romulus:
And the stars ran unobserved throughout the year.
As also (which is to be lamented) now nearly happens.

You also make mention, toward the end, of the Lilian or Pontifical [Gregorian] year — as ineptly established as it was approved. Which, although not most exactly consistent in all its numbers (for who could have achieved this without consulting heaven?), I nonetheless judge altogether more suitable than that earlier one [the Julian], which committed an anticipation of so many days — on account of some difference between the Equinoctial [tropical] and the Julian year — from the beginning of the years of Christ, or (as they preferred) from the Council of Nicaea. Nor can so enormous a divagation easily creep in hereafter, according to that Gregorian method; but (to say what the truth is) it is not possible to disentangle these matters with the highest precision (if any were necessary) by any such shortcuts and by-paths. Rather, one must have a subtle knowledge of the very motion of each luminary for any centuries whatever — such as not even at this time do we have ascertained, from the customary Tables, without every trace of error: as the calculation of the first Month of this Year, in the Sun and Moon (for the longitude of [the] place [of] 35 degrees), drawn from our observations and Tables, and compared with the Alphonsine and Copernican numbers (which I have also attached on a certain sheet, so that you may reason out the rest from it), will teach you. These things, then, which it was convenient to send now, take in good part. I shall subjoin in due time, the Maker of the heavens favouring, more and greater things about the heavenly [matters]. And although, by these more serious matters, I now make too much business for you, I impart along [with this], nonetheless, some poetic jeux besides — out of the many I have, composed here and there by various [hands] for my sake since I left Denmark, and sent to me — not that they smack of anything worthy of your Heroic vein, but only that you may see that my emigration hence is at heart for others too. I have selected chiefly these, in which the causes of our departure are somehow insinuated. If that most excellent Dousa the younger — whom the fates (alas!) snatched from us so prematurely — were still alive, he would doubtless have meditated something of more cultivated genius about these matters, as he had also before adorned our Cometographic [work] with his mellifluous leisures.

Our Tengnagel, returned hither, reported to me that you very much desire that I migrate to you, into Batavia [Holland] — which I credit to your love toward us; just as I have also understood, not obscurely, in this regard, the desires of the most illustrious prince Maurice [of Nassau] and of the Estates [the States-General], and their favour toward me — to say nothing of the English. And indeed my mind would not shrink from that change of place, since the skilful talents of the Dutch have always pleased me more than those of the Germans. But I cannot conceal from you that, meanwhile — while I tarry here on the borders of Germany and Denmark beyond a year, so as to leave my countrymen enough time to look at the matter more rightly (still mindful of the Fatherland, to which we owe much) — it became known to the Sacred Imperial Majesty, by the report of certain of the chief [men] at Court, that I am lingering here. Wherefore His Majesty, through the most ample Lord Rudolf Coraduz [Corraducius], Vice-Chancellor of the Empire — with the mediation also of Lord Doctor Thaddaeus Hagecius [Tadeáš Hájek], most closely joined to me by old Friendship — ordered me summoned to Prague, these [men] giving sure hope that the Imperial Majesty, if I come, will pursue and promote me and my studies with most clement favour. About which I have already received, this summer, several letters from there insinuating similar things. Moreover the most Serene Elector of Cologne — to whom the Emperor attributes much — commended me and all my [affairs] to him "of the better stamp" (as they say), in a letter written to His Imperial Majesty in his own hand on this account, filled with exquisite favour toward me; and likewise in others, to the Magnificent Lord Barvitius, the Emperor's privy Councillor and supreme Secretary. The same Cologne [Elector] also gave special letters, both to Lord Heinrich Rantzau, my kinsman-by-marriage and dearest host, and to me, by which he persuades [me] that this journey will not be unfruitful. Nay, he has even, of his own accord, promised that if — beyond all his expectation — it should not turn out that the Emperor is willing to foster and sustain my studies kindly and liberally, he will himself do that very thing at his own expense, not unwillingly. Wherefore these means, so conveniently offered — especially with the Emperor, the supreme Head of the Empire (since I strive to constitute and stabilize Astronomy universally) — I judge must be tried before all [else]. I have therefore resolved to set out for Prague at the very first opportunity, that I may most humbly learn the Emperor's most clement will. But if everything should answer our wishes less [well] from there, then at last I shall determine something certain about choosing other seats; and shall either cross over to you, or even to the English, or rather to the King of the Gauls [France], who, I know, will receive me and my studies most liberally, and will munificently perpetuate their conservation in that Kingdom — and perhaps, in this regard, will not yield to the old King of the Spains, Alfonso [X], as far as circumstances there conveniently allow. Whatever it shall be, the heaven and Urania, which include all lands, will not be excluded from all lands — however much our Denmark, through the ignorance and malevolence of certain [men], has not sufficiently attended to such sublime rites. I shall signify by letter, when opportunity is given, how this journey into Bohemia has succeeded, so that you may foresee and estimate from it what needs to be done. But I cease to detain you longer. It is yours to take in good part this prolixity, which the veneration of your sublime genius — and the excellence of the matter of which we treat, and your benevolence toward me — has extorted.

Farewell, illustrious and great Man, and live long and happily for the Republic of Letters.

Given at Wandsbek, from the Rantzau Castle near Hamburg, on the 23rd day of August, Old Style, of the common Christian Year 1598.
Most devoted to Your Most Illustrious Amplitude,
Tycho Brahe,
with his own hand.

Augusti st. vet. … 1598" — explicitly Old Style (stylo veteri), written from Wandsbek, Heinrich Rantzau's castle near Hamburg, where Tycho was based in his first exile year. Agrees with the catalogue label. The companion letter is the earlier 9 July 1598 Scaliger letter (15980709001); cf. the later precession exchange 16000310001 / 16000623001.
- Recipient. Joseph Justus Scaliger (1540–1609), son of Julius Caesar Scaliger, the foremost chronologist of the age, then at the University of Leiden (Academia Lugdunensis = Lugdunum Batavorum, not Lyon). His De emendatione temporum (1583; rev. 1598) is the work under discussion. He was the prime mover in trying to draw Tycho to Holland.
- The scientific spine (chronology × astronomy). The letter is a sustained technical exchange on the foundations of chronology:
- Equinox-observation accuracy. Tycho praises Scaliger's approval of his equinox observations and explains why precise equinox timing demands instruments able to read the Sun's meridian altitude to 1⁄6 of a minute (10″), with parallax and refraction corrected — accuracy neither the ancients nor Regiomontanus/Copernicus commanded. Hipparchus's equinoxes "limp" by up to a quarter-day (his instrument read only to 5′); even Ptolemy Tycho will not accuse of a whole-day error, since Ptolemy's obliquity was good to 20″ (though "perhaps borrowed from Hipparchus").
- The moving-poles dispute (the core disagreement). Against Scaliger's notion of mobile equatorial poles distinct from the world-pole, Tycho argues — from the changed stellar latitudes and the changing obliquity since Timocharis/Hipparchus/Ptolemy — that it is the Ecliptic (with the Sun's path), not the Equator, that shifts; the equatorial pole is the world-pole. He demonstrates with the pole-star (the last star in Ursa Minor's tail): polar distance 12⅖° in Hipparchus's day → 3° "25 years ago" → 2° 51½′ now; it will close to 27½′ in c. 500 years (at the start of Cancer / the solstitial colure) but never coincide with the pole, the stars keeping the fixed ecliptic poles.
- Heliacal risings & the year. On Sirius (Canicula) rising on nearly the same Julian day for 15 centuries — Tycho shows the constancy is accidental, a coincidental cancellation of the declination change (2°40′) against the Julian–sidereal-year drift, not a general rule (it already drifts to the 3rd day from Ptolemy to now, and would reach the 5th). Arcturus refutes Scaliger's fixed-date claim outright: 2 March in Hesiod, 3 March under Meton, 9 March under Ptolemy, c. 19 March now. Hence "nothing certain about the reckonings of the years can be borrowed" from heliacal risings.
- The sidereal year & precession constant. Tycho defends the sidereal year (Egyptians/Babylonians → Thābit → Copernicus) but rejects Copernicus's libration of the Earth's axis (since the Earth has no such motion), preferring to "save" any inequality by a universal solar hypothesis — a moving solar ecliptic. His adopted year is "a little less than the Gelalaean (Jalālī)," set from Regiomontanus's century of observations, and good for 3–4 centuries; over longer spans it grows, but never to the Alphonsine value Scaliger favours. He states his precession = 51″/year, equal to the solar motion in the tropical−sidereal year difference of ≈20⅔ minutes of time, giving 1° per 70 years 7 months (vs Ptolemy's slower rate; see the crux below).
- The gifts. Tycho sends, in secret, his 1000-star catalogue ("such as no prior centuries have seen"), reduced to epoch 1600, with the precession back-corrections (Albategnius −10°, Ptolemy −21⅖°, Hipparchus −24¼°, Timocharis −25¾°); a sub-catalogue of 100 stars with declinations/RA for 1600 & 1700; a solar Ephemeris for the current year (long. 37°); and the Astronomiae Instauratae Mechanica "placed first in order," with instrument designs — urging Scaliger to commission a sextant and quadrant (his Danish student to help graduate them), lest "the stars run unobserved through the year" (Ovid, Fasti).
- The calendar. On the Gregorian ("Lilian/Pontifical") reform that Scaliger detested: Tycho judges it "as ineptly established as approved," yet "altogether more suitable" than the drifting Julian; true precision, though, needs exact lunar-solar theory, which even now the standard Tables lack.
- The requested books. A Greek-printed book on Hipparchus's observations (published in Italy) and a corrected al-Battānī (Albategnius, "Mahomet of al-Raqqa") with his tables — Tycho wants pre-Ptolemaic data to refine the sidereal year; also the Jewish equinox datum and the Actian-year star-phases book of Ptolemy (the Phaseis). His methodological refrain: comparison-years must be equally distant from a leap-year (the reason his own tables append a quinquennium).
- The patronage decision (the documentary heart). This is one of the clearest statements of Tycho's 1598 patronage manoeuvre: while based at Wandsbek "beyond a year" to give Denmark time to reconsider, he has been summoned to Prague by Rudolf II, through Vice-Chancellor Rudolf Coraduz (Corraducius) and mediated by his old friend Tadeáš Hájek (Thaddaeus Hagecius); the Elector of Cologne (Ernst of Bavaria) has commended him to the Emperor in an autograph letter and to secretary Johann Anton Barvitius, even promising to fund Tycho himself if the Emperor declines. Tycho resolves to try Prague first; failing that, he will go to the Dutch (Scaliger, Maurice of Nassau, the States-General), the English, or — preferably — Henri IV of France, whom he hopes will rival Alfonso X of Castile as an astronomical patron. The Tychonic flourish: "heaven and Urania, which include all lands, will not be excluded from all lands."
- Persons & places. Frans Tengnagel (Tycho's envoy, warmly received by Scaliger); Regiomontanus; Copernicus; Hipparchus, Ptolemy, Timocharis (the three ancient observers); Thābit ibn Qurra (Thebit); al-Battānī (Albategnius); "Christian Johnson" (Christianus Iohannis, a former Danish household-student now with Scaliger); Hesiod, Meton, Euctemon (the Arcturus testimonies); Consul Paul Hainzel of Augsburg (the 1569–70 great-quadrant garden — see below); Janus Dousa the younger (d. 1596, the Leiden poet who versified Tycho's comet book De mundi… recentioribus phaenomenis); Maurice of Nassau; Rudolf II; Rudolf Coraduz; Tadeáš Hájek; the Elector of Cologne; Johann Anton Barvitius; Heinrich Rantzau; Henri IV ("King of the Gauls"); Alfonso X ("the old King of the Spains"). Place-note: the "Horizon of the Greeks"/"Boeotian region" (pole ≈40°) is the reference latitude for the heliacal-rising computations, chosen to match Hesiod/Meton.
- The Augsburg datum. "25 years ago … at Augsburg, by a quadrant of 14 cubits in its Radius, in the garden near Consul Hainzel's estate" = Tycho's great quadrant built with Paul Hainzel at Göggingen near Augsburg in 1569–70 (the famous large radius graduated to read fine fractions of a minute). "25 years" is a round approximation from 1598 (the instrument is c. 28 years prior); translated as written, not corrected.
- Latin & Greek tags. "si parva licet componere magnis" — Virgil, Georgics 4.176. Zoili — carping critics, after Zoilus the maligner of Homer. κοσμοπολίτην — "citizen of the world." "Et inobservata per annum / Sydera currebant" — Ovid, Fasti I (on Romulus's unastronomical calendar). cosmice / acronyce — cosmical (rising with the Sun) vs acronychal (rising at sunset). pragmatεία (πραγματεία) — "practical undertaking." Measure idiom: sexta minuti pars = 10″; duodecima gradus pars = 5′; quincunx gradus = 5⁄12° = 25′; tertia gradus pars = 20′; semis (minuti) = 30″. The Annus Gelalaeus = the Jalālī (Persian, Malik-Shāh / Omar Khayyām) solar year; Annus Actiacus = the Alexandrian year reckoned from Actium.
- Cruces (named, not smoothed).
1. "centenis 30" (Ptolemy's precession rate). The Dreyer page image confirms this as the printed reading: "non autem centenis 30 (uti voluit PTOLEMÆUS)." Read as printed it gives "130 [years per degree]," whereas Ptolemy's documented value is 1° per 100 years. The intent is clear from context (Tycho's 70 y 7 m vs a slower Ptolemaic rate vs Scaliger's still-slower one), but the numeral "30" is anomalous — reproduced verbatim and rendered "a hundred [years and] 30," flagged here as a probable textual corruption in the tradition (not silently emended to "100").
2. "fusile" → "fuisse." In "hallucinatum fusile non dixerim," the working transcription's fusile is a corruption of fuisse (the long-s ligature fuiſſe mis-resolved; the digitised text layer of the Dreyer page image mangles the parallel clause the same way, and the later "erroneas fuisse antecessorum" preserves the correct form). Translated as fuisse ("that he was…").
3. Obliquity "66 gradibus." "ea quam PTOLEMAeI Abacus in 66 exacte ponit gradibus" refers to the pole-star's ecliptic latitude (≈66° in antiquity), "taken in a broad manner," not to the obliquity itself; rendered accordingly.
- Correction. Latin corrected from the corrupt reading "hallucinatum fusile non dixerim" to Dreyer's "hallucinatum fuisse non dixerim" (TBOO VIII, pp. 101-102); the published English translation already reflected the correct sense and required no change.
