The original

IOANNES KEPLER AD TYCHONEM BRAHE.

CUM hucusque, Nobilissime et Præstantissime Domine TYCHO, partim occupationes tuæ, partim febris mea non passæ fuerint, ut rationes adventus mei in Bohemiam Magnificentiæ Tuæ ex animi mei sententia recenserem, scripto id rectissime præstitum iri putavi. Duobus potissimum argumentis Magnificentia Tua, in literis ad me in Styriam missis, in Bohemiam me vocavit, altero a contractu nostro, quem Maio mense et antea talem inivimus, ut pollicerer, interventu Cæsareæ voluntatis, Magnificentiæ Tuæ per biennium adesse, laboresque tuos in Astronomia toti Europæ iam pridem desideratissimos, quantum in me est, maturare et adiuvare: altero argumento a præsenti mea calamitate deducto, dum me iubes optima spe ad te proficisci, nec dubitare, quin vel commendationibus tuis ad Maiestatem Cæsaream, vel propria opera auxilioque meis incommodis sis consulturus, adeoque et viaticum compensaturus. Recte sane de duabus hisce rebus egisti. Nam mihi cum necessitas imponeretur e Styria migrandi, omnino de utraque hac cogitandum fuit. Etsi enim, quod primum attinet, omnis noster contractus expiravit et annihilatus est, sublato fundamento, ut retinerem salarium meum Styriacum (quod interdictu Provinciæ mihi ademtum est) utque magistratus meus eius temporis consentiret (cuius loco, cessante officio, iam agnosco Principem Wirtembergicum, vigore cum obligationis, tum literarum Reversalium) tamen cum, durante meo priore officio, res a Magnificentia Tua incepta et Cæsareæ Maiestati proposita sit, Cæsarea Maiestas etiam consenserit, providendum certe mihi fuit, ne imprimis Cæsaream Maiestatem, tum etiam Magnificentiam Tuam ludificarer, quin potius vel cum damno meo Pragam irem, ibique rem vel penitus confectam (quam spem literæ tuæ in adventum meum præbuerunt) quantum in me esset, amplecterer, quantum vero in Principe meo situm, per Legatum eius Dominum Doctorem DOLDIUM, qui Pragæ esset, expedirem; vel si adhuc præter opinionem tuam, in dubio suspensa futura esset, uti tum vires meas explorarem, sumptuum rationem inirem, et quantisper sine pernicie mea, incertum in eventum, meis impensis, nullo salario, expectare possim, perpenderem, ubi, si tantæ moræ, quantas postulat aula Imperatoria, supra facultates meas essent, negotium ipse præsens legitime et cum Magnificentiæ Tuæ meique ipsius excusatione penitus desererem.

Quod ad alterum, etsi mihi dubium nunquam neque fuit, neque est, quin et impense me ames, et optime promotum velis, et huius rei luculentissimas in loco tam splendido, tanta summi Monarchæ gratia, habeas occasiones, si moram ego ferre possem: tamen rationes, quibus præsens meum infortunium ope divina sublevem, aliæ mihi sunt, partim necessariæ, partim (ut sunt res meæ tenues) magis utiles. Cum enim iure obligatorio tenear, operam meam priore Magistratu, cui illam ex concessu Principis mei Nutricij addixeram, liberatam, eidem Nutricio meo renunciare, rectissime sane mihi consuluissem, si recta via Grätio ivissem in Wirtembergiam. Certum enim habeo, qui eo veniunt exules, maxime si alumni fuerunt Principis, illis et de victu statim et de conditionibus primo quoque tempore prospici. Ego vero præter hæc omnia etiam a Præceptoribus meis in Academia Tubingensi iam pridem eas habeo promissiones, ut merito ex coniunctione Principis cum Academia magna mihi spes nasci debeat. Atque hæc via esset omnium compendiosissima ad mea mitiganda incommoda. Altera est, ut si me causæ forsan aliæ (qualis est nostra iam tractatio) Pragam deducerent, uti tum in ea urbe, nempe in conventu Legatorum ex plerisque provincijs, statim mihi prospicerem, primo omnium Legatum Wirtembergicum alloquerer, an commode, parvo sumptu, commendationibus ipsius adiutus, in Wirtembergiam ire possim, an concessionem mihi ab illo Principe impetraret, commendationibus iterum me iuvaret, ut propter redimendum viaticum in propinquiorem Academiam, qualis est Witeberga, Iena, Lipsia etc. promoverer. Hæc altera via est, Nobilissime Domine TYCHO, sublevandis meis miserijs, minus quidem splendidas conditiones pollicens, quam ex aula sperantur Imperatoria, sed tamen expeditiores, in præsentia certiores, et mihi, qui ob inopiam non sum sustinendis diuturnis moris, acceptiores.

Explicavi, quid de utroque tuo argumento sentiam: iam quo animo huc venerim, hîcque iam commorer, recensebo. Voluisse me, statim atque in exilium ire sum iussus, Wirtembergiam repetere, iam pridem ex literis meis tenes. Supervenerunt literæ tuæ, quæ et mihi et affinitati amicisque meis satis efficaces sunt visæ, quæ me permoverent, ut dimisso in Magnificentiæ Tuæ et Astronomiæ gratiam, inque meum ipsius, si Deus ita vellet, maius incrementum, altero mei iuvandi modo, retento vero alterius arbitrio pro Wirtembergico Bohemicum iter arriperem. Bona vero et uxorem cum privigna visum est in omnem eventum Lincij relinquere, ubi essent in itinere versus Wirtembergiam.

Mutavi propositum de uxore et privigna (bona enim, sicut dixi, Lincij reliqui) hoc metu, qui iam in me exit, ne quis ex nobis ægrotaret, reliquis absentibus, inter peregrinos. Itaque Pragam veni magno sumptu. Sexaginta expendi in vecturam personarum, viginti in viaticum, quadraginta amplius in duo vasa, repleta bonis mobilibus sive suppellectili, idque tantummodo Lincium usque. Taceo quanto iam in ære sim hospitis mei. Restant mihi impensæ non plures, quam quibus ad moram quatuor hebdomadum, et deinde ad iter aliud (breve tamen) indigeo. Intra has quatuor hebdomadas expectabo eventum nostri negocij. At nihilominus, dummodo per valetudinem liceat, in omnem eventum mihi apud Legatos Principum de commoda conditione legitime circumspiciam, sic tamen, ut si quid interea Magnificentia Tua effecerit, id ratum, cætera irrita sint. Ubi hoc tempus transierit, atque Magnificentia Tua postulaverit a me, ut diutius maneam, id non aliter mihi possibile est, nisi aut viaticum mihi a Magnificentia Tua pro vectura et victu restituatur 150 fl., ut duo vasa mea huc advehuntur, ne quæ convasata sunt, situ pereant, aut Magnificentia Tua prædem se præstet pro me apud illos, a quibus necessaria mihi ad victum coëmenda sunt. Quorum illud si præstetur, ad semestre usque, DEO vitam prorogante, expectare potero: sin hoc autem, tantisper durare potest mora mea, dum id Magnificentiæ Tuæ et Creditoribus allubescat. Interea Astronomia et labores Magnificentiæ Tuæ tantæ mihi curæ erunt, quantam feret valetudo mea. Quin etiam si negocium ex animi sententia apud Cæsaream Maiestatem conficias, viaticumque, uti spem fecisti, impetres, ab eo summam eam, quæ interea mihi a Magnificentia Tua commodata fuerit, demptam Magnificentiæ Tuæ restituam. Quin etiam id intra has 4 septimanas operam dabo, ut aliquis alius mihi id salarium, quod Grätij habui, in hoc biennium pendat, pro quo obligatus illi sim, exacto tempore in eius servitium transire. Habet igitur Magnificentia Tua summam consiliorum meorum: quæ ut mihi inopi et deserto et exuli non in peiorem partem interpretetur, obnixe rogo.

Vale.
17. Oct.
Magnificentiæ et Nobilitatis Tuæ Officiosissimus
M. IO. KEPLER.

JOHANNES KEPLER TO TYCHO BRAHE.

Since hitherto, most noble and most excellent Lord Tycho, partly your occupations, partly my fever, have not allowed me to review with Your Magnificence, according to my mind, the reasons for my coming into Bohemia, I have thought it would most rightly be done in writing. By two arguments especially did Your Magnificence, in the letter sent me into Styria, call me into Bohemia: the one, from our contract — which in the month of May, and before, we entered on these terms: that I should promise, with the intervention of the Imperial will, to be present to Your Magnificence for two years, and, so far as in me lies, to mature and assist your labours in Astronomy, long since most desired by all Europe; the other argument, drawn from my present calamity — when you bid me set out to you with the best hope, and not to doubt that either by your recommendations to the Imperial Majesty, or by your own work and aid, you would see to my troubles, and even compensate my travel-money. You acted rightly indeed about these two things. For when the necessity of migrating from Styria was laid on me, I had altogether to think of both. For as to the first: although our whole contract has expired and been annihilated — the foundation removed by which I retained my Styrian salary (which has been taken from me by the Province's interdict), and by which my magistrate of that time would consent (in whose place, my office ceasing, I now recognise the Prince of Württemberg, by force both of obligation and of the letters of Reversal) — yet, since, while my prior office lasted, the matter was begun by Your Magnificence and proposed to the Imperial Majesty, and the Imperial Majesty also consented, I had certainly to take care not to make a mockery, first of all, of the Imperial Majesty, and then also of Your Magnificence — but rather to go to Prague even at my own loss, and there either embrace the matter fully accomplished (the hope your letter gave me at my coming), so far as in me lay; or, so far as lay in my Prince, expedite it through his Legate, Lord Doctor Dold, who was at Prague; or, if it should yet — against your expectation — be left hanging in doubt, then to test my resources, reckon my expenses, and weigh how long, without my ruin, I can wait at my own cost, for an uncertain outcome, with no salary — where, if such delays as the Imperial court requires were beyond my means, I might myself, [being] present, legitimately and with excuse to Your Magnificence and to myself, wholly desert the business.

As to the second: although it never was, nor is, doubtful to me that you both love me dearly, and wish me best advanced, and have the most splendid occasions for this, in so splendid a place, with such great favour of the supreme Monarch — if I could bear the delay — yet the means by which, with divine help, I may relieve my present misfortune are otherwise, partly necessary, partly (my affairs being slender) more useful. For since I am held by binding law to renounce my service, freed from my prior magistrate (to whom I had assigned it by the grant of my Nurturing Prince), back to that same Nurturing Prince [of Württemberg] — I should indeed have counselled myself most rightly, had I gone straight from Graz into Württemberg. For I hold it certain that those exiles who come thither — especially if they were the Prince's alumni — are at once provided for, both as to keep and as to conditions, at the first opportunity. And besides all this, I have long since from my Teachers in the Tübingen Academy such promises that, deservedly, from the conjunction of the Prince with the Academy, great hope ought to arise for me. And this would be the most compendious way of all to mitigate my troubles. The other is: that if other causes (such as our present dealing) should perhaps lead me to Prague, then in that city — namely, in the assembly of the Legates from very many provinces — I should at once provide for myself; first of all I should address the Württemberg Legate, [to learn] whether I can conveniently, at small cost, aided by his recommendations, go into Württemberg; or whether he would obtain me a leave from that Prince, and again help me with recommendations, that — for the sake of redeeming travel-money — I might be advanced to a nearer Academy, such as Wittenberg, Jena, Leipzig, etc. This is the other way, most noble Lord Tycho, of relieving my miseries — promising indeed less splendid conditions than are hoped from the Imperial court, but yet quicker, for the present more certain, and to me — who, by reason of poverty, am not [fit] for sustaining long delays — more acceptable.

I have explained what I think of both your arguments; now I will review with what mind I came hither, and now stay here. That I wished, as soon as I was ordered to go into exile, to return to Württemberg, you have long known from my letters. Then your letters came — which seemed effective enough both to me and to my kindred and friends — to move me that, letting go the other mode of helping myself, for the sake of Your Magnificence and of Astronomy, and for my own greater increase (if God so willed), I should seize the Bohemian journey instead of the Württemberg one — yet keeping the option of the other open. But it seemed best, in any event, to leave my goods and my wife with her daughter [stepdaughter] at Linz, where they would be on the road toward Württemberg.

I changed the plan about my wife and stepdaughter (for the goods, as I said, I left at Linz), from this fear, which now comes upon me: lest one of us should fall ill, the others being absent, among strangers. And so I came to Prague at great expense. Sixty [florins] I spent on the transport of persons, twenty on travel-money, forty more on two casks filled with movable goods or furniture — and that only as far as Linz. I say nothing of how much I am now in my host's debt. There remain to me expenses no more than [those] I need for a stay of four weeks, and then for another journey (a short one, however). Within these four weeks I will await the outcome of our business. But nonetheless, so far as my health allows, in any event I will legitimately look about, among the Princes' Legates, for a convenient position — yet so that, if Your Magnificence accomplishes anything meanwhile, that shall hold, and the rest be void. When this time has passed, and Your Magnificence requires of me that I stay longer, that is not possible for me otherwise, unless either travel-money be restored me by Your Magnificence for transport and keep — 150 florins — so that my two casks be brought hither, lest what is packed perish from disuse; or Your Magnificence stand surety for me with those from whom the necessaries for my keep must be bought. If the former be done, I can wait up to half a year (God prolonging life); but if the latter, my stay can last as long as it shall please Your Magnificence and the creditors. Meanwhile Astronomy and Your Magnificence's labours will be as much my care as my health shall bear. Nay, even, if you accomplish the business with the Imperial Majesty according to [your] mind, and obtain the travel-money, as you gave hope, I will restore to Your Magnificence, deducted from it, that sum which shall meanwhile have been lent me by Your Magnificence. Nay further, within these 4 weeks I will take pains that someone else pay me, for this two-year period, the salary which I had at Graz — for which I should be obliged, the time being expired, to pass into his service. Your Magnificence has, then, the sum of my counsels; which I earnestly beg you not interpret to the worse part toward me, [a man] poor and deserted and an exile.

Farewell.
17 October.
Most dutiful to Your Magnificence and Nobility,
M[aster] Johannes Kepler.

Apparatus

  • Direction & correspondents. Incoming, Johannes Kepler → Tycho — written from Prague (place omitted in the source), 17 October 1600, in the weeks after Kepler's return following the April quarrel and reconciliation.
  • Significance — the foundational negotiation of the Tycho–Kepler partnership. This is the formal letter in which Kepler, newly exiled from Styria, sets out the terms, finances, and contingencies of his coming to work with Tycho — the relationship that would yield Kepler's Astronomia nova, his planetary laws, and the Rudolphine Tables. Its threads:
  • The two-year contract. Tycho had called Kepler on two grounds: (1) their contract of May 1600 — that Kepler, "with the intervention of the Imperial will," assist Tycho's astronomy for two years; and (2) Tycho's promise to relieve Kepler's "calamity" by recommendation to the Emperor and to compensate his travel-money.
  • The Styrian exile. Kepler's Graz salary "taken away by the Province's interdict" — the Counter-Reformation expulsion of Protestants from Styria (Sept 1600), which Kepler refused to recant. His default allegiance reverts to the Prince of Württemberg (his Nurturing Prince, patron of the Tübingen Stift where Kepler was an alumnus).
  • The Württemberg fallback. Kepler frankly weighs going straight to Württemberg/Tübingen (where exiled ducal alumni get immediate support, and his old teachers have made promises) — a "more compendious" path than the uncertain imperial court — or being promoted via the Württemberg Legate (Dr. Dold) at Prague to a nearer university (Wittenberg, Jena, Leipzig).
  • The finances (vivid and exact). Kepler details his journey costs — 60 fl. transport of persons, 20 fl. travel-money, 40+ fl. for two casks of goods, only as far as Linz — his debt to his host, and that he can fund only a four-week stay. To stay longer he needs either 150 florins of travel-money (to fetch his two casks) or Tycho's surety with the tradesmen; with the former he can wait half a year. He will repay any loan from the imperial travel-money once obtained.
  • The family. He left his goods, wife [Barbara], and stepdaughter [Regina] at Linz, then brought wife and stepdaughter on to Prague for fear of illness "among strangers."
  • The commitment. "Astronomy and Your Magnificence's labours will be as much my care as my health shall bear" — and a plea not to be misjudged, "[a man] poor and deserted and an exile."
  • Cross-references. A keystone of the Tycho–Kepler convergence thread; read with the April-1600 quarrel/reconciliation letters (brahe-t_16000408001, brahe-t_16000000001, brahe-t_16000803001) and the Ursus-analysis Kepler letter quoted at brahe-t_16000911001.
  • Persons & places named. Johannes Kepler; Tycho; Rudolf II (the "supreme Monarch"); Dr. Dold (the Württemberg Legate at Prague); the Prince/Duke of Württemberg (Friedrich I); the Tübingen Academy; Kepler's wife (Barbara) and stepdaughter (Regina) (unnamed). Places: Prague, Styria/Graz, Linz, Württemberg, Tübingen, Wittenberg, Jena, Leipzig.
