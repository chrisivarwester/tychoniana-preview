The original

MAGNIFICO ET NOBILISSIMO VIRO, DOMINO IOHANNI GEORGIO HERWARTO AB HOHENBURG, SERENISSIMO UTRIUSQUE BAVARIAe DUCI A CONSILIJS INTIMIS, PRÆSIDI SCHWABAe ET STATUUM PROVINCIALIUM CANCELLARIO, AMICO SUO INPRIMIS HONORANDO.
MONACHI.

TEMPUS est, Nobilissime et praestantissime vir, amice inprimis honorande, ut plurimis tuis inde ab initio huius anni ad me humanissime datis literis aliqua plenius, quam semel atque iterum factum est, respondeam, ne quid scrupuli aut suspicionis de meo erga te constanti amore haec nimia intermissio atque in rescribendo raritas suggerere poterit. Distuli autem hoc scribendi officium partim invite ob multiplices intervenientes alias curas et distractiones, partim data opera, ut ea, quae circa Lunaris hypotheseos restitutionem moliebar, interea ad finem exoptatum, aliquatenus saltem, si non integre, perduci, atque tua dubia in hoc sidere eo expeditius resolvi possent. Ut itaque hanc moram interlapsam, licet tam tua expectatione quam mea opinione iusto diuturniorem, in meliorem partem pro solito tuo candore interpreteris, te peramanter rogatum habeo. Quia vero multae et variae tuae, de quibus loquor, erant literae, et nimis longum foret, nec etiam admodum necessarium ad singula omnium contenta respondere, repetam saltem principaliora, et ea, quae in ijs negotium Astronomicum, de quo inter nos iucunde agitur, potissimum concernunt, solummodo attingam, praetermissis coeteris, quae librorum e Bibliotheca Serenissimi Principis vestri mecum communicatorum mentionem faciunt, de quibus postmodum summatim, siquidem nunc libros illos remittam, nonnulla subiungam.

Ad literas itaque primas die 10 Ianuarij scriptas sic habe. Proponis in ijs quatuor quaestiones, quarum prima est de parallaxibus Solis et Lunae, quomodo eae secundum meam mentem colligendae sint. Parallaxes Solis e Tomo primo Progymnasmatum tibi transmisso pag. 80 iuxta triplicem eius a Terra remotionem facile venabere. Lunarium vero parallaxium canonem manuscriptum existimo, cum coetera ad Lunae apparentias atque eclipses facientia impartirer, tunc adiunctum fuisse. Licet, ut dicam, quod res est, parallaxes Lunae adhuc quadam alia limitatione indigent, quam brevi quoque favente Numine adipisceris.

Secunda quaestio rationem aequationis temporis in Luna retro constituenda expetit, quod quidem praestari posset, si, qualis illa annua variatio, quam veteres non animadverterunt, tum temporis fuisset, et a quo puncto Zodiaci penderet, siquidem apogaeum Solis non respiciat, certo constaret. Verum ex quo in neoterica reformatione revolutionum Lunarium haec annua variatio, quae per se exigua est, et sextantem gradus modice excedit, alijs quibusdam medijs absorbetur, et aequationis temporis, quoad Lunam diversa quaedam tam ab hac priori, quam antecessorum forma, per ipsam experientiam in motu Lunae, alijs atque alijs anni temporibus multipliciter et sedulo explorata reparatur, iccirco supervacaneum duco de prioribus illis tanquam otiosis et a nobis iam antiquatis ulterius verba facere. Ubi coeteras in Luna reformationes acceperis, videbis annuae illi insinuationi subventum esse, nec novus ille aequandi temporis modus, sine quo coetera subsistere nequeunt, ibi praetermittetur, et tam nostris quam veterum temporibus adaptatus.

Tertio de longitudinibus locorum quorundam a me usurpatis certior fieri desideras, ut Regiomontis Prussiae, quam assumpsisse REINHOLDUS in catalogo eius locorum insignium depraehenditur, Par. 46 M. 45, licet id discrete indicare neglexerit, ego eandem totidem partium, prout tu quoque recte colligis, assumendam duco. Hierosolymarum longitudinem e PTOLEMÆI Geographia petendam existimo, quod loca in viciniore Palaestina illi rectius quam alijs patuerint. Romae longitudinem COPERNICUS gradibus 5 Cracovia occidentaliorem facit, sicque evaderet ea part. 40½, quod tamen nimium esse suspicor, ego vix 39° attingere, aut certe non multum excedere crediderim. Negotium hoc de longitudinibus locorum non parum habet perplexitatis, cum observationes eclipsium Lunarium in varijs hinc inde versus ortum et occasum remotis Regionibus diligentissime acceptae sufficienter in promptu non sint. Quae ignoratio Astronomiae quoque, praesertim quoad Lunam, nonnihil officit. Nos qua fieri potuit circumspectione praecipuas Europae civitates in competentem dispositionem redegimus, ut suo tempore videbis, correctis etiam, quantum licuit, earundem latitudinibus, in quibus, cum facilime pateant, pudor est, tantopere nihilominus communiter aberrari, veluti REINHOLDUS, qui Regiomontis latitudinem ex APIANO 26 M. iusto minorem mutuatus est. Quin et ipse APIANUS elevationem Augustae Vindelicorum 5 Minutis e sua, uti puto, observatione iusto decliviorem fecit, et CYPRIANUS adhuc plus, nempe quinta parte gradus defecit.

Ad quartum quaesitum scias tabulam prosthaphaereseωn Lunarium, quam postea iuxta priorem hypothesin supputari feci, non multum differre ab altera Hamburgi minus diligenter impressa, nisi quod quaedam perperam istic duobus in locis excusa emendet, et omnia scrupulosius, utpote singulis secundis, exhibeat. At cum tabula illa per omnia apparentijs non satis praecise correspondeat, siquidem ob conturbationes rerum mearum in Dania factas, dum peregre sum, tam cito revideri et castigari non potuerit, quod nunc concesso meliori ocio tandem effectui daturum spero, cumque redintegratas illas tam aequalium quam inaequalium motuum Lunae tabulas tecum, ubi penitus absolutae fuerint, communicare decreverim, quorsum opus circa has exauthoratas pluribus occupari? Quis enim Cerere reperta glande vescitur?

Alterae tuae literae die 16 Ianuarij exaratae de Triangulorum compendiosa per προσθαφαιρέσεις resolutione solerter agunt, et a nostris ratiocinijs non admodum aliena habent, quare ijs diutius non immorubor, sed pergam potius ad eas, quae octavo Februarij scriptae sunt, praetermissis duabus intermediantibus ob causam superius dictam. Agis inibi de calculo locorum Lunae ad tres dies in libro nostro Epistolarum pag. 56 inter reliquos Planetas assignatorum, quodque ea ex observatione petita Tabulis nostris non satis consentiant, inducis. Fateor quidem id aliquatenus sic esse, non tamen tanta invenitur discrepantia, quantam tua supputatio mihi transmissa suggerit, uti ex nostra, quam apposui, repetitione liquet, duobus posterioribus locis satis convenientibus, et primo saltem illo nonnihil exorbitante, nam ut minutula quaedam praeteream, ubique differentiam illam, loco Lunae, respectu polorum Eclipticae per Instrumenta observato, et illi, qui in propria orbita existit, quem calculus solummodo praebet, varie intercidentem negligis: forte quod tabellam diversitatem hanc ostendentem cum prioribus non acceperis, quam, ut tibi postmodum ad manus sit, hîc additam habes, siquidem renovatis etiam tabulis, quas alias, ut promisi, obtinebis, aeque applicari poterit. Quomodo autem repurgati hi canones loca Lunae, tribus illis diebus satis praecise convenientia, expediant, in alia charta denotatum invenies, idque quoad solas longitudines. Nam in latitudinibus ex observationibus salvandis, et parallaxibus, quae sese immiscent, extricandis, etiamnum laboramus. Neque tamen in latitudinibus a priori ordinatione evidens discrimen subrepere poterit. Quam vero priores illae apparentijs congruerint, tute satis expertus es, et quam proxime in tuo calculo assecutus, ut hac de re frustra moveatur dubitatio, nisi quis velit scrupulosius, quam sensus oculorum fert, singula trutinando nodum in scirpo quaerere.

Quas postea die 20 Februarij ad me misisti literas, restitutionem quarundam peculiarium affixarum a CHRISTOPHORO ROTHMANNO Landtgraviano olim Mathematico adiunctam habuere, cuius communicatio mihi grata fuit; licet enim ab ipso laudatissimae memoriae Principe, dum adhuc in vivis erat, mihi aliquot inerrantium siderum ipsius Instrumentis facta verificatio transmissa sit, ut eam per triangula expenderem atque cum meis pariter inventis conferrem, quemadmodum in Epistolis inter nos commutatis videre est: tamen non tot erant numero stellae, neque tam diligenter in trutinam Triangulorum et calculi vocatae. Ex ijs, quae nos capite secundo Progymnasmatum Astronomicorum circa fixarum restitutionem tractavimus, nostras rationes, et quanta diligentia hanc provinciam susceperimus et administraverimus, perspicies, collatione autem inde cum hisce Landtgravianis longitudinibus facta, eas ubique 5 vel 6 scrupulis nostras excedere animadvertes, nec in latitudinibus maiorem esse differentiam, quam haec in longitudinibus varietas proxime postulat. Qua vero occasione factum existimem, quod Landtgravianae observationes stellarum longitudines per unciam circiter unius gradus plus iusto extenderint, in dicto Progymnasmatum capite pag. 274 reperies, et alias in Epistolis hac de re inter nos affatim ventilatum est. Non est quod tibi persuadeas, ROTHMANNUM unquam hanc stellarum verificationem suo nomine in publicum editurum, nam animum ab his studijs iam prorsus abiecit, et per procurationes forenses victum quaerit, quatenus adversa valetudo, qua miserrime conflictatur, id admittit, nec scio, an adhuc superstes sit. Nam cum ultimo ad me de eo scriberetur, ipsum cum extremo sanitatis et rei familiaris dispendio vitam aegerrime trahere relatum est. Quare non opus est, ut verearis, tibi aliquando imputari posse, quod huius fixarum ab eo peractae emendationis me participem reddideris, nisi id celâro, quod tamen nihilominus libenter facio, licet, ut dicam id quod censeo, eius rectificatio parum mihi prodesse poterit, cum iam diu ante meas circa fixarum correctionem lucubrationes consignârim, et pro maiori parte typis imprimi curârim, uti liber, quem habes, ostendit. Supplevi postea plures, quo numerus millenarius consummaretur. Quam millenarum stellarum exactam restitutionem Augustissimo Imperatori RUDOLPHO, Domino nostro Clementissimo, in membrana descriptam ante annum, cum primum Pragam venissem, humilime obtuli.

Postea in ijsdem literis de resolutione Triangulorum per προσθα‐φαιρέσεις recte quidem infers, quod non multum contineat facilitatis atque compendij, si de planis solummodo intelligis: attamen in sphaericis non contemnendam suppeditat promptitudinem, et citius quam vulgaris fert methodus, calculum absolvit. Quin etiam in ipsis Planis Triquetris non tantum, uti existimas, a scope exacto deflectitur, si subtilissime ex integro canone RHETICI alicubi, prout opus est, emendato, omnia perficiantur, veluti reiterata tua exempla in hîc praesenti quadam charta te monere poterint; nuspiam enim 4 vel 5 scrupul. secund. quae canonis insufficientia excusat, quaeque etiam per se non sunt alicuius momenti, deviatio committitur.

Attingis insuper ibidem sex illas in Luna animadversiones, quas praefato Progymnasmatum libro sub calce capitis sexti assignavi, easque cum calculo e tabulis Lunae tibi antea exhibitis confers, adiuncto tuae numerationis epilogismo, quem postquam introspexissem, animadverti, utut non modicam ibidem adhibueris diligentiam, nonnunquam tamen id, quod intendebas, non satis assecutum, quemadmodum recognita eorundem nostra via computatio, quam tribus prioribus ex Epistolis recensitis calculationibus subiunxi, te in prima pagina, haec, uti dispicies, continente, edocebit, licet inficiari non possum, calculum hunc, ex primis Lunae tabulis quacunque sedulitate elaboratum, in quibusdam, praesertim postremis duobus, in Octobri anni 1586 factis denotationibus, nimium exorbitare, cui tamen enormitati posterior in Lunae curriculo adhibita correctio ita medetur, ut nihil dubij, quod mentionem mereatur, relinquat, veluti in secunda pagina, tribus illis ex Epistolarum libro sumptis Lunae locis et ad renovatum calculum expensis, haec quoque sex, quae Progymnasmatum liber suppeditat, ad normam eiusdem reformatae supputationis, sub incudem numerorum revocantur. Ubi tamen id considerandum venit, quod ea, quae etiamnum ibi residua videtur ab annotatione in libro Progymnasmatum facta discrepantiola, quae tamen nusquam tria vel quatuor minuta excedit, non ita stricte accipienda sit, ac si tabulae neotericae tantundem omnino a coelitus facta observatione aberret. Nam loca longitudinis Lunae in dicto libro apposita vix sunt exquisita scrupulositate ex observationibus, quae etiam non ubique sufficientes in promptu fuêre, ita recensita, ut pro 3 vel 4 scrupulis quis fidem interponere possit, quod et revisae observationes atque subtilius castigatae, sicubi ad adscriptum tempus in voluminibus observationum nostrarum inveniuntur, satis probant, prout etiam a nobis ijs in locis, ubi opus fuit, ad memoratam paginam subnotatum est. Haud enim duxi necessarium praecisionem quoad longitudinem Lunae istic proponere, cum parallaxes solummodo scrutari intentio nostra foret. Et sane si dextre ab ijs, qui in observationum Astronomicarum palaestra diu multumque exercitati sunt, omnia secundum rei exigentiam et difficultatem aequa lance expendantur, non invite fatebuntur, quod trium vel quatuor minutorum, imo etiam duodecimae partis unius gradus in hac pragmatείa disconvenientia nostram determinationem convellere nequeat. Cum enim Luna non tam splendidum et notabile vibret lumen atque Sol, quo in Instrumentis per umbram aut illuminationem pinnacidiorum centraliter citra omne dubium discerni possit, longe difficilius quam ☉ aut etiam coetera astra, quae per rimulas dioptrales satis praecise denotantur, observationi subtiliori patet, unde fit, quod marginem aliquem Lunae praesertim maxime plenum inter observandum collimari oporteat, ubi haec etiam difficultas incidit, quod vix dirimere detur, an quis plus vel minus iusto circa limbi illius contactum solummodo dignoscendum collineet, alijs atque alijs observatoribus pro diverso acumine visus non in eodem puncto scopum petitum attingentibus. Taceo nunc, quod parallaxes Lunae eius observationem reddant magis lubricam, quam in ullo alio sidere, et licet hae quoad longitudinem iuxta nonagesimum Eclipticae ab horizonte gradum praecaveri queant, tamen id non diu durat, et rarissime per serenitatem ob nubes et alia impedimenta tales occasiones suppetunt. Et quorsum quaeso maior scrupulositas, quam quae facile in sensus cadit, hîc solicite sectanda? Cum ea omnia, quae ex motu Lunae emendato deducuntur, si per experientiam ponderanda sint, sensuum adminiculo indigeant, qui pariter ut prius circa minima et imperceptibilia hallucinari possunt. Haec vero ob id eo fusius inculco, ne quis sibi polliceatur, tam exactam undiquaque certitudinem in Lunae apparente motu obviam esse atque circa Solem et reliquos Planetas, fixasque stellas, modo debita adhibeatur diligentia, praestari poterit. Interim tamen non ferendum duco, ut nimis lato modo, utpote in sexta vel quarta solummodo gradus parte, Lunae exponantur φαινόμενα, prout veteres, qui ad maiorem praecisionem vix aspirare ausi sunt, admiserunt, imo usitatas tabulas, sive Alphonsinas sive Copernicaeas, aut quascunque alias assumpserimus, intra unum aut sesquialterum gradum subinde Lunae loca coelo correspondentia vix praebere. Veruntamen hoc insuper admonendum duco, quod etiamsi Luna non tam subtiliter per Instrumenta observabilis sit, ut de centri eius situ exactissimo nihil prorsus dubij relinquatur, tamen, quantum ad Eclipses attinet, modo eae per ingressum et egressum umbrae terrenae accurate denotentur, promptius patent, et inaequalitas illa, quae circa novam et plenam contingit, simplicior est, nec tam varijs anfractibus obnoxia, atque ea, quae iuxta alias digressiones fit, in quibus maior quidem subest perplexitas, quam ulli veterum aut recentium Astronomorum (absit invidia dicto) hactenus vel semel suspicari potuerint, nedum ut ei sufficienter per oportunam hypothesin et hinc deductam numerationem providere sustinuerint. Licet vero haec omnia ita se habeant, polliceri nihilominus ausim, Lunae cursum in tabulas recentiores a nobis digestum exactius apparentijs satisfacturum, quam cuivis per Instrumenta facile assequi in proclive est, siquidem non nisi a selectissimis et minime dubijs observationibus per annos plures quam 20 exanthlatis derivatus, idque Instrumentis minime fallacibus, quae imitari paucissimis conceditur. Atque haec de Lunae motu observando examinandoque satis superque dicta sunto.

Quantum epochas mediorum motuum in hac ipsa Luna ad tempora PTOLEMÆI retro constituendas, quarum in ijsdem etiam literis mentionem facis, concernit, scias eam in his denotationem, quae tibi transmissa est, non fuisse genuinam, sed per incuriam nescio quam ex antiquata et reiecta quadam assumptione, a quodam studiosorum meorum descriptam, cum verior illa ad manus non esset, atque ea, quam pro rata habui, neque ijs, quos accepisti, neque Prutenicis numeris consentit, quam modo substituere minus necessarium duco, siquidem tota illa prior ratio Lunaris circuitionis, aptiore nunc inventa, a nobis repudiatur. In hac vero posteriore redintegratione, ubi eam adeptus fueris, epochas ad PTOLOMÆI tempora correctas unâ obtinebis.

Pro libris praestantissimi illius Mathematici FRANCISCI VIETAe, quorum simul cum literis die 26 Aprilis mihi inscriptis copiam fecisti, gratias ago plurimas. Retulit ante annum mihi, cum Pragae unâ essemus, ADRIANUS ROMANUS, insignis etiam Mathematicus, eundem VIETAM de Triangulorum compendijs peculiaribus nonnulla in publicum edidisse, quae videre averem. Pollicitus is quidem est, se mihi exemplar missurum, sed necdum respondit eventus.

Ad eas, quas huc nono Maij direxisti, non opus arbitror multis respondere, cum praecedentia sufficiant. Id saltem addere lubet, mihi nequaquam esse ingratum accuratum tuum illud studium in disquirendis subtiliterque ponderandis ijs, quae a nobis circa Lunam prius elaborata erant, imo cuperem ulterius, tam in his quam alijs, idipsum pari diligentia a te fieri, et sicubi aliquid dubij inciderit, ingenue me admoneri, plus enim saepe vident oculi quam oculus, et te candide agere veritatis solius eruendae amore, non ut cum praeiudicio quodam nostra insecteris, prout plaerique scioli facere praesumunt, sufficienter perspectum habeo. Tentavit etiam excellens ille Italorum Mathematicus ANTONIUS MAGINUS, in Luna, nonnulla e quaternione illo de motu eius Hamburgi impresso, quem a quodam meo domestico in Italiam profecto, me inscio, adeptus est, idque etiam bona, ut ex literis eius apparet, intentione fecit, sed non pari tecum felicitate metam petitam attigit, veluti patet ex calculo, quem literis suis inclusum ante aliquot hebdomadas ad me misit, cuius copiam unâ facio.

Huc usque scripseram, vel potius amanuensi meo dictâram, antequam Benaticâ ante duos circiter Menses discederem, et constitueram non saltem hîc Pragae has ipsas literas supplere, et quaedam alia adiungere, ac calculum, quem mitto, revidere, et in quibusdam etiam explicare, antequam eas ad te mitterem, prout in brevioribus illis, quas (cum ante mensem libros communicatos restituerem) ad te dedi, me pollicitum existimo. Verum cum indies nova me occupent negocia, ut id, etiamsi quam maxime vellem, satis cito expedire nequeam (habeo insuper quaedam prae manibus pro Caesarea Maiestate, Domino nostro clementissimo, peragenda, quae non sinunt me alijs sedulo attendere, priusquam illa absoluta fuerint) cogor hasce utut immaturas et incompletas ad te dirigere, quo pollicitationi meae vel aliqua saltem ex parte, si secundum totum nunc non liceat, satisfiat. Adiunxi ea, de quibus inibi mentio facta est, quatenus nunc inveniri poterant: nam amanuensis ille, qui et antecedentia scripserat, et quae apponenda erant, conservârat, non amplius apud me est, sed me inscio, postquam huc Pragam veni, contra datam fidem, nescio equidem qua de causa, ab alio, uti opinor, instigatus, se subduxit, et ad Witebergenses, ubi officium aliquod scholasticum sperat, redijt: unde vereor omnia, prout oportuit, hîc addita esse, imo an ea ipsa, quae adiunxi, recte se habeant, et satis accurate consignata sint, ambigo; neque enim recognoscendi modo suppetit otium. Tuum itaque erit omnia circumspecte expendere, et sicubi opus fuerit, castigare, quod pro tuo in hisce exacto iudicio non difficulter praestiteris. Cuperem vero vel has ipsas, vel earum exemplar (nullum enim mihi ad manus est) remitti, ut desiderata per meliorem oportunitatem suppleam, et si quae in annectendis omissa aut minus elaborate peracta sunt, resarciam et perfectiora reddam.

Ad ♀ stellam olim iuxta ALBINOVANI Poëtae opinionem in aurora non apparentem, qua de re denuo in ultimis tuis quaestionem movisti, ut multa nunc respondeam, temporis angustia impedit, nec etiam aliae nunc succurrunt rationes, quam eae, quas ante annum ad te perscripsi. Licet enim in novitio tuo calculo, praesertim ad primum tempus coniunctionis ♀ et ☿, latitudinis diversitas non tanta sit, quanta antea admissa est (neque enim hîc dimidium gradum attingit, quem PTOLOMÆUS non semper hac in parte assecutus est, nedum eius imitator COPERNICUS) attamen quomodocunque coniungantur ♀ et ☿ etiam in ipso longitudinis et latitudinis minuto, haec ab ipso, ut plane non appareat, obscurari nequit, cum unum luminosum corpus alterum non obfuscet, nec visibilis diameter ☿ij tanta est, ut id efficere possit, quemadmodum iam dudum monui. Si itaque citra Poëtarum Privilegia Poëta is veridicus est, crediderim potius ♀ ob exhalationes et meteora quaedam candida aërique similima obvelatam delituisse, praesertim cum 8 saltem gradibus a ☉ elongaretur, ideoque radijs eius quodammodo obnoxia, minusque ob diem illucescentem conspicua; sed de his tute rectius dispicies. Addam SCALIGERI literas nonnulla in fine de hoc ipso negotio continentes, si exemplum satis cito obtinuero.

Fuit apud me Benaticae vere praeterito M. IOHANNES K0PLERUS, insignis apud Styriacos Mathematicus, cum quo tibi etiam per literas familiaritatem intercedere ab ipso cognovi. Retulit vero inter alia se aliquando nonnulla tibi scripsisse, de Hypothesibus meis in controversiam vocatis, atque quod eae nec in COPERNICO, nec APOLLONIO PERGÆO, ut quidam (iam ante 14 dies mortuus) praetendit, invenirentur, ostendisse, atque certis indicijs non alterius, sed meas esse collegisse. Rogo itaque peramanter, velis eas KÖPLERI literas mecum aliquantisper communicare, aut saltem ea verba, quae hoc negocium concernunt, inde transsumpta et tuae manus subscriptione in veritatis testimonium ratificata transmittere, idque per primam Postam a vobis huc redeuntem, et Agenti (uti vocant) Principis vestri id, quicquid erit, mihi tradendum committere. Feceris mihi sic rem apprime gratam; incumbit enim nunc aliqualis necessitas, cur sic fieri averem, de qua alias rescisces.

Invitârat nuper Serenissimi Principis vestri Legatus me ad coenam, a quo humanissime et lautissime exceptus fui, et crebra tui, eaque (uti decuit) honorifica facta est inter nos mentio. Quamprimum ea, quae nunc pro Caesare elaboro, parata fuerint, ut liberior esse queam, constitui illum vicissim ad me invitare, siquidem quod accessum non sit detrectaturus, iam benigne mihi addixerit. To the magnificent and most noble man, Lord Johann Georg Herwart von Hohenburg, privy councillor to the most Serene Duke of both Bavarias, President of [the margraviate of] Burgau in Swabia and Chancellor of the provincial Estates, his especially-to-be-honoured friend.
At Munich.

It is time, most noble and most excellent man, friend especially to be honoured, that I reply somewhat more fully than has been done once or twice, to your many letters most kindly sent me since the beginning of this year — lest this excessive interruption, and rarity in replying, could suggest any scruple or suspicion about my constant love toward you. I deferred this duty of writing partly unwillingly, on account of the manifold other cares and distractions intervening; partly on purpose, so that the things I was working at concerning the restitution of the lunar hypothesis might meanwhile be brought to the wished-for end — at least in some part, if not entirely — and your doubts in this star [the Moon] could thereby be resolved the more expeditiously. And so I most lovingly ask you to interpret this delay that has slipped in — though longer than is fair, both by your expectation and by my own opinion — in the better part, according to your wonted candour. But since your letters of which I speak were many and various, and it would be too long, nor indeed quite necessary, to respond to each of the contents of all, I shall at least take up again the more principal points, and shall touch only on those things in them which chiefly concern the astronomical business of which we treat pleasantly between us; passing over the rest, which make mention of the books communicated to me from the Library of your most Serene Prince, about which I shall afterwards add a few things summarily, since I am now returning those books.

As to the first letter, then, written on 10 January, take it thus. In it you propose four questions, of which the first is about the parallaxes of the Sun and Moon, how they are to be gathered according to my mind. The Sun's parallaxes you will easily hunt out from the first volume of the Progymnasmata sent to you, page 80, according to its threefold distance from the Earth. But the manuscript canon [table] of the lunar parallaxes I think was attached at the time when I shared the other things conducing to the Moon's appearances and eclipses. Although — to say what the truth is — the Moon's parallaxes still need a certain further limitation, which you too will shortly obtain, God favouring.

The second question seeks the method of the equation of time in the Moon, to be established retrospectively — which indeed could be done, if it were certainly settled what that annual variation (which the ancients did not notice) was at that time, and from what point of the Zodiac it depended, since it does not regard the Sun's apogee. But since, in the modern reformation of the lunar revolutions, this annual variation — which is in itself slight, and modestly exceeds a sixth of a degree [≈10′] — is absorbed by certain other means; and since the equation of time, as regards the Moon, is repaired by a form different both from this earlier one and from that of our predecessors — a form explored by experience itself in the Moon's motion, manifoldly and diligently, at various and various seasons of the year — therefore I consider it superfluous to make further words about those earlier matters, as idle and now antiquated by us. When you have received the other reformations in the Moon, you will see that that annual insinuation has been provided for; nor will that new mode of equating time — without which the rest cannot stand — be passed over there; and [it is] adapted both to our times and to those of the ancients.

Thirdly, you desire to be informed about the longitudes of certain places used by me — such as Königsberg in Prussia, which Reinhold is found to have assumed at 46° 45′ in his catalogue of notable places (although he neglected to indicate it distinctly): I judge it is to be assumed at the same number of degrees, as you too rightly gather. The longitude of Jerusalem I think must be sought from Ptolemy's Geography, because the places in nearer Palestine were known to him more correctly than to others. Copernicus makes the longitude of Rome 5 degrees more westerly than Krakow, and thus it would come out at 40½°, which however I suspect to be too much; I should believe it scarcely reaches 39°, or certainly does not much exceed [it]. This business of the longitudes of places has not a little perplexity, since observations of lunar eclipses, most diligently taken in various regions remote on this side and that, toward east and west, are not sufficiently at hand. Which ignorance also somewhat hinders Astronomy, especially as regards the Moon. We, with what circumspection could be used, have reduced the chief cities of Europe into a competent arrangement, as you will see in due time, having also corrected their latitudes as far as was permitted — in which [latitudes], since they lie open most easily, it is a shame that people nonetheless commonly err so greatly: as Reinhold, who borrowed from Apian the latitude of Königsberg 26 minutes less than is just. Nay, even Apian himself made the elevation [latitude] of Augsburg 5 minutes lower than is just, from his own observation, as I think; and Cyprian [Leovitius] yet more, namely fell short by a fifth part of a degree [12′].

To the fourth query, know that the table of lunar prosthaphaereses, which I afterwards had computed according to the earlier hypothesis, does not differ much from the other one printed less carefully at Hamburg — except that it corrects certain things wrongly printed there in two places, and exhibits everything more scrupulously, namely down to single seconds. But since that table does not correspond precisely enough to the appearances in all respects — seeing that, on account of the disturbances of my affairs made in Denmark while I am abroad, it could not be revised and corrected so quickly (which I hope now, better leisure being granted, to bring at last into effect) — and since I have resolved to communicate to you those restored tables, both of the equal and of the unequal motions of the Moon, when they shall be entirely completed: to what end is there need to be occupied at length with these discredited ones? For who, when grain has been found, feeds on acorns?

Your other letter, written on 16 January, deals skilfully with the compendious resolution of Triangles by prosthaphaereses, and has things not very alien from our reasonings; wherefore I shall not dwell longer on them, but shall rather proceed to those written on the eighth of February, passing over the two intervening ones for the reason said above. In it you treat of the calculation of the Moon's places for three days, assigned among the other Planets on page 56 in our book of Letters [the 1596 Epistolae astronomicae], and you bring forward that those [places], sought from observation, do not agree well enough with our Tables. I confess indeed that it is somewhat so; yet not so great a discrepancy is found as your computation sent to me suggests, as is clear from our recalculation, which I have appended — the two latter places agreeing well enough, and only that first one deviating somewhat. For (to pass over certain tiny matters) everywhere you neglect that difference, variously falling between the Moon's place as observed by Instruments with respect to the poles of the Ecliptic, and that place which exists on its proper orbit, which the calculation alone supplies — perhaps because you did not receive, along with the earlier [materials], the little table showing this diversity, which you have added here, so that it may afterwards be at hand for you; since it can equally be applied to the renewed tables too, which you will otherwise obtain, as I promised. But how these purged canons dispatch the Moon's places, agreeing precisely enough on those three days, you will find noted on another sheet — and that, as regards longitudes only. For in saving the latitudes from the observations, and in extricating the parallaxes that mingle themselves in, we still labour. Nor, however, will an evident discrepancy be able to creep into the latitudes from the earlier arrangement. But how closely those earlier [tables] agreed with the appearances, you have yourself sufficiently experienced, and how nearly you attained it in your calculation — so that on this matter doubt is moved in vain, unless someone wishes, weighing each point more scrupulously than the sense of the eyes bears, to seek a knot in a bulrush.

The letter which you afterwards sent me on the 20th of February had attached a restitution of certain particular fixed stars by Christoph Rothmann, formerly the Landgrave's Mathematician, whose communication was welcome to me. For although a verification of several of the fixed stars, made with his [the Landgrave's] own Instruments, was transmitted to me by the most praiseworthy-memory Prince himself while he was still alive — that I might weigh it by triangles and compare it equally with my own findings, as may be seen in the Letters exchanged between us — yet the stars were not so many in number, nor so diligently called into the balance of Triangles and of calculation. From the things which we treated in the second chapter of the Astronomical Progymnasmata concerning the restitution of the fixed stars, you will perceive our methods, and with how great diligence we undertook and administered this province [task]; and from a comparison made thence with these Landgravian longitudes, you will observe that they everywhere exceed ours by 5 or 6 minutes, and that there is no greater difference in the latitudes than this variation in the longitudes closely requires. But by what occasion I think it came about that the Landgravian observations extended the stars' longitudes by about a twelfth of a degree more than is just, you will find in the said chapter of the Progymnasmata, page 274; and this matter has elsewhere been amply ventilated between us in the Letters. There is no reason for you to persuade yourself that Rothmann will ever publish this verification of the stars under his own name, for he has now wholly cast his mind off from these studies, and seeks his living through forensic agency-work, insofar as the adverse health with which he is most wretchedly afflicted allows it; nor do I know whether he is still alive. For when last it was written to me about him, it was reported that he drags out his life most painfully, with the utmost loss of health and of family fortune. Wherefore there is no need for you to fear that it could ever be imputed to you that you made me privy to this emendation of the fixed stars performed by him — except [on condition] that I keep it quiet, which nonetheless I willingly do; although, to say what I judge, his rectification will be able to profit me little, since I long ago set down my studies concerning the correction of the fixed stars, and had them for the greater part printed in type, as the book you have shows. I afterwards supplied more, so that the number of a thousand might be completed. Which exact restitution of a thousand stars, described on parchment, I humbly offered to the most August Emperor Rudolf, our most Clement Lord, a year ago, when I had first come to Prague.

Afterwards in the same letter, concerning the resolution of Triangles by prosthaphaereses, you rightly infer that it contains not much of ease and compendium, if you understand it only of plane [triangles]; yet in spherical [triangles] it supplies a readiness not to be despised, and dispatches the calculation more quickly than the common method bears. Nay, even in plane triangles themselves there is not so great a deflection from the exact mark as you think, if everything be perfected most subtly from the complete canon [trigonometric table] of Rheticus, corrected here and there where need is — as your reiterated examples, on a certain sheet here present, can advise you; for nowhere is a deviation committed of [more than] 4 or 5 seconds, which the insufficiency of the canon excuses, and which moreover are in themselves of no moment.

You touch, moreover, there upon those six observations in the Moon which I assigned in the aforesaid book of the Progymnasmata at the foot of the sixth chapter, and you compare them with the calculation from the Moon's tables exhibited to you earlier, with the epilogism [summary computation] of your numeration attached. After I had examined it, I observed that, although you applied no small diligence there, nevertheless you sometimes did not sufficiently attain what you intended — as the computation of the same by our method, reviewed (which I subjoined to the three earlier calculations reviewed from the Letters), will teach you on the first page, which contains these, as you will discern. Yet I cannot deny that this calculation, elaborated from the first lunar tables with all diligence, in some points — especially the last two, among the markings made in October of the year 1586 — deviates too much; which enormity, however, the later correction applied in the Moon's course so remedies that it leaves no doubt worth mention: as on the second page, where, with those three places of the Moon taken from the book of Letters and weighed against the renewed calculation, these six too which the book of the Progymnasmata supplies are recalled, under the anvil of numbers, to the norm of the same reformed computation. Here, however, this comes to be considered: that the little discrepancy which still seems there to remain from the annotation made in the book of the Progymnasmata — which nonetheless nowhere exceeds three or four minutes — is not to be taken so strictly, as if the modern tables strayed altogether by just that much from observation made from the heavens. For the places of the Moon's longitude set down in the said book are scarcely reviewed with exquisite scrupulosity from observations (which moreover were not everywhere sufficiently at hand) in such a way that one could pledge faith for 3 or 4 minutes — as both the revised observations, and those more subtly corrected, wherever they are found for the noted time in the volumes of our observations, sufficiently prove; just as it has also been noted by us, in those places where it was needful, against the mentioned page. For I did not deem it necessary to propose precision as regards the Moon's longitude there, since our intention was to scrutinize the parallaxes only. And surely, if everything be weighed in an even balance — according to the matter's exigency and difficulty — by those who have long and much exercised themselves in the wrestling-school of astronomical observations, they will not unwillingly confess that a disagreement of three or four minutes — nay, even of a twelfth part of a degree — in this practical undertaking cannot overturn our determination. For since the Moon does not dart so splendid and notable a light as the Sun, whereby in the Instruments it could be discerned centrally, beyond all doubt, by the shadow or illumination of the sights [pinnacidia], it lies open to subtler observation far more difficultly than the Sun, or even than the other stars, which are denoted precisely enough through the dioptral slits; whence it comes about that some margin of the Moon, especially when most full, must be sighted in observing — where this difficulty too occurs, that it can scarcely be determined whether one sights more or less than is just about the mere discerning of that limb's contact, different observers, according to the diverse acuteness of their sight, not attaining the sought mark at the same point. I pass over now that the Moon's parallaxes render its observation more slippery than in any other star; and although these [parallaxes], as regards longitude, can be guarded against near the ninetieth degree of the Ecliptic from the horizon [the nonagesimal], yet that does not last long, and most rarely, on account of clouds and other impediments, do such occasions present themselves during clear weather. And to what end, I ask, should a greater scrupulosity than easily falls under the senses be anxiously pursued here? — since all those things which are deduced from the Moon's corrected motion, if they are to be weighed by experience, need the help of the senses, which, just as before, can be deceived about the least and imperceptible things. But I inculcate these things the more fully for this reason: lest anyone promise himself that so exact a certainty on every side is to be met with in the Moon's apparent motion as can be furnished about the Sun, and the rest of the Planets, and the fixed stars, provided due diligence be applied. Meanwhile, however, I deem it not to be borne that the Moon's phaenomena should be set forth in too broad a manner — namely within only a sixth or fourth part of a degree — as the ancients, who scarcely dared aspire to greater precision, admitted; nay, [I deem it not to be borne] that the customary tables, whether Alphonsine or Copernican, or whatever others we take up, scarcely give the Moon's places corresponding to heaven within one or one-and-a-half degrees from time to time. Nevertheless I deem this must moreover be advised: that even though the Moon is not so subtly observable by Instruments that nothing of doubt at all is left about the most exact position of its centre, yet, as regards Eclipses — provided they be accurately marked by the ingress and egress of the earthly shadow — they lie open more readily; and that inequality which happens about the new and full [moon] is simpler, and not subject to so many windings, as that which occurs at the other digressions [elongations], in which there lies a greater perplexity than any of the ancient or recent Astronomers (let it be said without envy) have hitherto even once been able to suspect, much less have managed to provide for it sufficiently by an opportune hypothesis and a numeration deduced therefrom. But although all these things are so, I should nonetheless dare to promise that the Moon's course, digested by us into more recent tables, will satisfy the appearances more exactly than it is easy for anyone to attain by Instruments — seeing that it is derived only from the most select and least doubtful observations, drawn out through more than 20 years, and that with Instruments least fallacious, which it is granted to very few to imitate. And let these things be said, enough and more than enough, about observing and examining the Moon's motion.

As far as concerns the epochs of the mean motions in this very Moon, to be established retrospectively to the times of Ptolemy — of which you also make mention in the same letter — know that the notation in these matters which was transmitted to you was not genuine, but was, through some carelessness, copied by one of my students from a certain antiquated and rejected assumption, since the truer one was not at hand; and that the notation which I held as valid agrees neither with those [numbers] you received, nor with the Prutenic numbers — which I deem it less necessary now to substitute, since that whole earlier method of the lunar circuit is repudiated by us, a more fitting one having now been found. But in this later restoration, when you have obtained it, you will at the same time obtain the epochs corrected to Ptolemy's times.

For the books of that most excellent Mathematician François Viète, a copy of which you furnished me together with the letter inscribed to me on 26 April, I give very great thanks. A year ago, when we were together at Prague, Adriaan van Roomen, also a distinguished Mathematician, reported to me that the same Viète had published certain things on particular compendia of Triangles, which I should be eager to see. He indeed promised that he would send me a copy, but the outcome has not yet answered to it.

To those [letters] which you directed hither on the ninth of May, I think there is no need to reply at length, since the preceding points suffice. This at least it pleases me to add: that that accurate zeal of yours, in investigating and subtly weighing the things earlier elaborated by us concerning the Moon, is by no means unwelcome to me; nay, I should wish that the same be done by you further, both in these and in other matters, with equal diligence, and that, wherever any doubt occurs, I be candidly advised — for eyes often see more than an eye — and I hold it sufficiently clear that you act candidly out of love of eliciting truth alone, not so that you may attack our work with some prejudice, as most smatterers presume to do. That excellent Italian Mathematician Giovanni Antonio Magini also attempted, in the Moon, some things from that gathering [quaternio] on its motion printed at Hamburg — which he obtained, without my knowledge, from a certain member of my household who had set out into Italy — and he did this, too, with good intention, as appears from his letter; but he did not attain the sought goal with felicity equal to yours, as is clear from the calculation which, enclosed in his letter, he sent me some weeks ago, a copy of which I enclose along [with this].

Thus far I had written — or rather had dictated to my amanuensis — before I departed from Benátky about two months ago; and I had resolved not only to supplement these very letters here at Prague, and to add certain other things, and to revise the calculation which I send, and even to explain it in some points, before I sent them to you — as I think I promised in those shorter [letters] which I gave you (when, a month ago, I returned the books communicated [to me]). But since new business daily occupies me, so that I cannot dispatch it quickly enough, even if I most greatly wished — (I have moreover certain things in hand to be carried out for His Imperial Majesty, our most clement Lord, which do not allow me to attend diligently to other things before they are finished) — I am compelled to direct these [pages] to you, however immature and incomplete, so that my promise may be satisfied at least in some part, if it may not now be satisfied as to the whole. I have attached the things of which mention is made therein, insofar as they could now be found; for that amanuensis — who had both written the foregoing and had kept the things that were to be appended — is no longer with me, but, after I came hither to Prague, without my knowledge and against his pledged faith, instigated by another (as I think), withdrew himself — I do not indeed know for what cause — and returned to the Wittenbergers, where he hopes for some scholastic office. Whence I fear lest not everything has been added here as it ought; nay, I am in doubt whether the very things I attached are correct, and accurately enough set down; for there is at present no leisure for reviewing them. It will therefore be yours to weigh everything circumspectly, and, wherever need be, to correct it — which by your exact judgment in these matters you will perform without difficulty. But I should wish either these very [pages], or a copy of them (for I have none at hand), to be sent back, so that I may supply what is wanting through a better opportunity, and, if any things in the appended materials have been omitted or done less elaborately, may repair them and render them more perfect.

As to the star Venus [♀], once not appearing at dawn according to the opinion of the Poet Albinovanus — about which you again raised a question in your last [letter] — the narrowness of time prevents my replying much now; nor do other reasons now occur to me than those which I wrote to you a year ago. For although in your new calculation — especially at the first time of the conjunction of Venus [♀] and Mercury [☿] — the difference of latitude is not so great as was earlier admitted (for here it does not reach half a degree, which Ptolemy did not always attain in this part, much less his imitator Copernicus), nevertheless, however Venus and Mercury be conjoined, even in the very minute of longitude and latitude, Venus cannot be obscured by it [Mercury] so that it does not appear at all, since one luminous body does not darken another, nor is the visible diameter of Mercury so great that it could effect this, as I advised long ago. If therefore — apart from the Privileges of Poets [poetic licence] — that Poet is truthful, I should rather believe that Venus lay hidden, veiled by exhalations and certain white meteors most similar to air, especially since it was elongated at least 8 degrees from the Sun [☉] and therefore somewhat subject to its rays, and less conspicuous on account of the dawning day; but you will discern these things more rightly yourself. I shall add a letter of Scaliger, containing at the end some things about this very business, if I obtain a copy quickly enough.

There was with me at Benátky, last spring, Master Johannes Kepler, a distinguished Mathematician among the Styrians, with whom I have learned from him that you too have a familiarity by letters. He reported, among other things, that he had at some time written some things to you about my Hypotheses being called into controversy, and that he had shown that they are found neither in Copernicus nor in Apollonius of Perga — as a certain man (now dead these 14 days) pretended — and that he had gathered, by certain indications, that they are not another's, but mine. I therefore most lovingly ask that you would communicate Kepler's letter with me for a little while, or at least transmit those words which concern this business, excerpted from it and ratified by the subscription of your hand in testimony of the truth; and that you do this by the first Post returning hither from you, and entrust it — whatever it shall be — to your Prince's Agent (as they call him), to be delivered to me. You would thus do me a thing especially welcome; for some necessity now presses, on account of which I should be eager that it be done so — of which you will learn at another time.

The Legate of your most Serene Prince had lately invited me to dinner, by whom I was most kindly and most sumptuously received, and frequent — and (as was fitting) honourable — mention of you was made between us. As soon as the things I now elaborate for the Emperor shall be ready, that I may be freer, I have resolved to invite him in turn to me, since he has already kindly assured me that he will not decline the visit. Then we shall converse more fully about you, and I shall show him some of those things which have been ventilated between us in mutual letters, together with certain of my Instruments — which now have partly been brought hither from Benátky, the Emperor so willing, and partly (as I hope) will shortly be here — so that, having returned home, he may report about them to you; although the most important part of them, together with the rest of my equipment, is still detained at Magdeburg, to be conveyed hither, however, at the expense of the Reverend Chapter of that place, for the favour of His Imperial Majesty, by the Elbe (unless it be too dried up on account of the continual heat), as quickly as it can be done. These are the things which at this time I deemed should be written and intimated to you; and I ask you again and again that you receive everything candidly, and reply in good time to our expectation.

Farewell.
Given at Prague, the 28th day of August, New Style, the year 1600.
Most devoted to Your Magnificence,
Tycho Brahe,
I wrote with my own hand.

[Postscript:] If you could lend me by turns some of the books noted in the schedule likewise attached, sending them to me successively, you would thus do me a thing no less welcome than before. I shall restore them in good faith, as was done with the earlier ones too. You have here, moreover, the Calculation of a Solar Eclipse, computed from my newer tables. Thence you will easily gather that a method somewhat different in the Moon's motion has since been discovered, from that earlier one communicated to you before. The Sun's altitude was observed, at the beginning of the obscuration, [at] 60° 43′, at an elevation of the Pole of 50° 4½′, according to my observation. From this seek the time, and compare it with our numbers. This observation of which I speak was made here at Prague by a certain quadrant of brass [orichalc] belonging to the Generous Lord Johann Friedrich Hoffmann, exhibiting single minutes, and elaborated in imitation of one of mine, which is the third in number in the Mechanica of Astronomy. If anything has been marked from the heavens with you in the same Eclipse, make me a sharer in that observation.

The carelessly-printed Hamburg "quaternio" on lunar motion (a pirated tract Tycho's runaway household-member carried to Italy, whence Magini got it).
  • The prosthaphaeretic method (§5: prosthaphaeresis). Two passages treat the resolution of triangles by prosthaphaereses (Greek, retained) — the additive-subtractive trigonometric technique (precursor to logarithms): Tycho concedes it offers little economy for plane triangles but a "readiness not to be despised" for spherical ones, faster than the common method, provided one works from Rheticus's complete trigonometric canon (the Opus Palatinum / great Canon), "corrected where need is" — claiming deviations no greater than 4–5 seconds. The same theme links to Viète and Adriaan van Roomen (van Roomen reported, at Prague the year before, that Viète had published "particular compendia of triangles").
  • Rothmann's fixed-star verification. Christoph Rothmann (Hesse-Kassel mathematician under Landgrave Wilhelm IV, d. 1592 — "the most praiseworthy-memory Prince") had compiled a restitution of certain fixed stars, forwarded to Tycho via Herwart. Tycho judges the Landgravian longitudes run ≈5–6′ (≈ a twelfth of a degree) above his own — an error he had diagnosed (Progymn. p. 274) and "amply ventilated" with Kassel in the 1596 Epistolae. He reports Rothmann abandoned astronomy, lives by "forensic agency-work" in wretched health, "nor do I know whether he is still alive" — a notable biographical datum (Rothmann in fact died c. 1600). Tycho assures Herwart the sharing carries no risk to him and that Rothmann's rectification can profit Tycho little, his own thousand-star catalogue being long since complete and largely printed.
  • The longitudes/latitudes of places. Königsberg longitude 46° 45′ (after Erasmus Reinhold, author of the Prutenic Tables); Jerusalem from Ptolemy's Geography; Rome — against Copernicus's 40½° (5° west of Krakow) — Tycho argues scarcely 39°. He lists latitude errors: Reinhold borrowing Königsberg's latitude 26′ short from Peter Apian; Apian making Augsburg 5′ low; Cyprianus [Leovitius, the Augsburg astronomer] short by a fifth of a degree (12′).
  • The priority dispute & Ursus's death (the urgent thread). "A certain man, now dead these 14 days" who claimed Tycho's world-system hypotheses were already in Copernicus or Apollonius of Perga = Nicolaus Reimers Ursus (Bär), the Imperial mathematician and Tycho's bitter antagonist, who died in August 1600 — fixing this passage to mid-August. Kepler (with Tycho at Benátky "last spring," i.e. 1600) had written to Herwart demonstrating the hypotheses are Tycho's own, not found in Copernicus/Apollonius; Tycho urgently asks Herwart to send Kepler's letter, or a notarised extract under Herwart's own hand "in testimony of the truth," by the first post via the Bavarian Prince's Agent — "some necessity now presses." This is documentary ammunition for the Ursus libel suit (cf. 15991209001, 15990918001, 15980820001). The "certain man… 14 days" dating and the Ursus identification are mutually corroborating.
  • The Venus-at-dawn crux (Albinovanus). Herwart's recurring literary-astronomical question: the Poet Albinovanus (Pedo Albinovanus, the Augustan poet) reported Venus invisible at dawn. Tycho's reply: a Venus–Mercury conjunction cannot account for it ("one luminous body does not darken another," Mercury's disc far too small), so — granting the poet truthful "apart from poetic licence" — Venus was likely veiled by white atmospheric exhalations/meteors and dimmed by proximity to the Sun (only c. 8° elongation) in the brightening dawn. Tycho offers to enclose a relevant Scaliger letter.
  • The Imperial/Bavarian court frame. Tycho is at Prague working "for the Emperor" (Rudolf II); a Bavarian Legate has feasted him and will be shown Tycho's letters and instruments; the instruments are partly arrived from Benátky (by the Emperor's will), partly still detained at Magdeburg, to come down the Elbe at the cathedral Chapter's expense "unless it be too dried up by the continual heat" (cf. the Elbe-transport thread in 16000324002 / 16000322001). A vivid composition detail: the letter was dictated to an amanuensis at Benátky, who then deserted to Wittenberg ("instigated by another"), carrying off the appended materials — so Tycho sends it "immature and incomplete" and asks for it (or a copy) back.
  • Persons named. Herwart von Hohenburg; Erasmus Reinhold; Ptolemy; Copernicus; Peter Apian; Cyprianus Leovitius; Christoph Rothmann and Landgrave Wilhelm IV of Hesse-Kassel; Rheticus; Emperor Rudolf II; François Viète; Adriaan van Roomen; Giovanni Antonio Magini; Johannes Kepler; Apollonius of Perga; Ursus (unnamed — "a certain man, dead these 14 days"); Albinovanus (the poet); Joseph Justus Scaliger; the Bavarian Legate.
  • Latin & Greek tags. "Quis Cerere reperta glande vescitur?" — "Who, having found grain [Ceres], feeds on acorns?" (the old proverb of progress past primitive food). "nodum in scirpo quaerere" — "to seek a knot in a bulrush" = to manufacture difficulty where none exists (Plautus/Ennius; Terence). "plus vident oculi quam oculus" — "eyes see more than an eye" (two heads better than one). "sub incudem numerorum revocantur" — "are recalled under the anvil of numbers" (re-forged by calculation). "absit invidia dicto" — "let it be said without envy." Greek code-switches retained: προσθαφαιρέσεις (prosthaphaereses), φαινόμενα (phaenomena), πραγματεία (pragmateia, "practical undertaking"). Measure idiom: sextans gradus = ⅙° (10′); uncia / duodecima pars gradus = 1⁄12° (5′); quinta pars gradus = ⅕° (12′); sesquialter gradus = 1½°; scrupulum / scrupulum secundum = minute / second of arc.

1 defect found:
- text error (high). Our Latin the transcribed original substitutes 'Solis' (the Sun) for Dreyer's 'Lunae' (the Moon) in the postscript; the English then compounds the error by asserting a newly-discovered method in solar theory. Dreyer's reading is also the only one that coheres with the rest of the letter, which is entirely a discussion of revised lunar tables/theory and never otherwise revisits solar theory — a scholar reading the published English would wrongly conclude Tycho flagged a solar-theory revision here. Dreyer prints: Inde facile colliges, paulo aliam eſſe in [Moon-symbol]æ motu postea adinventam rationem, quam prior illa tecum antea communicata. (TBOO VIII p.353, line c. 14: the crescent-moon glyph + 'æ' genitive suffix = 'Lunae', immediately followed on the next line by the same convention '☉is altitudo' = 'Solis altitudo' for the Sun — the two symbols are distinct and used correctly elsewhere on the same page.).

Repaired. Verified directly against the Dreyer page image (TBOO VIII p.353 of 12): the crescent-moon symbol followed by 'ae' is clearly legible and distinct from the separate sun-glyph in the same sentence ('☉is altitudo'). The correction also restores internal coherence, since the letter's postscript concerns lunar tables and eclipse observation throughout, never revisiting solar theory. No remaining uncertainty on this point.
