The original

VIRTUTE, ERUDITIONE ET GENERE VIRO INPRIMIS CLARO, DOMINO OLIGERO ROSÆKRANTZIO A ROSENHOLMIO, AMICO INTEGERRIMO, CONSANGVINEO FIDELISSIMO, AFFINI AMANTISSIMO, AD PROPRIAS MANUS.

S. P.

QUAS circa æquinoctium vernum huius anni ad me e Rosenholmiano tuo dedisti literas, OLIGERE desideratissime, ante paucos dies hîc accepi. Excusas ab initio tarditatem in rescribendo, et quod meæ præpostere ac sero ad te perlatæ sint; alterum hoc ferendum est ob viarum intercapedinem et Tabellariorum incuriam, prius amicitia nostra facile in meliorem partem interpretatur. Percipio ex hisce, quod descriptum verborum Domini CORADUCIJ, Procancellarij Imperialis, quibus me, cum Dresdæ essem, de clementissima Cæsaris voluntate certiorem reddidit, legeris, idque tibi placuisse. Scripsit vero idem CORADUCIUS circa finem Ianuarij huius anni alteras ad me, quibus ex Clementissimo ipsius Cæsareæ Maiestatis mandato me Pragam benigne evocat, quarum exemplar misi ad D. D. VITUM WINSHEMIUM, communem nostrum amicum, oblata nimirum occasione per Tabellarium Hamburgum subito euntem, quo is illud ad te dirigeret, quod et se, nacta confestim oportunitate, fecisse; paulo post mihi rescripsit, ut te illud nunc accepisse existimem, vel saltem dum in Scania esses, simile extractum apud sororem SOPHIAM vidisse, ut ob id reiteratione non sit opus. Coactus autem sum iter hoc Pragense diutius procrastinare, quam vellem, partim ob itinerum impedimenta, per aquarum illuviem post nivem subito resolutam præter solitum incidentia, partim quod Libri Cæsari destinati tam cito ac cuperem plane absolvi nequierint, superveniente etiam Filiæ natu maioris gravi invalitudine, alijsque nonnullis obstaculis concurrentibus, quæ volens prætereo. Fuit interim Pragæ D. D. THOMAS FRANTZIUS, WINSHEMIJ Gener, quem nosti; is huc reversus, multa de Clementissima Cæsareæ Maiestatis erga me voluntate, et quam benigne quamque honorifico loco me habere constituerit, prout a Domino Procancellatio CORADUCIO sibi, ut ea mihi indicaret, iniunctum aiebat, retulit: quæ tamen hîc sigillatim attingere nolo, verum ubi ipsemet illic præsens expertus fuero, quo in statu res meæ futuræ sint, te plenius de singulis edocebo. Profectus est dictus FRANTZIUS ante paucas hebdomadas hinc Hamburgum, ut inde ad comites in Orientali Frisia, quorum causas in aula Cæsaris agit, se conferret, cumque ante abitum eius semel atque iterum familiarius invicem conversaremur, tuique subinde honorifica facta esset mentio, aiebat se te quoque invisurum, prout ab illo petieras, si modo res eius id ullatenus tolerarent. Ego vero, ut id faceret, ipsum adhortabar. Quod si præstiterit, plura ab illo percipies, quam calamus nunc sustinet.

Cæterum quæ de Insulula ista, quæ tanti mihi constitit, scribis et mones, tanquam ex candido et mihi bene cupienti animo profecta, candide et sincere accipio. Argumenta, quæ adducis, satis probabilia sunt, et licet apud me irrefragabilem Geometricamque demonstrandi vim non mereantur, siquidem tam hæc quam plura eiusmodi replicare et validiora ijsdem opponere non sit difficile, tamen tuis et aliorum hac in parte consilijs non prorsus resistendum censeo; aliquantulum saltem temporis ad deliberandum, quid hac in re faciendum vel omittendum sit, mihi concedi cupio, ne nimis præcipitanter quid agat. Neque enim certi quid mecum statuere potero, antequam Pragæ Cæsaris Clementissimam de meis rebus voluntatem omnimode perspectam habuero; tunc demum satis erit temporis de his atque alijs quidpiam concludere, nec sane consultum videtur prius sponte Insulam restituere: imo si retinerem, donec illi, prout in cæteris (quæ plus emolumenti attulerunt) factum est, quocunque tandem prætextu mihi eam adimant, forte plus commodi quam incommodi hinc sum habiturus. Quid si aliquando mihi obijcerent, quod non fuissent eam recepturi, nisi sponte restituissem, volenti nullam fieri iniuriam, et pleraque alia, quibus lubens supersedeo? Minas certe eorum, quantæcunque sint, non reformido, fretus causæ bonitate; viderint ipsi; spero quod Deus me aliquando a tantis iniurijs vindicabit, cuius præsidio dum nitor, fortuna omnis superanda ferendo est. Plura et particulariora nunc nec vacat nec lubet. Accingo me, quantum possum, ad iter Pragense, relicturus Dresdæ Gynæceum, donec in Bohemiam excurrero. Tu vero, mi exoptatissime OLIGERE, unâ cum Coniuge tua et Nepte mea, resalutata a me peramanter tam matre tua quam illa, felicissime vale. Salutem ex te dixi Viris hîc Eruditis, quorum familiari notitia prius usus es, quod et antea feci, sæpe enim tuam memoriam iucunde recolimus, illi vero te vicissim resalutant, et optima quæque tibi et tuis precantur.

Iterum vale.
Dabantur Wittebergæ die 7. Maij Anni 1599.
Tuus absens ut præsens
TYCHO BRAHE,
manu propria.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the man especially renowned in virtue, erudition, and birth, Lord Holger Rosenkrantz of Rosenholm, [my] most upright friend, most faithful kinsman, most loving relative — to his own hands.

Heartiest greeting.

The letter you sent me about the vernal equinox of this year, from your Rosenholm, most longed-for Holger, I received here a few days ago. You excuse, at the outset, your tardiness in replying, and that mine reached you out of order and late; the latter must be borne, for the distance of the roads and the carelessness of couriers; the former our friendship easily interprets in the better part. I perceive from this that you have read the copy of the words of Lord Coraduz, the Imperial Vice-Chancellor, by which he informed me, when I was at Dresden, of the Emperor's most clement will — and that it pleased you. But the same Coraduz wrote me another [letter], about the end of January this year, by which, by the Imperial Majesty's most clement mandate, he kindly summons me to Prague; a copy of which I sent to Lord Doctor Vitus Winshemius, our common friend (an occasion offered by a courier suddenly going to Hamburg), that he might direct it to you — which he wrote back, a little after, that he had done at the first opportunity; so that I think you have now received it, or at least, while you were in Scania, saw a similar extract with [my] sister Sophie — so there is no need to repeat it. But I was compelled to put off this Prague journey longer than I would: partly for the hindrances of the roads (beyond the usual, from the flood of waters after the snow suddenly thawed); partly because the Books destined for the Emperor could not be quite finished as soon as I wished; my elder Daughter's grave illness also supervening, and some other obstacles concurring, which I willingly pass over. Meanwhile Lord Doctor Thomas Frantzius, Winshemius's son-in-law, whom you know, was at Prague; returned hither, he reported much of the Imperial Majesty's most clement will toward me, and how kindly and in how honourable a place he has resolved to hold me — as, he said, had been enjoined on him by Vice-Chancellor Coraduz, that he should tell me — which, however, I will not touch on here in detail; but when I myself, present there, have learned in what state my affairs will be, I will more fully inform you of the particulars. The said Frantzius set out a few weeks ago hence to Hamburg, to go thence to the Counts in East Frisia, whose causes he agitates at the Emperor's court; and since, before his departure, we conversed familiarly together once and again, and honourable mention was made of you, he said he would visit you too, as you had asked of him, if his affairs would in any way allow it — and I exhorted him to do it. If he does, you will learn more from him than the pen now sustains.

For the rest: what you write and advise about that little island, which cost me so much, I accept candidly and sincerely, as proceeding from a candid mind that wishes me well. The arguments you adduce are probable enough; and although with me they do not merit an irrefragable, geometric force of proof — since both these and more of the kind it is not hard to rejoin, and to oppose stronger ones — yet I think your counsels and others' in this part are not entirely to be resisted: I desire that at least a little time be granted me to deliberate what should be done or omitted in this matter, lest one act too precipitately. For I cannot determine anything certain before, at Prague, I have thoroughly perceived the Emperor's most clement will about my affairs; then at last there will be time enough to conclude something about these and other things — nor does it seem advisable to surrender the Island voluntarily before [that]. Nay, if I should keep it until they take it from me by whatever pretext at last (as was done with the other [holdings], which brought more profit), perhaps I shall have more advantage than disadvantage from it. What if they should sometime object that they would not have taken it back, had I not surrendered it of my own will — that "no injury is done to one willing" — and many other [things], which I gladly forbear? Their threats certainly, however great, I do not dread, relying on the goodness of my cause; let them see to it. I hope God will one day vindicate me from such great injuries; relying on whose protection, "every fortune must be overcome by enduring." More and more particular things — now there is neither leisure nor inclination. I gird myself, as much as I can, for the Prague journey, about to leave the womenfolk at Dresden until I have run over into Bohemia. But do you, my most longed-for Holger — together with your wife and my niece — fare most happily; both your mother and she being most lovingly greeted by me. I have given a greeting from you to the learned men here, with whose familiar acquaintance you formerly enjoyed (as I did before), for we often pleasantly recall your memory; and they in turn greet you back, and pray every best thing for you and yours.

Again, farewell.
Given at Wittenberg, the 7th day of May, the year 1599.
Yours, absent as present,
Tycho Brahe, with my own hand.

[Postscript:] To send the books you ask for by this courier has seemed neither convenient nor advisable; you shall have them another time; or take pains that you receive, from Ramel, the copies offered to the King — which he without doubt spurned — about which matter I wrote before to [my] sister Sophie. For now I at least add certain Poems of two Poets, whom you know, and whose vein you are wont to approve.

Apparatus

  • Recipient. Holger Rosenkrantz of Rosenholm, Tycho's consanguineus et affinis. Written from the Wittenberg winter/spring of 1599, as Tycho prepared for Bohemia. The MS is a later Langebek afskrift.
  • The imperial summons (chain of evidence). Tycho recounts how Coraduz (Vice-Chancellor) had informed him of the Emperor's will at Dresden, then (about end of January 1599) sent a second letter summoning him to Prague — a copy of which Tycho forwarded via Winshemius to Holger (and which Holger's wife/Tycho's sister Sophie also had in extract). The Prague journey was delayed by thaw-flooded roads, the unfinished books for the Emperor, and his elder daughter's grave illness (Magdalene; cf. brahe-t_15990925001).
  • Thomas Franz — the jurist/East-Frisia agent (key for the "Doctor Franz" identity). D. D. Thomas Frantzius, Winshemii Gener = Thomas Franz, Winshemius's son-in-law, a jurist who "agitates the causes of the Counts in East Frisia at the Emperor's court" — i.e. the "Doctor Franz, the jurist, now Chancellor of the Count of Emden" of brahe-t_16000918001 and the Frantzius Iurisconsultus courier of brahe-t_16001008001. He had relayed Coraduz's favourable account of Tycho's prospects. His first name is Thomas — so he is not the Wolfgang Franz addressed in brahe-t_16000327002 (a distinct person; see the corrected note there).
  • Significance — Tycho's reply on the surrender of Hven (the central matter; cross-ref to brahe-t_15990307001). Holger had advised Tycho to surrender Hven voluntarily (his counsel survives as brahe-t_15990307001, the bracketed/enciphered letter). Tycho here answers that advice: he takes it "candidly," grants the arguments "probable" (though not of "geometric" force, and answerable), and will not resist the counsel outright — but insists on time, and will not surrender "that little island, which cost me so much," voluntarily before he has secured the Emperor's will at Prague. Indeed, he reasons it better to let them take Hven "by whatever pretext" (as with his other Danish holdings) than to yield it freely — lest they later say "no injury is done to one willing." He fears no threats, "relying on the goodness of my cause," closing with the Virgilian tag "fortuna omnis superanda ferendo est" ("every fortune must be overcome by enduring," Aeneid V.710). A first-rate document of Tycho's stance on the Hven question.
  • Persons named. Holger Rosenkrantz (and his wife Sophie Brahe, "my niece," and his mother); Coraduz; Vitus Winshemius; Thomas Franz (the jurist/East-Frisia agent); Sophie Brahe (Tycho's sister); Tycho's elder daughter (Magdalene); the Counts of East Frisia; Rudolf II.
  • Classical tag (kept/identified). fortuna omnis superanda ferendo est — Virgil, Aeneid V.710.
  • Calendar. Dateline Wittebergae die 7. Maij Anni 1599; Wittenberg/Saxony kept the Julian (Old Style) calendar. Header agrees with the label.
  • §1 Dreyer collation. Closing postscript (the books withheld via the courier; Ramel; the appended poems), restored from Dreyer VIII pp.155–156 (dropped by the working transcription).
