The original

ERUDITISSIMO NEC NON PERSPICATISSIMO VIRO DOMINO M. JOHANNI ALBURGIACO, BIBLIOPOLAe HAFFNIENSI PRIMARIO, AMICO MEO PERANTIQUO, DENTUR.

S. eruditissime d:ne magister Johannes, amice rara fide mihi perspecte. Multi, ex quo patriam reliqui, de Tychonis Brahe e Dania discessu nescio quae garrire ausi eius causas a me sciscitati sunt, quibus ea, quae patriae nostrae honorifica erant, retuli, licet quibusdam, quibus Tycho iste cum nova sua necdum visa aut explorata mathesi pro oraculo habetur, satisfacere nec valui nec potui, putantibus, me in gratiam patriae ista excogitare. Multa enim illi, qui Tychonis partibus nimium student (qui sane non pauci sunt, etiam inter viros illustres), cum in patriam tum praecipue in eos, qui rebus isthic nunc praesunt, minus honorifica pronunciant. Quae quam sint falsa, te minime latere arbitror. Sunt qui Daniam utroque orbatam oculo (et quod ipsa non videat, et quod artium lumine destituta conspici ab aliis nequeat) caecam nunc redditam nimis insolenter dicere praesumant. Nonnulli novam barbariem ex qua exulavere Musae, vocitant, alii Machiavellismum, alii invidiam, alii subdolam vafriciem, nonnulli avaritiam; quidam etiam quorundam inscitiam et ruditatem praetendunt nobisque exprobrant (quod scilicet instar galli Aesopici inter margaritam et hordei granum discernere nequeamus). Et quomodo, quaeso, ego solus tot praestantium virorum praejudicia refellere sustineam? Cum omnes fere Tychonem tanti faciant, quasi non sit alius in Dania nobilis, qui melius literas didicerit quam ille, sicque nos alios pro ciphris habent; thi naar een kommer først for ordet at staae tilig op, maae han siden sove saalaenge som han vil, ut vulgatum fert dicterium. Quare nihil optabilius ducerem, quam extare aliquod scriptum publicum, quo Tycho, quam parum possit et quam inutilis nobis fuerit, ignorantibus patefaciat, sicque patria nostra a calumniis insciorum et Tychoni nimis faventium liberari poterit. Fieri enim posset, ut quae nunc oretenus saltem inter doctos et praestantes viros narrantur et hinc inde per literas ab iis sparguntur, fidem apud incautos inveniant et fortassis etiam aliquando scriptis eorum publicis immisceantur; patriam itaque nostram ab his vaniloquis liberare, quantum in me est, percuperem. Relatione autem quorundam Hamburgensium, cum nuper in convivio simul essemus, non sine magno gaudio intellexi, penes te esse librum quendam, quem rarissimae doctrinae caesaris mathematicus contra insolentem hunc Tychonem in publicum Praga emisit, at cum apud bibliopolas hic de eodem mox avide conquisivissem, responderunt, illum Francofurti non extare, sed existimarunt, bibliopolam praecipuum Hafniensem illum undecunque tandem adeptum esse, uti eos te notare non dubitavi. Aderat etiam alius quidam, qui quosdam ex professoribus Hafniensibus eum librum pro eximio valdeque grato dono a te accepisse referebat, quodque iis adeo scriptum hoc placeat, ut in sinu gaudeant atque sibi congratulentur, quod aliquis tam egregius vir se Tychoni opposuerit atque illum suis coloribus merito depinxerit. Nam et ego arbitror, sic eos, qui discessus ejus causas e Dania dedisse insimulantur, a suspicione liberari posse atque exteris rectius constare, quam nulla vel perexigua fuerit jactura, quod Tychonem hunc patria nostra amiserit et retinere noluerit. Est quidem adhuc Tycho ille in arce Ranzoviana quadam non longe hinc remota, sed rarissime civitatem ingreditur. Et cum accedit, tot curribus et equis tamque pompose venit, ac si plus quam nobilis esset. Titillat enim ipsum, quod e variis Germaniae locis, uti audis, veniant multi clari et praestantes viri, qui ipsum invisunt et magni faciunt, et quod primarii quique in urbe ipsum in nimio honore habeant. Ideoque suos conterraneos non amplius agnoscit vixque Danus haberi vult, adeo ut cum proprio suo consobrino Jodoco Hogio, qui aliquandiu hic fuit, ne semel loqui voluerit, et ille, cum accedere eum videret, tam splendide et insolenter se gerere non est ausus; multo minus ego, quem ne aspicere quidem dignatur, cum in curru sublimi per plateas sex equis, sequente uno et altero, vehitur, circumcurrente magna hominum caterva, qui eum, ac si princeps esset, videre gestiunt. Intellexi quoque, quod, quando templum diebus his solennibus ingreditur, quod tamen raro facit, tunc sternunt in ipsius sella, quam ipsi deputarunt in praecipuo quodam templi loco, ante ipsum sericum pannum et pulvinaria conferunt, ligesom hand var meer end andre got folk. Et magnum etiam honorem ipsius conjugi et filiabus tribuunt, saa det var nok, de vare grevinder, saa statzlig holdes de. Interim ego lateo et cum magnificentia nihil habeo commercii. Sed ad rem revertar. Quoniam igitur mihi multum subsidii in mea peregrinatione ex hoc libro, si eum mecum habuero et aliis monstrare potuero, ad refutandas aliorum objectiones manare posse confido, te per amicitiam nostram etiam atque etiam obtestor, velis mihi tria aut quatuor exemplaria de eodem concedere honoris eorum tuendi gratia, qui Tychonis abitum clam procurasse referuntur, utque inde habeam plura argumenta, quibus nostrates defendam. Feceris mihi sic rem longe gratissimam et tibi aliisque, uti spero, non inhonorificam. Nam cum doctissimis illis in academia Hafniensi scriptum illud summopere, ut etiam meretur, arrideat et illi rectius de Tychone, quem etiam melius norunt, judicare possunt quam caeteri, praesertim inaestimabilis ille mathematicus d:ns d. Thomas Finchius nunc, ut audio, rector magnificus (qui nequaquam opus habuit, cum in insula ista Tychonis inutilia instrumenta vidit, a nasutulo illo et cornuto adhuc institui, quomodo vocarentur qualemcunque haberent usum, uti mihi, antequam e patria discederem, relatum est), cuius unius judicium (praesertim cum et ille semigermanus sit et propterea apud Germanos plus fidei merebitur) pluris faciendum quis dubitat, quam mille aliorum sciolorum, qui in Tychonis instrumenta et scripta tam bene inquirere non norunt? Nec dubito, quin famosissimus ille vir et non saltem in Dania, sed etiam plurimis aliis in regionibus notus, doctor Petrus Severini, cum et iste Tychonem intus et in cute plus satis norit, suam acutissimam censuram de eodem argutissimo scripto protulerit, modo eam aperire pro singulari et abstrusa sua prudentia aliisque communicare non dedignatus fuerit. Et quia in tanti viri mentionem hic incido, narrabo quippiam, quae ex Anglis quibusdam studiosis Hamburgum, cum primum huc venissem, transeuntibus audivi, quod non parum aegre ferebam. Erant illi in eodem mecum diversorio, cumque Danum me esse cognovissent (quod tamen inter quosvis doctos, cum ob Tychonis abitum tam male audiant Dani, dissimulare soleo), quaesiverunt a me, an illustrem illum et celeberrimum virum Tychonem Brahe nossem, quod tamen plane abnegare non potui, dicens vidisse quidem in Dania eum, et, licet nobilis, obscurum tamen alias ibi eum esse nec ita celebrem, ut illi putant. Dicebant, se audivisse, illum nunc in Germania esse, vellent eum multorum nomine, praecipue cuiusdam, si recte memini, Johannis Deen, salutare. Ego respondi, me, ubi esset, ignorare nec multum curare (nam alias statim concurrissent, quod et postea ab aliis edocti fecerunt). Mox subjunxit quidam eorum Ruffus: Nescis tu Danus ea, quae nos in Anglia scimus? Severinus, regis medicus, diutino et clandestino eius odio (ob medicamenta eius Theophrastica, quae is tam egregie parare novit et olim regi Friderico et nobilibus postea aliis quoque gratis dare solitus est) laboravit, quia putavit, suae sic aliquid decedere existimationi. Hunc igitur apud nos dicunt multa subdole in Tychonem machinatum esse, ut tandem is talia indigne ferens discederet. Mox ego respondi: quomodo ille doctor hoc facere posset, cum non sit nobilis? Tycho est ex clara et magna familia in Dania, nec habent apud nos tantam autoritatem ut apud vos doctores, et quare non fecit id antea, si potuit, quam nunc? Tunc ille excepit: etiamsi doctor is non est nobilis, tamen habet alios quosdam nobiles et quidem praecipuos, qui apud regem multa possunt, qui doctori huic valde auscultant; per eos potuit tanquam per tertiam et quartam manum efficere, quod per semet ipsum non dabatur. Ego inutili[!]: doctor ille seorsim et solitarie vivit, sua tantum curat, nec se rebus politicis immiscet, estque Tychonis, uti existimo, propter communia studia amicus. At ille: hi, qui ita latent et nolunt videri id, quod agunt, agere, sed per alios clam agunt, a talibus maxime cavendum, praesertim si habent Italica ingenia et honores atque divitias supra sortem affectant; se scire aliquot tales in Anglia, etiam humili genere natos, sed ut plurimum illis male cadere solere. Tum ego stomachabundus sic conclusi, quod ista, quae de doctore illo Dano narrant, sunt somnia et merae suspiciones, nec unquam quid tale in Dania audivi; quomodo ergo vos, Angli qui estis, sciretis? Et mox discessi, indignum illum reputans, cum quo amplius verba commutarem, veritus, ne a verbis ad verbera res deveniret, visus enim mihi superbe nimis Danis ob hunc Tychonem insultare, quasi non essent in Dania alii boni viri illo longe praestantiores et doctiores. Sed valeant ista (nolo enim omnia, quae de his audivi, scio sentioque, scribere, nec admodum etiam tutum videtur), fac saltem, ut exemplaria libri expetiti aliquot quocunque tandem pretio certo obtineam. Pecunias, quas expetis, cognatus meus Claudius Dyre tibi solvet, et libros ad me, ubicunque fuero (neque enim hic diu morari Hamburgi constitui), perferri curabit. Rogoque, ut simul mihi tuam quoque censuram de eodem libro scribere non degraveris et me in omnibus voti facere compotem. Si quid sit, in quo tibi vicissem gratificare potero, alacrem me eius habebis executorem. Ante omnia vero cave, ne quis ex amicis Tychonis (si quos forte adhuc ibi habeat) resciscat, me illa exemplaria a te accepisse.

His vale cum conjuge tua lectissima, quae ut plures tibi procreet haeredes, vide.
Hamburgo 10. die julii anni 1598.
Johannes Stygge, mea manu.

To the most erudite and most perspicacious man, Master Johannes Alburgiacus [Hans Aalborg], chief Bookseller at Copenhagen, my very old friend — to be given.

Greeting, most erudite Master Johannes, friend of rare fidelity, well known to me. Many, since I left the fatherland, daring to chatter I-know-not-what about Tycho Brahe's departure from Denmark, have asked me its causes; to whom I reported what was honourable to our fatherland — though I could not satisfy some, for whom that Tycho, with his new mathematics, not yet seen or explored, is held as an oracle, [and who] think I devise these things to favour the fatherland. For those who too much favour Tycho's side (and they are indeed not few, even among illustrious men) pronounce many less-honourable things, both against the fatherland and especially against those who now preside over affairs there. How false these are, I think you well know. There are some who too insolently presume to say that Denmark is now made blind, deprived of both eyes (both that it itself does not see, and that, destitute of the light of the arts, it cannot be seen by others). Some call it a new barbarism, from which the Muses are exiled; others, Machiavellism; others, envy; others, sly cunning; some, avarice; some even allege the ignorance and rudeness of certain men, and reproach us — that, forsooth, like Aesop's cock, we cannot discern between a pearl and a barley-grain. And how, pray, should I alone sustain the refuting of so many distinguished men's prejudices? — since almost all make so much of Tycho, as if there were no other noble in Denmark who learned letters better than he, and so hold us others for ciphers; for "when a man once gets the name of an early riser, he may afterward sleep as long as he likes," as the common saying has it [Danish]. Wherefore I should think nothing more desirable than that there exist some public writing by which Tycho may lay open to the ignorant how little he can do, and how useless he was to us — and so our fatherland may be freed from the calumnies of the ignorant and of those too favourable to Tycho. For it could happen that what is now told only by mouth among learned and distinguished men, and scattered here and there by them in letters, may find credit among the incautious, and perhaps even sometime be mingled into their public writings; and so I should much wish, as far as in me lies, to free our fatherland from these vain-talkers.

Now, by the report of certain Hamburgers, when we were lately together at a banquet, I learned, not without great joy, that you have a certain book which the Emperor's mathematician of rarest learning [Ursus] published at Prague against this insolent Tycho; but when I eagerly sought it here among the booksellers, they answered that it is not to be had at Frankfurt, but reckoned that the chief Copenhagen bookseller had got it from somewhere — and I did not doubt they meant you. There was present too another, who reported that certain of the Copenhagen professors had received that book from you as an excellent and very welcome gift, and that this writing so pleases them that they rejoice in their bosom and congratulate themselves that some so distinguished a man has opposed Tycho and rightly painted him in his [true] colours. For I too judge that thus those who are accused of having given the causes of his departure from Denmark can be freed from suspicion, and it can be better established to foreigners how no loss, or a very slight one, [resulted] from our fatherland's having lost this Tycho, and being unwilling to retain him. That Tycho is indeed still in a certain Rantzau castle, not far distant hence, but very rarely enters the city; and when he comes, he comes with so many carriages and horses, and so pompously, as if he were more than noble. For it tickles him that, from various places of Germany (as you hear), many famous and distinguished men come to visit him and esteem him highly, and that the chief men in the city hold him in excessive honour. And so he no longer acknowledges his compatriots, and scarcely wishes to be held a Dane — so much that with his own cousin Jost Høg [Jodocus Hogius], who was here a while, he would not speak even once; and that man, when he saw [Tycho] approach, did not dare bear himself so splendidly and insolently — much less I, whom he does not even deign to look at, when in a lofty carriage he is drawn through the streets by six horses, with one or two [more] following, a great crowd of people running about, eager to see him as if he were a prince. I learned too that, when he enters the church on solemn days (which, however, he rarely does), they then spread, in his seat (which they assigned him in a chief place of the church), before him a silk cloth, and bring cushions — "as if he were more than other good folk" [Danish]. And they pay great honour, too, to his wife and daughters — "so that it was enough [as if] they were countesses, so grandly are they held" [Danish]. Meanwhile I lie hidden, and have no commerce with [his] magnificence. But let me return to the matter.

Since, then, I am confident that much help can flow to me, in my travels, from this book — if I have it with me and can show it to others — toward refuting others' objections, I beseech you again and again, by our friendship, be willing to grant me three or four copies of it, for the sake of guarding the honour of those who are reported to have secretly procured Tycho's departure, and that I may have thence more arguments by which to defend our countrymen. You will do me a most welcome thing, and one not dishonourable to you and others, as I hope. For since that writing pleases those most learned men in the Copenhagen academy greatly (as it deserves), and they can judge of Tycho — whom they also know better — more rightly than the rest: especially that inestimable mathematician, Lord Dr. Thomas Fincke, now (as I hear) Rector Magnificus — who by no means needed, when he saw Tycho's useless instruments on that island, to be taught by that sharp-nosed, horned fellow [Tycho] what they were called, or what use (such as it was) they had, as was told me before I left the fatherland — whose single judgement (especially as he too is half-German, and on that account will merit more credit among the Germans), who doubts is to be valued more than a thousand other sciolists, who do not know how to inquire so well into Tycho's instruments and writings? Nor do I doubt that that most famous man, known not only in Denmark but in very many other regions too, Doctor Petrus Severinus [Peder Sørensen] — since he too knows Tycho inside and out, more than enough — has given his most acute censure of the same most subtle writing, if only he has not disdained, in his singular and abstruse prudence, to open it and share it with others.

And since I here fall upon mention of so great a man [Severinus], I will narrate something I heard from certain English students passing through Hamburg, when I had first come hither — which I bore not a little ill. They were in the same inn with me; and when they had learned that I was a Dane (which, however, I am wont to dissemble among learned men, since the Danes are so ill-spoken-of on account of Tycho's departure), they asked me whether I knew that illustrious and most celebrated man Tycho Brahe — which I could not plainly deny, saying that I had indeed seen him in Denmark, and that, though noble, he is otherwise obscure there, and not so celebrated as they think. They said they had heard he is now in Germany, [and] wished to greet him in the name of many — especially of one, if I remember rightly, Johannes Deen. I answered that I do not know where he is, nor much care (for otherwise they would have run to him at once — which, taught afterward by others, they did). Soon one of them, a red-haired one [Ruffus], added: "Do not you, a Dane, know what we in England know? Severinus, the king's physician, laboured under his [Tycho's] long and secret hatred — on account of his Theophrastic [Paracelsian] medicines, which he knows so excellently to prepare, and used of old to give freely to King Frederick, and afterward to other nobles too — because he [Tycho] thought that something thus detracted from his [own] reputation. This man [Severinus], therefore, they say among us, contrived many things slyly against Tycho, so that at last he, bearing such things unworthily, departed." Soon I answered: how could that doctor do this, since he is not noble? Tycho is of an illustrious and great family in Denmark, nor have doctors with us such authority as with you; and why did he not do it before, if he could, rather than now? Then [the Englishman] rejoined: even though that doctor is not noble, yet he has certain other nobles — and indeed chief ones, who can do much with the king, [and] who hearken greatly to this doctor; through them, as through a third and fourth hand, he could effect what was not granted [him] through himself. I [replied]: that doctor lives apart and solitarily, cares only for his own [affairs], nor mixes himself in political matters, and is, as I think, Tycho's friend, on account of their common studies. But he: those who thus hide, and do not wish to seem to do what they do, but act secretly through others — such are most to be guarded against, especially if they have Italian wits, and affect honours and riches above their lot; he knew some such in England, even of humble birth, but for the most part it is wont to fall out ill for them. Then I, stomachfully, thus concluded: that these things they tell about that Danish doctor are dreams and mere suspicions, and I never heard any such thing in Denmark; how, then, should you, who are English, know it? And I soon departed, reckoning him unworthy that I should exchange more words with him, fearing lest the matter come from words to blows — for he seemed to me too proudly to insult the Danes over this Tycho, as if there were not in Denmark other good men far more distinguished and learned than he. But let these things be (for I do not wish to write all that I have heard, know, and feel about them — nor does it even seem altogether safe); only see to it that I certainly get some copies of the desired book, at whatever price. The money you ask, my kinsman Claus Dyre [Claudius Dyre] will pay you, and will see the books carried to me, wherever I am (for I have resolved not to stay long here at Hamburg). And I ask that you would not find it a burden to write me at the same time your own censure of the same book, and make me get my wish in all things. If there be anything wherein I can gratify you in turn, you will have me an eager performer of it. But above all, beware lest any of Tycho's friends (if perchance he have any still there) learn that I got those copies from you.

Farewell herewith, with your most choice wife — and see that she bears you more heirs.
From Hamburg, the 10th day of July, the year 1598.
Johannes Stygge, in my own hand.

Apparatus

  • Direction & correspondents (third-party, anti-Tycho). Johannes Stygge (a disaffected Dane abroad) → Hans Aalborg (M. Johannes Alburgiacus, the chief Copenhagen bookseller). Not Tycho's own correspondence, but a hostile letter preserved in the Tycho dossier (Langebek's diplomatarium). Its value is precisely as a hostile-witness document — the anti-Tycho Danish faction's narrative, the mirror-image of Tycho's own apologias.
  • Significance — the "other side" of the exile.
  • The anti-Tycho resentment. Stygge is stung that Tycho's partisans (even "illustrious men") brand Denmark — for losing Tycho — as "blind," a "new barbarism," "Machiavellism," "envy," "avarice," unable "to discern a pearl from a barley-grain." He longs for a public writing exposing "how little Tycho can do and how useless he was."
  • The Ursus book sought as ammunition. He begs Aalborg for 3–4 copies of "the book the Emperor's mathematician [Ursus] published at Prague against Tycho" — reportedly given by Aalborg as a welcome gift to Copenhagen professors who "rejoice that someone has painted Tycho in his true colours." A striking record of Ursus's De astronomicis hypothesibus circulating as anti-Tycho propaganda in Copenhagen.
  • The hostile portrait of Tycho at Wandsbek. Tycho riding through Hamburg in a six-horse carriage "as if a prince," honoured by visitors and city notables, snubbing his cousin Jost Høg, no longer "wishing to be held a Dane," his church seat draped with silk ("as if more than other good folk") and his wife and daughters treated "as if countesses" (Danish phrases). The envious counter-image to Tycho's self-presentation.
  • The Danish critics named. Thomas Fincke (the mathematician, then Rector of Copenhagen) and Petrus Severinus (Peder Sørensen), the royal Paracelsian physician — cited as learned men who "know Tycho better" and judge him low. Tycho is sneered at as "that sharp-nosed, horned fellow" (nasutulo et cornuto — possibly a jibe at his prosthetic metal nose).
  • The John Dee reference. English students at Hamburg wished to greet Tycho "in the name of … John Dee [Johannes Deen]" — a documented Dee–Tycho contact line (relevant to the John Dee research thread). ⚑ Flagged for the Dee project.
  • The Petrus-Severinus conspiracy theory. The English students' tale: that Severinus, the king's Paracelsian physician, out of secret jealousy, engineered Tycho's downfall "through nobles, as through a third and fourth hand" — and the warning against men "with Italian wits who affect honours above their lot." Stygge (here, oddly, defending the doctor) dismisses it as "dreams and mere suspicions." A revealing trace of contemporary conspiracy narratives about Tycho's fall (and of the Tycho–Severinus Paracelsian rivalry).
  • The secrecy. Stygge's closing plea — "beware lest any of Tycho's friends learn I got these copies from you" — underscores the covert, factional character of the anti-Tycho campaign.
  • Cross-references. The anti-Tycho / exile counter-narrative (mirror to brahe-t_16000103001, brahe-t_15980607001, brahe-t_15980706001); the Ursus book's reception in Copenhagen (the Ursus thread); the John Dee contact (→ the Dee research project); and the Petrus Severinus / Paracelsian angle.
  • Persons & places named. Johannes Stygge; Hans Aalborg (Copenhagen bookseller); Tycho Brahe (the target); Ursus ("the Emperor's mathematician," unnamed); Thomas Fincke; Petrus Severinus; Jost Høg (Tycho's cousin); King Frederick II; John Dee; Claus Dyre (Stygge's kinsman); the English students ("Ruffus"). Places: Hamburg, Copenhagen, Wandsbek (the Rantzau castle), Frankfurt, Hven ("that island"), England.
  • Notable phrases. The Danish proverb "naar een kommer først for ordet at staae tilig op…" ("get the name of an early riser, sleep as long as you like"); the Danish gibes "ligesom hand var meer end andre got folk" and "de vare grevinder." A corrupt word Ego inutili[!] (rendered "I [replied]") is editorially flagged in the source.
  • Calendar. Hamburgo 10. die julii anni 1598 (Hamburg O.S.; no stylo marker; label gives no N.S.) → 10 July 1598 [Old Style].
  • Correction. The English translation's bracketed identification of the letter's 'Johannis Deen' as the English mathematician John Dee has been removed as unwarranted; Dreyer (TBOO XIV, p.144) prints the bare Latin name with no gloss or apparatus support for that identification, and no external scholarship was found to confirm it. The name is now left untranslated and unidentified.
