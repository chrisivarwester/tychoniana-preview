Editorial note. One of Tycho's longest and most important letters of cosmological–theological argument: his reply to Caspar Peucer on (i) the nature and substance of the heavens (no solid orbs; not per se luminous; not "fiery"), (ii) the "waters above the firmament" — a sustained refutation of the supercelestial-waters reading of Genesis 1, with Hebrew exegesis and patristic/reformer authorities, (iii) the Milky Way and the New Star of 1572 as condensations of celestial matter, (iv) the wholly celestial matter of comets (against being made of exhalations), (v) metals/alchemy ("terrestrial astronomy"), (vi) Venus and Mercury, and (vii) Rothmann's Copernicanism. Two declared interventions in the source block: (1) the working transcription is densely peppered with digitised text layer-intruded Dreyer apparatus sigla — a stray superscript "A" (the footnote-anchor keying a word to witness A) tacked onto some 90 words, plus a couple of stray "" footnote-markers — these are non-textual and have been removed (not silently normalised; the same treatment as in the Scaliger letter of 23 Aug 1598). (2) The transcription lost or garbled the Hebrew: Dreyer prints the actual Hebrew lemmata of the Genesis/Psalms discussion, but the working text renders most as "XXX" and one as a scrambled fragment (עיקד for רקיע). Two short transcription lacunae (in the alchemy and Craig-Apologia sentences) are marked …. Dating: the letter carries no dateline (it ends simply "Vale"); the catalogue assigns 1590 (the label reads "1590. empty"), consistent with the internal evidence — the Apologeticum against Craig "not yet received," the Epistolae astronomicae and Progymnasmata Tome I in preparation, Bongars's embassy, and Peucer "freed in old age from so many hardships" (released from his Crypto-Calvinist imprisonment in 1586). The original

TYCHO BRAHE AD CASPARUM PEUCERUM.

CUM paulo ante nostratia, quæ quotannis circa Solstitium Æstivum celebrantur, Comitia tuas, excellentissime Domine D. PEUCERE, accepissem literas, et mihi illuc tam de more anniversario quam quod quædam ibidem transigenda haberem, necessario accedendum foret, distuli responsionem usque ad reditum meum et quietiorem in hoc, quod vocas, Uranoscopio, statum. Accidit autem, ut, dum Hafniam ad id tempus excurro, Dn. BONGERSIUM Gallum a Rege suo huc ablegatum offenderim, qui et alteras, licet prioribus breviores a te mihi inscriptas, reddidit, unâque pollicitus est, se ante reditum in Patriam me in hac Insula invisurum, meamque responsionem ad tuas hac occasione secum relaturum, tibique, quem denuo in Germania alloqui constituerat, traditurum. Interim vero negotijs, nescio quibus, urgentibus, hinc in Teutoniam secedit, brevi, uti dicebatur, reversurus. Dum vero ego tota hac elapsa æstate reditum eius, quamvis incassum, nulla interea certiore perferendi ad te literas sese offerente commoditate, expecto, plurimisque involvor distractionibus, rescriptionem utut forte nimis serotinam in hoc usque tempus differre coactus sum. Et licet ne iam quidem oblata aliqua has ad te mittendi in promptu sit ansa, nec adhuc curis omnibus relaxatus sim, attamen ne nimis diu procrastinando ad tuas plane surdus tibi videar, volui nunc tandem has ad te responsionis caussa dare, maximopere rogans, ut silentium hoc diutinum æqui bonique consulas.

Duo autem sunt potissimum, de quibus in hisce tuis literis, nempe de Cœli natura et substantia, tumque nubibus ac aquis, quas supra Cœlos existere opinaris, prolixe mecum disseris. Quoad primum, quantum ad ipsam Cœli tenuissimam purissimamque naturam et ab Elementaribus coinquinamentis corruptelisque immunitatem attinet, ego nullatenus tibi refragor. Nam et ego sic semper sensi, posteaquam Cœlum nullis solidis et duris confectum Orbibus ex motu non saltem Planetarum sed et Cometarum Æthereorum, quos quinos citra Orbis ullius realis concomitantiam Cœlestes carpere vias observavi, accurata me edocuerit confirmâritque experientia. At quod Ætheream Regionem per se lucidam splendescentemque existimas, an omnino hîc assentiendum sit, hæreo, ex quo in tenebris nocturnis, defæcato existente aëre, nullum ad nos reflectat lumen, id quod fieri oporteret, si actu lucidum esset, tenebrosis præsertim aquis et nubibus superne illud, uti tu asseris, circumdantibus, quæ luminis versus centrum repercussionem augerent. Quicquid enim claritatis et fulgoris nox alias opaca Cœlo sereno acquirit, id a Luna per reflectionem luminis Solaris aut etiam a Stellis fulgentibus obtinet, non autem ab ipso Cœli corpore. Id enim citra Stellas aut Luminaria nullam oculis lucem ingerit. Verum an aliquam per se habeat, non tamen tantam, quæ Terras percellat, quemadmodum et Lunam proprij luminis non prorsus orbam esse (licet ad intuitum nostrum, ut in Eclipsatione eius profundiore apparet, non pertingat) quidam existimant, studiosius, cum sit imperceptibile, non inquiro. Nec de hac quæstione, an Cœlum per se luce actuali polleat, disquirenda, multum laboro, cum hæc nostræ intentioni, quomodocumque hac de re statuatur, nihil officiat. Id saltem addam, quod lucem hanc Cœlestem divinæ naturæ proximam esse statuis, ideoque Æthereum Mundum a mutationibus vindicare, videtur nimium quid esse, et ARISTOTELIS opinioni, qui Cœlo divinitatem ideoque et æternitatem attribuit, quam proxime subscribere. Ego certe, si qua lux creata cum divina merito conferri posset, eam quam Deus Angelis et hominum Mentibus indidit, utpote intellectualem, longe propius accedere censuerim. Verumtamen quod hac ratiocinatione Ætheream et Elementarem sive Aëream potissimum Regionem non inter se confundis, ut quidam alij hoc tempore ex sententia IOHANNIS PENÆ Galli, ut opinor, præsumserunt, nec ullas aquas, nihilque vaporosum aut halituosum in Æthera ipsum ascendere censes, id admodum convenienter a te statuitur, nostræque de his sententiæ apprime quadrat. Neque ego, quod aliter sentires, tibi unquam tribui, vel ex tuis scriptis colligi posse asserui. Ideoque nuspiam hoc tibi a me obiectum in meis invenies literis, ut ob id mirer, qua inconsiderata mearum interpretatione id mihi imputes. Cuiusmodi est etiam illud quod mox subiungis, me Cœli naturam igneam affirmare: id meæ equidem sententiæ, qui Cœlum ab omni semper Elementari qualitate vindicandum existimavi, nullatenus correspondet. Quamvis autem per diversas appellationis hæbraicæ Etymologias, expositionem illam, quasi Cœlum sit stupendum quid ex igni et aqua, tanquam diversis naturis conflatum, composuerim, tamen id non eo cedit, quod ipsum aut igneum aut aqueum esse reverâ statuam, sed sic saltem in Etymologijs varie concinnandis ludere placuit. Neque enim hinc (cum metaphorice id saltem dici posse et non secundum Elementaris naturæ qualitates ipsemet diserte protestatus sim) serio aliquid probare elaboravi. Imo ipse Deus omnis materiæ expers in sacris literis ut Deut. 4, 24 et 9, 3, item Hæb. 12, 29, utut nemo de ipsius vera essentia, quæ imperscrutabilis est, quippiam ratiocinari potuerit, ignis nihilominus consumens appellatur. Quid igitur obstat, quo minus Cœlum et Sidera Cœlestia, quæ in morem flammantis alicuius, ex inferioris Mundi corporibus lucent et calefaciunt, ignis vocabulo, sumpta, per similitudinem quandam nostro captui apprime sese attemperantem, appellatione nominare, aut Etymologice explicare liceat? Propterea tamen Cœlum reverâ Ignem esse Elementarem, uti ferme cum PLATONE PARACELSUS prodidit, nequaquam infero. Imo ne quidem sub concava Lunæ sphæra ignem aliquem Elementarem, quemadmodum somniavit ARISTOTELES, Opticis rationibus omnino refragantibus, existere arbitror. Nec etiam superiorem Aëris Regionem, ob rapidissimum corporum Cœlestium motum, qui naturalis, non violentus est, inque tenuissima ac liquidissima Cœli essentia, absque omni solidorum corporum fractione aut collisione peragitur, solidissimam, quæ tua est opinio, existere, sicque ignis Elementaris vices supplere, astipulandum censeo. Quin potius quicquid Physici ab hoc Elemento pro compositione et conservatione rerum inferiorum petere soleant, id ego a Cœlestibus, ipso præsertim Sole, vivifico calore omnia imbuente, promanare iudico.

Alterum vero quod urges, est de aquis Cœlestibus, quas ex literali quodam Scripturæ sensu, supra extima Cœli mœnia evehere non dubitas. Fateor quidem ex verbis MOSIS in principio Geneseos prima id fronte quadantenus apparere. At si quis penitius et genuinum Scripturæ intellectum et rei naturam introspiciat, nisi omnino præoccupato sit iudicio, longe id secus se habere intelliget. Primo tenendum est vocem [רקיע] (quam CASTALIO non inepte liquidum, alij expansum verterunt) non eodem ubique sensu in creationis historia describenda a MOSE intelligi; sed alias generaliter et latius, totum hoc liquidum seu expansum, quod a Terra, ad summa usque Cœli culmina se extendit, uti ipse quoque indicas, denotat, alias pro huius parte tantum idque vel superiore, quæ lucida Siderum in se corpora continet, interdum vero pro inferiore hac liquidi regione, quæ Terram proxime circumdans visuique nostro magis patescens ad extima plus minus nubium cacumina sese extendit, accipitur. Quo quidem sensu Cœli vocabulum, in Scriptura crebro, uti et hoc loco, intelligitur, cum ea ad rudiorum hominum captum, qui illam duntaxat expansi partem, quæ proxime Terræ convexo incumbit, Cœli nomine appellant, sese accommodet. Quare cum MOSES hanc Creationis historiam rudi et simplici tantum populo, non Mathematicæ aut Physicæ scientiæ gnaris, conscripserit, eaque duntaxat quæ in oculos hominum incurrunt, non quæ invisibilia aut abscondita sunt, exponenda susceperit, quis non videat illum per aquas Cœlestes, nubes aquosas, quæ superne capitibus nostris imminent atque infra se partem illius expansi seu liquidi, quod ab aquis maris eas discernit, non immensam aliquam universæ Cœli regioni circumfusam aquarum molem intellexisse?

Quin et sequeretur, aut triplices a Deo creatas fuisse aquas, nempe maris, nubium et supercœlestem, quarum tamen media, nubium scilicet, a MOSE neglecta sit, aut binas saltem, utpote marinam et supercœlestem illam, de quibus duntaxat in rerum creaturum historia mentio facta est. At quis fidelissimum illum visibilem creaturarum descriptorem MOSEN tam oscitantem in mirabilibus Dei operibus referendis fuisse existimet, ut nubium aquas tam alto silentio intactas reliquerit, quod earum ne semel inter reliqua sensibilia Dei opera meminisse voluerit, cum tamen non parum admirationis habeat, eas sine fulcro in liquido aëre tam firmiter pendere absque ruinæ periculo, et insuper multiplicem in terra irriganda et fœcundanda utilitatem subministrare, quam homines plus quam cætera illa altiora, atque a quotidiano usu, prout illis videtur, remotiora magis æstimant atque respiciunt. Has inquam aquositates neglexisset MOSES et interim de nescio quibus supercœlestibus, quæ nec sensibus patent nec usum evidentem exhibent, supervacanee loquutus fuerit. Revera itaque MOSEN per geminas istas quas denotat aquas, interstitio firmamenti divisas, marinam scilicet inferiorem et nubium eius aliqua parte superiorem intelligere necesse est. Nam et sic [רקיע] sive expansum, quod CASTALIO opportune uti dixi liquidum vocat, discernit aquas ab aquis, has quadam sui portione intercedens; neque enim MOSES expresse dixit totum [רקיע] disgregare aquas ab aquis, ut tu ipsius sensum accipis. Imo et is semetipse explicat Gen. 9, dum ait Deum in nubibus posuisse arcum in testimonium, quod aquæ non amplius in Terrarum diluvium venturæ essent. Quas enim hîc aquas, nisi nubes signat, e quibus potissimum inundatio temporibus NOE acciderat, atque hinc cataractas Cœli sive meatus, quæ tunc aperiebantur, non extra Fixarum orbem, uti tu vis, petendas esse liquet. Ex IERHEMIA insuper cap. 10 et 51 ubi sequentia verba utrobique habentur: »Qui fecit Terram virtute sua, stabilijt orbem habitabilem sapientia sua, et prudentia sua extendit Cœlos, quo vocem suam edente, fremitus aquarum est in Cælis, qui effert vapores ab extremitate Terræ, fulgura cum ipsa pluvia facit, et producit ventum e thesauris suis«, idipsum adhuc manifestius colligitur. Hinc etenim liquido patet, aquas illas Cœlestes in ea aëris Regione, unde nubes, fulgura et pluvia producuntur, intelligendas atque eandem pro Cœlo accipi.

Præterea maxima ex aquis supracœlestibus concessis absurda sequuntur. Nam si ullæ eiusmodi aquæ illic existant, quæ extima Cœli theatra suo circumquaque ambitu includant et complectantur, unâ vel moveantur vel quiescant necesse est? Si immotæ permanent, aut rapidissimam Cœli gyrationem impediunt, aut oportebit maximum esse interstitium inter motum et quiescens, ut rapidissimus ille cursus successive in quietem desinat. Immensum itaque spatium stellis destitutum nullique usui accommodum relinqui, quod inconveniens esset, oportebit. At si unâ cum Cœlo convolventur istæ aquæ, quî poterint concitatissimam illam rapiditatem perferre, perpetuoque unâ cum hac inconsumtibiles permanere aut illi nulla actione officere? Adde quoque de magnitudine nimia harum aquarum respectu cæterarum, quas MOSES subcœlestes vocat, per quas Maria Terrestrem globum interluentia intelligenda esse, nemini est dubium. Si enim aquæ illæ, quas supra octavam vis esse sphæram, saltem unicam vel dimidiam Terræ Diametrum in sua densitate occupent, quod sane quam minimum esset respectu tantæ circumferentiæ et pro usu illo, quem tribuis ad lucem increatam a creaturis, modo id tibi concedendum foret, arcendam, cumque ad hanc octavam sphæram terminandam sint ad minimum 14000 Semid. Terræ iuxta nostram hypothesium Cœlestium ordinationem (quod et Ptolemaica 20000 circiter Semid. requirat et Copernicea speculatio in immensum hîc abeat) ratiocinare quæso, quantæ futuræ sint illæ aquæ, quæ tantum globum undiquaque vel in tantillula, uti dixi, crassitie ambiant, cuius Semidiameter tot millia Semid. Terræ continebit. Certe deprehendes aquarum istarum molem vix eam habere ad maria sive aquas subcœlestes æstimationem, quam habet tota aqua marina ad unicam guttam. Proinde si aquæ illæ Terram contingerent operirentque, uti ab initio ante discretionem factum fuisse dicitur, in ipsum Cœlum sua quantitate alte ascenderent. Qualis ergo esset aquarum supra et infra expansum, de quibus MOSES loquitur, comparatio et proportio? Quapropter MOSEN aquarum supercœlestium nomine nequaquam aqueam aliquam, vastissimæ Ætheris capacitate undiquaque circumfusam, uti tu censes, molem, ac non mea solum, verumetiam præstantissimorum Theologiæ Professorum ut MELANTHONIS, CASTALLIONIS, CALVINI et aliorum sententia, aquosas Cœli nubes eum intelligere minime dubium est.

Sed tu nihilominus pro tua illa de Aquis supercœlestibus opinione asserenda multum laboras, utque aquis illis usum aliquem, ne forte supervacaneæ sint, constituas, ais illas quasi necessitate quadam illic dispositas, ut interstitium quoddam inter conditum orbem et lucem in eo creatam et Cœlos Cœlorum, quos inhabitat Deus in luce æterna, increata et inaccessa creaturis, præbeant. At quamnam hîc lucem æternam et increatam, quosnam Cœlos Cœlorum intelligis? Siccine infinito et immenso finitos præscribere limites, æterna cum temporaneis, invisibilia cum visibilibus confundere non indecorum ducis? Solus etenim Deus infinitus et increatus existit; proinde ipsi dimensio, locus, spatium, situs et id genus alia, quæ creaturarum sunt attributa, competere nequeunt. Et si lux illa, quam vocas æternam et increatam, aliud quid præter ipsum Deum est, iam duo sunt æterna increataque, ideoque duo dij et duo principia. At nihil increatum et æternum est præter ipsum Deum, qui et unicus est. Quin et si qua esset lux æterna, extra Mundum corporeum, illa oculis humanis, etiam semotis omnibus interstitijs, non caperetur. Verissime enim in Opticis asseritur, omnia quæ videntur tempore comprehendi. Unde sequitur, quæ extra tempus et æterna sunt, temporaneis oculis non patere. Ascribitur etiam Aquis istis Cœlum ex tua opinione circumdantibus et hæc utilitas, quod excursum visionis nostræ abscindant et definiant, ne forte extra Mundum aut in infinitum abeat. At quid hoc est dicere, cum tanta vis facultati visuali nullatenus insit? Imo si stellæ non essent lucida corpora et sponte sua oculis sese ingererent, nulla alias oculorum in tanta distantia res cerneret. Frustra igitur visioni nostræ densissimam et obscurissimam illam supra Cœlos aquarum interstitium prætendis, cum sua natura et conditione ab augustissima increatarum maiestate prohibeatur.

Facessat itaque ista de aquis supercœlestibus ob creatarum et increatarum inter se rerum distinctionem, visionisque nostræ radium cohercendum ac definiendum, tam devijs fœcunda absurditatibus opinio. Nihil etiam ad eam quam tantopere defendendam suscipis opinionem in vulgata nominis [שמים] quasi [מים] ibi explicatione præsidij obtinebis. Est enim hæc arbitraria et proprietati sanctæ linguæ, quæ nec talem vocum, præsertim in appellativis nominibus, compositionem, nec ullum facile sine compensatione literarum, quod in hac voce sit, si composita esse statuatur, defectum admittit, contraria. Vocabulum itaque [שמים] nulla vulgari compositionum lege conflata est, sed simplex existit, et stupendum vel admirandum quid denotat a verbo [שמם], quod obstupescere vel admirari significat, non aliter quam a […] formatur […] et alia id genus, deductum. Ideoque PAGNINUS in suo lexico hoc nomen suo radicali verbo non immerito statim subiunxit. Revera etenim Cœlum admirandum et stupendum quiddam existit sive motuum leges perennes nec cuivis obvias, sive materiam, ex qua constat, quæ inexplicabilis est, spectemus.

At excipis, nubes has aquosas, de quibus ego MOSEN intelligo, non esse perpetuas, eo quod interdum sive nonnunquam dissolvantur et huc illuc volitent et ferantur, ideoque MOSEN per aquas Cœlestes, non quæ momentanea, sed quæ semper durant, intellexisse. Verum quidem est, nubes, quas ego pro aquis Cœlestibus habeo, non semper in quolibet Horizonte apparere, sed ut plurimum; attamen si non actu, saltem potentia semper ibi sunt. Et vis illa, qua in sublimi coalescunt, atque ne decidant, sustinentur, perpetuo illic adest. Adde quod universam Terram in toto suo circuitu respiciendo vix dabitur omnes Horizontes simul et semel a nubibus superne impendentibus immunes esse, quin in aliquo saltem Terreni globi Horizonte conspiciantur.

Ad Scripturæ demum testimonia ulterius provocas, ex quibus literali quadam nimisque rigida pro sententia tua stabilienda explicatione auxilium, sed frustra, imploras. Neutiquam enim illa, quæ pro te huc allegas ex psal. 104: »Qui contignat in nubibus tabernacula sua« aut ex psal. 18: »Posuit tenebras latibulum suum, in circuitu tabernaculi sui tenebrosam aquam et densas nubes«, de aquis et nubibus ullis supercœlestibus, sed de aëris Regione veluti in cœnacula nubibus distincta, uti TREMELLIUS et IUNIUS item MOLLERUS et alij non inconvenienter etiam exponunt, intelligenda veniunt, nisi forte cui et ibidem ventorum atque ignium seu fulminum mentio fiat, hæc quoque supra orbem stellatum locum habere censes. At ex eiusdem psalmi vers. 13, ubi sic dicitur: »Qui irrigat montes e cœnaculis suis, ut a frugibus operum tuorum satietur terra«, ex quo eadem vox hæbraica [עליות] quam PAGNINUS in suo dictionario hæbraico cœnacula seu nubes denotare existimat, utrobique habetur, nubium hîc aquas montes et terram irrigantes et fœcundantes intelligendas esse, quis inficias iverit?

Præterea cum non parum absurditatis ingerat, imo naturaliter considerando plane impossibile sit aquas supra Cœlos absque fulcris et repagulis cohiberi atque convolvi, tu per Terræ in liquido aëre stabilitatem id excusare laboras. Attamen inconsequenter, cum non eadem subsit ratio. Terra enim et omnia globosa Mundi corpora proprio innituntur centro, omnesque eorum partes huc naturaliter tendunt, adeo ut si a Sole vel Luna aut cæteris sideribus aliquantulam portiunculam violenter avulsum statuamus, ea mox proprium Astrum repetit, suum affectans centrum. Tanta enim vis meditullijs Mundanorum corporum divinitus indita est. Quod sane de aquis extramundanis non consimiliter admitti potest, cum prædictum globum rotundum non totaliter compleant. Ideoque nisi certis fulcris et repagulis cohiberentur, aquas marinas, unde disgregatæ erant, adeoque Terram gravitatis suæ centrum, necessario reviserent. Nec valet argumentum: Deus potuit Terram crassam et gravem in tenui firmare aëre. Ergo et nubes supra Cœlum. Scis enim a posse ad inesse (ut vulgo loquuntur) non sequi inductionem. Proptereaque in Psalmis diserte dicitur: »Deus omnia quæ voluit fecit in Cœlo et in Terra«. Et Deus est ordinis atque utilitatis author. Ideoque Cœlestia Elementaribus confundere coinquinareque absque manifesto usu nequaquam volet.

Porro meam de via lactea sententiam non tam reijcis, quam abundius illustras. Ego enim, etsi Cœlum liquidissimum et tenuissimum quid, Astrorum motibus ubique pervium, non tamen plane incorporeum et immateriale, sed omnia corpora Cœlestia ex eadem cum Cœlo constare substantia in unum aliquem densiorem globum, lucis receptioni accommodum, sapientiaque Opificis compactum esse statui. Quemadmodum videmus in hac Mundi natura, ea, quæ in Mari ubique pervio generantur, ex aquea constare substantia, atque in eandem facile resolvi, licet hæc ipsa iam concreta, liquida et pervia non sint. Nam et Metalla omnia Gemmæque his affines in Mari radices habent, quod paucissimis ante PARACELSUM innotuit. Astra autem Cœli licet e materia constant Cœlesti, ab ipso tamen Cœlo densitate et lumine plurimum differre, non magis mirum esse debet, quam aurum et argentum cæteraque Metalla e mari (uti dixi) originem trahentia nihilominus sua forma, dura compagine cæterisque appropriatis multimode ab hoc discrepare, ut de alijs, quæ in Terra atque Aëre e Terrestri Aëreaque substantia prioribus dissimillima conformantur, nihil addam. Dum itaque Galaxiam ex Cœli substantia compactiva atque coagmentata constare volo, non aliud dico, quam eam cum reliquis stellis eandem habere materiam, quæ tamen hîc sparsa atque in latum certo ductu per totum Cœlum diffusa cernatur, in illis autem ad formam unius rotundi corporis arctius compacta sit. Atque hanc esse a vero non alienam, de via lactea, sententiam, ipse intuitus ocularis, attenta inspectione et collatione cum reliquis Stellis præsertim tenuiore lumine apparentibus facta, haud obscure testatur.

Imo Stellam illam Novam et supra modum admirandam, quæ circa finem anni 1572, iuxta huius, de qua loquimur, Galaxiæ confinia, ubi etiam Cassiopeæ Astrum visitur, exorta est, eandem cum via lactea habuisse materiam, sed tamen magis in unum globum compactum sentio. Atque ut id certius statuam, hiatus semicircularis ad magnitudinem quasi Lunæ dimidiæ in margine viæ lacteæ eodem prorsus in loco, ubi Stella Nova situm habuit, adhuc Cœlo existente apprime defæcato, et latente Luna, attente intuenti apparens, quam me nunquam antea ibidem vidisse memini, omnino persuadet. At dices: Si ex eadem et perpetua cum reliquis Sideribus Galaxiæ substantia extitit hæc Stella, cur non etiam perpetuo ijsdem coæva permansit? Ad hanc dubitationem nolo in præsentiarum multa respondere, in fine Operis, quod de Ascititijs Cœli Phænomenis molior, meam in his atque similibus aperturus sententiam. Id solummodo hoc loco dicam: Quæ miraculose et præter communem ordinem nonnunquam in rerum natura proveniunt, ijsdem cum Cœlo diu consuetis subijci legibus, non necesse habent.

Sed quid Sapphirinus ille Cœli color te iterum movet, ut hunc ex nubibus et aquis lucem, quam Cœlo astribuis, extra viam lacteam potissimum obscurantibus, provenire, sed frustra, existimes? Imo Sapphirinus iste color propter imbecillitatem visus nostri ingeritur oculis. Licet enim aër per se totaque Ætherea Regio omnis coloris expertia sint, tamen cum in ista profundissima immensitate visus non inveniat, ubi sistatur, color ille cæruleus oculis per accidens ingeritur, quemadmodum sæpe videmus omnia, quæ multum distant, eiusmodi oculis apparere, quamvis per se talem colorem non habeant. At hæc a rei Opticæ scriptoribus satis superque declarata sunt, ut non opus sit, e nubibus supracœlestibus hunc Sapphirinum colorem incassum mutuari, qua etiam de re in superioribus literis te admonui.

De Nova autem hac, cuius mentionem feci, Stella, in priore huius, de quo dixi, Operis tomo affatim tracto. Ibi, quæ et in Landgravianis et tuis, tum etiam aliorum circa hanc pronuntiatis, desiderem, brevi cognosces. Tum etiam, quod eius decreta longe maiora universalioraque quam tibi visum est, existere, et necdum forte incoata esse nedum præterijsse, non citra iustas rationes sentiam.

Nullatenus etiam tecum statuere possum ex materia aliqua Elementari productam fuisse hanc Stellam. Quod tamen non assertive pronuntias. Demonstro enim illic, eam mole sui corporis globum, quem Terræ et Maria efficiunt, plus quam ter centies exsuperasse. Quæ ergo materia hinc progrediens tanto corpori suppeteret, si modo aliquid exhalationum Terrestrium in Cœlum usque ascendere posse concedendum foret? In qua ego sententia neutiquam sum vel fui unquam, licet tu mihi id circa Cometarum procreationem immerito attribuis, dum ais me ex elevata in Cœlum et coctionibus prius attenuata ac depurata in Orbe sublunari materia, accessuque novæ aucta et compacta, Cœlestique lumine perfusa, Cometarum corpora nasci opinari. Id equidem ego nunquam asserui; nec ex literis meis ad te vel alios datis, tale quid colligetur. Multo minus ex scriptis publice editis. Nam in libro secundo de recentioribus Cœli Phænomenis (quem te accepisse puto) aperte habetur, me Cometarum materiam ex Elementari substantia quomodocunque subtiliata atque exaltata constare, nullatenus existimare. Quod vel illa quæ pag. 254 apposui verba liquide testantur, quæ sic habent: »Ego materiam omnium Cometarum prorsus Cœlestem esse iudico, siquidem in ipso Cœlo procreantur«. Observavi præteritis aliquot annis exactissime quinque Cometas, quos omnes in ipso Cœlo versatos fuisse, Parallaxeos exilitas evidenter convicit, adeo ut quidam eorum Sole multo altiores extiterint. Et sane eo usque Elementarem aliquam evolare materiam, quæ in tale et tantum corpus concrescat, quis crediderit? Nam et ipsa moles maior extitit, quam quod Terra ipsi materiam suppeditare potuerit, cum quidam ex ipsis et hanc integram magnitudine vera superârint. De toto autem hoc negotio, cum alibi ex professo tractem, hîc pluribus supersedeo.

Quæ de Metallorum generatione, quod ex una eademque constent prima materia, licet non in singulis æqualiter digesta, depurata atque exaltata, huc exempli loco, ut inferiorum rerum Naturam cum superiori aliquatenus conferrem, adduxi, si tuis, quas foves, rationibus minus probentur, meaque in his sententia tibi non satis explorata sit, equidem id mihi nullam parit admirationem, ex quo sciam, ea, quæ in Academijs et a vulgaribus Philosophis de Metallorum natura et ortu traduntur, opinabilia saltem esse, nec in lumine naturæ fundari. Qui enim resolutionem eorum non novit, compositionem vix percipiet. Arte ergo opus est Pyronomica, quæ ex Hermetica procedit Philosophia. Ad quam omnes, qui in Academijs Philosophiæ incubuerunt, plane imperiti accedunt. Credas me, qui non minus laboris et studij tum quoque … huius inferioris et Terrestris Astronomiæ (sic enim hanc artem subinde vocare soleo) quam in superiorem et Cœlestem illam collocavi, ipsa experientia edoctum esse, rem ita se habere, prout breviter ultimo indicavi. Quæ cum tibi minus perspecta sint, utpote in tractatione Spagirica non versato, uberius ea attingere otiosum duco. Unicum tantum de hoc suggeram, quod ex hac metallorum cum Cometarum collata conformatione illicere potuisses, me Cometas ex Cœlesti prorsus constare materia, omnis Elementaris qualitatis expertes, innuere voluisse.

Dispositionem operis mei Astronomici tibi placere lætor. Nec de Venere et Mercurio, quod Sole non minus altiores quam humiliores reddantur, magna erit apud rem ipsam penitius considerantes in persuadendo difficultas. Neque id quod adducis, Veneris stellam aliquando eodem die simul et vespertinam et eöam apparuisse, nostra ratiocinia ullatenus impedit, quin et idipsum a me semel observatum esse prioribus tibi indicavi literis. Nec quicquam hinc obstaculi, quo minus hypothesium nostrarum ordinatio rite constet, suggeri comperio. Sed de his in præsentiarum hæc sufficiant, vel potius circa quæstionem illam, quæ Cœlum supremum Aquis obnubilat, tanquam minus Astronomicam, imo ab omni pene Philosophia abhorrentem, nimia et quoque supervacanea esse poterint.

CHRISTOPHORUM ROTHMANNUM Mathematicum Landgravianum hypotheses Copernicæas Terræque in his astructam mobilitatem serio tueri, totumque Cœlum nihil aliud quam Aërem Elementarem adeoque Animalem esse, existimare, ex quo dubitas, mitto ad te particulam libri Epistolarum Astronomicarum, quas me aliquando publicandum tibi significavi, continentem potissimum præter ipsius Illustrissimi Principis ad me de rebus Astronomicis perscriptas, unâ cum nostra ad singulas responsione, huius quoque ROTHMANNI et meas literas, quibus ultro citroque de toto hoc negotio disputamus. Ex his plenius percipies, quæ sit illius atque mea, tum in talibus tum etiam nonnullis alijs huc pertinentibus sententia. Quam ut pro iudicij tui acumine expendere, atque quod tibi probabilius videatur, discernere velis, te etiam atque etiam rogatum habeo. Nam et in postremis literis, cum is ad arbitros appellet, ego te præ cæteris elegi, ut ex ijs ipsis percipies, sicubi otium fuerit eas perlegere, quod ut facias, tuamque eruditam de rebus illis controversis censuram mecum impertiaris, a te etiamnum amanter peto.

Plura de his non addam, ne nimium excrescat Epistola. Nec ad posteriores tuas, quas BONGERSIUS, uti ab initio dixi, reddidit, multis respondebo, cum per se breviores illæ fuerint, et solummodo ea, quæ his prolixioribus continebantur, denuo summatim inculcârint. Id saltem paucis indicabo, me nequaquam existimare Cœlum Elementaribus mutationibus obnoxium, aut quippiam Elementare, quod huic inferiori Naturæ conforme sit, illi inesse, velut sub finem eiusdem Epistolij mihi tribuere videris. A qua suspicione, si non alia, saltem hæc tibi de Epistolis Astronomicis, quam mitto, particula me vindicabit. Scriptum nostrum Apologeticum, quod tibi inspiciendum dijudicandumque misi, te non accepisse suspicor, ex quo in neutris literis huius mentionem facias. Sed levis est iactura; brevi enim, volente Deo, idipsum, tomo secundo adiectum, typisque nostris excusum obtinebis. Tum demum … præstantissimi illius Mathematici et Philosophi Aristotelici IOANNIS CRAIGIJ Scoti obiectiones nostras assertiones de situ Cometarum Olympico oppugnantes, non tamen, uti spero, expugnantes, sufficienter … interponere censuram. Sed hîc scribendi cursum sisto et Deum rogo, ut te in hac senecta e tot tantisque ærumnis liberatum, clementer etiam diu superstitem atque incolumem conservare dignetur. Vale.

Tycho Brahe to Caspar Peucer.

When, a little before our home Assembly which is held yearly about the summer solstice, I had received your letter, most excellent Lord Master Peucer, and I had necessarily to go thither, both by the anniversary custom and because I had certain [matters] to transact there, I deferred my reply until my return and a quieter state in this — as you call it — Uranoscope [star-watching place]. But it happened that, while I went over to Copenhagen at that time, I met Master Bongars [Bongersius], a Frenchman sent here by his King, who delivered also a second letter (though shorter than the prior one) inscribed to me by you, and at the same time promised that he would visit me on this Island before his return to his homeland, and would carry my reply to yours back with him on this occasion, and would deliver it to you, whom he had resolved to address again in Germany. But meanwhile, urged by I-know-not-what pressing affairs, he withdrew hence into Germany, soon to return, as was said. But while I awaited his return — though in vain — throughout this past summer, no more reliable convenience of conveying a letter to you presenting itself meanwhile, and [while] I am wrapped up in very many distractions, I have been forced to defer the reply (over-late, perhaps) until this very time. And although not even now is any handle of sending this to you ready to hand, nor am I yet relaxed from all cares, nevertheless, lest by procrastinating too long I should seem to you plainly deaf to yours, I have wished now at last to give this to you for the sake of a reply, asking most greatly that you take this long silence in fair and good part.

Now there are two [topics] chiefly about which, in this letter of yours, you discourse with me at length — namely, the nature and substance of the Heaven, and then the clouds and waters which you think exist above the Heavens. As to the first, in so far as it pertains to the most tenuous and most pure nature of the Heaven itself, and its immunity from Elemental defilements and corruptions, I in no way gainsay you. For I too have always thought thus, since accurate experience has taught and confirmed me that the Heaven is fashioned of no solid and hard Orbs — from the motion not only of the Planets but also of the aethereal Comets, of which I have observed five plying their celestial ways without the accompaniment of any real Orb. But that you think the Aethereal Region per se luminous and shining — whether this is altogether to be assented to, I am in doubt, since in the darkness of night, the air being clear, it reflects no light to us — which ought to happen, if it were actually luminous, especially with the dark waters and clouds (as you assert) surrounding it above, which would increase the repercussion of light toward the centre. For whatever of brightness and splendour the otherwise-opaque night acquires under a serene Heaven, it obtains from the Moon by the reflection of the Sun's light, or also from the shining Stars, not from the body of the Heaven itself. For this, apart from the Stars or Luminaries, throws no light into the eyes. But whether it has some [light] per se — not, however, so great as to strike the Earth (just as some think the Moon too is not wholly deprived of its own light, although it does not reach our sight, as appears in its deeper Eclipse) — I do not inquire more studiously, since it is imperceptible. Nor do I labour much about this question, whether the Heaven per se is strong in actual light, to be examined — since this, however it be determined, in no way obstructs our intention. This only I will add: that you hold this celestial light to be nearest to the divine nature, and therefore vindicate the Aethereal World from changes — [this] seems to be something excessive, and to subscribe very closely to Aristotle's opinion, who attributed divinity, and therefore also eternity, to the Heaven. I, certainly, if any created light could deservedly be compared with the divine, should judge that the one which God implanted in the Angels and in the Minds of men — being intellectual — comes far nearer. But that, by this reasoning, you do not confuse the Aethereal and the Elemental (or chiefly Airy) Region with each other — as certain others in this time have presumed, from the opinion of Jean Pena the Frenchman, as I think — nor judge that any waters, nor anything vaporous or exhalation-like, ascends into the aether itself: that is most fittingly determined by you, and squares perfectly with our opinion about these [matters]. Nor did I ever attribute to you that you thought otherwise, nor assert that it could be gathered from your writings. And so nowhere in my letters will you find this objected to you by me — that I should wonder by what inconsiderate interpretation of mine you impute it to me. Of which kind is also that which you presently subjoin, that I affirm the nature of the Heaven to be fiery: that in no way corresponds to my opinion, who have always thought the Heaven must be vindicated from every Elemental quality. But although, through various Etymologies of the Hebrew appellation, I composed that exposition — as if the Heaven were some astonishing thing conflated from fire and water, as from diverse natures — yet this does not amount to my really determining it to be either fiery or watery; but it pleased [me] only thus to play in variously composing Etymologies. For neither did I labour to prove anything seriously hence (since I myself expressly protested that this could only be said metaphorically, and not according to the qualities of an Elemental nature). Nay, God himself, devoid of all matter, in the sacred writings — as Deut. 4:24 and 9:3, likewise Heb. 12:29 — although no one could reason anything about his true essence (which is inscrutable), is nonetheless called a "consuming fire." What therefore prevents [us] from being permitted to name the Heaven and the Celestial Stars — which, in the manner of something flaming, shine and warm [things] from the bodies of the lower World — by the word "fire," taken by a certain similitude perfectly accommodating itself to our grasp, [whether] to name [them by] the appellation or to explain [them] Etymologically? Yet I by no means infer therefrom that the Heaven is really Elemental Fire, as Paracelsus (with Plato, well-nigh) reported. Nay, I do not think that even under the concave sphere of the Moon there exists any Elemental fire, as Aristotle dreamed — Optical reasonings altogether gainsaying [it]. Nor do I judge it must be agreed that the upper Region of the Air is most solid (which is your opinion) — on account of the most rapid motion of the Celestial bodies, which is natural, not violent, and is performed in the most tenuous and most fluid essence of the Heaven, without any breaking or collision of solid bodies — and that thus it supplies the place of Elemental fire. Rather, whatever the Physicists are wont to seek from this Element for the composition and conservation of lower things, I judge to emanate from the Celestial [bodies], especially the Sun itself, imbuing all things with vivific heat.

But the other [topic] which you press is about the Celestial waters, which, from a certain literal sense of Scripture, you do not doubt to carry up above the outermost walls of the Heaven. I confess indeed that, from the words of Moses at the beginning of Genesis, this appears in some manner at first glance. But if anyone look more deeply into both the genuine understanding of Scripture and the nature of the thing — unless his judgment be altogether preoccupied — he will understand that it is far otherwise. First, it must be held that the word [raqiaʿ, the firmament/expanse] (which Castalio [Castellio] not ineptly rendered "liquid," others "expanse") is not understood by Moses in the same sense everywhere in the describing of the creation-history; but sometimes generally and more broadly it denotes this whole liquid or expanse, which extends from the Earth up to the highest summits of the Heaven (as you yourself also indicate); sometimes [it is taken] only for a part of this, and that the upper [part], which contains in itself the luminous bodies of the Stars; but sometimes for this lower region of the liquid, which, closely surrounding the Earth and lying more open to our sight, extends to the outermost tops, more or less, of the clouds. In which sense indeed the word "Heaven" is frequently understood in Scripture (as also in this place), since it accommodates itself to the grasp of the ruder men, who call by the name of "Heaven" only that part of the expanse which lies nearest upon the convexity of the Earth. Wherefore, since Moses wrote this Creation-history for a rude and simple people only, not versed in Mathematical or Physical science, and undertook to expound only those things which strike the eyes of men, not those which are invisible or hidden — who does not see that by "Celestial waters" he understood the watery clouds, which hang above over our heads, and below themselves [have] that part of the expanse or liquid which separates them from the waters of the sea — not some immense mass of waters poured around the whole region of the Heaven?

Nay, it would also follow that either three waters were created by God — namely of the sea, of the clouds, and the supercelestial — of which, however, the middle (namely of the clouds) was neglected by Moses; or at least two, namely the marine and that supercelestial one, of which alone mention was made in the history of created things. But who would think that most faithful describer of the visible creatures, Moses, to have been so drowsy in relating the wonderful works of God that he left the waters of the clouds untouched in such deep silence — that he was unwilling even once to make mention of them among the rest of the sensible works of God — when nonetheless it holds no little admiration that they hang so firmly, without support, in the fluid air, without danger of falling, and besides furnish manifold utility in watering and making fertile the earth, which men esteem and regard more than those other higher [waters], more remote (as it seems to them) from daily use? These watery things, I say, Moses would have neglected, and meanwhile would have spoken superfluously about some supercelestial [waters] which neither lie open to the senses nor exhibit an evident use. In reality, therefore, it is necessary [to understand] that Moses, by those twin waters which he denotes, divided by the interval of the firmament, understood the marine [as] the lower and [the waters] of the clouds, in some part, [as] the upper. For thus too the [raqiaʿ] or expanse — which Castalio opportunely (as I said) calls "liquid" — separates waters from waters, intervening between them by a certain portion of itself; for Moses did not expressly say that the whole [raqiaʿ] divides waters from waters, as you take his sense. Nay, he himself explains [it] in Gen. 9, when he says that God set the rainbow in the clouds as a testimony that the waters would no more come into a flood of the lands. For what waters does he here signify, except the clouds, from which especially the inundation in the times of Noah had occurred — and hence it is clear that the "cataracts of Heaven," or channels, which were then opened, are not to be sought beyond the orb of the Fixed [stars], as you wish. From Jeremiah moreover, chapters 10 and 51, where the following words are had in both places: "He who made the Earth by his power, established the habitable world by his wisdom, and by his prudence stretched out the Heavens; at whose uttering of his voice there is a tumult of waters in the Heavens; who brings up vapours from the end of the Earth, makes lightnings with the rain itself, and produces wind from his treasuries," the same is gathered still more manifestly. For hence it plainly appears that those Celestial waters are to be understood [as] in that Region of the air whence clouds, lightnings, and rain are produced, and that the same [region] is taken for "Heaven."

Besides, the greatest absurdities follow from the supercelestial waters being granted. For if any waters of this kind exist there, which include and embrace the outermost theatres of the Heaven with their circuit all around, [then] they must of necessity either be moved or be at rest together [with the Heaven]. If they remain immobile, either they impede the most rapid rotation of the Heaven, or there must be a very great interval between the moving and the resting, so that that most rapid course may come to rest successively. An immense space, therefore, destitute of stars and fit for no use, must be left — which would be unsuitable. But if those waters are revolved together with the Heaven, how will they be able to endure that most violent rapidity, and to remain perpetually, together with it, inconsumable, or to obstruct it by no action? Add too [the matter] of the excessive magnitude of these waters with respect to the rest, which Moses calls subcelestial, by which it is doubtful to no one that the Seas flowing about the Terrestrial globe are to be understood. For if those waters which you wish to be above the eighth sphere occupy even one or a half Earth-Diameter in their density (which truly would be the very least, with respect to so great a circumference, and for that use which you assign — to ward off the uncreated light from the creatures, if that were to be granted you) — and since, to terminate this eighth sphere, there are at least 14,000 Earth-radii [Semidiametri Terrae] according to our ordering of the Celestial hypotheses (whereas the Ptolemaic would require about 20,000 radii, and the Copernican speculation here goes off into the immense) — reckon, I pray, how vast those waters would be, which surround so great a globe on every side, even in that tiny thickness (as I said), [a globe] whose Radius will contain so many thousands of Earth-radii. Surely you will find that the mass of those waters scarcely has that estimation to the seas (or subcelestial waters) which the whole marine water has to a single drop. Accordingly, if those waters touched and covered the Earth (as is said to have been the case at the beginning, before the separation), they would by their quantity ascend high into the Heaven itself. What, then, would be the comparison and proportion of the waters above and below the expanse, of which Moses speaks? Wherefore it is by no means doubtful that Moses, by the name of "supercelestial waters," understood no watery mass poured around on every side in the vastest capacity of the Aether (as you think), but — not by my opinion alone, but also by the opinion of the most excellent Professors of Theology, such as Melanchthon, Castellio, Calvin, and others — the watery clouds of the Heaven.

But you nonetheless labour much to assert that opinion of yours about the supercelestial Waters; and, so as to establish some use for those waters, lest perchance they be superfluous, you say they are disposed there as by a certain necessity, to provide a certain interval between the created orb and the light created in it, and the "Heaven of heavens," which God inhabits in eternal light, uncreated and inaccessible to creatures. But what here do you understand by "eternal and uncreated light," what "Heaven of heavens"? Is it thus that you deem it not unbecoming to prescribe finite limits to the infinite and immense, to confuse the eternal with the temporal, the invisible with the visible? For God alone exists [as] infinite and uncreated; therefore dimension, place, space, situation, and other things of the kind — which are attributes of creatures — cannot belong to him. And if that light which you call eternal and uncreated is something other than God himself, then there are now two eternals and uncreated, and therefore two gods and two principles. But nothing is uncreated and eternal except God himself, who is also unique. Nay, even if there were some eternal light outside the corporeal World, it would not be apprehended by human eyes, even with all intervals removed. For it is most truly asserted in Optics that all things which are seen are comprehended in time. Whence it follows that the things which are outside time and eternal do not lie open to temporal eyes. There is also ascribed to those Waters surrounding the Heaven (in your opinion) this utility too: that they cut off and define the excursion of our vision, lest perchance it go outside the World or into the infinite. But what is this to say, since no such power is at all in the visual faculty? Nay, if the stars were not luminous bodies and did not of their own accord present themselves to the eyes, the eyes would otherwise discern no thing at so great a distance. In vain, therefore, do you set before our vision that densest and darkest interval of waters above the Heavens, since by its nature and condition it is forbidden by the most august majesty of the uncreated.

Away, therefore, with that opinion about supercelestial waters — [introduced] for the sake of the distinction between created and uncreated things, and for restraining and defining the ray of our vision — an opinion fruitful in such erratic absurdities. Nor will you obtain any support for that opinion which you undertake to defend so greatly, in the common explanation of the name [shamayim, "heavens"] there, as if [from esh + ] [mayim, "waters"]. For this is arbitrary and contrary to the property of the holy language, which admits neither such a composition of words (especially in appellative nouns), nor easily any defect — such as would be in this word, if it were determined to be composite — without a compensation of letters. The word [shamayim], therefore, is conflated by no common law of compositions, but exists [as] simple, and denotes something astonishing or admirable, derived from the verb [shamem], which means "to be astonished" or "to wonder" — no otherwise than from […] is formed […], and others of the kind. And therefore Pagninus, in his lexicon, not undeservedly subjoined this noun immediately to its radical verb. For in reality the Heaven is something admirable and astonishing, whether we regard the perennial laws of [its] motions, not obvious to everyone, or the matter of which it consists, which is inexplicable.

But you take exception, that these watery clouds — by which I understand Moses [to mean] — are not perpetual, because sometimes (or now and then) they are dissolved and flit and are borne hither and thither, and that therefore Moses, by "Celestial waters," understood not those which are momentary, but those which always endure. It is indeed true that the clouds, which I hold for the Celestial waters, do not always appear in every Horizon, but for the most part; nevertheless, if not actually, at least potentially they are always there. And that force by which they coalesce on high, and are sustained lest they fall, is perpetually present there. Add that, regarding the whole Earth in its entire circuit, it will scarcely be granted that all Horizons are at once and at one [time] free of clouds hanging above — but that they are beheld in at least some Horizon of the Earthly globe.

Finally, you appeal further to the testimonies of Scripture, from which you implore help — but in vain — for establishing your opinion by a certain too-rigid literal explanation. For by no means are those [passages] which you allege here on your behalf — from Psalm 104: "Who frames his tabernacles in the clouds," or from Psalm 18: "He set darkness as his hiding-place; around his tabernacle dark water and thick clouds" — to be understood of any supercelestial waters and clouds, but of the Region of the air, as it were distinguished into upper-chambers by clouds, as Tremellius and Junius, likewise Möller [Mollerus] and others, not unfittingly expound — unless perhaps anyone, to whom mention is made in the same place of winds and fires or lightnings, deems these too to have a place above the starry orb. But from verse 13 of the same Psalm, where it is said thus: "Who waters the mountains from his chambers [coenacula], that the earth may be satisfied with the fruit of thy works" — since the same Hebrew word [ʿaliyyot, "chambers"], which Pagninus in his Hebrew dictionary thinks denotes "chambers or clouds," is had in both places — who would deny that here the waters of the clouds, watering and making fertile the mountains and the earth, are to be understood?

Besides — since it introduces no little absurdity, nay, considering [it] naturally it is plainly impossible, that waters be held above the Heavens without supports and bars and be revolved — you labour to excuse this by the stability of the Earth in the fluid air. But [you do so] inconsistently, since the reckoning is not the same. For the Earth and all globular bodies of the World rest on their own centre, and all their parts naturally tend thither — so much so that if we should suppose some little portion violently torn from the Sun or Moon or the other stars, it soon seeks again its own Star, aiming at its own centre. For so great a force is divinely implanted in the midpoints of the World-bodies. Which surely cannot be admitted in like manner of the extramundane waters, since they do not totally fill the aforesaid round globe. And therefore, unless they were held by fixed supports and bars, they would necessarily revisit the marine waters, whence they had been separated, and so the Earth, the centre of their gravity. Nor is the argument valid: "God could fix the gross and heavy Earth in the thin air; therefore [he could fix] the clouds too above the Heaven." For you know that from "can" to "is" (as they commonly speak) the inference does not follow. And therefore it is expressly said in the Psalms: "God has done all that he willed in Heaven and on Earth." And God is the author of order and utility. Therefore he will by no means [be willing] to confuse and defile the Celestial with the Elemental without manifest use.

Furthermore, my opinion about the Milky Way [via lactea] you do not so much reject as illustrate more abundantly. For I — although [I hold] the Heaven [to be] something most fluid and most tenuous, everywhere pervious to the motions of the Stars — yet [hold it] not plainly incorporeal and immaterial, but [I have] determined that all the Celestial bodies consist of the same substance as the Heaven, compacted, by the wisdom of the Maker, into some denser globe fit for the reception of light. Just as we see in this nature of the World that those things which are generated in the everywhere-pervious Sea consist of a watery substance, and are easily resolved into the same, although these things, now concreted, are not [themselves] liquid and pervious. For all Metals too, and the Gems akin to them, have their roots in the Sea — which was known to very few before Paracelsus. But that the Stars of the Heaven, although they consist of Celestial matter, yet differ very much from the Heaven itself in density and light, ought to be no more a wonder than that gold and silver and the other Metals — drawing their origin from the sea (as I said) — nonetheless differ from it in manifold ways by their form, hard structure, and other appropriated [qualities]; to say nothing of the others which, in the Earth and Air, are formed from Terrestrial and Airy substance most unlike the former. When therefore I wish the Galaxy to consist of the Heaven's substance, compacted and joined together, I say nothing other than that it has the same matter as the rest of the stars — which, however, is here seen scattered and diffused in breadth, by a certain track, through the whole Heaven, but in those [stars] is more tightly compacted into the form of one round body. And that this opinion about the Milky Way is not far from the truth, ocular intuition itself — by attentive inspection and comparison with the rest of the Stars, especially those appearing with a fainter light — testifies not obscurely.

Nay, I am of the opinion that that New Star, admirable beyond measure, which arose about the end of the year 1572, near the confines of this Galaxy of which we speak (where also the Star of Cassiopeia is seen), had the same matter as the Milky Way, but yet more compacted into one globe. And so that I may establish this more surely: a semicircular gap, of about the magnitude of a half-Moon, in the margin of the Milky Way, in that very place where the New Star had its position — still apparent to one attentively gazing while the Heaven is perfectly clear and the Moon hidden, [a gap] which I do not recall ever having seen there before — altogether persuades [me of it]. But you will say: "If this Star arose from the same and perpetual substance as the rest of the Stars of the Galaxy, why did it not also remain perpetually coeval with them?" To this doubt I am unwilling to reply much for the present, being about to open my opinion in these and similar [matters] at the end of the Work which I am attempting on the Ascititious [adventitious] Phenomena of the Heaven. This only will I say in this place: the things which sometimes arise miraculously and beyond the common order in the nature of things need not be subject to the same laws long customary with the Heaven.

But why does that sapphire colour of the Heaven move you again, that you should think (but in vain) it arises from the clouds and waters obscuring — chiefly outside the Milky Way — the light which you attribute to the Heaven? Nay, that sapphire colour is thrown into the eyes on account of the weakness of our sight. For although the air per se, and the whole Aethereal Region, are devoid of all colour, yet, since in that most profound immensity the sight does not find where to be stayed, that azure colour is thrown into the eyes per accidens — just as we often see all things which are very distant appear to the eyes in this manner, although per se they do not have such a colour. But these things have been more than sufficiently declared by the writers on Optics, so that there is no need to borrow this sapphire colour, in vain, from supercelestial clouds — about which matter too I admonished you in [my] earlier letter.

But about this New Star, of which I made mention, I treat amply in the first Tome of the Work I spoke of. There you will shortly learn what I find wanting in the pronouncements both of the Landgrave and yours, and also of others, about this [star]. And then too [you will learn] that I think — not without just reasons — that its decrees [significations] are far greater and more universal than has seemed to you, and have perhaps not yet even begun, much less passed.

Nor can I in any way determine with you that this Star was produced from some Elemental matter — which, however, you do not pronounce assertively. For I demonstrate there that it surpassed, in the bulk of its body, the globe which the Earth and Seas make up, more than three hundred times. What matter, then, proceeding hence, would suffice for so great a body, if indeed it were to be granted that anything of Terrestrial exhalations could ascend up to the Heaven? In which opinion I am by no means, nor ever was — although you wrongly attribute it to me concerning the procreation of Comets, when you say that I think the bodies of Comets are born from matter elevated into the Heaven and previously attenuated and purified by concoctions in the sublunary Orb, increased and compacted by the accession of new [matter], and suffused with Celestial light. This indeed I never asserted; nor will any such thing be gathered from my letters given to you or to others — much less from my publicly published writings. For in the second book on the more recent Phenomena of the Heaven (which I think you have received) it is openly held that I by no means think the matter of Comets consists of Elemental substance subtilized and exalted in whatever way. Which even those words I appended on page 254 plainly testify, which run thus: "I judge the matter of all Comets to be wholly Celestial, since they are procreated in the Heaven itself." I have observed, in some years past, most exactly five Comets, all of which the slightness of [their] Parallax evidently proved to have dwelt in the Heaven itself, so much so that some of them stood much higher than the Sun. And truly, who would believe that any Elemental matter flies up so far, [matter] which grows together into such and so great a body? For the very bulk was greater than that the Earth could supply matter to it, since some of them surpassed even the whole [Earth] in true magnitude. But about this whole business, since I treat [it] professedly elsewhere, here I forbear [to say] more.

The things which I adduced here, by way of example — about the generation of Metals, that they consist of one and the same prima materia, although not equally digested, purified, and exalted in each — so that I might to some extent compare the Nature of lower things with the higher: if they are less approved by those reasonings of yours which you cherish, and my opinion in these is not sufficiently explored by you, this indeed begets in me no wonder, since I know that those things which are handed down in the Academies and by vulgar Philosophers about the nature and origin of Metals are merely opinable, and not founded in the light of nature. For he who does not know the resolution of them will scarcely perceive the composition. There is need, therefore, of the Pyronomic art, which proceeds from the Hermetic Philosophy — to which all who have lain over Philosophy in the Academies come quite unskilled. Believe me, who have bestowed no less labour and study … on this lower and Terrestrial Astronomy (for so I am wont from time to time to call this art) than on that higher and Celestial one, [and who] have been taught by experience itself that the matter is so, as I briefly indicated last. Which, since they are less perceived by you — being not versed in the Spagyric treatment — I deem it idle to touch upon more amply. Only this one [thing] will I suggest about it: that from this comparison of the conformation of metals with comets, you could have inferred that I meant to hint that Comets consist of wholly Celestial matter, devoid of every Elemental quality.

I rejoice that the arrangement of my Astronomical work pleases you. Nor, concerning Venus and Mercury — that they are rendered no less higher than lower than the Sun — will there be great difficulty in persuading those who consider the matter itself more deeply. Nor does that which you adduce — that the star of Venus has sometimes appeared on the same day at once both as evening and as morning [star] — at all impede our reasonings; nay, I indicated to you in [my] earlier letter that this very thing was once observed by me too. Nor do I find any obstacle suggested hence, [against] the ordering of our hypotheses being duly established. But let these [things] suffice about these matters for the present — or rather, about that question which clouds the supreme Heaven with Waters, [a question] as less Astronomical, nay, well-nigh abhorrent to all Philosophy, they may be even excessive and superfluous.

That Christoph Rothmann, the Landgrave's Mathematician, seriously defends the Copernican hypotheses and the mobility of the Earth constructed in them, and thinks the whole Heaven to be nothing other than Elemental — and so Animate — Air — about which you are in doubt: I send you a portion of the book of Astronomical Letters [Epistolae astronomicae], which I signified to you I would some time publish, containing chiefly — besides [the letters] of the most Illustrious Prince [the Landgrave] himself written to me about Astronomical matters, together with our reply to each — also the letters of this Rothmann and mine, in which we dispute back and forth about this whole business. From these you will more fully perceive what his opinion and mine is, both in such [matters] and in some others pertaining hither. Which [letters] I ask you again and again to weigh, according to the acumen of your judgment, and to discern what seems to you more probable. For in [his] last letters, since he appeals to arbiters, I have chosen you before the rest — as you will perceive from those very [letters], if ever there be leisure to read them through; which I still affectionately beg you to do, and to share with me your erudite appraisal of those controverted matters.

I will add no more about these, lest the Letter grow too much. Nor will I reply at length to your later [letters], which Bongars (as I said at the beginning) delivered, since they were shorter in themselves, and only repeated summarily, anew, those things which were contained in these more prolix [letters]. This only will I indicate in few [words]: that I by no means think the Heaven subject to Elemental changes, nor that anything Elemental, conformable to this lower Nature, is in it — as you seem to attribute to me toward the end of that same little Letter. From which suspicion, if nothing else, at least this portion of the Astronomical Letters which I send will vindicate me to you. I suspect that you have not received our Apologetic writing, which I sent you to be inspected and judged — since you make mention of it in neither letter. But the loss is light; for shortly, God willing, you will obtain it, appended to the second Tome and printed by our types. Then at last … I judge [it fitting] to insert sufficiently the appraisal … [concerning] the objections of that most excellent Mathematician and Aristotelian Philosopher, John Craig the Scot, assailing — but not, as I hope, taking by storm — our assertions about the Olympic [celestial] situation of Comets. But here I halt the course of my writing, and pray God that he may deign to preserve you — freed in this old age from so many and so great hardships — clemently, [and] also long surviving and unharmed. Farewell.

Caspar Peucer (1525–1602), Wittenberg physician, astronomer, and theologian, Melanchthon's son-in-law, leader of the Philippist/Crypto-Calvinist party — imprisoned 1574–1586 for his Eucharistic theology, then physician at the Anhalt court (Dessau). Tycho's closing prayer ("freed in this old age from so many and so great hardships") alludes to that long captivity, fixing the letter after 1586 and (with the other internal markers) to 1590. The exchange is a meeting of two of the age's most learned Lutherans on the frontier of astronomy and Genesis-exegesis.
- The argument (the spine). A point-by-point reply to two questions in Peucer's (lost) letters:
1. Nature/substance of the heavens. Tycho agrees the heavens are tenuous, pure, free of elemental corruption, and have no solid orbs (proven by five comets moving without any real orb). But he doubts Peucer's claim that the aether is per se luminous (it reflects no light at night), rejects making celestial light "nearest to the divine" (too Aristotelian — divinity/eternity of the heavens; created intellectual light in angels/minds is nearer the divine), and clarifies that his Hebrew etymology of shamayim as "fire + water" was metaphorical play, not a claim that the heaven is fiery (God himself is called "consuming fire," Deut 4:24, 9:3; Heb 12:29). He denies elemental fire below the Moon (contra Aristotle, by optics) and denies a "most solid" upper air (Peucer's view); the Sun's vivific heat, not an element of fire, conserves the lower world.
2. The "waters above the firmament" (the long centre). Tycho refutes the supercelestial-waters reading of Gen 1:6–7 with: (a) Hebrew philology — raqiaʿ (Castellio "liquidum," others "expansum") is used in three senses (whole expanse / upper star-region / lower cloud-region); Moses wrote for "a rude and simple people," describing only the visible; (b) the clouds argument — otherwise Moses absurdly neglected the cloud-waters (which hang unsupported, irrigate the earth) while inventing invisible supercelestial ones; the rainbow (Gen 9) and the Flood "cataracts" prove the waters meant are clouds; Jer 10/51 locates the "tumult of waters in the heavens" in the cloud-region; (c) the physical absurdities — supercelestial waters must move (and endure heaven's rapid rotation inconsumably) or rest (leaving a vast useless gap); and their magnitude would be monstrous, since the 8th sphere lies at ≥ 14,000 Earth-radii in Tycho's system (Ptolemaic c. 20,000; Copernican "into the immense") — the supposed shell would dwarf the seas "as all seawater to one drop"; (d) the theological objection to Peucer's purpose for the waters (an interval before the uncreated "heaven of heavens") — to bound the infinite, or posit a created-yet-uncreated light, yields "two gods"; optics shows eternal things are invisible to temporal eyes; the visual ray needs no watery wall; (e) the etymology — shamayim is not a compound of esh (fire) + mayim (water) but a simple noun from shamem ("to be astonished"), per Pagninus; (f) the Psalm passages (104:3; 18:11; 104:13, with ʿaliyyot = "chambers/clouds") read by Tremellius–Junius and Möller as the air-region; (g) the disanalogy with Earth's stability (world-bodies rest on their own centre; extramundane waters, not filling the globe, would fall back) and the logical point a posse ad esse non valet. Authorities marshalled for the cloud-reading: Melanchthon, Castellio, Calvin, Tremellius, Junius, Möller, Pagninus.
3. Milky Way & New Star. The heavens are fluid yet not immaterial; stars are the same substance as the heaven, condensed into denser light-receiving globes (analogy: metals/gems "have their roots in the sea," pace Paracelsus). The Galaxy is that same matter diffused in a band; the New Star of 1572 (by Cassiopeia, at the Galaxy's edge) was the same matter compacted — Tycho adduces a semicircular gap he saw in the Milky Way's margin exactly at the nova's site. Why did it not endure? — miraculous, beyond-the-common-order things need not obey the heavens' usual laws (full answer reserved for the Progymnasmata).
4. Sapphire sky-colour = a per accidens effect of the eye's weakness in the immense void (optics), not borrowed from supercelestial clouds.
5. Comets wholly celestial. Tycho denies (against Peucer's attribution) that he ever held comets made of sublunary exhalations; quotes his own De recentioribus phaenomenis p. 254: "I judge the matter of all comets to be wholly celestial, since they are procreated in the heaven itself." Five comets, by tiny parallax, were celestial (some higher than the Sun); the 1572 nova exceeded the Earth-globe >300× — no exhalation could supply such bulk.
6. Alchemy ("terrestrial astronomy"). Metals share one prima materia, variously "digested, purified, exalted"; academic philosophy of metals is mere opinion, not the lumen naturae; the Pyronomic art from Hermetic philosophy (the spagyric/chymical practice) is needed — Tycho claims as much labour in this "lower and terrestrial astronomy" as in the celestial.
7. Venus & Mercury circle the Sun (rendered both above and below it); Venus seen as morning- and evening-star the same day is no obstacle (Tycho saw it once). Rothmann: Tycho sends Peucer a portion of the Epistolae astronomicae (the Landgrave + Rothmann correspondence) and names Peucer the arbiter of the dispute over Rothmann's Copernicanism and his "elemental/animate air" heaven. The Apologeticum against Craig (cf. the Craig letter of 14 Nov 1591) Peucer has not received; it will appear in Tome II.
