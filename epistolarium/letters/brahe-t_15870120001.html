Editorial note. The longest-but-one letter in the corpus and a cornerstone of the Tycho–Rothmann correspondence — a sustained scientific letter covering: the 1585-comet observation-comparison and its near-zero parallax; the Paul Wittich instrument-priority story (the parallel-slit sights, the transverse-point division from Homelius, the Nonius subtlety, the sextant); Petrus Ramus on "astronomy without hypotheses," refuted; refraction in detail (the 10-foot armilla; the star-refraction values; the Cygnus-tail / Algol discrepancy; the aether/air diaphaneity against Alhazen & Witelo); the great star-by-star demonstration that the fixed stars' latitudes vary with the changing obliquity (Spica as base, then Gemini, Capella, Orion, Regulus, Aldebaran, Altair, Antares, Libra, Sirius, Procyon); the 1584 lunar eclipse and the meridian-difference method; praise of Copernicus (and the Buchanan Sphaera); and the Frauenburg/Königsberg pole-elevations with Copernicus's own wooden triquetrum. The dateline ("Vraniburgi 20 die Ianuarij, Anno 87") is Old Style (Danish Julian); it agrees with the catalogue label. Cross-check: page-collated against Dreyer's Opera Omnia, vol. VI pp. 86–104. The dense numeral spine is confirmed against the edition of record — of some 70 distinct numerals (the obliquity cluster pp. 87/94; the full Spica-based star-by-star latitude series pp. 94–99; the Frauenburg 54°22¼′ / Königsberg 54°43′ / Reinhold's 54°17′-26″-short poles p. 103; the 1584-eclipse timings H.11ᵐ12 / H.13ᵐ8 / H.15ᵐ0 + the Kassel/Rostock meridian reductions p. 100; the refraction values p. 92), every legible value matches. ERVDITISSIMO VIRO VARIAQVE DOCTRINA, IN PRIMIS VERO INSIGNI ASTRONOMIÆ PERITIA EXCELLENTIA D. CHRISTOPHORO ROTHMANNO, ILLUSTRISS. PRINCIPIS WILHELMI LANDTGRAVII HASSIÆ, ETC. MATHEMATICO AULICO, AMICO SUO PLURIMUM DILECTO.

QVAS ad me superiori Anno Mense Aprili, tum Mandato Illustrissimi Principis tui, tum etiam propria erga me, meaque studia beneuolentia adductus, dedisti literas, Eruditissime ROTHMANNE, longe mihi erant acceptissimæ, eo præsertim nomine, quod reconditiorem circa res Astronomicas mecum collationem multifariam continerent. Cur vero Illustrissimi Principis literis, et tuis etiam, vestra expectatione tardius respondeam, excusationem meam Illustriss. Principi exposui. Nec dubito ipsius Celsitudinem pro Heroico illo animi sui candore, eam æqui bonique accepturam. De Obseruationibus etiam cælestibus, tum vestris, tum meis, in trutinam inuicem vocatis, latius in literis meis ad Illustrissimum Principem egi, eiusque Celsitudini nonnullas e nostris, vt clementi ipsius petitioni morem gererem, communicaui. De his itaque copiosius ad te perscribere non duxi operæ pretium, ne eandem cantilenam tædiose recinere videar. Siquidem ex ijs quæ ad Illustriss. Principem a me transmissa sunt, hæc proculdubio tibi cognoscenda venient. De ijs itaque potissimum, quæ proprijs tuis literis, tam prioribus, quam secundo missis, continebantur, nunc vberius tecum agam. Conuenientiam illam propemodum exactam, inter tuas et nostras in Cometa Anni 85. Obseruationes, Illustriss. Principi tuo, et tibi adeo placuisse, vt admirationem inde iucunditatemque non exiguam perceperitis, mihi non minus intellectu volupe erat. Quodque eam ob causam mihi tuum pereruditum scriptum (licet non ad Colophonem perductum) de hoc Cometa transmisisti, vt congruentiam vestrarum Obseruationum cum meis plenius inde cognoscerem, longe mihi gratissimum fuit. Perlegi scriptum illud semel atque iterum, mihique mirum in modum placuit. Optandum sane foret, vt ea quæ de præteritis aliquot Cometis, superioribus annis conspectis, plerique Germanorum vestrorum effutierunt potius, quam per exquisitas et infallibiles Obseruationes Mathematice demonstrârunt, pari diligentia et certitudine elaborata fuissent. Nec enim tantum inter eorum et nostra placita in situ et motu Cometarum discrepantiæ, et ab ipso cælo veritatisque quærendæ nucleo deuiationis, non sine fastidio tum deprehendissem. Cometam illum longe supra Elementarem orbem, in altissimo æthere cursum suum absoluisse, rectissime mecum statuis, quamuis omnium pene Philosophorum, tam veterum, quam recentium refragentur sententiæ. In hoc saltem perexiguum, et nullius etiam momenti inter nos reperitur discriminis, quod tu plane nullam illi adfuisse parallaxin colligas; ego vero eam vnius circiter ab initio scrupuli, et postea saltem dimidij adinueni. Quamuis, vt verum fatear, et ipse eandem subinde plane nullam, ob tenuitatem eius tam exilem, discernere potuerim. Verum quia ex motu proprio inferre licuit, eum longe infra octauam Sphæram, vtut supra Solem constitutum fuisse, ideo aliqualem, licet perexiguam vixque perceptibilem aspectus diuersitatem obtinere consentaneum erat. Quin non Mars saltem, præsertim Acronychus, Parallaxin obseruabilem ingerat, vt nos in Principio Anni 82. experimentum fecimus; sed ipse etiam ♃, vt de ♄ Stella nihil dicam, cum sensibus in tenuissima illic differentia non relinquatur locus. At discrimen hoc inter nos adeo exiguum est, vt frustra de hoc verba facere videar. In Longitudinibus et Latitudinibus Cometæ a te animaduersis, paulo maior licet et ipsa quoque nullius pene momenti, reperitur inter nostras Obseruationes differentia, quæ tantilla est, vt vix quina vel sena scrupula ad summum attingat. Imo etiam multo sæpius plane consentiat. Euenit autem, vt opinor, hæc minutula inter nos discrepantia, eam potissimum ob causam, quod tu loca Fixarum, quarum vtrinque vsus fuit, paulo aliter, quam ego, assumseris. Tibi enim Aldebora in part. 4. M. 7. ♊ cum Latitudine part. 5. M. 29. Aust. collocatur, quam ego a meis Obseruationibus in part. 4. M. 1. ♊ cum Latitudine 5. 31. Merid. constituo, discrimine saltem in Longitudine 6. scrupulorum, in Latitudine vero duorum inter vtramque positionem incidente. Lucidam vero in capite ♈, quam tertiam appellatis, tu in part. 1. Min. 58. ♉, cum Latitudine part. 9. M. 57. Bor. Ego vero in part. 1. M. 54. ♉, cum Latitudine, part. 9. M. 57. Bor. assumebam, differentia in Longitud. saltem 4 in Latitudine prorsus nulla nobis interueniente. Cumque ego ijsdem potissimum Fixis, in peruestigandis huius Cometæ apparentijs vsus fuerim, leuiusculum quod varietatis ex hac licet perexigua diuersitate, inter nos in locis Cometæ constituendis contingere potuit. Quamuis discrimen hoc etiam in ipsis Fixarum locis adeo sit exiguum, vt vix mentionem mereatur. Situm vero harum duarum Fixarum, quo ad longum et latum, exactissime se habere, vt nostra annotatio præbet, ipsa distantia inter vtramque, quam ego eandem pene vobiscum, aliquoties part. 35. scrup. 32¼. cælitus accepi, comprobat. Sin autem ex complementis Latitudinum, et angulo per differentiam longitudinis comprehenso, Triangulorum rationibus intercapedinem rimeris, in tua assignatione fere scrupulum vnum abundat. Hoc vero tantillum, licet per se non magni momenti, tam subtiliter expendi ridebunt merito vulgares Cæli contemplatores, quibus dena etiam scrupula non reputanda æstimantur. Idque non immerito, cum ad semisses vel etiam integros gradus (pudet dicere) sæpenumero cæcutiant. Diuersitas Meridianorum nostrorum quæ quintam horæ partem, meo iudicio, saltem adæquat, ab initio, cum celerior erat Cometæ motus, non nisi vnico scrupulo Obseruationes nostras variare potuit. In fine vero, cum tardior fieret, insensibilis peneque nulla differentia hinc causabatur. Discrimen inter assumtam vtrinque Declinationem maximam nihil fere importat. Ego enim præsupposui eam part. 23. M. 31. licet dimidio scrupulo (quod negligendum hoc loco duxeram) maiorem adinuenerim, quam tu part. 23. scr. 30. saltem vno scrupulo nostra minorem efficis. Neque sane intelligere possum, quomodo hanc, et non potius eandem mecum, vel adhuc maiorem Declinationem maximam, ex Obseruationibus Solis Illustriss. Principis, per Quadrantes, licet tunc non adeo correctos, habitis, colligere potueris. Ego enim in Obseruationibus Solaribus mihi transmissis, vbique fere inuenio Altitudinem maximam Solstitialem part. 62. M. 12. quibus si Parallaxin addideris, prouenit vera part. 62. M. 13. S. 20. Hinc ablata Altitudine Æquatoris, qualis tunc præsupponebatur part. 38. M. 42. euadit declinatio Eclipticæ ab Æquatore part. 23. M. 31⅓. a nostra insensibiliter differens. Si vero Altitudinem maximam part. 62. M. 13. vel 14. quæ etiam alicubi offertur, et veriorem Poli Altitudinem in consilium adhibueris, adhuc maior euadet, vt ob id non videam, cur vel ex ipsis Illustriss. Principis Obseruationibus tunc cognitis, cum nostra Declinatione maxima standum non foret, cum ea similis, et potius maior, quam minor collatis singulis, inde eruatur. Sed non dubito, quin per correctiora vestra Instrumenta mecum postea deprehenderitis, Declinationem hanc maximam nunc esse part. 23. M. 31½. quemadmodum et nos tum præterito Anno, tum aliquot præcedentibus pluribus machinis experti sumus. Quamuis Solis Altitudo meridiana, in vtroque Solstitio adhibita Parallaxi eam præbeat saltem part. 23. M. 27. plus 4. scrupulis minorem, consentientem quidem melius cum Copernici et Coætaneorum annotatione, sed ob Refractionem Solarem, in decliuiori situ iuxta Brumam prouenientem, errori adeo sensibili obnoxiam. Sol enim nobis non integre 11. gradibus, in ipso Meridie iuxta Solstitium Hybernum, eleuatur. Nec multo altior Copernico Fruenburgi extitit. Peto autem peramanter, vt mihi prima quaque occasione, inter alia significes, qualem illic per Neoterica vestra et correctiora Instrumenta Solis Eleuationem maximam et minimam deprehenderitis, tum quoque eandem circa vtraque Æquinoctia, vel potius per totum Anni curriculum, vt collationem cum nostris Obseruationibus eo plenius instituere liceat, et Stellarum etiam, tum Circumpolarium, tum aliarum Altitudines Meridianas exactiores adiungere velis, vtque integrum tuum scriptum de Cometa vltimo, mecum impertiri non graueris, etiam atque etiam vna rogo. Nec enim dubito, te nunc illi extremas imposuisse manus.

Quod autem in portione eius mihi transmissa Cælum totum aëreum, nec e solida materia constare, imo nil aliud quâm Aërem ipsum esse asseueras, id facile tibi concesserim, modo aërem qui supra Lunam sit, multo subtiliorem quam hunc Elementarem intelligas, adeo vt Ætheris potius liquidissimi et subtilissimi, quam Aëris elementaris nomen mereatur. Nec enim ego, multis ab hinc annis, in ea fui sententia, orbes aliquos realiter in cælo existere, materiamque cæli duram et imperuiam esse, Stellasque tantum ad motum orbium conuolui. Multa siquidem hinc sequuntur absurda, et si nihil aliud, sane tot Cometæ in æthere cursum præcedentibus aliquot annis exercere certissime a nobis animaduersi, qui nullius orbis ductum concomitabantur, idipsum sufficienter refellunt. Iudicaui itaque semper naturalem motus scientiam singulis Planetis congenitam, vel potius diuinitus inditam esse, qua in liquidissimo et tenuissimo æthere cursus sui normam regularissime et constantissime obseruare coguntur, nullius fulciminis vel promotoris indigi, quemadmodum ipsa etiam Terra, in aëre liquido, nullo fulcro stabilita, solo proprio vndique innixa centro, firmatur. Vt autem sic reuera statuam, et alia quædam, quam vel Ptolemaica, vel Coperniana circa Reuolutionum cælestium dispositionem ratiocinatio, multo his competentior a me, ipsa experientia docente, nouiter explorata, (de qua nunc non est dicendi locus) plurimum confirmat. Quod autem celeberrimus ille nostri Æui Philosophus PETRVS RAMVS existimârit, sine Hypothesibus per Logicas rationes Astronomiam constitui posse, caret fundamento. Proposuit quidem ille mihi ante annos elapsos 16. cum Augustæ Vindelicorum vna essemus, hanc opinionem, et hortator simul erat, vt postquam per Hypotheses Siderum cursum in exactum ordinem redegissem, idem sine his tentare affectarem. Id enim fieri posse, hanc rationem addidit, quod legisset Ægyptios facilimam olim habuisse Astronomiæ cognitionem; Cumque hypothesium ratio difficilis, et intricata videatur, oportere eos alia compendiosiore, et planiore via siderum cursus cognouisse, ideoque citra omnes hypotheses. At ego illi resistebam, ostendens sine hypothesibus Phænomena cælestia non posse in scientiam certam redigi, neque vt intelligantur, excusari. Facilitatem vero illam Ægyptiacam saltem in Æquatorijs Planetarum, quibus se a supputatione tædiosa liberârunt, cum Ephemeridum expedita ratio nondum in vsu foret, extitisse. Verum cum is, vir alias perspicaci ingenio præditus, et veritatis, si quis alius, amans, penetralia artis huius non penitus perspexisse mihi videretur, varietatemque in motu siderum statis anni temporibus nequaquam recurrentem non animaduertisse, quicquam hac in parte obtinere ab ipso nec potui, nec volui. Habet is plerosque adhuc asseclas, qui idem fieri posse sperant, sed qui rem ipsam neque intelligant, neque vnquam in effectum sint deducturi. Cum enim omnia constent numeris, ponderibus, et mensura, sine his etiam, quicquam in mundo visibili explicari nequit. Hypotheses autem nihil aliud, quam mensuram apparentis motus per circulum aliasque figuras ostendunt, quas Arithmetica in numeros resoluit. Sine his si quis Astrorum motus comprehendere velit, fortunam, quod dici solet, inuocet, et supramundanam extra hominum intellectum, planeque incorpoream, et plus quam angelicam rationem imaginetur, necesse est. Sed hæc de his satis.

De Instrumentis et Obseruationibus hinc factis, restat vt nunc plenius tecum colloquar. Non est quod dubites, Witichium illum Instrumentorum instaurandorum rationem, quam Illustriss. Principi tuo proposuit, hîc cum mecum esset, accepisse, quamuis non omnia pari ratione, vel animaduerterit, vel illic satis fideliter exposuerit. Cum enim is Anno, ni fallor, 80. huc accessisset, se in meam familiaritatem facile insinuauit, partim quod hominem ob ingeniosam in Mathematicis, præsertim quo ad Geometriam attinet, solertiam magnifacerem, partim quod is se perpetuo mecum mansurum, mihique in studijs Astronomicis fidum Achatem toto vitæ tempore futurum, sponte offerret. Vnde factum est, vt liberaliter illi mea inuenta communicârim, neque quicquam eorum celârim, quæ tunc in promtu habebam, vel etiam in posterum elaborare decreueram. Verum cum is paulo vltra anni quadrantem mecum commoratus esset existimaretque se iam satis suis desiderijs potitum, prætendebat auunculum suum Vratislauij e viuis decessisse, cuius hæreditas lucrum magnum, si mature adesset, illi adferret. Impetrata itaque facile discedendi copia, intra 7. vel 8. hebdomadas huc se reuersurum spondebat. Verum ex eo tempore, nec ipsum, nec quicquam literarum ab eo profectum vidi, neque de eo, vbinam esset, audiui, priusquam Illustrissimi Principis tui literas, D. HENRICO RANZOVIO scriptas, perlegerem, ex quibus statim intellexi, eum Instrumentorum formam et rationem, quam hîc viderat, magna ex parte illic expressisse. Quod forsan et alibi ab ipso interea factitatum est. Et sane hoc pacto mihi rem non ingratam effecit, siquidem percuperem multis in locis quam plurimos extare, qui exquisitis et minime fallacibus Instrumentis siderum apparentias mecum scrutarentur. Attamen si mea Inuenta multis annis, et longa Experientia nec paucis sumtibus et laboribus comparata, pro suis venditat, nec fatetur per quem ea habuerit, rem a viro bono et grato, ac sinceritate integritateque Mathematica alienam committit. Intelligo autem partim e tuis literis, partim e relatione mei domestici, qui vobiscum fuit, eum non omnia, prout hîc viderat, satis competenter Illustrissimo Principi ordinasse. Diuisionem quidem, qua nos in omnibus fere Instrumentis similiter vtimur, satis recte indicasse comperio, et in pinnacidiorum rimulis pro nocturnis Stellarum Obseruationibus apprime oportunis (quod inuentum cum primum ille hîc vidisset, se non frustra in Daniam venisse aiebat, multoque auro præstantius æstimabat) quædam, licet inperfecte, imitatum esse. Dum enim compendium in his quærebat, et per binas rimulas, idem quod nos quaternis efficere præsumebat, veritatis quærendæ dispendium fecit, nostrisque inuentis detrahendo, sibijpsi et Obseruationum certitudini derogauit. Refert quidam meorum, qui tunc temporis, cum hîc esset, ab eo audiuerit, ipsum, quando nostrorum Instrumentorum fabricam imitaturus esset, dixisse, se paucioribus rimulis idem effecturum, hocque compendium sibi ipsi reseruaturum. Quam vero egregie id præstiterit, vos non sine multarum Obseruationum dispendio experti estis. Ego vbique, præsertim in Quadrantibus, quaternas in inferioribus pinnacidijs, cum superioris pinnacidij 4. lateribus vndique parallelas adhibeo rimulas, quas auctiores et laxiores interdum efficio, prout Stellarum magnitudo efflagitat. Alias enim per binas rimulas lateralem et superius sitam, Stellarum transitus et altitudines centraliter exacteque nequaquam discernuntur, quod ipsa facile ostendit Experientia. Et nisi vos hac edocti, duplicem rimulam visualem viceuersa inferiori pinnacidio applicassetis, etiamnum in obseruandis adamussim sideribus, frustra laboraretis. Diuisionis autem illius per puncta transuersalia inuentionem, (quam tute cuiuscunque sit, ingeniosam prædicas) primum Lipsiæ ab Homelio habui, ante annos plus minus 24. cum annum ætatis agerem 17. verum saltem in linea recta, vtpote in Radio Astronomico pluribus particulis diuidendo, ipsius imitatione ea tunc vtebar. At cum postea per Quadrantes, Altitudines etiam Siderum perscrutari inciperem, et in minoribus Instrumentis vulgarem diuisionem minimis portiunculis non sufficere satis experirer, ad Nonnianam illam, quam Propositione tertia de Crepusculis tradit, subtilitatem me conuerti, eandemque adhuc exactiorem pluribus subdiuisionibus reddidi, supputatis etiam Tabulis, e quibus cuiuslibet puncti altitudo scrupulose sine morâ cognosceretur. Cumque res ipsa doceret, ne hoc quidem inuentum Nonnianum id habere in recessu, quod in accessu pollicebatur, cæpi cogitare, an diuisio illa prædicta, qua linea recta subtilissime per puncta transuersalia in minores secatur particulas, etiam curuis et circularibus applicari possit. Et quamvis ab initio non par videbatur ratio, ob rectarum et curuarum disconuenientiam, tamen cum in tam paruo interuallo, vt quinorum vel denorum scopulorum lineæ curuæ paruo interstitio a se inuicem ductæ, non differant sensibiliter a rectis, negotium hoc satis commode succedere animaduerti, hacque idcirco diuisione in Quadrantibus, Sextantibus, Armillis ab hinc multis annis felici successu semper vsus sum, eamque vobis communicatam, quacunque tandem occasione, gaudeo, vt pari præcisione mecum Sidera perscrutari eo commodius possitis, ita, vt nostræ Obseruationes simul collatæ eundem veritatis scopum scrupulose, vt par est, (quod etiam non sine voluptate fieri experior) attingant. Sextantis Instrumentum a me ante annos plus minus 18. cum Augustæ Vindelicorum degerem, excogitatum, (quod etiam ibidem PETRO RAMO cum mihi adesset, vna cum Quadrante illo permagno 14. Cubitorum, quem extra vrbem in horto quodam Consulis HENZELII construi curaueram, ostendi, qui eorum a me descriptionem auide efflagitabat) ab eo tempore semper in vsu habui, eo quod certa ratione et multa Experientia per Radium, cuiuscunque tandem foret magnitudinis vel diuisionis, ratas distantias nequaquam inueniri, compertum haberem. Quare per Sextam circuli partem (vt commodior fieret Instrumenti tractatio) regulis applicatam, idem tentare certiore euentu aggrediebar. Atque ob id Instrumento huic Sextantis nomen indidi. Is vero, quem Witichius hîc vidit, qui chalybeus erat, catenulâ illa carebat, qualem vos adhibuistis. Per Cochleam enim transuersam distantiæ in Instrumento arctabantur, dilatabanturque. Et licet Cochlea illa in directum porrigebatur, tamen quia clauiculos, vbi affixa erat, conuolubiles habuit, motioni nihilominus circulari sufficiebat. Hanc etiam commoditatem Witichius, quo minus eam indicârit, neglexisse videtur, vt ob id catenulam illam eius loco applicare vobis fuerit necessarium. In pinnacidijs quædam aliter, quam tunc in isto Sextante habebam, limitâstis, quæ mihi admodum commode ordinata videntur. Verum iste Sextans, quo per vnicum Obseruatorem distantiæ capiebantur, dudum apud me in vsu esse desijt. Eius enim loco binis alijs, quos Trigonicos (ob discrimen eius quem habeo bifurcati) appello, in capiendis Stellarum remotionibus assidue vtor, qui singuli binos requirunt Obseruatores, et per lineas circa Cylindrum in centro quiescentem parallelas, exactissime Stellarum sibi inuicem, et cum toto Cæli circuitu adamussim consentientia præbent interualla, nulloque pene negotio Obseruationem absoluunt. Ideam vero horum aliquomodo repræsentatam Illustrissimo Principi inspiciendam (ni fallor) vltimo misi. Sed et vester Sextans, licet alia quadam et similiori primo meo fabricatus sit ratione, satis exactas et meis apprime consentientes exhibet Siderum distantias velut e Catalogo intercapedinum aliquot Stellarum, Illustrissimo Principi tuo nunc transmisso, perspicere potes.

De Refractionibus, quæ ad Illustrissimum Principem vltimo perscripsi, non est quod dubites, rem penitus ita se habere. Nam et hoc proxime elapso Anno per Armillam 10. pedum in Diametro, qui circa axem Polis mundi correspondentem conuolubilis existit, deprehendi, Solem circa Solstitium æstiuum, Refractionem Declinationis, iuxta 30. gradum altitudinis, tam ante quam post Meridianum, insinuare maiorem semisse vnius scrupuli; circa Horizontem vero eandem 28. Minutorum efficere, idque cum Sol inferiori limbo Horizontem visibilem stringeret. Si vero hæ Declinationum Refractiones in altitudinem, habita etiam ratione Parallaxeos, quæ versus Horizontem Refractionem ipsam nonnihil inhibet, deriuentur, adhuc maiores euadent. To the most erudite man, of varied learning, but especially excelling in distinguished skill of Astronomy, Master Christoph Rothmann, Court Mathematician of the most illustrious Prince Wilhelm, Landgrave of Hesse, etc., his much-beloved friend.

The letter which you gave me last year in the month of April — moved both by the Mandate of your most illustrious Prince and by your own benevolence toward me and my studies — most erudite Rothmann, was far most acceptable to me, especially on this account, that it contained in manifold ways a more recondite conference with me about Astronomical matters. But why I reply to the most illustrious Prince's letter, and to yours too, later than your expectation, I have set forth my excuse to the most illustrious Prince. And I do not doubt that his Highness, by that Heroic candour of his mind, will take it in fair and good part. About the celestial observations too — both yours and mine, called to the balance against one another — I have dealt more amply in my letter to the most illustrious Prince, and communicated some of ours to his Highness, that I might comply with his clement request. About these, therefore, I judged it not worthwhile to write more copiously to you, lest I seem to re-sing the same song tediously — since from those [things] which were transmitted by me to the most illustrious Prince, these will doubtless come to your knowledge. About those [things], therefore, chiefly which were contained in your own letters — both the earlier and the second sent — I will now deal with you more fully. That almost exact agreement between your observations and ours in the Comet of [15]85, [and that it] pleased your most illustrious Prince and you so much that you perceived no small admiration and pleasure thence, was no less delightful for me to learn. And that on that account you transmitted to me your most erudite writing on this Comet (though not brought to the colophon), that I might thence more fully learn the congruence of your observations with mine, was far most welcome to me. I read through that writing once and again, and it pleased me wondrously. It were truly to be wished that those [things] which most of your Germans rather blurted out about some past comets, seen in former years, than Mathematically demonstrated by exquisite and infallible observations, had been elaborated with equal diligence and certainty. For then I should not have found, not without disgust, so great discrepancies between their tenets and ours in the position and motion of comets, and so great deviation from the heaven itself and from the kernel of the truth to be sought. That that Comet completed its course far above the elementary orb, in the highest aether, you most rightly establish with me — although the opinions of almost all Philosophers, both ancient and recent, gainsay [it]. In this only is found between us a very tiny discrepancy, and one of no moment: that you gather that it had no parallax at all, but I found it [to be] about one minute at the beginning, and afterward at least a half. Although, to confess the truth, I myself could from time to time discern none at all, on account of its so slight tenuity. But because from the proper motion it was lawful to infer that it was situated far below the eighth Sphere, though above the Sun, therefore it was consonant that it obtain some — though very tiny and scarcely perceptible — diversity of aspect. [The more so] since not Mars alone, especially when acronychal, introduces an observable parallax (as we made trial at the beginning of the year [15]82), but Jupiter [♃] itself too — to say nothing of the star Saturn [♄], where in so very slight a difference no place is left for the senses. But this discrepancy between us is so tiny that I seem to speak of it in vain. In the Longitudes and Latitudes of the Comet noted by you, a difference is found between our observations, a little greater [yet] itself too of almost no moment, which is so tiny that it scarcely reaches five or six minutes at most. Nay, it very often plainly agrees. But this minute discrepancy between us comes about, as I think, chiefly for this cause: that you assumed the places of the Fixed [stars], of which use was made on both sides, a little otherwise than I. For by you Aldebaran is placed at 4° 7′ Gemini, latitude 5° 29′ South, which I, from my observations, establish at 4° 1′ Gemini, latitude 5° 31′ South — a discrepancy of at least 6 minutes in longitude, but of two in latitude, falling between the two positions. But the bright [star] in the head of Aries, which you call the third, you [put] at 1° 58′ Taurus, latitude 9° 57′ North; but I assumed [it] at 1° 54′ Taurus, latitude 9° 57′ North — a difference of at least 4 in longitude, none at all in latitude intervening between us. And since I used chiefly these same Fixed [stars] in investigating this Comet's appearances, a slight variety could come about between us in establishing the Comet's places from this (though very tiny) diversity. Although this discrepancy too in the very places of the Fixed [stars] is so tiny that it scarcely merits mention. But that the position of these two Fixed [stars], as to longitude and latitude, stands most exactly as our notation gives, the very distance between the two — which I, nearly the same as you, several times took from the heaven [at] 35° 32¼′ — confirms. But if you investigate the interval from the complements of the latitudes and the angle comprehended by the difference of longitude, by the reckonings of Triangles, in your assignation nearly one minute is in excess. But that this little [thing], though of no great moment in itself, [is] weighed so subtly — the vulgar contemplators of the Heaven will deservedly laugh, to whom even ten minutes are reckoned as not to be reckoned. But I do not doubt that by your more correct Instruments you have afterward found out with me that this maximum Declination is now 23° 31½′, as we too found by experiment, both in the past year and in several preceding [ones], by several machines — although the Sun's meridian Altitude in each Solstice, parallax applied, gives it at least [at] 23° 27′, more than 4 minutes smaller, agreeing indeed better with the notation of Copernicus and his contemporaries, but on account of the Solar Refraction arising in the lower position near the winter [solstice], subject to so sensible an error. For the Sun is not elevated for us a full 11 degrees, at the very noon near the winter Solstice. Nor was it much higher for Copernicus at Frauenburg. For I do not doubt that you have now put the last hand to it.

For I, [now] for many years, have not been of the opinion that any orbs really exist in the heaven, and that the matter of the heaven is hard and impervious, and that the Stars are revolved only by the motion of the orbs. For many absurdities follow thence; and if nothing else, surely so many Comets observed by us most certainly to exercise [their] course in the aether in some preceding years, which accompanied the leading of no orb, sufficiently refute this. I have therefore always judged that a natural science of motion is congenital — or rather divinely implanted — in each Planet, by which they are compelled to observe the norm of their course most regularly and constantly in the most fluid and subtle aether, needing no support or promoter — just as the Earth too, in the fluid air, stabilized by no support, resting on its own centre alone on every side, is firmed. But that I really establish [it] thus, another reasoning too about the disposition of the celestial Revolutions, far more competent than either the Ptolemaic or the Copernican, newly explored by me (experience itself teaching), much confirms — about which there is now no place for speaking. But that that most celebrated Philosopher of our age, Petrus Ramus, thought that Astronomy could be constituted without Hypotheses, by Logical reasonings, lacks foundation. He indeed proposed this opinion to me 16 years ago, when we were together at Augsburg, and was at the same time an exhorter that, after I had reduced the course of the stars into exact order by Hypotheses, I should affect to attempt the same without these. He added this reason that it could be done — that he had read that the Egyptians of old had a most easy knowledge of Astronomy; and since the reckoning of hypotheses seems difficult and intricate, they must have known the course of the stars by some other more compendious and plainer way, and therefore without all hypotheses. But I resisted him, showing that without hypotheses the celestial Phenomena cannot be reduced to a sure science, nor be excused [accounted for] so as to be understood. But since he — a man otherwise endowed with perspicacious talent, and a lover of truth if anyone [is] — seemed to me not to have thoroughly perceived the inner chambers of this art, and not to have noticed the variety in the motion of the stars by no means recurring at fixed times of the year, I could obtain nothing from him in this part, nor wished [to]. He still has very many followers who hope the same can be done — but who neither understand the matter itself nor will ever bring [it] into effect. For since all things consist of number, weight, and measure, without these too nothing in the visible world can be explained. Without these, if anyone wish to comprehend the motions of the Stars, he must (as the saying is) invoke Fortune, and imagine a supramundane reckoning, beyond the human intellect and plainly incorporeal and more than angelic. But when he had stayed with me a little beyond a quarter of a year, and thought himself now sufficiently possessed of his desires, he pretended that his uncle at Breslau had departed this life, whose inheritance would bring him great gain if he were present in good time. Having therefore easily obtained leave to depart, he promised to return hither within 7 or 8 weeks. But from that time I have seen neither him nor any letter set forth from him, nor heard of him, where he might be, before I read the letter of your most illustrious Prince written to Lord Heinrich Rantzau, from which I at once understood that he had there, for the most part, reproduced the form and reckoning of the Instruments which he had seen here. Which perhaps was done by him meanwhile elsewhere too. And in truth, by this means he did me a thing not unwelcome, since I should much wish that in many places there be very many who would scrutinize the appearances of the stars with me by exquisite and least-fallacious Instruments. Yet if he sells my inventions — acquired by many years, long Experience, and no small expense and labour — as his own, and does not confess through whom he had them, he commits a thing alien to a good and grateful man, and to Mathematical sincerity and integrity. But I understand, partly from your letters, partly from the report of my domestic who was with you, that he did not order everything for the most illustrious Prince competently enough, as he had seen [it] here. The division indeed, which we use similarly in almost all Instruments, I find he indicated rightly enough; and in the slits of the sights [rimulae pinnacidiorum], especially opportune for nocturnal observations of the stars (which invention, when he first saw [it] here, he said he had not come into Denmark in vain, and esteemed [it] more precious than much gold), he imitated certain [things], though imperfectly. For while he sought a compendium in these, and presumed to effect with two slits the same as we [do] with four, he made a loss of the truth to be sought, and by detracting from our inventions, derogated from himself and from the certainty of [his] observations. One of my [people] reports that he heard from him at that time, when he was here, that he himself, when he should imitate the construction of our Instruments, said that he would effect the same with fewer slits, and reserve this compendium for himself. But how excellently he accomplished that, you have found by experience, not without the loss of many observations. I everywhere — especially in the Quadrants — apply four slits in the lower sights, parallel on every side to the four sides of the upper sight, which I sometimes make larger and looser, as the magnitude of the stars demands. For otherwise, by two slits (a lateral one and one set above), the transits and altitudes of the stars are by no means discerned centrally and exactly — which Experience easily shows. And unless you, taught by this, had applied a double visual slit vice versa to the lower sight, you would even now labour in vain in observing the stars exactly. But the invention of that division by transverse points (which you — whosesoever it be — proclaim ingenious) I first had at Leipzig from Homelius, more or less 24 years ago, when I was in the 17th year of [my] age — but only on a straight line (namely on the Cross-staff, to be divided into several little parts); by his imitation I then used it. But when afterward I began to investigate the Altitudes of the stars too by Quadrants, and found by experience that in smaller Instruments the vulgar division does not suffice for the least little portions, I turned to that subtlety of Nonius [Nunes] which he hands down in the third Proposition De crepusculis, and rendered the same still more exact by several subdivisions, Tables too being computed, from which the altitude of any point might be known scrupulously without delay. And when the matter itself taught that not even this Nonian invention had in the issue what it promised in the approach, I began to consider whether that aforesaid division — by which a straight line is most subtly cut by transverse points into smaller particles — could be applied also to curves and circles. And although at first the reasoning did not seem equal, on account of the disconvenience of straights and curves, yet since in so small an interval (as of five or ten points), curved lines drawn at a small interstice from one another do not differ sensibly from straights, I noticed that this business succeeds conveniently enough; and therefore I have for many years now always used this division in Quadrants, Sextants, [and] Armillae with happy success, and I rejoice that it has been communicated to you (by whatever occasion), so that you may the more conveniently scrutinize the stars with equal precision with me — so that our observations, collated together, reach the same mark of truth scrupulously, as is fitting (which too I experience comes about not without pleasure). The instrument of the Sextant, devised by me more or less 18 years ago when I was living at Augsburg (which I also showed there to Petrus Ramus when he was with me, together with that very great Quadrant of 14 cubits which I had had built outside the city in a certain garden of Consul Hainzel — [Ramus] who avidly demanded a description of them from me), I have had in use ever since, because I had found by sure reasoning and much Experience that by the Cross-staff, of whatever magnitude or division it might be, settled distances are by no means found. Wherefore, by [applying] the sixth part of a circle to rules (that the handling of the Instrument might become more convenient), I undertook to attempt the same with a surer outcome. And on that account I gave to this Instrument the name of Sextant. But that one which Wittich saw here, which was of steel, lacked that little chain which you applied. For by a transverse Screw [Cochlea] the distances in the Instrument were narrowed and widened. And although that Screw was extended in a straight [line], yet because it had revolvable pegs where it was fixed, it nonetheless sufficed for circular motion. This convenience too Wittich seems to have neglected, in that he did not indicate it — so that on that account it was necessary for you to apply that little chain in its place. In the sights you have limited certain [things] otherwise than I then had in that Sextant, which seem to me quite conveniently ordered. But that Sextant, by which distances were taken by a single Observer, has long since ceased to be in use with me. For in its place I assiduously use two others, which I call Trigonic (for distinction from the bifurcated one which I have), in taking the distances of the stars, which each require two Observers, and by lines parallel about a Cylinder resting at the centre exhibit most exactly the intervals of the stars consonant with one another and with the whole circuit of the Heaven, and complete the observation with almost no trouble. But the Design [Idea] of these, somehow represented, I sent (if I am not mistaken) lately to the most illustrious Prince to be inspected. But your Sextant too, although built by a certain other reasoning, and one more similar to my first, exhibits the distances of the stars exact enough and perfectly consonant with mine, as you can perceive from the Catalogue of the intervals of some stars now transmitted to your most illustrious Prince.

About the Refractions, which I last wrote to the most illustrious Prince, there is no [reason] for you to doubt that the matter stands thoroughly so. For in this last-elapsed year too, by an Armilla 10 feet in Diameter, which is revolvable about an axis corresponding to the Poles of the world, I found that the Sun, about the summer Solstice, insinuates a Refraction of Declination, near the 30th degree of altitude, both before and after noon, greater than a half of one minute; but about the Horizon makes the same [at] 28 minutes — and that when the Sun grazed the visible Horizon with [its] lower limb. But if these Refractions of the Declinations be derived into altitude, account being had too of the Parallax (which toward the Horizon somewhat inhibits the Refraction itself), they come out still greater. In the fixed stars, however, this diversity does not seem so great — although in these too I find [it] greater than can be gathered from your assignation written to me. For the brighter [star] in the right side of Perseus [Algol/Mirfak region — see Apparatus], you, at altitude 9° 42′ (as [it is] least in the Meridian toward the North), assert the Refraction to be at least 2⅓′; which I, however, in the same star's altitude (also in the Meridian to the North, at its lowest, which here is 14° 16′), found to be 2½′, a little greater than yours — when it ought rather to be smaller, since for you this star was observed lower by more than 4½ degrees. So too the right shoulder of Perseus, at altitude 17° 45½′, I noticed makes a Refraction of 1⅚′; so that on that account the altitudes of the Cassiopeia stars, which you took below the Pole for investigating its altitude, do not exactly correspond to the upper [ones], such that the Pole be exactly intermediate. For they are not free of all suspicion of Refraction, since they scarcely reach the 20th degree of altitude with you in that position — whereas at the 18th degree the Refraction reaches nearly two minutes, as is clear from the preceding indication. These, therefore, I leave to you to examine more considerately. But the most evident discrepancy in the refractions, between your and my observations, is recognized from this: that you indicate the tail of Cygnus, at altitude 5° 18′, to make a Refraction of at least 5⅔′ — whereas I, by diligent and several times repeated Observation, found the head of Algol, at altitude 5° 21½′ (nearly similar to yours in the tail of Cygnus), to make a Refraction of 9¼′, nearly double yours. These, therefore, investigate by occasion more accurately, with well-corrected Instruments, and you will see the whole matter stands thus — unless perhaps (which, however, I should not easily believe) the diverse nature of the Horizons introduces a greater insinuation of Refraction here than with you. Whence it would follow that in more Northern places it comes out ampler than in Southern — and that even if the air be quite pure and serene (which is necessarily required in this observation especially) on both sides near the bound[ary]. A deeper knowledge of which thing your observations, more exactly repeated (as I advised), will successively supply. Wherefore, although you hold it persuaded that no Refraction occurs, not even in the Sun, in a position higher than 20°, because the vapours do not ascend higher — so that you may more probably prove that the matter of the heaven does not differ from the air, the [theory] of Alhazen and Witelo being repudiated (on account of diverse transparencies, the excessive Refraction which would reach to the zenith somewhat by degrees) — yet, since experience (as I indicated above) testifies that even in the Sun, beyond the 30th degree, some — though very tiny — trace of Refraction appears, [which] can scarcely be excused by vapours (since they are not raised so high), and the fixed stars too at altitude 18° admit a Refraction of nearly two minutes (as we proved before by the right shoulder of Perseus at [its] least northern altitude), it will surely be necessary to establish that that celestial air, as you wish, is far subtler and more transparent than this Elemental Air — or rather, speaking more properly, that the diaphaneity of the Aether is purer and more perspicuous than the diaphaneity of the Air. But since there is not so great a difference between these two transparencies as between Air and water, and [since] it is successively diminished where they touch one another — so that the supreme portion of the Air, contiguous to the Moon's Revolutions, differs almost nothing from the aether itself (for that an Element of fire exists there, as the Aristotelians feign, Experience disproves by this sole reasoning, since far greater Refraction of the stars would come about hence, if their light reached us through triple transparencies) — therefore too a very tiny Refraction is caused, except in so far as it is increased by vapours in the lower position; and when the stars appear higher, it is rendered plainly insensible, nor grows so much as the aforesaid reasoning of Alhazen and Witelo induces. For they thought the Heaven consists of solid transparent matter, far diverse from the Air itself.

That you have noticed that the fixed Stars change their latitudes, and that proportionally, according to the variation of the obliquity of the Ecliptic, pleases me greatly. For I too long since gathered with myself that some such thing had happened — since our observations, collated with the Ptolemaic notation, attested that most stars in the summer and northern semicircle toward the Ecliptic had increased [their] Latitude as much as the change of Obliquity would require, but in the southern [semicircle] had diminished the same nearly as much; and that the contrary happened by like reckoning in the winter semicircle. And therefore it was indicated, not obscurely, that this comes about through the change of the maximum Declination, on account of the approach and recession of the Ecliptic to the Stars themselves. But since I found certain Stars — even among the more illustrious, especially the bright [star] of Vultur [Altair] and Aldebaran and the Greater Dog [Sirius] — to cry out against this speculation in the Ptolemaic numbers of Latitude, I began to examine this business by a deeper search, so that the doubts which arose might be cut away. And since this matter is of great moment, and noticed by no one else (that I know), you alone excepted, I will set forth somewhat more fully, from my considerations, certain [things] in this part, so that it may be better permitted to take aim at the mark of truth, all doubts set aside. Since I held the numbers of Longitudes and latitudes of the Stars assigned in the Ptolemaic Table [Abacus] not undeservedly suspect — because they do not everywhere agree with one another and with the Heaven itself, account being had of the intervals of the stars (which remain unvaried) — I scrutinized the whole matter somewhat more deeply, and that from the Declinations of certain fixed Stars which the same Ptolemy, in chapter 3 of book 7 of the Almagest, recounts as observed by Timochares, Hipparchus, and himself; to which I applied their Longitudes, such as it was fitting them to have at each of those times. In which matter too, not much trusting the Ptolemaic intervals, I used a peculiar caution, so that I constituted Spica of Virgo [♍] as the foundation of the rest. For it seems consonant that it was observed more surely, because it was near the Equinoctial, and because it is so often repeated by Ptolemy. And since he himself asserts that in the time of his observations it declined a half-degree toward the South, [whereas] Timochares found [it] 1° 24′ and Hipparchus at least 36′ more Northern than the Equator, it follows — Spica's Latitude being assumed on both sides [as] 2° Southern (as it was explored by the Moon at that time, and as it is also found now nearly) — that its Longitude was, for Timochares 21° 53′, for Hipparchus 23° 53′, for Ptolemy 26° 38′ of Virgo; and therefore from Timochares to Hipparchus by 2 degrees, from Hipparchus to Ptolemy by 2¾°, this and all the other fixed Stars were advanced. Wherefore, these places of Spica being presupposed, I applied the Longitudes of certain other Fixed [stars], whose Declinations were established from the ancients, to the time of the observations of those same authors — namely Timochares, Hipparchus, and Ptolemy — and that by this law: that whatever difference, forward or backward, as to the interval of Longitude from Spica, they are now found by our observations to have, I constituted just as much also in the elongation from Spica's place fitting their time, so that the true Longitude of the Stars with respect to this Star might become known, the Ptolemaic notation being neglected (which is more rarely found complete in all [its] numbers). For it is not possible that the Longitudes of the Fixed [stars] vary otherwise in one Star than in another, since all are in one orb, and move uniformly about one and the same Pole. Whence it will be necessary that the differences of Longitudes were the same in Ptolemy's times, in all Stars, which are even now found. When, therefore, I had adapted the Longitudes of the Stars by this reasoning, hence, and from the Declinations which Ptolemy recounts in the cited chapter, the maximum obliquity of the Zodiac being applied, which in their age was 23° 51⅓′, I inquired also into the Latitudes of the same — which could be done not difficultly. For from the given difference of the Star's Longitude from the nearest Tropic, one angle of the Triangle is established, of which two sides also are given — one from the complement of the Declination (or the addition to the Quadrant, as the business requires), the other from the difference of the Poles of the Ecliptic and the Equator; therefore the third [side] easily becomes known by the laws of Spherical Triangles, by which the very Latitude of the Star sought, such as it then was, comes [to be] known. In this manner, I say, I examined the Latitudes of the Stars from the diverse assumed Declinations of the Ancients, the due Longitudes being applied. And although in this way too I found not in all Stars the same consonance I hoped — because their Declinations were either not observed scrupulously enough by the Ancients, or because some errors lurk here and there in the assignation of the numbers (which, however, that I might guard against as far as in me lay, I used also a certain old copy of the Almagest, published more or less 70 years ago at Venice, which expresses the Declinations not in cyphers [Arabic numerals], as the Trapezuntine version, but in aliquot parts of degrees, by letters) — nonetheless I judged that a greater certainty underlies these than the Latitudes assigned in the Ptolemaic Catalogue; and wherever an evident incoherence appeared, I took the Ptolemaic numbers too into counsel. But what, from these, I explored in the Latitudes of certain Fixed [stars], drawn out from their times down to now, I will now indicate, for the most part, with what brevity is possible.

First, considering the Stars about the summer Tropic, I find from the Declinations of both heads of Gemini [♊] that they now obtain a greater Latitude than in the Ancients' times, and that according to the very reckoning of the changed Declination of the Ecliptic. For the Northern head of Gemini [Castor], by the aforesaid tenor, is found to have had, in Timochares's time, a Latitude of nearly 9° 43′, [in] Hipparchus's 9° 42′, [in] Ptolemy's nearly 9° 45′ — whence it is gathered that it was about their times 9° 43′ nearly, which is now 10° 2′. Therefore increased by 19 minutes, as the recession of the Ecliptic in that place requires. In like manner the Southern head of Gemini [Pollux], from Hipparchus's Declination and the rest (of which I spoke) given, is found to have had a Latitude of 6° 19½′, but from Ptolemy's nearly 6° 22½′, of which the mean is 6° 21′, the Latitude fitting the Star in their age, which is now found by exact reckoning to be 6° 38′ — so that on that account too it has changed [its] Latitude nearly as much as the altered obliquity of the Zodiac requires. So too Capella is found to have obtained, [for] Hipparchus and Ptolemy, a Latitude from the assigned Declinations of 22⅚° (although the Ptolemaic numbers establish this at 22° 30′); whence we shall make this more probable, somewhat limiting [it], if we put the Latitude then [at] 22⅚°, which is now found to be 22° 50½°′ [22° 50½′] — also varied in nearly due manner by reason of the changed Ecliptic. A document of the same matter is afforded also by both shoulders of Orion. For the right had a Latitude from the Declinations of Timochares and Hipparchus of very nearly 16⅓° (some error underlies the Ptolemaic Declination in this place), which is now found to obtain 16 1/10, also rendered smaller in nearly due manner. The left shoulder of the same Orion, from Hipparchus's and Ptolemy's Declinations, obtained in that age a Latitude of nearly 17° 5′, which it now has [at] 16° 53′ — also diminished as it were proportionally, by reason of the approaching Ecliptic. Not otherwise the Heart of Leo [♌] [Regulus] — which star Ptolemy observed a little more diligently — obtained for itself a Latitude of 0° 10′ North; although this agrees more precisely with Hipparchus's Declination than with his own, we by accurate observations found this 26 minutes more Northern than the Ecliptic, so that 16 minutes now are in excess from the Ancients' findings — which, effected on account of the recession of the Ecliptic, appears conformable enough to our intention. But on the other side the Star Aldebaran introduces some impediment, since the Ptolemaic numbers give its Latitude [as] 5⅙° Southern — which too is not so alien from his Declination. Whose Latitude, however, I could not find smaller than 5° 30½′, a third of one degree greater than the Ptolemaic, when rather it ought to be smaller from the assumed Hypothesis of the Ecliptic's change. Whence in the observation of this Star, as to latitude, I judge that no small error underlies the Ptolemaic Declination itself — which the Declinations of the same [star] by Timochares and Hipparchus prove, although neither do these stand absolutely in their numbers. For Timochares's Declination gives the Star a Latitude of 5° 56′, greater than just; Hipparchus's 5° 33′, somewhat smaller than due — although each exceeds the Ptolemaic (as the matter requires). Nor do I see how it could have been possible that the Declination of this Star (which then dwelt about the first decury of the Dodecatemorion of Taurus [♉]) was changed from Timochares to Ptolemy by 2¼°, whereas, about the very Equinox, Spica of Virgo meanwhile altered the same by no more than 1° 54′. And therefore I judge that no slight error about the Latitude of Aldebaran crept in upon Ptolemy (provided the numbers drawn down to us stand rightly). And if we take the mean between Timochares's and Hipparchus's Latitude — which, namely, comes out from their Declinations — as more probable, limiting an otherwise perplexed matter, the latitude of the eye of Taurus then comes out [at] 5¾°, which does not deviate much from the inquiry we intend. But that the Latitude which Ptolemy attributes to this Star is utterly erroneous appears from this — that he assigns in his Catalogue to the Northern eye of Taurus a Latitude of 3° 0′, which we found [at] nearly 2⅔°, and therefore by the Ptolemaic assignation a little more smaller than the changed Obliquity of the Ecliptic requires — whereas Aldebaran showed the contrary. And the distance which is between both these Stars in the eyes, which by repeated Observation I found to be 3° 10¼′, proves that the Ptolemaic assignation in one or the other (or both) evidently deflects. For if, from the complement of the Latitude of each Star which he attributes to them, and the difference of Longitudes (namely, from two known sides with the comprehended angle), we inquire the distance, it comes out [at] 2° 19′, falling short of the true by 51 minutes. Wherefore, if the latitude of the more Northern eye from the Ptolemaic numbers be nearer the truth, Aldebaran must necessarily have had a greater Latitude than he assigns. For it is not possible that it was then nearer the Northern eye than now. From these [things], therefore, it appears that the Latitude which Ptolemy assigns to Aldebaran is less settled, and therefore in this — in that it seems to gainsay our intention — does not merit credit.

I come now to the Stars looking to the winter semicircle of the Ecliptic, and especially near the winter Tropic, where (that the demonstration of the proposed matter may come out more evident) occurs the bright [star] of Vultur volans [Altair, in Aquila], whose Latitude, although the Ptolemaic Catalogue exhibits [it at] 29° 10′ — so that on that account at first sight it seems to gainsay our opinion — yet, when I draw out its Latitude from the Declinations of the Ancients, so far is this Star from disturbing what we intend, that none is found to stabilize this very [thing] more exactly and surely. For this was, about the times of the Ancients of whom I speak, nearest as to Longitude to the winter Tropic, and therefore its Latitude was easily clear even from the Declination alone. For in the year of Timochares's observations, when it was scarcely a sixth part of one degree before the Solstitial Colure itself, its Declination was found [at] 5° 48′, and therefore the Latitude (the maximum Declination of that age being assumed) comes out [at] 29° 39½′ — to which the Hipparchan notation too is a supporter, yielding exactly the same Declination, because this varied insensibly about the Solstice. Thence the Star's Latitude too proceeded [at] 29° 39′. And although the Ptolemaic declination, which was two minutes greater, seems to render this Latitude narrower by one and a half minutes (because he perhaps did not apply the most exact precision in the very minutes), yet this imports nothing, but sufficiently attests, together with the rest, that the Latitude of the bright [star] in Aquila was, in that age of the world, very nearly 29° 39′. And since it is now found [at] 29° 21′, it is manifest that it has meanwhile narrowed [its] Latitude by 18 minutes — as much as the nearer approach of the Ecliptic in that place demands — so that even from this single Star near the winter Tropic (especially if the heads of Gemini near the summer Tropic in the other half be used together, vice versa) it can be sufficiently proved that the fixed Stars vary their Latitudes proportionally by reason of the changed obliquity of the Zodiac. Since so unanimously in this Star the observed Declinations of those three most excellent Artificers agree (which too were nearly unvaried, because the Star, near to the Tropic as to longitude, changed [its] Declination insensibly, as I said) — whence too, if any doubt can lurk in its Longitude, it will insinuate no error in those [things] which we intend in that place. But Antares too, in the same half of the Ecliptic, further confirms the same, which is found, both from Timochares's and Hipparchus's Declinations, [at] 4° 14′ (an evident error must underlie the Ptolemaic Declination in this Star, because it gives its Latitude [at] 4° 37′, which, however, he himself in the Catalogue of Fixed [stars] puts [at] 4° 0′, much dissenting from himself) — whereas now the same is 4° 24′. It is therefore manifest that the Latitude in this Star too has been increased not much differently from what the recession of the Ecliptic in that place demands. The Southern pan of Libra [♎] too — since credit must rather be given to Hipparchus's Declination, because this seems more probable — obtained in that age a Latitude of nearly a half-degree North, which is now found to be 26 minutes, rendered narrower on account of the approach of the Ecliptic. Although not only Timochares's Declination, but Ptolemy's own too, deviates intolerably in this Star. For the former gives a Latitude of scarcely 17 minutes, but the latter at least 2 — whereas the Ptolemaic Catalogue itself exhibits it [at] 40 minutes, which, though it be too much, and more than is necessary, [yet] supports our intention, and exists nearer the truth. The Latitude of the Northern pan too, both Timochares's and Hipparchus's Declination gives [at] nearly 8° 48′, which from our Observation is now 8° 36′ nearly — so that on that account in this Star too the Latitude is found narrowed by reason of the approach of the Ecliptic. For [the fact] that the Ptolemaic Declination exhibits it [at] at least 8° 25′ (with which his Table nearly agrees, indicating the same [at] 8° 30′) — whence what we propose seems to be shaken — we do not heed. For that the Ptolemaic Declination and Latitude is less certain easily appears from this, that it admits a Declination changed from Hipparchus to himself [of] 1° 24′, whereas Spica of Virgo meanwhile varied the same by only 1° 6′. Wherefore I judge one must rather stand by the Declinations of Timochares and Hipparchus in that Star, since they agree with each other and give the same interval of change which Spica of Virgo is gathered to effect for them.

From these Stars, therefore, dwelling both about the Northern and the Southern semicircle of the Ecliptic, I judge it has been demonstrated more than sufficiently that, for the variation of the Ecliptic's Obliquity, the Latitudes of the fixed Stars are altered along [with it] proportionally. Nor would any place for doubt about this matter seem to remain, did not that most splendid [star] in the mouth of the Greater Dog [Sirius] hint something contrary. Whose Latitude, from Timochares's Declination (which was 16⅓), is found [at] 39° 18′; from Hipparchus's (which was 16°) comes out the same [at] 39° 10′, such as Ptolemy too puts in his Table. For his Declination too, a quarter of a degree smaller than Hipparchus's, gives the Star a Latitude of 39° 7⅓′, which approaches nearly to a sixth part of a degree beyond the whole [degrees] — since he had no reckoning of two or three minutes in this part — so that, both by Hipparchus and by Ptolemy, this Star's Latitude is found to have then been 39⅙° Southern, which, however, we in this age have found [at] 39° 31′, which is greater than the Ancients' tenets by a third part of one degree, when rather it ought now to be just as much smaller, other things being equal. The resolution of which knot is surely not easily obvious. For the uniform consensus of all difficultly admits that some error lurks in the Ancients' Observations; nor does it seem credible that they so sensibly hallucinated in so Illustrious a Star, which is the greatest and most shining of all the Fixed [stars]. And since our observations too in this very Star lack all doubt, I cannot sufficiently wonder how this exhibits a greater Latitude now than of old. For it cannot be conceded that it is not subject to the same laws as the rest of the Fixed [stars], but [must] equally with them vary [its] Latitude at the nod of the Ecliptic's Obliquity. Wherefore, ventilating this matter variously, I can build nothing else than that the Ancients' Observations in the Declination of this Star — though perhaps had well enough from the heaven — were nonetheless less rightly transmitted to us by the fault of the transcribers. For a similar error could be committed in copying the single [data]. For [the fact] that the notation of its Latitude in the Ptolemaic Table subscribes to the Declinations makes little for the matter. Since otherwise the Latitudes assigned there are often found to dissent not only from the Declinations but from the Heaven itself, if they be called to the balance by the distance of the stars. Nor can any prejudice be brought against this assertion — about the change of the Latitude of the Stars to the varied Obliquity of the Ecliptic — on account of this single Star (though more shining than the rest), [against] those [things] which we have proved by several documents to stand rightly in so many others; especially since the brighter of the Lesser Dog [Procyon] supports our position, which in Ptolemy's times was near the Colure of the Solstices, and then had, from his numbers, a Latitude of 16° 10′, which it now obtains [at] 15° 57′, smaller on account of the approach of the Ecliptic — though not by so much as that distance from the Tropic requires, because Ptolemy did not reckon the five minutes which are wanting. But [the fact] that Spica of Virgo was established by the Ancients to obtain two degrees in Latitude, such as it is also found to retain in this age (or, if we apply the highest precision, at least a half-minute smaller), when rather it ought now to be 3 or 4 minutes greater, from the changed Obliquity of the Ecliptic — this nonetheless cannot shake this our position. Since those Ancients had almost no reckoning of 3 or 4 minutes; for they noted both the Longitudes and Latitudes of the Stars only in sixths of one degree, as may be seen in the Ptolemaic Table. There is added this too, that from the visible conjunction [occultation] of the Moon with this Star they several times investigated its Latitude together with the Longitude — whereas the Lunar Parallaxes by no means stood so surely for them but that a hallucination of a few minutes could insinuate itself in this way, especially since they used the Ptolemaic Hypothesis in the Moon (for no other was known to the Ancients) — which our observations too attest sometimes exhibits a far greater approach of the Moon to the Earth than the truth of the matter demands, so that it turns out consonant that the Parallaxes deduced thence are less settled. But these I now treat perhaps more copiously than the laws of a Letter permit; wherefore, these foundations being laid, I leave to you, most learned Rothmann, everything to be weighed more deeply, and more to be superadded to these. Now I proceed to the rest.

That in the Latitudes of the Stars a discrepancy of a few minutes falls between the most illustrious Prince's observations and mine comes about chiefly for this: that the Declinations of the Stars, before that correction of the perpendicular which you signified to me in your last letter, were less settled by two minutes; the maximum Obliquity of the Zodiac too being assumed somewhat diversely, varying somewhat along [with it] in these. But I greatly rejoiced when I understood from your later letters that you had found out this error of the perpendicular; for otherwise your Declinations everywhere dissented from mine by nearly two minutes, because the Altitude of the Equator came out one minute smaller than the true, and [that] of the Stars one greater. Nor do I doubt that, if after this restitution of your Quadrant exactly to the level, you examine the Solar Altitudes exactly (Parallax too applied) with my restitution in the Sun's course, you will prove that I have reached the very mark with every possible subtlety, and that especially in the more Northern Signs. For in the Southern [signs], especially Aquarius [♒] and Scorpio [♏] (in Sagittarius [♐] and Capricorn [♑], on account of the very tiny change of Declination, you know these are void), on account of sensible Refraction, you will find some discrepancy — which, however, can be guarded against by this very [refraction] previously known from elsewhere. In the Lunar eclipse of the year 1584, which you diligently seek — no less than [in] the Lunar obscurations of some preceding years — I found, accurately observed by me, that the first beginning was at 11ʰ 12ᵐ from the noon of 7 November, the Middle at 13ʰ 8ᵐ, the last End at 15ʰ 0ᵐ (for these times are not always equal, as has hitherto been believed) — as I investigated by an infallible reckoning, from the distance of certain Equatorial Stars from the Meridian. But that Wittich found the middle of this eclipse at Kassel [at] 13ʰ 3ᵐ, as you wrote me, lacks exquisite precision. For your Meridian is at least a fifth of an hour (as I said above too) more Western than ours. The middle of the eclipse ought therefore to have been there, just after midnight, [at] 0ʰ 56ᵐ [i.e. 12ʰ 56ᵐ] — half a quarter-hour being in excess in the Wittichian Observation. How diligent, therefore, this notation of time was, let its author see, whose diligence in investigating the stars from the heaven has long been perceived by me — or, to speak more truly, [is] suspect. Heinrich Brucaeus, eminent Mathematician among the Rostockers, our singular friend, observed the same Eclipse there, and found its middle at 1ʰ 4ᵐ after midnight, at a time a little earlier than ours — when rather it ought to be nearly the same, since the difference of Meridians is insensible. But he, by a brass Quadrant — but a smallish one, once granted him by me, from the Altitudes of the Stars (taken only in degrees), scrutinized the times in a broader manner — which in the Stars, especially [those] tending to the Meridian, is subject to evident error. Nonetheless, hence it is still more clear that Wittich's observation was less exact, and therefore does not suffice for more rightly knowing the interval of our Meridians. And since by an observation of the Moon near the 90th degree [of the ecliptic] the same can be accomplished, diligent care being applied, I have ready very many such Lunar considerations (when [the Moon] lacked Parallax of Longitude), faithfully taken from the heaven in some preceding years. If, therefore, I knew on what days the Moon was ascertained by you in the same manner, I would certainly communicate mine too, that a mutual comparison might be instituted. Nay, you will find three such Moon-observations transmitted to your most illustrious Prince, in the assignation of the places of all the Planets, within one Revolution of the whole Heaven, exactly investigated here three times from the beginning of this year, in this very month of January, by my Instruments. If the Moon's place, about some of these times, was investigated by you near the 90th degree of the Ecliptic from the Horizon, and the moments of time stand exactly, you have what you desire. To attempt the same by Lunar Parallaxes in another position than near the 90th degree, the scrupulousness here required by no means certainly admits, on account of the Parallaxes in the numbers of the Tables not free of all doubt. But in numbering these times most subtly, do not trust the Clock too much, unless it observe the Revolution most exactly and move everywhere equally in the intermediate hours, and exhibit the single Minutes (with the Seconds too) distinctly. Which [things] I do not doubt the most illustrious Prince has ready, since he has so artful and excellent an Automaton [clock]. From the Altitudes of the Stars too this subtle practice does not succeed conveniently enough, especially if [the stars] nearer the Meridian be elevated; nor near the Horizon are they free of fault, on account of the inconvenience of Refraction. It will therefore be more advisable to investigate these moments of time from the very Azimuths of the Stars — provided the meridian-line have first been diligently explored, not from the Sun, but from the Stars, but especially from the Pole-star itself, when it is at its maximum Azimuthal removal from the Pole on either side toward rising and setting (which reckoning is subject to not the least error, and can conveniently be done). I am wont most easily to attain the exact moments of time, without any suspicion of error, by the Equatorial Armillae, numbering the distances of the Sun and Stars from the Meridian in the Equator itself (which is the measure of times), in whatever position they exist. If it befall us (the Deity favouring) to observe on both sides the truest times of the Lunar Eclipses imminent this and the future year, we shall be rendered more certain about the intervals of the Meridians and the Moon's motions. But it must be noticed that in a total eclipse with tarrying, the time of the ingress of the Moon's whole body into the Earth's shadow, and of its first egress, is better discerned than the other two moments of the first beginning and last end. Wherefore those rather are accurately to be attended to. It is expedient too to use several Observers, disjoined from one another, who are strong in keenness of sight. The Calculation of the Lunar motion I do not now send, because I have resolved to examine it more often and longer, by several Eclipses and by the Moon's places near the Quadratures, near the mean distance of the Epicycle, and elsewhere too, before I communicate my inventions in this part to others. For I wish that what proceeds from me in Astronomical [matters] be either nothing, or far most exact — so that they do not reach to a greater precision [than that] of the human senses. For it is better to leave these untouched than to handle [them] drowsily and with a ruder Minerva. The motions of Venus and Mercury I have explored scrupulously enough, by manifold Observations. In the three higher Planets I require more acronychal observations, especially in places more distant from one another on the Eccentric; which when I shall have attained (God favouring), I hope — the same Deity directing our endeavours — to consecrate to Posterity the whole of Astronomy, completed in all its numbers, far more perfectly than has been done by our predecessors. For which [end] may God deign to bestow vigour of mind and body, that this most excellent and most ancient of all, and plainly divine, science, at last purged from blots and errors, may be restored into a truth of the Appearances congruent with the Heaven itself.

Nicolaus Copernicus, a vast and incomparable Athlete in this work, had he not been destitute of exquisite Instruments subject to no error, would have left us far surer [things]; for in science and talent he was eminent, if anyone [was], and most perfectly skilled in those [things] which were required from Geometry and Arithmetic for constituting this Art. Nor was he inferior to Ptolemy in this part — nay, in certain [things], especially in the elegance of the Hypotheses and in finding a compendious Harmony, he far surpassed him. Nor does the absurdity, apparent at first sight, in the Earth's Revolution stand in the way — since, if a circular motion regular about alien centres be built [in], as the Ptolemaic Hypotheses bear in all the Planets except the Sun, one sins far more absurdly and intolerably against the very principles of the Art than by attributing some motion to the Earth — which, since it will be natural, comes out insensible. Nor do so many inconveniences arise hence as most suppose; and those [things] which were lately published in the Spheric Poem of that most distinguished Poet Buchanan the Scot, my eminent friend while he was alive, have no place here. For he did not notice that, the terrestrial motion being posited, the sea and the circumfluent and nearest Air are revolved together by an equal impulse, and therefore no violence is caused, nor absurdity, [of] as much as he thinks comes about in all those [things] which he adduces to the contrary. But it is not [a matter] for this place to discuss these more copiously. But these we shall some time, God willing, discuss more amply in our Astronomical Work, and perhaps introduce another reckoning of Hypotheses, which will seem absurd neither Mathematically nor Physically, and will not sin against the very principles of the Art. You ask that I communicate to you the exact knowledge of the Elevation of the Pole which is at Frauenburg, for more deeply exploring the Copernican observations had there. Know, therefore, that three years ago I sent, on that account, a certain one of my students devoting [their] labour to Astronomy — Elias Olai [Morsing] by name — to Frauenburg by ship, an exquisite Instrument capable of the minutest divisions being attached; who, having there conveniently obtained a place for Observing among the Canons of Ermland (by whom too he was benevolently received), staying more than a whole Month, obtained very many exquisite Observations consonant with one another. And therefore, both from the Meridian Altitudes of the Sun in its Northern Declination, and from the maximum Elevations of the Fixed [stars] found there, and collated with those which the same Instrument afforded here, we found the Elevation of Frauenburg [at] 54° 22¼′, exceeding the Copernican notation by nearly 3 minutes. Which too I always suspected, since I saw the Solar Eccentricity and Apogee, and so the whole course of the Sun, discrepant so enormously from his tenets in so brief an interval of years. For that he investigated the Pole's Altitude and the maximum Declination from the maximum and minimum Altitude of the Sun in each Solstice, and that less congruently — because he did not avoid the involvement of Refraction in the low position, and perhaps had no reckoning of Parallax either — I probably gathered from his writings; and that on that account about three minutes were wanting to the Pole's altitude, I judged consonant even before I had a trial made. But meanwhile, while my student was at leisure for Observations at Frauenburg, he was called thither by the most illustrious Prince Georg Friedrich, Margrave of Ansbach — Master Maenius, Professor of Mathematics at Königsberg in Prussia, being sent to him — to examine the Polar Altitude there with equal diligence; which too he effected for some weeks in the Prince's garden, an opportune place being granted. And from the Altitudes of the Sun and Stars there several times acquired, the Elevation of the Pole at Königsberg in Prussia is found [at] 54° 43′ nearly. Which I judged should be indicated to you at the same time, since Reinhold ordered the Prutenic Tables to that place, and established its Latitude [at] 54° 17′, smaller than just by 26 minutes (wrongly imitating Apian's erroneous Catalogue). Returning to me, this my student brought with him a certain Instrument composed by Copernicus himself, and long had in use, sent me as a gift by Lord Johannes Hannov, Canon of Ermland. And it is that Parallactic [instrument] whose construction he explains in book 4, chapter 15 [of De revolutionibus], which they commonly call the Ptolemaic Rules [regulae = triquetrum], because it was once much in use with Ptolemy too. But it is entirely of wood, and fir-wood, and the two equal Rules contain 4 ells in Length, and exhibit a division of 1414 particles, instituted in the vulgar manner, such that any smaller difference presupposes the number five. Hence it is easy to gather with what [kind of] machines that excellent Copernicus, [thus] equipped, tried to derive the celestial motions to us — so that on that account the errors which crept in are to be imputed not to the Art, or to his industry, but to the means of the Instruments, which, less exquisite, he used. I too have long had ready two such instruments of Rules, usefully elaborated in brass, and subtly divided into ten times more little portions — of which one, after the manner of Ptolemy, rests on an erected column, and is conveniently revolved on it, by raising and lowering the longer Rule in which the divisions exist. In the other, the same longer Rule is revolved only to the level of the Horizon, and at the same time, in a Circle whose diameter is 20 feet, shows the Azimuths, the other two equal sides effecting the angle of Altitude by their Elevation and depression. And both are larger than this Copernican [one], and most diligently elaborated. Nonetheless, in precisely investigating the Altitudes of the Sun and Stars, I do not apply so much credit to them as to the Quadrants and Sextants, especially the largish [ones]. Nor do I think it possible to reduce the construction of Rules to the most exact precision which is perpetually consistent with itself. Yet I do not easily bring my mind [to believe] that Copernicus erred much in the Altitude of Spica of Virgo, which he proposes in book 3, chapter 2 — whether he obtained it by this Parallactic Instrument or by any other reasoning — and it is most probable that its Declination arising thence is much surer than the Wernerian Observation. For otherwise the motion of the eighth Sphere would be too accelerated, and would anticipate the Albategnian speed more than is fair. By which argument too it is proved that your Star-Longitudes are a little further on than the advance of this Sphere admits. But I see that, while I converse with you too gladly about those [things] which make for the Astronomical matter, I exceed the measure in writing a Letter. And this I rather admit, both because I am persuaded that this prolixity will not be tedious to you, and also that you may have the tardiness hitherto admitted in replying recompensed by a more copious response. Since, therefore, your desires, and those [things] which you signified to me by letter, have been (as I think) abundantly satisfied, it remains that I now draw in the sails, and hold you commended to God the best and greatest.

Farewell, and by no means forbear to confer with me further about the Astronomical business, at least by Letters — since in person (which would be more convenient) it is not permitted; nay, labour to ascend the stars with me by ladders least fallacious, continuing the work begun at every opportunity of serene weather. Again, farewell.
Given at Uraniborg, the 20th day of January, in the Year [15]87. Tycho Brahe.

Apparatus

  • Edition of record. Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia vol. VI (Epistolae astronomicae); first printed by Tycho in his own 1596 Epistolarum astronomicarum libri, Liber I; no MS witness recorded. Cross-check: page-collated at vol. VI pp. 86–104; the long star-latitude numeral series and the whole numeral spine are confirmed against Dreyer (anchors + the one derived-obliquity flag in the Editorial note).
  • Sender / recipient. Tycho (Uraniborg) → Christoph Rothmann, Court Mathematician to Landgrave Wilhelm IV of Hesse-Kassel. This is the great natural-philosophical and observational letter of the Tycho–Rothmann exchange, answering Rothmann's letters of April [1586] (the substance to the Landgrave is a parallel letter).
  • The 1585 comet & parallax. Near-exact agreement of the Kassel and Hven observations; the comet "in the highest aether." The only difference: Rothmann finds no parallax, Tycho ~1′ then ½′ (since the comet, by proper motion, sat below the 8th sphere though above the Sun). Tycho notes that Mars (esp. at opposition) and even Jupiter give observable parallax. The 5–6′ longitude/latitude differences trace to different fixed-star places (Aldebaran 4°7′ vs 4°1′ Gemini; the "third star of Aries-head"). Meridian difference Kassel–Hven ≈ ⅕ hour. Max obliquity: Tycho 23° 31½′ (from the Landgrave's solstitial altitude 62°12′ + parallax), Rothmann 23°30′; the winter-refraction trap (Sun only c. 11° high) that pulls it to a false 23°27′.
  • Fluid heavens. Tycho agrees the heavens are "air" if the supralunar "air" = a far subtler aether; no real solid orbs (the comets refute them); each planet has a divinely-implanted "science of motion" in the fluid aether, like the Earth resting on its own centre. He foreshadows his own world-system ("more competent than the Ptolemaic or Copernican").
  • Ramus on hypothesis-free astronomy. Tycho recounts Petrus Ramus (Augsburg, 16 years earlier ≈ 1570) urging astronomy "without hypotheses" (the Egyptian-ease argument); Tycho's rebuttal: without hypotheses there is no science; "all things consist of number, weight, and measure"; hypotheses are the measure of apparent motion by circles, which arithmetic resolves into numbers.
  • The Wittich instrument-priority story (a key document). Paul Wittich of Breslau visited Hven (1580, ~¼ year), promised to be Tycho's "faithful Achates," then left for an "uncle's inheritance" at Breslau and never returned; Tycho learned (from the Landgrave's letter to Rantzau) that Wittich had reproduced Tycho's instruments at Kassel — selling them "as his own without confessing through whom." Specifics: the transverse-point division (Tycho had it from Homelius at Leipzig, age 17, c. 24 yr earlier, first on the cross-staff; then Nonius/Nunes De crepusculis prop. 3; then his own application to curves); the parallel-slit sights (rimulae) — Wittich's "compendium" of 2 slits instead of 4 lost precision; the Sextant (devised at Augsburg c. 18 yr earlier, shown to Ramus with the 14-cubit quadrant in Hainzel's garden; the steel sextant with a screw not a chain; the later two-observer Trigonic sextants).
  • Refraction (detailed). Measured by a 10-foot armilla: solar declination-refraction at 30° altitude > ½′, at the horizon 28′. Star-refraction discrepancies with Rothmann: Algol/Perseus's right side (Tycho 2½′ at 14°16′; Perseus's right shoulder 1⅚′ at 17°45½′), and the big one — Rothmann's Cygnus-tail 5⅔′ (alt 5°18′) vs Tycho's Algol-head 9¼′ (alt 5°21½′), nearly double. Against Rothmann (who would deny all refraction above 20°, to make heaven = air), Tycho argues from the persistent small refraction that the aether's diaphaneity is purer than the air's (but not so different as air-to-water), denies the fire-element (else "triple transparencies" would refract more), and rejects Alhazen/Witelo's solid-transparent heaven.
  • The fixed-star-latitude-variation demonstration (the technical core — cf. the Herwart letter of 16 Nov 1599). Rothmann had noticed stars' latitudes vary with the changing obliquity; Tycho confirms it by an exhaustive reconstruction. Method: take Spica as base (its Timochares/Hipparchus/Ptolemy declinations → longitudes 21°53′ / 23°53′ / 26°38′ Virgo, fixing the precession steps 2° and 2¾°); for each star, reconstruct the ancient longitude (same Spica-interval as now) and, with the ancient obliquity 23°51⅓′ and the recorded declination (from Almagest VII.3, using a 70-year-old Venice edition that gives declinations in letters not the Trapezuntine cyphers), solve the spherical triangle for the ancient latitude, then compare to now. Stars worked through: Castor (N Gemini-head, 9°43′→10°2′, +19′), Pollux (S head, 6°21′→6°38′), Capella (22⅚°→22°50½′), Orion's right (16⅓→16 1/10) and left shoulder (17°5′→16°53′), Regulus (0°10′→0°26′ N, +16′), Aldebaran (a crux — Ptolemy's latitude erroneous; checked against the Taurus-eye distance 3°10¼′), Altair (Vultur, 29°39′→29°21′, −18′), Antares (4°14′→4°24′), Libra's S and N pans, and the two cruces: Sirius (latitude now larger not smaller — Tycho ascribes it to transcription error in the ancient declination, since Sirius cannot escape the common law) and Procyon (supports). Conclusion: the latitude-variation law holds; the obliquity itself is essentially what it was (cf. the constancy argued in the Herwart letter of 16 Nov 1599).
  • The 1584 lunar eclipse & meridian method. The eclipse of 7 Nov 1584: begin 11ʰ12ᵐ, mid 13ʰ8ᵐ, end 15ʰ0ᵐ (Uraniborg, by stars' meridian-distances). Wittich's Kassel mid (13ʰ3ᵐ) is wrong (Kassel ⅕ hr west → should be c. 12ʰ56ᵐ); Brucaeus at Rostock 1ʰ4ᵐ after midnight (his small brass quadrant, degrees only, less exact). The best meridian-difference method: the Moon near the 90th ecliptic degree (no longitude-parallax); times by equatorial armillae / azimuths off the Pole-star, not a clock alone. The lunar-motion calculation withheld until perfected ("either nothing, or far most exact").
  • Copernicus, Buchanan, Frauenburg. Praise of Copernicus ("a vast Athlete," hampered only by crude instruments); the Earth's motion less absurd than the alien-centre violation (the sea and air move with the Earth — so George Buchanan's anti-motion Sphaera "has no place"); Tycho promises "another reckoning of hypotheses" (his system). Elias Olai Morsing sent to Frauenburg (3 yr earlier) → pole 54° 22¼′ (Copernicus c. 3′ low, from neglected refraction/parallax); then to Königsberg (via Margrave Georg Friedrich of Ansbach and Master Maenius) → 54° 43′ (Reinhold's Prutenic 54°17′ too low, after Apian). Morsing brought back Copernicus's own triquetrum (gift of canon Johannes Hannov): fir-wood, 4-ell rules, 1414 crude divisions — proof that Copernicus's errors were the instruments', not the man's. Tycho's two brass triquetra (one column-mounted, one with a 20-foot azimuth circle) are better, but he trusts quadrants/sextants more. Copernicus's Spica altitude (De rev. III.2) probably reliable (better than Werner's), else the 8th-sphere motion would outrun the al-Battānī rate — proving Kassel's star-longitudes "a little too far on."
