The original

NOBILISSIMO DOMINO TYCHONI BRAHE DANO, URANIEBURGI DOMINO ETC D. ET AMICO SUO OBSERVANDO. S.

NOBILISS. vir, Domine et amice observande. Uno die mensis Febr. huius anni redditae fuerunt mihi plures literae tuae, atque hae diversis temporibus scriptae; quas cum perlegissem, nostro quoque D. CURTIO legendas dedi. Ab eo ipso die totum hoc tempus huc usque valetudine adversa transegi, initio quidem interpollatim recurrebat ingens et intolerabilis dolor ventriculi flatulentus. Postea cum diebus pluribus durasset, iterumque intermisisset aliquando dies 14, aliquando 20, tandem post gravissimum insultum erupit in Ictericiam, quae post dies aliquot febrem continuam protulit. Haec Ictericiae coniuncta me afflixit per menses tres, nimirum August. Sept. et Octob. Salutis spes fuit exigua, sive quis naturam et cursum utriusque morbi, sive corpus subiectum considerasset. Medici certe male ominari consueverunt ei aegroto, quando Icterus excipit febrem. Iam vero praeter ea accessit aetas senilis annorum 66. Quid igitur de recuperanda sanitate sperare potui? Interea non exhorrui mortem: imo libentissime deposuissem hoc corpusculum, si divinae voluntati placuisset. Sum enim iam pertaesus huius aerumnosae vitae, video etiam patriae nostrae magnas impendere calamitates, quarum utinam non essem spectator. Etsi autem sola Dei bonitate ex morbo difficili liberatus sum: tamen, mi TYCHO, vires mei corpusculi ita labefactatae sunt, ut eas nunquam redituras facile intelligam. Ac futuras eas subinde languidiores, varia symptomata, quibus creberrime impetor, praenunciant. Nam et Catharri graves, et pristinus dolor ventriculi, et arenulae, et alij dolores varij, quasi agmine facto in me irruunt, mirifice affligunt et divexant. Addo, me propemodum attingere limites Tympanitis. Sed, ut dixi, ista non expavesco, ut qui sciam Dei consilio et voluntate omnia fieri. Deo me totum commendo, eundemque oro, facere velit, ut reliquum vitae ei soli vivam in timore, expectans cum alacritate diem discessus mei. Itaque iam omnibus alijs studijs, maiori ex parte, nuncium mitto, uni illi studio intentus, ut bene mori addiscam.

Literas, inquam, illas tuas, quas D. CURTIO me dedisse legendas dixi, morbo illo diuturno impeditus, nondum recipere potui. Hodie, qui est dies ultimus Novembris novo stylo, cum hasce postremas tuas scriptas 28 Septembris a tabellario Magdeburgensi cum scripto illo acerbo sane tui istius fastuosi, praefracti, mordacis et malesani antagonistae accepissem, una et altera pagina perlecta, et obiter perlustrato eo scripto, altero mox die, et epistolam tuam et scriptum illud legenda dedi D. CURTIO, et profecto fuerunt ei admodum grata, quod de te iam diu ne verbum quidem. Postera die, quae fuit 2 Decembris, ad ipsum redeo, ut eius sententiam de scripto illo audirem (nam tabellarius Magdeburgensis discessum parabat, quem noluissem vacuum meis literis redire illuc). Is primum iubet te salvere plurimum, tibique significare scriptum illud tui Scoti non inniti ullis firmis rationibus. Omnem doctrinam parallaxium suis quibusdam phantasmatibus prorsus tollere velle, hostile, non amicum scriptum esse. Itaque censere, omnino abs te refutandum esse. Dixit etiam se illud scriptum accuratius perlecturum esse. Ego quoque, dum modo firmiori sim futurus valetudine, diligenter illud perlegam, deque eo plenius cum D. CURTIO conferam. Haec dum scribo, subijt in mentem Scotus ille, subtilis doctor dictus, quod de eo referant scriptores, fuisse a discipulis confossum stylis pugillaribus. Ego, tuum hunc antagonistam Scotum, quod admirandam et praeclaram Astronomicae doctrinae partem de parallaxibus, suis sophismatis penitus evertere cupiat, ab omnibus studiosis Astronomiae, Baculis Iacobaeis, Gnomonibus et Circinis multo iustius illo subigendum et confodiendum esse censerem. Hoc tu solus, cui nec facultas nec voluntas deest, calamo felicissime praestare poteris. B.V. Datae Pragae die 2. Decemb. novo stylo, Anno D. 1592.

Privilegium Caesareum emendatum, hodie mihi a D. CURTIO traditum, tibi mitto.

THAD.

To the most noble Master Tycho Brahe the Dane, lord of Uraniborg etc., his master and friend to be esteemed — greeting.

Most noble sir, my master and friend to be esteemed. On a single day in the month of February of this year several letters of yours were delivered to me, these written at various times; and when I had read them through, I gave them also to our Master Curtius to read. From that very day I have passed all this time up to now in ill health: at the outset, to be sure, a huge and intolerable flatulent pain of the stomach kept recurring intermittently. Afterwards, when it had lasted for several days, and had again let up — sometimes for 14 days, sometimes for 20 — at last, after a most grievous attack, it broke out into jaundice [Ictericia], which after some days brought on a continuous fever. This [fever], joined with the jaundice, afflicted me for three months, namely August, September, and October. The hope of recovery was slight, whether one considered the nature and course of either malady, or the body that was its subject. Physicians, certainly, are accustomed to forebode ill for that patient in whom jaundice follows upon a fever. And now, besides these, there has come on the old age of 66 years. What, then, could I hope concerning the recovery of health? Meanwhile I have not shuddered at death: nay, I would most gladly have laid down this poor body, had it pleased the divine will. For I am now thoroughly weary of this wretched life; I see too that great calamities hang over our homeland — of which would that I were not a spectator. But although by the goodness of God alone I have been freed from a hard sickness, yet, my Tycho, the strength of my poor body has been so shaken that I readily understand it will never return. And that it will be from time to time the more feeble, various symptoms, by which I am very frequently assailed, foretell. For grievous catarrhs, and the old pain of the stomach, and gravel [arenulae], and various other pains rush upon me as though in a marshalled column, afflict me wondrously, and torment me. I add that I am all but reaching the borders of tympanites [dropsy of the belly]. But, as I have said, I do not take fright at these things, as one who knows that all things come to pass by God's counsel and will. I commend my whole self to God, and beseech him that he will be willing to grant that I may live the remainder of my life for him alone, in [his] fear, awaiting with eagerness the day of my departure. And so now, for the greater part, I send word of dismissal to all other studies, intent upon that one study alone: to learn well how to die.

Those letters of yours, I say, which I told you I had given to Master Curtius to read, I have not yet been able to recover, hindered by that long sickness. Today, which is the last day of November by the new style, when I had received this latest letter of yours, written on 28 September, from the Magdeburg courier — together with that truly bitter screed of that haughty, headstrong, biting, and ill-minded antagonist of yours — and had read through one page and another, and looked over that screed in passing, then on the very next day I gave both your letter and that screed to Master Curtius to read; and they were assuredly most welcome to him, since for a long time now [there had been] not even a word about you. On the following day, which was 2 December, I return to him, that I might hear his opinion of that screed (for the Magdeburg courier was making ready to depart, and I should not have wished him to return thither empty of my letters). He first bids you a hearty good-day, and [bids me] signify to you that that writing of your Scot rests upon no firm reasonings; that to wish to overthrow the whole doctrine of parallaxes utterly by certain phantasms of his own is the writing of an enemy, not a friend; and so he judges that it must by all means be refuted by you. He said too that he would read that writing through more carefully. I also, provided I shall be in firmer health, will read it through diligently, and will confer about it more fully with Master Curtius. While I write this, there has come into my mind that famous Scotus, called the Subtle Doctor — what the writers report of him, that he was stabbed through by his pupils with writing-styluses [stylis pugillaribus]. As for me, I should judge that this Scot, your antagonist — because he desires to overthrow utterly, by his sophisms, that admirable and excellent part of astronomical doctrine concerning parallaxes — ought far more justly than that other one to be subdued and stabbed through by all the students of Astronomy, with cross-staffs [Baculi Iacobaei], gnomons, and compasses [Circini]. This you alone, in whom neither the ability nor the will is wanting, will be able to accomplish most happily with your pen. Fare you well. Given at Prague, 2 December by the new style, in the year of the Lord 1592.

The amended Imperial privilege, handed to me today by Master Curtius, I send to you.

THAD.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Thaddaeus Hagecius = Tadeáš Hájek z Hájku (Thaddaeus Hagecius ab Hayck, 1525–1600), the Bohemian court physician, astronomer, and alchemist at Prague — Emperor Rudolf II's protomedicus. Tycho's long-standing scientific ally and his chief conduit to the Imperial court; the observer of the 1572 nova whose Dialexis (1574) Tycho read closely. It was Hagecius who, after this correspondence, helped open the way to Tycho's reception by Rudolf II in 1599.
  • D. CURTIUS — Jacobus Curtius (Jakob Kurz von Senftenau, d. 1594), Imperial Aulic vice-chancellor (Reichsvizekanzler) under Rudolf II and an accomplished mathematician-patron in Prague. He acts here as a learned referee of Tycho's dispute and as the channel for the Privilegium Caesareum (the Imperial printing privilege Tycho was seeking).
  • The "Scot" / "Scotus." tui Scoti … tuum hunc antagonistam Scotum = "your Scot … this Scot, your antagonist." The antagonist is John Craig (Johannes Craig / Craigius), the Scottish physician and Aristotelian who disputed Tycho's denial of the celestial spheres and, in particular, attacked Tycho's parallax-based proof that the comets and the 1572 nova lay beyond the Moon. Hagecius exploits a sustained pun on "Scotus": the antagonist is literally the Scot (Craig), but the name also summons Johannes Duns Scotus, the doctor subtilis. Hagecius recalls the (apocryphal) tale that Duns Scotus was stabbed to death by his pupils with their styli (writing-styluses), and turns it into a learned witticism: Craig deserves, even more justly, to be "stabbed through" by astronomers — not with styluses but with the instruments of the science he assails. The wordplay is preserved, not flattened.
  • scriptum illud acerbo … antagonistae — "that bitter screed … of [your] antagonist": Craig's polemical writing against Tycho, which Tycho had forwarded with his own letter of 28 September. Curtius's verdict — that it "rests upon no firm reasonings" and "must by all means be refuted" — is reported, not endorsed by Hagecius as his own settled judgement (he reserves his fuller reading for when his health allows); the hedge is kept.
  • Astronomy. doctrina parallaxium / de parallaxibus → the doctrine of parallaxes / concerning parallaxes — the crux of the whole exchange: parallax measurement was Tycho's instrument for placing comets and the nova in the supralunary heavens, and Craig's phantasmata ("phantasms," i.e. baseless fancies) sought to dissolve it.
  • Instruments. Baculis Iacobaeis = cross-staffs (radius astronomicus / Jacob's staff; the edition's convention); Gnomonibus = gnomons; Circinis = compasses (a pair of compasses / dividers — here the draughtsman's-and-observer's instrument, not a magnetic compass). The trio names the working tools of the practical astronomer, weaponised in the conceit.
  • Medicine. dolor ventriculi flatulentus → flatulent pain of the stomach; Ictericia / Icterus → jaundice; febris continua → continuous (continual) fever; Catharri → catarrhs; arenulae → gravel (renal/urinary grit — period term, kept); Tympanitis → tympanites (drum-tight tympanitic dropsy of the abdomen; cf. hydrops, the edition's convention) — "I am all but reaching the borders of tympanites," i.e. approaching that fatal condition. The clinical sequence (recurrent gastric pain → jaundice supervening on fever → bad prognosis) is rendered without modernising the nosology.
  • bene mori addiscam — "to learn well how to die" (the ars moriendi topos); the rhetorical pivot of the opening section, kept as the sententia it is.
  • patriae nostrae magnas impendere calamitates — "great calamities hang over our homeland": Hagecius's foreboding for Bohemia (the gathering religious-political tensions of the 1590s); reported as his apprehension, not as fact.
  • Closing formulae. S. = Salutem ("greeting," the edition's convention); B.V. = Bene Vale ("Fare you well"); Datae Pragae = "Given at Prague"; THAD. = the writer's abbreviated signature (Thaddaeus).
  • Privilegium Caesareum. "The amended Imperial privilege" — the corrected/renewed Imperial printing privilege for Tycho's works, transmitted via Curtius; a concrete fruit of Hagecius's court brokerage.
  • Calendar / dating crux (flagged, not resolved). The letter is New Style throughout (Hagecius, in Catholic Imperial Prague, writes Gregorian; Tycho on Hven and his Magdeburg courier were on the Julian Old Style — Tycho's enclosed letter is dated 28 September, presumably O.S.). Hagecius's own narrative carries an internal inconsistency: he writes "Hodie, qui est dies ultimus Novembris novo stylo" ("Today, which is the last day of November, n.s.") = 30 November; says he gave the documents to Curtius "altero mox die" (the very next day); then "Postera die, quae fuit 2 Decembris, ad ipsum redeo" ("On the following day, which was 2 December, I return to him"); and dates the letter "die 2. Decemb. novo stylo." From 30 November, "the next day" is 1 December and "the following day" 2 December — so the two intermediate steps span 30 Nov → 1 Dec → 2 Dec, which is internally coherent only if "altero mox die" and "Postera die" denote the same following day described twice (30 Nov + 2 = 2 Dec leaves a one-day gap if read as three distinct consecutive days). Per the edition's convention the dateline is reproduced as written and the discrepancy is recorded, not reconciled: the header date follows the source's own subscription, 2 December 1592 N.S., matching the catalogue label; the narrative day-count is flagged here. A second minor discrepancy: Hagecius gives his age as "annorum 66," though a 1525 birth-year makes him 67 in late 1592 — reproduced as written, not corrected.
