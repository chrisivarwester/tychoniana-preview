Replies in hendecasyllabic verse cast as prose, expressing delight at Pratensis's letter and lamenting the displacement of true learning by scholastic barbarism.

Editorial note. The hendecasyllabic poem that the rubric announces — absent from the working transcription when this file was first made — has now been recovered and supplied from Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII pp. 9–10, taken from the digitised text. Long-s misreadings are silently normalised (suêre→fuêre, srugum→frugum); a stray footnote-anchor caret after Monstrabit is dropped; one genuinely uncertain word is flagged in the Apparatus (the subject is in "quas nunc is … intulêre Musis", where the plural verb wants a plural subject — ii/hi). The poem is a warm verse-letter answering Pratensis's of 7 May 1572: a humanist exhortation to scorn scholastic "Barbarism" and climb to the study of Earth and Heaven, then practical business — a prospective alchemical assistant ("that undaunted Dane"), the playfully mythologised delay in delivering felled timber ("despoiling the Nymphs," forbidden by Ceres until the harvest rites are done), and a request for distilling cucurbits from the potter Andreas.

The original

Sequitur mea responsio, quae carmine constat hendecasyllabico instar prosae consignato.

DOCTORI MEDICO JOHANNI PRATENSI, INPRIMISQUE SUO COLENDO AMICO. VULCANUM, URANIAM, JOVEM FAVENTES.

Servus noster, ut est domum reversus, asportavit epistolam remissam a te, care Pratensis, qua relecta, dici vix ego credo posse, quanta nobis hinc animi fuit voluptas. Namque haec abditiore visa vere est rerum cognitione plena, plusque antiquae sophiae tenere, quam nunc suetum est (proh dolor) in scholis doceri. Sed nemo tamen invidebit illis, spero, Barbariem merasque nugas, quas nunc dulcibus intulêre Musis. Namque hunc quis revocabit? quis reducet? Monstrabitne viam, suapte si quis mavult sponte via carere vera. Sed tu, docte Pratensis, nil moratus illos (ut facis) altiora scande, et Terrae simul et Poli supernos perlustrans aditus labore suavi, vulgo subtrahe te caduca temnens. Major gloria namque rebus altis debetur, nec habent pusilla laudem. Quid cessas? remoras habere nullas mens prognata polo potest, sed illa sublimis penetrat domos Deorum, libans Ambrosiam et suave nectar, quod dijs Jupiter ipse miscet, et dijs (si qui sunt) genitis simul ministrat etc. Sed cur digredior, mororve multis? Praestat nunc, ut epistolae tuae remittam responsum, brevibus quod his habeto.

Imperterritus ille Danus ad me quamprimum fuerit reversus illinc, mox se conferat huc iubeto, huncque praesentem cupio videre praesens, et cognoscere num sit aptus ille nostris moribus, et quid ipse sese facturum statuat, velitne furnis inservire meis fovens labores Vulcani et studijs vacare nostris, mecumque perque aliquod manere tempus. Tum demum statuam, quid inde fiet.

De Nymphis etiam scias, Pratensis, illas me spoliasse sat revera Alnis quercubus et suis capillis. Verum quod spolia illa non fuêre ad vos hactenus, ut decet, relata, in causa est Dea quae vocatur Alma, et frugum genetrix Ceres, quod illa cernens prae foribus suos labores instare, agricolas iuvare causam Nympharum vetuit, simul minata, se non propitiam futuram in illud tempus, quando solet venire messis, si Nymphas colerent diebus illis, quos sacros Cereri dedit vetustas. Coguntur miseri parere Divae, quae fructus tribuit Soli quotannis. Silvestres potius volunt vocare Nymphas, quam Cereris carere donis. Ast quando fuerint peracta sacra, quae nunc frugiferae Deae celebrant, Nymphas agricolae iuvare debent, vel si forte alicui interesse sacris (ut fit) displicet, otiosus ille Nympharum spolijs onustus ad vos mittetur, faciam ut ferat ferenda. Sed nunc scribere plura vix vacabit. Ergo care Pratensis, valeto. Et cum sis medicus, valere semper ut possis, facile est: saluta amicos. Fac me posse cucurbitas habere, quas vestrum figulum parare nobis (ut spero) impiger Andreas procurat. Dicas huic etiam precor salutem. Herrevadi, 17. Maij Anno 1572.

Tycho Brahe tibi dicatus.

English translation machine translation, Secure

There follows my reply, which consists of a hendecasyllabic poem set down in the manner of prose.

To the Doctor of Medicine Johannes Pratensis, and most especially to his cherished friend. May Vulcan, Urania, and Jove be favorable.

Our servant, as soon as he had got home, brought the letter sent back by you, dear Pratensis; and when I had read it over, I scarcely think it can be told how great a delight of mind it gave us. For it seemed truly full of a more hidden knowledge of things, and to hold more of the ancient wisdom than it is now the custom (alas!) to teach in the schools. Yet no one, I hope, will envy those [schools] the Barbarism and the mere trifles which [they] have now brought in upon the sweet Muses. For who will call it back? who will lead it back again? Does he [only] point out the road [to ruin] who, of his own will, prefers to do without the true way? But you, learned Pratensis — heeding them not at all (as is your wont) — climb to higher things, and, surveying with sweet toil the upper approaches alike of Earth and of Heaven, withdraw yourself from the crowd, scorning the things that perish. For greater glory is owed to lofty matters, and little things win no praise. Why do you hold back? A mind born of heaven can brook no hindrance, but, sublime, it pierces to the homes of the Gods, sipping Ambrosia and sweet nectar — which Jupiter himself mixes for the gods, and serves at the same time to the gods (if any there be) [that are] begotten — and so on. But why do I digress and delay with many words? Better now that I send back the answer to your letter, which take in these few [lines].

As soon as that undaunted Dane has come back to me from there, bid him betake himself hither at once; and I desire, myself present, to see him in person, and to learn whether he is suited to our ways, and what he resolves to do — whether he is willing to tend my furnaces, fostering the labors of Vulcan, and to give himself to our studies, and to stay with me for some space of time. Then at last I shall decide what is to come of it.

About the Nymphs, too, you should know, Pratensis, that I have indeed despoiled them well enough of their alders and oaks and their tresses [their leafage]. But that those spoils have not yet been delivered to you, as is fitting, the cause is the Goddess called Alma, and Ceres, mother of the crops: for she, seeing her own labors pressing at the door [the harvest near], forbade the farmers to assist the Nymphs' cause, threatening at the same time that she would not be favorable at that season when the harvest is wont to come, if they should court the Nymphs on the days antiquity made sacred to Ceres. The poor wretches are forced to obey the Goddess, who grants the soil its fruits each year; they would sooner summon the woodland Nymphs [later] than go without Ceres' gifts. But once the rites are accomplished that they now celebrate for the fruit-bearing Goddess, the farmers must aid the Nymphs; or if it happens to displease anyone to take part in the rites (as happens), that idle fellow, laden with the Nymphs' spoils, shall be sent to you — I will see that he carries what is to be carried. But now there will scarcely be leisure to write more. So, dear Pratensis, farewell. And since you are a physician, it is easy for you to be able to be always well: greet our friends. See that I can get the cucurbits [gourd-shaped distilling vessels] which your potter — the tireless Andreas, I hope — is seeing to make for us. Bid him too, I pray, good health. Herrevad, 17 May, in the year 1572.

Tycho Brahe, devoted to you.

Apparatus

  • Sender. Tycho Brahe (1546–1601), writing from Herrevad in May 1572, before De nova stella and his establishment on Hven; in this period resident at Herrevad Abbey with his maternal uncle Steen Bille, already deep in alchemy (the "labors of Vulcan," the furnaces and cucurbits below).
  • Recipient. Johannes Pratensis = Hans Philipsen / Johannes Pratensis (1543–1576), Tycho's physician-friend at Copenhagen (later professor of medicine there), one of his closest correspondents in 1572 and, per Tycho Brahe and Prague (2002), "the first to hear Tycho's ideas on the nova of 1572".
  • Place. Herrevad — Herrevad Abbey (Herrevadskloster) in Scania, where Tycho was then living and working with Steen Bille; kept as is by the edition's convention (Dreyer's page prints the dateline place as the garbled "Herizuad" = Herrevad.)
  • Occasion. Tycho's reply to a letter of Johannes Pratensis dated 7 May 1572 (Copenhagen, = idx brahe-t_15720507001). The two were actively exchanging Latin verse as letter-content in these weeks, months before the Carmen ad fratrem was printed.
  • carmen hendecasyllabicum … instar prosae consignato — "a hendecasyllabic poem set down in the manner of prose": the reply is in eleven-syllable (Phalaecian) verse, but written out continuously, not laid out line-by-line as verse. This is a distinct poem from the Carmen ad fratrem ejus gemellum of 1572 (which is in dactylic distichs); the rubric is Dreyer's, from cod. Vindob. lat. 10686.66 fol. 40r. The translation follows the as-prose layout.
  • The superscription. VULCANUM, URANIAM, JOVEM FAVENTES — "(invoking) Vulcan, Urania, and Jove favorable": the three patrons frame the letter's three strands — Vulcan (the alchemical furnace-work), Urania (astronomy), Jove (good fortune/friendship). A characteristic Tychonic sign-off flourish in embryo.
  • The humanist exhortation. The opening movement praises Pratensis's letter as full of "more hidden knowledge" and "ancient wisdom," and contrasts it with the "Barbarism and strange trifles" of the contemporary "schools" — Tycho urging his friend to "climb to higher things" and survey "Earth and Heaven," scorning the perishable crowd. The Neoplatonic image of the heaven-born mind "piercing to the homes of the Gods, sipping Ambrosia and nectar" is the period commonplace, rendered without sharpening.
  • The "undaunted Dane." A prospective alchemical assistant — Tycho asks that the young man, once back, come to Herrevad so Tycho can judge whether he is "willing to tend my furnaces, fostering the labors of Vulcan, and give himself to our studies." Concrete evidence of Tycho's 1572 alchemical establishment and his recruiting for it.
  • The Nymphs / Ceres allegory (the literary centrepiece). A practical matter — the delivery of felled timber (alders and oaks, "the Nymphs' tresses") — is dressed as myth: the spoils cannot yet be sent because Ceres (the harvest-goddess, here also Alma) has forbidden the farmers to work for the woodland Nymphs during the days "antiquity made sacred to Ceres," on pain of a poor harvest; once the harvest rites are done, the timber will come. A deliberate superimposition of literal timber-clearing on a mythological-religious conceit, rendered faithfully rather than flattened to its prose sense.
  • cucurbitas — cucurbits: gourd-shaped earthenware distilling vessels (the lower vessel of an alembic), to be made by the potter Andreas. Another alchemical-apparatus detail, consistent with the furnace-work above. figulum = potter.
  • Textual note (poem now supplied). The hendecasyllabic poem, absent from the working transcription, is supplied from Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII pp. 9–10 (cod. Vindob. lat. 10686.7, apograph; with corrections from 10686.66) and taken from the digitised text. One genuine reading-crux remains: in "Barbariem mirasque nugas, quas nunc is … intulêre Musis" the plural verb intulêre wants a plural subject, so the transmitted is is probably ii/hi ("they," i.e. the schools/barbarians); flagged [ii?], not silently emended. Long-s misreadings (suêre→fuêre, srugum→frugum) are normalised and declared.
  • Calendar. 17 May 1572, Old Style (Julian); Denmark kept O.S. until 1700. The dateline is internal to the poem (Herrevad, 17 May 1572) and matches the edition label.
  • Correction. Four Latin corruptions in the hendecasyllabic poem corrected per Dreyer, Opera Omnia VII pp.9-10: a corrupted "mirasque" plus a phantom bracketed "is [ii?]" restored to "merasque" (with the phantom word removed); "Monstrabit" restored to the interrogative "Monstrabitne"; "siet" restored to "fiet"; and "(ut sit)" restored to "(ut fit)". English corrected to match at three of the four places where sense was affected: "strange" to "mere" trifles, the flat statement recast as a rhetorical question, and "(as may be)" to "(as happens)"; the fiet/siet correction needed no English change since the existing rendering already carried the future sense.

The translation and its checking

Grade Secure · collated against Dreyer's page images, Opera Omnia VII pp. 9–10; defects the audit noted: text error (4) (the translator's own grade before the audit was Probable).

Collated against Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia (the edition of record), reading the printed page images. Dreyer TBOO VII pp.9-10 read as page images, with particular attention to the hendecasyllabic poem per the audit's note that it was restored by an earlier pass. The restoration does NOT hold up clean: four word-level corruptions cluster inside the poem itself (merasque->mirasque plus a phantom 'is [ii?]' insertion; the dropped -ne on Monstrabitne; fiet->siet; ut fit->ut sit), while the prose second half of the letter (the Danish assistant, the Nymphs passage, the cucurbits, the closing 'Herrevadi/Herizuad. 17. Maij Anno 1572. TYCHO BRAHE tibi dicatus.') matches Dreyer exactly. Individually each defect is low/medium severity and none invents a fact wholesale, but their concentration in precisely the passage flagged for verification means the 'restored' poem cannot be certified clean — hence serious and grade C rather than a pass.

4 defects found:
- text error (medium). Dreyer's poem reads 'merasque' (mere/sheer trifles) with no 'is'/'ii' present at all; our transcribed original corrupts the word to 'mirasque' (strange/wondrous) and inserts a phantom, editorially-flagged 'is [ii?]' that is simply not in Dreyer. The English follows the corrupted Latin ('strange' rather than 'mere'). Dreyer prints: Barbariem merasque nugas, quas nunc dulcibus intulêre Musis.
- text error (medium). Dreyer's poem has the interrogative enclitic -ne (Will he show the way...?); our transcribed original drops it, turning a rhetorical question into a flat statement. Dreyer prints: Monstrabitne viam.
- text error (low). Dreyer prints 'fiet' (it will become/happen, future indicative of fio); the transcribed original has 'siet' (an archaic subjunctive of esse), a different word. Dreyer prints: quid inde fiet.
- text error (low). Dreyer prints 'ut fit' (as happens/is the case); the transcribed original has 'ut sit' (subjunctive of esse), changing the parenthetical's grammatical mood and licensing the looser English 'as may be' rather than 'as happens'. Dreyer prints: (ut fit).

Repaired. All four defects verified directly against Dreyer TBOO VII pp.9-10 page images (not the digitised text layer): merafque (l.14), Monftrabitne (l.16), fiet (l.30 p.9), and (ut fit) (p.10 l.3) all read exactly as Dreyer's claim states. No other part of the letter was touched; every changed word is accounted for in outcomes above.
