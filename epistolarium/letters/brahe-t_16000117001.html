TYCHO BRAHE TO MARTIN BACHAZEK.

Greeting.

Most excellent and most learned Lord Master Martin Bachazek, friend to be honoured: from your last letter to me I perceived, not obscurely, your devotion to me and singular goodwill — both that you lately received so kindly Master Nicolaus, my fellow-countryman, and that you were willing to further, with your counsel too, the business to be expedited in France in my name by my cousin Claus Bille; and also that you procure for me some fair copies of the Poems entrusted to you, in which you rightly conjectured that a certain Epigram wrongly had "TUTO" for "TYCHO." For it ought, as you aptly restored it, to close thus at the end:

"By your money, Rudolf; by your labour, Tycho."

And if you have found any other such slips of the scribe, you will do well to correct them. But since one kindness allures another, and I see that you favour my countrymen — especially if, supported by my commendation, they come to you — I could not omit to recommend to you, by this letter, a young Dane who, three days ago, accompanied hither (from Wittenberg, where he had studied a while) my elder son — whom I had sent into Denmark to bring away the instruments remaining there, and to convey them first to Lübeck, then to Hamburg, which was done. For he greatly desired to be introduced to you, and asked me on this account, having understood that there is no common familiarity between us, and that I esteem you highly, as is fitting; wherefore I could not but grant his wishes. That you may know in few [words] who and what he is, take it thus: he is sprung from that part of Denmark which others call the Cimbric Chersonese, [and] our countrymen call Jutland — in the chief city there, Ribe, which has a cathedral church and a famous and rich chapter of Canons, where also his father holds the chief office and acts as Superintendent, in place of the old Bishops — a man exceedingly learned, and known to me by old acquaintance, who is called Master Peter Hegelund. This son, John by name, devoted diligent effort to letters a while in the University of Copenhagen, and afterward continued his liberal studies at Wittenberg with no less assiduity, making in them, for his age, an excellent and praiseworthy progress; and he is moreover of honourable and composed manners, so that there is no doubtful hope that he will, by God's grace, attain to some illustrious fruit. Wherefore, my excellent Bachazek, I ask you again and again to hold this young Dane commended to you "of the better mark" (as they say), and to aid his endeavours, and promote him to every best thing by your counsel. You will thus do me a thing exceedingly welcome, and bind me — otherwise most loving of you — on many counts. He would wish to stay a while at Prague, that he may weave to its end the web of his studies, begun, in your College, and learn the manners of the people there, and so successively pave a way for himself and prepare the means of travelling further, and perhaps of traversing Italy. Give heed therefore, most loving Lord Bachazek, that he may rather dwell there with you conveniently, and — if a convenient place be [open] — in your College, so that he need not spend too much of his money (which he needs for travelling further), but may live frugally, yet so that necessaries be not lacking, and at the same time be free for the humaner studies. About which he himself will set out his view to you more fully; and I, when I am present with you (God favouring) — which I hope will be as soon as the Imperial Majesty has returned to Prague — shall speak with you further, both of these and other matters. Meanwhile bring it about that I may learn that this our intercession has had weight with you (of which I doubt nothing); and send the copies of the Poems, as many as you have transcribed; and continue to love me back, most devoted to you.

Farewell.
Given in haste from the Venetian castle, 17 January, the year 1600.

[Postscript:] POSTSCRIPT. There has arrived, with my son, from the Baltic shore, another of my countrymen, Master Christian Longomontanus by name, summoned hither by me: for he had already before, while I dwelt in Denmark, been a member of my household on our island of Uraniborg for eight years, and had rendered me diligent service in the Astronomical Exercises. But when the rites of Urania were there disturbed, through the ignorance and malevolence of certain men, he — I being unwilling to look on so great ruins, but withdrawing thence with my whole household into Germany — departed likewise, and with our good leave first sailed into Prussia. Afterwards, having traversed Poland, he came through Silesia and the rest of the more southerly part of Germany to Rostock; where, when he had learned that I had meanwhile settled for a while at the Rantzau castle near Hamburg, he came to me of his own accord, and having stayed there with me some time — since meanwhile I was summoned to Prague by the Imperial Majesty, and a little after undertook the journey into these regions — he accompanied me to Magdeburg. And when I was sending back my Gynaeceum [the household women], which until then I had kept with me, to Wandsbek, until I should ascertain the most clement will of the Imperial Majesty by an excursion to Prague (which excursion, however, was then hindered by a plague raging widely through Bohemia), I joined him to the Gynaeceum, that he might more safely conduct it back, since his fidelity and devotion toward me had long been clear to me. And when he had rightly carried this out, he travelled somewhat into the Cimbric land, his fatherland, and afterwards, having returned to Rostock, there awaited the outcome of my affairs in Bohemia. But now, with my son, summoned (as I said) anew, he has appeared before me again. And although I had resolved to make further use of his work in the Astronomical studies here at Benátky, yet — since I do not yet have all things duly arranged here, and have besides a numerous household, and a certain Mathematician of the Elector of Brandenburg with his wife is daily expected (for whom the Emperor has written), so that this house assigned to me can scarcely conveniently hold more — and since the aforesaid Master Christian himself does not refuse to live at Prague and diligently to carry out thence the things I shall signify for him to do in Astronomical matters: this plan therefore pleased me too, that he should live there at my expense, until I provide otherwise for him, and labour on my behalf at what I shall prescribe to be continued in this kind of studies. Wherefore I again refer this matter to your aid together with your counsel, and commit all things to your prudence and fidelity, and most diligently commend to you the man — who has always been dear and acceptable to me — asking you by our friendship that you be willing to provide for him some fitting convenience, both as to lodging and as to victuals, whether this can be suitably done in your College or elsewhere nearby; and to apprise me of each particular through the same man, who within a day or two will return hither with my carriage. You will thus do a thing especially welcome, to be repaid in turn by any service, so far as lies in my power. Farewell. Given as before.

Apparatus

- Recipient. Martin Bachazek (Martin Bacháček z Nauměřic, 1539/41–1612), mathematician and Rector of the Charles University (Carolinum / Collegium) at Prague — Tycho's Prague academic contact. The MS is in Basel.
- Significance — a student-recommendation, and the famous "Rudolf/Tycho" distich. Two parts:
1. Thanks & the epigram correction. Tycho thanks Bachazek for receiving "Master Nicolaus, my fellow-countryman" (a Dane of Tycho's circle — probably Claus Mule/Nicolaus Mulaeus), for furthering his cousin Claus Bille's business in France, and for fair-copying commendatory Poems. Bachazek had spotted a scribal error — "TUTO" for "TYCHO" — and rightly restored the closing pentameter, which Tycho quotes: "Aere Rudolphe tuo, Tycho labore tuo" — "By your money, Rudolf; by your labour, Tycho" — the neat epigram of the imperial patronage (Rudolf's purse, Tycho's toil), kept in Latin and translated.
2. The recommendation of Johannes Hegelund. Tycho commends a young Dane who had accompanied his elder son (Tycho the Younger, back from the Denmark instrument-errand) from Wittenberg: John Hegelund, son of Peter Hegelund (Peder Jensen Hegelund, 1542–1614, Superintendent of Ribe — the Lutheran successor of the old bishops — "known to me by old acquaintance"), of Ribe in Jutland (the Cimbrica Chersonesus). Having studied at Copenhagen and Wittenberg, he wishes to continue at the Prague Collegium, then perhaps travel to Italy; Tycho asks Bachazek to lodge him in the College so he may live frugally, and promises to speak more once back at Prague.
- Persons named. Martin Bachazek; Claus Mule (M. Nicolaus, probable); Claus Bille (Tycho's cousin); Peter Hegelund (Superintendent of Ribe) and his son John Hegelund; Rudolf II (in the distich); Tycho's elder son (Tycho the Younger).
- Place-name pun. ex arce Venetiensi — "from the Venetian castle": Benátky nad Jizerou (whose Czech name Benátky means "Venice") is here Latinised as Venetia — a learned pun for Benátky, not the Italian Venice. Rendered "the castle of Benátky," with the pun noted.
- Calendar. Dateline 17 Ian. A. 1600; New Style (imperial). Header agrees with the label.

  • Plague delayed Tycho's own trip to Prague. Recovers the reason Tycho did not himself go to ascertain the Emperor's will in person, but instead sent Longomontanus back with the Gynaeceum to Wandsbek: an outbreak of plague then "raging widely" through Bohemia made the excursion inadvisable.
  • Corroborates the plague chronology elsewhere in the edition. Dovetails with the 3 August 1600 Rosenkrantz letter's own account of "the plague, with the Emperor's Court dispersed for almost a whole year" — the two letters describe the same episode from different points in its course, sharpening the timeline of Tycho's 1599 movements between Wandsbek, Magdeburg and Prague.
