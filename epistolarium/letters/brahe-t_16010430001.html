The original

SERENISSIMO ET POTENTISSIMO PRINCIPI AC DOMINO, DOMINO FERDINANDO MAGNO DUCI ETRURIÆ ETC. DOMINO SUO CLEMENTISSIMO. FLORENTZ.

AD Serenissimam Celsitudinem Vestram ante Anni quadrantem per Filium meum natu maiorem, Legationem Persicam in Italiam comitantem, submisse dedi literas, quas Sereniss. Celsni Væ recte esse redditas ex Epistola eiusdem Filij nuperrime ad me data lubenter cognovi. Scribit is inibi nonnulla, quæ animum meum non parum perculerunt: Legatum nimirum Persicum illum primarium natione Anglum, in cuius familia Filius meus tunc fuit, cum Senas pervenisset, ipsum ad se seorsim vocasse, atque hæc insinuasse: Serenissimam Celsnem Vam illi videlicet Legato Anglo, cum ex literis ad ipsam a me scriptis intellexisset, Filium meum in ipsius comitatu esse, dissuasisse, ut illum Romam secum adduceret, præsertim illo tempore, id enim non absque gravi periculo vitæ Filij, et ipsiusmet Legati impedimento fieri posse, siquidem meum nomen in illis oris iam antea satis innotuisset, et potissimum ea de causa periculum subesse, quod in Italia rumor increbuisset, Sacræ Cæsareæ Mati me authorem et suasorem fuisse, ne Monachos Capucinianos hîc Pragæ diutius pateretur, sed eos dimitteret. Voluit itaque Legatus ille Anglus, ut Filius meus Senis remaneret, nec Romam unâ accederet, quod et factum est. Si itaque hæc, ut ille retulit, ex Sereniss. Celsnis Væ consilio et admonitione facta sunt, non dubito, quin clementissimo erga me et Filium meum animo et bona ac sincera intentione profecta sint. Sin autem Legatus iste ex suo cerebro talia deprompsit, et sub prætextu nominis Væ Saæ Celsnis (uti suspicor) protulit, facile conijcio, eum id fecisse, ut sic honeste a Filio meo liberaretur, ne illi ulterius oneri esset, siquidem, uti hîc Pragæ satis cognovimus, viatico pro tam magnifica legatione et tanto itinere emetiendo non satis instructus erat: sed sit quicquid velit, gaudeo Filium meum Senis in Sereniss. Væ Celsitudinis ditione et urbe commorari, ubi eum commode degere, et satis tutum fore, omnino mihi persuadeo, cum Væ Sereniss. Celsnis Regio sit per se libera, et ijs, quæ forte alibi in Italia incidere possent, periculis non obnoxia, ideoque a Sereniss. Celsne Va, qua possum submissione et reverentia peto, velit sibi Filium hunc meum commendatum habere non dedignari, et si res ipsius ita tulerint, ut tam diu in Væ Sereniss. Celsnis territorio subsistere possit, donec Linguam Italicam mediocriter addidicerit, id equidem mihi apprime acceptum foret; quo peracto, ut Sereniss. Celso Va eum Sereniss. et Potentissimo Rege Galliarum, inclyto suo Affini, per literas commendare dignetur, quo in ipsius Aula per unum vel alterum annum, donec et illam linguam calluerit, pro servitore recipiatur, demisse et officiosissime eam rogatam vellem: quicquid hac vel alia ratione Sereniss. Celso Va in Filium meum contulerit clementissimi beneficij, id tanquam in me ipsum collatum perpetua animi submissione et gratitudine, quantum vires meæ patientur, agnoscam.

Cæterum quod Monachos Capucinianos attinet, certo sciat, atque indubitate Sereniss. Celso Va statuat, quod nunquam vel minima cogitatione mihi in mentem venerit apud Sacram Cæsaream Maiestatem, Dominum meum Clementissimum, quidpiam de ijs loqui, aut an hîc manere debeant vel secus, ullo verbo mentionem facere, nedum ut quidpiam in his eius Maiestati suggererem aut consulerem, cum talia ad me plane non pertineant, nec ipsius Maiestas unquam de eiuscemodi rebus, quæ Ecclesiasticas Personas aut res concernunt, mecum loquatur, sed solummodo de ijs, quæ Philosophica et Astronomica, quibus ipsius Maiestas, sicuti multi alij Reges et Principes, laudabiliter afficitur, nec ego me alienis immisceo: vix suppetit otium et commoditas propria tractandi et exequendi. DEUM itaque sancte et conscientiam meam intemerate testari possum, magnam mihi hac in parte fieri iniuriam, tam hîc quam alibi, uti etiam in quibusdam alijs calumnijs, quæ ab imperitis seu mearum rerum ignaris hîc sparsæ sunt, de quibus brevi ad ipsius Maiestatem, quæ me in omnibus ijs, quæ apud ipsius Maiestatem a me dicta et peracta esse nonnulli comminiscuntur, insontem novit, referre animus est. Quin et Sereniss. Celsnis Væ Magnificentissimus hic Orator satis perspectum habet, eum de Capucinis rumorem meram esse in me calumniam, et scit forte, unde illa dissuasio apud Cæsarem provenerit, ut me non saltem ille, sed et Illustrissimus ac Reverendissimus Dominus Nuncius, qui nomine P. M. hîc moratur, me satis de isto figmento, uti et in alio quodam huic ferme simili, excusatum habet, siquidem ab Illustri et Magnifico Domino CORADUCIO Imperij Procancellario satis superque cognoverit, rem longe aliter se habere, meque omni culpa in eiuscemodi de me promulgatis nugis vacare. Quæ ob id latius ad Sereniss. Celsnem Vam qua possum submissione perscribo, ut illa quoque, si tale quid ad eius aures pervenit, meam innocentiam cognitam habeat, nec tam iniuriosas calumnias in meæ famæ extenuationem aut Filij mei detrimentum redundare sinat, sed id potius sua authoritate, sicubi opus fuerit, clementissime amoliatur, sique vicissim ex mea tenuitate proficisci poterint officiola, quæ Sereniss. Celsni Væ ullo modo accepta esse queant, utatur me perpetuo tanquam suo servitore fideli et obsequentissimo. DEUS Opt. Max. Serenis. Celsnem Vam Reipub. tam politicæ quam literariæ bono per multos Annos incolumem et prosperam florere faciat.

Dabantur Pragæ 30 die Aprilis Anno 1601.
Serenissimæ Celsitudinis Vestræ
Reverenter et submisse
addictissimus
TYCHO BRAHE,
manu propria scripsi.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the most serene and most powerful Prince and Lord, Lord Ferdinando, Grand Duke of Tuscany etc., his most clement Lord. Florence.

To Your most serene Highness, a quarter of a year ago, I humbly sent a letter by my elder son, accompanying the Persian Legation into Italy — which letter, I gladly learned from a recent letter of that same son, was duly delivered to Your most serene Highness. He writes therein some things which struck my mind not a little: namely that the chief Persian Legate, an Englishman by nation, in whose household my son then was, when he had reached Siena, called him aside and insinuated this: that Your most serene Highness — having understood, from letters written to You by me, that my son was in his company — had dissuaded the English Legate from bringing him to Rome, especially at that time; for that could not be done without grave danger to my son's life, and hindrance to the Legate himself, since my name had already become well enough known in those parts, and that the danger lay chiefly in this — that a rumour had grown in Italy that I had been the author and instigator, to the Sacred Imperial Majesty, that he should no longer tolerate the Capuchin monks here at Prague, but dismiss them. The English Legate therefore wished that my son should remain at Siena, and not go on to Rome with him — which was done. If, then, these things were done — as he reported — by Your most serene Highness's counsel and admonition, I do not doubt they proceeded from a most clement disposition toward me and my son, and from a good and sincere intention. But if that Legate produced such things out of his own brain, and put them forth under the pretext of Your Highness's name (as I suspect), I easily conjecture that he did it so as to be honestly rid of my son, lest he be a further burden to him — since, as we have learned well enough here at Prague, he was not well enough supplied with travel-money for so magnificent a legation and so great a journey. But be it what it will: I rejoice that my son tarries at Siena, in Your most serene Highness's dominion and city, where I am wholly persuaded he may dwell conveniently and be safe enough, since Your most serene Highness's region is of itself free, and not subject to the perils that might perchance arise elsewhere in Italy. Therefore I beg of Your most serene Highness, with what submission and reverence I can, that You would not disdain to hold this my son commended to You; and if his affairs so allow that he may stay in Your Highness's territory long enough to learn the Italian tongue tolerably, that would indeed be most welcome to me. Which done, I would most humbly and dutifully ask that Your most serene Highness deign to commend him by letter to the most serene and most powerful King of France, Your renowned kinsman, that he might be received as a servant in his Court for a year or two, until he master that tongue too. Whatever, by this or another means, Your most serene Highness shall confer of most clement benefit upon my son, I shall acknowledge as conferred upon myself, with perpetual submission and gratitude of mind, so far as my powers allow.

For the rest, as concerns the Capuchin monks: let Your most serene Highness know for certain, and conclude beyond doubt, that it never came into my mind, even by the least thought, to speak anything of them to the Sacred Imperial Majesty, my most clement Lord — to make mention by any word whether they ought to stay here or not — much less to suggest or counsel His Majesty anything in these matters; since such things in no way pertain to me, nor does His Majesty ever speak with me of such things as concern Ecclesiastical Persons or affairs, but only of those that are Philosophical and Astronomical, with which His Majesty (like many other Kings and Princes) is laudably taken; nor do I meddle in others' business — scarcely have I leisure and convenience to handle and carry out my own. I can therefore call God to witness, holily, and my conscience inviolately, that a great injury is done me in this part, both here and elsewhere — as also in certain other calumnies which have been scattered here by the unskilled, or by those ignorant of my affairs — about which I intend shortly to report to His Majesty, who knows me innocent in all those things that some falsely invent to have been said and done by me before His Majesty. Indeed Your most serene Highness's most magnificent Orator here has clearly enough perceived that this rumour about the Capuchins is a mere calumny against me, and perhaps knows whence that dissuasion at the Emperor's [court] arose; so that not only he, but also the most illustrious and most reverend Lord Nuncio, who stays here in the name of the Supreme Pontiff [the Pope], holds me well enough excused of that fiction — as also in another very similar one — since he has learned, fully and more, from the illustrious and magnificent Lord Coraduz, Vice-Chancellor of the Empire, that the matter stands far otherwise, and that I am free of all blame in such trifles published about me. All which I therefore write more fully to Your most serene Highness, with what submission I can, so that You too — if any such thing has reached Your ears — may have my innocence known, and not let such injurious calumnies redound to the lessening of my fame or to my son's detriment, but rather most clemently remove it by Your authority, wherever there be need. And if in turn any small services can proceed from my slenderness, that can in any way be acceptable to Your most serene Highness, use me ever as Your faithful and most obedient servant. May God, Best and Greatest, make Your most serene Highness flourish unharmed and prosperous for many years, for the good of the Commonwealth, both political and literary.

Given at Prague, the 30th day of April, the year 1601.
Your most serene Highness's,
reverently and submissively, most devoted,
Tycho Brahe, written in my own hand.

Apparatus

- Recipient. Ferdinando I de' Medici, Grand Duke of Tuscany (Magnus Dux Etruriae, 1549–1609), at Florence.
- Significance — Tycho the Younger's Italian journey, the Capuchin calumny, and a bid for the French court. Three interlocking threads:
1. The son and the Persian embassy. Tycho's elder son (Tycho Brahe the Younger) had travelled into Italy accompanying the Persian Legation — the 1600–01 Safavid embassy jointly led by the Persian Husain Ali Beg and the Englishman Sir Anthony Sherley ("the chief Persian Legate, an Englishman by nation," in whose household the son was). (Cf. the same embassy at Prague, brahe-t_16001110001.) At Siena the son was left behind and not taken on to Rome.
2. The Capuchin calumny (the political danger). The stated reason: a rumour in Italy that Tycho had urged Rudolf II to expel the Capuchin monks from Prague — which, in Counter-Reformation Italy, made the Protestant Tycho's name (and his son's presence in Rome) perilous. Tycho vehemently denies it ("it never came into my mind … I never speak with His Majesty save of Philosophical and Astronomical things"), calling God and conscience to witness, and reports that the Tuscan ambassador, the Papal Nuncio, and Vice-Chancellor Coraduz all hold him cleared of the "fiction." He suspects Sherley invented the Grand Duke's "advice" as a pretext to shed the son — Sherley being notoriously short of travel-money for the grand legation.
3. The French-court plan. Tycho asks Ferdinando to keep the son in Tuscany until he learns Italian, then recommend him to "the King of France, Your renowned kinsman" — i.e. Henri IV, the Medici's Affinis through Maria de' Medici's marriage (October 1600) — for a year or two at the French court to learn French.
- A window on Tycho's court position. The disclaimer is a precise statement of Tycho's self-understood role at Rudolf's court: strictly the philosophical/astronomical adviser, deliberately aloof from confessional politics — and beset by "calumnies … scattered by the unskilled," which he means to lay before the Emperor.
- Persons & places named. Ferdinando I de' Medici; Tycho Brahe the Younger (the elder son); Sir Anthony Sherley (the English co-legate of the Persian embassy, unnamed but identified); Henri IV of France ("King of France, Your kinsman"); the Papal Nuncio at Prague (nomine P.M. = in the name of the Pontifex Maximus; unnamed — Filippo Spinelli held the post c. 1599–1603); the Tuscan Orator/ambassador at Prague (unnamed); Rudolf von Coraduz (Vice-Chancellor); the Capuchin monks. Places: Prague, Florence, Siena, Rome, Italy.
- Cross-references. Links to the Persian-embassy thread (brahe-t_16001110001) and to Tycho's court-calumny / self-defence thread; a key document for the biography of Tycho Brahe the Younger.
- Calendar. Pragae 30 die Aprilis Anno 1601 (imperial reckoning) → 30 April 1601 [N.S.]; header and label agree.
