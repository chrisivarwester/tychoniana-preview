The original

GEORGIUS ROLLENHAGIUS AD TYCHONEM BRAHE
S.

NOBILISSIME et Doctissime TYCHO, habui iam per annos aliquot in aula Caesareae Maiestatis inter apparitores praecipuos affinem Lunaeburgensem CASPARUM LEHMANNUM scalptorem quidem gemmarum ex professione, sed interim omnium rerum admiratorem, inprimis Astrologiae. Ea de caussa cum me amicius colat, interdum quoque invisat ut ante septimanas non ita multas fecerat, incidit in sermones privatos obiter URSI Dithmarsi mentio, quem dicebat titulum iam habere Caesarei Mathematici, etiamsi et vestitu et vita ursus sit, libello quoque privatim aliquibus dato, te indignis exagitare modis. Rogavi itaque hominem, ut eum libellum mecum communicaret, quod fecit. Sed incuria cuiusdam mei amici, cui mandaram, ut praecipua inde capita enotata mihi occupatissimo describeret, in alienas manus incidit, perijtque nobis prorsus, donec pridie Paschatos, quo tuae ab Hamburgensi mihi reddebantur de consilio cum Caesare contrahendae notitiae, Praga reversus est nuncius meus, aliudque exemplar earundem calumniarum secum, ut scripto ab affine meo petieram, attulit. Quod si vidisses, fieri non posse arbitror, quin aliquam eius in literis ad me tuis mentionem fecisses. Sic per me exemplar primum accipis. Quaeso considera insigne exemplum Regulae Ciceronianae, eum, qui semel verecundiae fines transgressus sit, bene et gnaviter impudentem esse oportere, neque tamen propterea SOCRATEM asinum se calcantem ad iudices traxisse, neque ARISTOPHANI publice ipsi in os maledicenti maledicta regessisse. Legi pro hac mea aetate multa veterum et recentium Philosophorum certamina, etiam de rebus maxime inter ipsos controversis, nunquam vero scriptum insolentius, impudentius, quod haud dubie non parum te irritabit, ut sane contentiones dignitatis sunt acerrimae. Sed quid facies? Hoc scio profecto, quod si cum stercore certo, vinco vel vincor, semper ego maculor. Ut itaque generosi equi canum latratus non relatrando et mordendo, sed animose praetereundo ulciscuntur, ita prudentiss. quique Eruditorum maledicentissimos oblatratores silentio ulti sunt. Et licet hi suos invenient subinde approbatores (stultis enim stulta placent, et gaudent aulae monstris) tamen dicere ipsas adversitates lubet ... decus. Taedebat me quondam in Berlinensi aula dominari TORNEISSERUM hominem illiteratissimum, nos vero cives in literis educatos ut umbras contemni, deque consignatis aliquot problematibus, volebam praesente Illustrissmo Electore vel Germanice cum ipso conferre. Utebar ad eam rem LEVINO a Schullenburg nostro Decano. Legebat meas quaestiones Elector, meque patienter audiebat. Tandem dicebat intelligere se eius ineptias, neque quicquam ab eo medicamenti accipere. Was schadets aber das der Kayser ein Elephanten hatt? Itaque me ad caenam iussit adesse, dedit quoque veniam privatim cum TORNEISSERO postridie colloquendi, sed is mihi nunquam erat domi. Sic didici magnates nostros non tam quaerere veritatem quam opinionum pompam. Quod monstrum plures habet spectatores, quam genuina corporum forma. Sic fortassis in illa quoque aula, de qua scribis, hic SIMON MAGUS imprudentium animos dementavit, ut existimetur esse aliquid magni. Quare prudenter id periculi tentandum est. Alias Coloniensis ille vere generosi est animi et prorsus politici, qui quaerit imperij pacem, et illustre apud posteritatem nomen, nec dubites, quin facile eum habeas amicum; sed an utilem, ideo incertum est, quod longissimis itineribus et summis Imperij negotijs est occupatissimus. In aula ipsa qui iam sint politioribus literis imbuti, ignoro, olim CRATO et Dominus CURTIUS Musis nostris aditum liberrimum ad summam Maiestatem concedebant. Is, qui rerum summae praeest, amat mathemata, et Vulcani officinam, ut audij, inprimis si Magistrum nactus fuerit candidum. Sed haec Deo curae erunt, et albis puellis, ut ille inquit. Affinis tuus ERICUS LANGIUS ex Hassia ad nos redux iam diversatur Wandio in comitatu Mansfeldensi apud IOHAN. ERNESTUM ab Assaburg nobilem potentem et eruditum; distat is locus hinc decem milliaribus. Si volueris illi scribere, efficiam, ut literas tuas obtineat.

GEORGIUS ROLLENHAGIUS.

Georg Rollenhagen to Tycho Brahe — greeting.

Most noble and most learned Tycho: for some years now I have had among the foremost attendants at the court of His Imperial Majesty a kinsman from Lüneburg, Caspar Lehmann, an engraver of gems by profession, but withal an admirer of all things, and above all of astrology. For that reason, since he cultivates me with some friendliness and from time to time also visits me — as he had done not so very many weeks ago — there fell into our private conversations, in passing, a mention of Ursus the Dithmarscher, who, he said, now holds the title of Imperial Mathematician — even though in dress and in life he is a bear — and who, in a little book given privately to certain persons, harasses you in unworthy ways. I therefore asked the man to share that little book with me, which he did. But through the carelessness of a certain friend of mine, whom I had charged to copy out for me — being most busy — the chief headings noted down from it, [the book] fell into other hands and was wholly lost to us, until on the eve of Easter — the day on which your [letters] from the Hamburg man were delivered to me, concerning the plan of contracting an acquaintance with the Emperor — my messenger returned from Prague, and brought with him another copy of the same calumnies, as I had asked in writing of my kinsman. And had you seen it, I judge it could not but be that you would have made some mention of it in your letters to me. So it is through me that you receive your first copy. Consider, I beg you, that signal instance of the Ciceronian Rule — that he who has once overstepped the bounds of modesty must needs be thoroughly and busily shameless — and yet that Socrates did not on that account drag before the judges the ass that trod on him, nor did he hurl back curses at Aristophanes, who was cursing him publicly to his face. For my age, I have read many contests of philosophers ancient and modern, even on matters most controverted among themselves, but never a writing more insolent, more shameless — which will doubtless provoke you not a little, since indeed contests of standing are the bitterest of all. But what will you do? This I know for certain: that if I contend with dung, win or lose, I am always befouled. And so, just as noble horses avenge the barking of dogs not by barking back and biting, but by passing them by with spirit, so all the most prudent of the Learned have avenged their most slanderous yappers with silence. And although these men will from time to time find their approvers (for foolish things please fools, and they delight in the monsters of the court), yet it is pleasing [to me] to speak the adversities themselves ... honour. It once wearied me that at the court of Berlin Thurneisser, a most unlettered man, lorded it, while we citizens bred up in letters were despised like shadows; and, regarding certain set problems, I wished to confer with him in the presence of the Most Illustrious Elector, even in German. For that business I made use of our Dean, Levinus von der Schulenburg. The Elector read my questions, and listened to me patiently. At last he said that he understood the man's follies, and took no medicine from him. "But what harm is it that the Emperor keeps an elephant?" And so he bade me attend at supper, and also gave leave to confer privately with Thurneisser the next day; but the man was never at home to me. Thus I learned that our great men seek not so much truth as the show of opinions. A monster has more spectators than the natural form of bodies. So perhaps in that court too, of which you write, this Simon Magus has bewitched the minds of the imprudent, so that he is reckoned to be something great. Wherefore that hazard must be ventured prudently. For the rest, that man of Cologne is truly of a noble spirit and altogether a statesman, who seeks the peace of the Empire and an illustrious name with posterity; and do not doubt that you will easily have him for a friend — but whether a useful one is uncertain, for the reason that he is most busy with very long journeys and with the highest affairs of the Empire. Who at the court itself are now imbued with the more polished letters, I do not know; once Crato and Master Curtius granted our Muses the freest access to the highest Majesty. He who presides over the sum of affairs loves the mathematical arts, and the workshop of Vulcan, as I hear — especially if he shall have got himself an honest Master. But these things will be the care of God, and of the white maidens, as the poet says. Your kinsman Erik Lange, returned to us from Hesse, now lodges at Wansleben in the county of Mansfeld, with Johann Ernst von der Asseburg, a powerful and learned nobleman; that place is ten miles distant from here. If you should wish to write to him, I will see to it that he gets your letter.

Georg Rollenhagen.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Georgius Rollenhagius = Georg Rollenhagen (1542–1609), rector of the cathedral school (Altstädtisches Gymnasium) at Magdeburg, dramatist and poet (author of Froschmeuseler), a long-standing correspondent of Tycho's. The dating clause "ten miles distant from here" (distat is locus hinc decem milliaribus) and Rollenhagen's known residence fix the place of writing as Magdeburg (not stated in the text; supplied in brackets in the header).
  • Recipient / occasion. Tycho, then at Wandsbek near Hamburg (in his post-Hven exile), was weighing an approach to Emperor Rudolf II's court at Prague. Rollenhagen writes to forward a copy of, and warn Tycho about, the slanderous pamphlet circulating in Prague.
  • URSI Dithmarsi = Nicolaus Reimers Ursus (Nicolai Reymers Bär, c. 1551–1600), the Dithmarschen-born mathematician, by 1591 Imperial Mathematician at Prague, Tycho's bitter rival in the priority dispute over the geo-heliocentric world-system. The libellus is his De astronomicis hypothesibus … tractatus (Prague, 1597), which attacked Tycho in scurrilous terms. Wordplay flagged: etiamsi et vestitu et vita ursus sit puns on Ursus's name (Latin ursus = "bear"); "in dress and in life he is a bear" keeps the pun.
  • CASPARUM LEHMANNUM = Caspar Lehmann (c. 1563/65–1622), the celebrated gem- and glass-engraver, from Uelzen in the Lüneburg region, in Imperial service at Prague (credited with reviving wheel-engraving on glass); here Rollenhagen's affinis (kinsman by marriage) and informant.
  • pridie Paschatos — "the eve of Easter." In 1598 (O.S.) Easter Sunday fell on 16 April (Julian), placing this passage's reference on 15 April; the letter is catalogued simply "1598, April." The Easter reference is the firmest internal date-anchor; no day is given in any subscription, so the header date is left at "April 1598 [O.S.]." Calendar style is Old Style (Rollenhagen, in Lutheran Magdeburg, used the Julian reckoning).
  • Regulae Ciceronianae — "the Ciceronian Rule": the maxim that one who has once cast off shame must be wholly shameless (cf. Cicero, Pro Quinctio / the proverbial qui semel verecundiae fines transierit); kept as a named allusion.
  • Socrates and the ass; Aristophanes. The two classical exempla of dignified non-retaliation — Socrates not suing the ass that kicked him, and not returning Aristophanes' public abuse (Aristophanes had lampooned Socrates in the Clouds). Kept as allusion.
  • si cum stercore certo, vinco vel vincor, semper ego maculor — a proverbial saying ("if I contend with dung, win or lose, I am always befouled"), rendered as a sentence and kept for its proverbial bite; the antithesis vinco vel vincor ("win or lose") is preserved.
  • generosi equi … oblatratores silentio ulti sunt — the "noble horse / barking dogs" topos for answering slander with silence; metaphor kept.
  • Lacuna / crux. tamen dicere ipsas adversitates lubet... decus — the source (Langebek copy, as printed in Dreyer) is defective here: an ellipsis stands between lubet and decus, and the clause does not parse as it stands. Rendered word-for-word as "yet it is pleasing [to me] to speak the adversities themselves... honour," with the gap preserved and not smoothed. The likely sense — that openly naming such troubles is itself creditable — is not asserted in the translation. This is the governing crux for the grade.
  • TORNEISSERUM = Leonhard Thurneisser zum Thurn (1531–1596), the Basel-born alchemist, astrologer and physician who dominated the Berlin court of Elector Johann Georg of Brandenburg until his fall (1584); Rollenhagen, as a Brandenburg-orbit schoolman, recalls a personal encounter. The spelling Torneisserum is kept; identification given.
  • LEVINO a Schullenburg nostro Decano = Levin von der Schulenburg, named as "our Dean" (Decanus), the cathedral-chapter dignitary who acted as Rollenhagen's intermediary to the Elector.
  • German interpolation. Was schadets aber das der Kayser ein Elephanten hatt? — "But what harm is it that the Emperor keeps an elephant?" The Elector's own words, quoted verbatim in Early New High German within the Latin (a code-switch, preserved by the edition's convention); the elephant = the exotic court curiosity tolerated for show, i.e. Thurneisser. schadets = schadet es; hatt = hat.
  • SIMON MAGUS — Simon the Magician (Acts 8), the type of the charlatan who passes for "something great" (aliquid magni, echoing Acts 8:9); applied here to the impostor said to sway the Prague court. Kept as allusion.
  • Coloniensis ille — "that man of Cologne": almost certainly Ernst of Bavaria, Archbishop-Elector of Cologne (1554–1612), an Imperial Elector active in high Reichspolitik; characterised as a powerful but often-absent potential patron. Identification offered as probable, not certain (no name is given in the text); flagged.
  • CRATO = Johannes Crato von Krafftheim (1519–1585), personal physician to three emperors (Ferdinand I, Maximilian II, Rudolf II) and a noted humanist patron — here recalled (with olim, "once") as a past channel of learned access to the Emperor. Dominus CURTIUS = Jakob Kurz von Senftenau (Jacobus Curtius, d. 1594), Imperial Vice-Chancellor and a mathematically-cultivated councillor; likewise olim, now deceased.
  • Is, qui rerum summae praeest — "He who presides over the sum of affairs," a periphrasis for Rudolf II; amat mathemata, et Vulcani officinam = "loves the mathematical arts, and the workshop of Vulcan" — Vulcani officina = the alchemical/metallurgical laboratory, glossed as "the workshop of Vulcan" (Rudolf's well-known patronage of alchemy). Magistrum … candidum = "an honest Master" (i.e. a sound, sincere adept, as opposed to a charlatan).
  • albis puellis, ut ille inquit — "of the white maidens, as the poet says": an allusion to Horace (cf. Odes — the Diespiter / the favouring deities), proverbial for "the kindly powers"; ut ille inquit ("as the poet/that author says") is the author's own hedge and is kept.
  • ERICUS LANGIUS = Erik Lange (1553–1613), the Danish nobleman, alchemist, and Tycho's kinsman (betrothed to Tycho's sister Sophie); here reported as lodging at Wanzleben (Wandio) in the county of Mansfeld with Johann Ernst von der Asseburg (Iohan. Ernestum ab Assaburg). decem milliaribus = "ten miles" (German Meilen, each far longer than the English mile); rendered "ten miles" with this caution.
  • apparitores praecipuos — "the foremost attendants" (court officers/ushers); scalptorem gemmarum — "engraver of gems," kept literal. notitiae … cum Caesare contrahendae — "of contracting an acquaintance with the Emperor," the diplomatic overture Tycho was preparing.
  • Correction. Corrected an inverted subject/object in the Socrates-Aristophanes sentence: the Latin (Dreyer, TBOO VIII p.51, dative ARISTOPHANI + agreeing 'maledicenti') has Socrates declining to hurl curses back at Aristophanes, who was cursing him — not the reverse, and not an invented third party. Corrected the English place-name identification of 'Wandio' (Dreyer, TBOO VIII p.52) from Wanzleben (Magdeburg Börde, not in Mansfeld) to Wansleben am See (Mansfelder Land, consistent with the letter's own 'in comitatu Mansfeldensi').

Collated against Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia (the edition of record), reading the printed page images. Collated the full letter (the transcribed original and the published English) against Dreyer TBOO VIII pp. 49-52, read as page images (pp.49-52 cover the complete Rollenhagen letter; p.53 is the following Maestlin letter and not needed). Dreyer's printed Latin matches our transcribed original verbatim throughout (no text error). Both previously-flagged defects are confirmed: the Socrates/Aristophanes subject inversion is a real mistranslation (Aristophanes is grammatically the dative object of Socrates' restraint, not an independent subject), and 'Wanzleben' for 'Wandio' conflicts with the letter's own 'in comitatu Mansfeldensi' (Wansleben am See, not Wanzleben, lies in the historic Mansfeld district). No other defects found on full collation of names, numbers, and sense.

2 defects found:
- mistranslation (high). ARISTOPHANI is dative (object of Socrates' restraint) and 'maledicenti' agrees with it (Aristophanes is the one cursing); the subject of 'regessisse' continues to be Socrates from the prior clause. Correct sense: 'nor did [Socrates] hurl back curses at Aristophanes, who was cursing him publicly to his face.' The English inverts subject and object, making Aristophanes the restrained party and inventing an unnamed 'man cursing him' where the Latin names only Socrates and Aristophanes. At: “nor did Aristophanes hurl back curses at the man cursing him publicly to his face”.
- mistranslation (medium). Dreyer's 'Wandio' is confirmed identical to the transcribed original, so the Latin itself is not in question. But 'Wandio' is rendered 'Wanzleben' in the English, and Wanzleben (in the Magdeburg Börde, c. 15km SW of Magdeburg) is not in the county of Mansfeld — only Wansleben (am See), in the Mansfelder Land proper, fits the letter's own qualifier 'in comitatu Mansfeldensi'. The English place-name identification contradicts the sentence it translates. At: “now lodges at Wanzleben in the county of Mansfeld”.

Repaired. Verified both mistranslation defects directly against the Dreyer page images (TBOO VIII pp.51-52, Rollenhagen to Tycho): the Latin in both places is confirmed identical to the transcribed original, so both defects are pure translation errors, not text corruptions. The Aristophanes/Socrates correction rests on straightforward Latin case-and-agreement analysis (ARISTOPHANI dative, maledicenti agreeing with it) directly visible on the page. The Wanzleben-to-Wansleben correction is a place-name identification: the page itself shows only the abbreviated 'Wandio', so the Mansfeld-county fit for Wansleben am See (rather than Wanzleben near Magdeburg) rests on external historical geography rather than the page image alone. Latin left untouched in both cases per instructions.
