To the most learned and excellent man, Master Johannes Kepler, distinguished mathematician, now living in Styria or Austria, his especially beloved friend.

Your most longed-for and most learned letter, most learned Sir — and, for the surpassing gifts of your talent, most dear to me, though not yet known to me by face — I received last summer at Wittenberg, a little before I departed thence, when I was on the point of the Bohemian journey. And so no opportunity was then given of answering it, nor afterwards until this time, on account of the vicissitude of my affairs and the new setting-up of them in foreign places. But having now gained a little leisure, together with the convenience of sending a letter to you, I judged that I should put off writing back no longer. What at the outset you recount at length, to clear yourself concerning that mischievous Ursus, did not indeed need so many words and so elaborate an explanation, since I otherwise hold you sufficiently excused, and impute nothing to you in that he inserted your letter — and that too, as I now learn from you, mutilated and corrupted — for the sake of adorning himself (for so perhaps he had need) into that scurrilous and slanderous writing which he published, without your knowledge and against your will. Concerning my Hypotheses snatched away by him and vended as his own, there would not be so great a question, had he not, in that shameless book teeming with fabrications and calumnies, blushed not to gnaw — with a scurrilous and frenzied pen, and, so far as in him lies, to defame — my honour and reputation, nay, my fatherland, my lineage, my most honourable house, with the most impudent and wicked lies, and to scatter such things abroad in print; even though he himself confesses in the preface that I had deserved well of him, and I have a certain poem of his — though poured out (as is his way) from a tasteless vein, and written in his own hand — in which he thanks me that, when he was once at Uraniborg with me, I not only fed him generously with food and drink for several days, but also gave him money on his departure. See what thanks he returns for these and other kindnesses! He took occasion for these abuses from the fact that in the first volume of our Astronomical Letters there stands a letter of Christoph Rothmann, the Landgrave's mathematician, in which he calls that impure Ursus a scoundrel — for what sort of man he was, Rothmann had been taught by experience itself at his Prince's court, and also by the report of others from the places where Ursus had formerly lived. I, however, in a certain other letter to the said Rothmann, merely report it by way of mimicry, and at the same time hint by what guile he had secretly filched certain things of mine — some of which I recovered, and which are still in my keeping, written in his own hand (which he cannot deny). And indeed, had I then had leisure to spare from weightier studies to review those Letters, as to each particular content, while they were being printed at our press, I should not have suffered the book published by me to be defiled with the name of that savage and rank Ursus — not that he could be reckoned unworthy of such an attached epithet, or that any wrong would thus be done him, speaking by men's customary judgments (as not only Rothmann, but also many others who know him well, are aware). But because I do not willingly, in my writings, censure anyone's life and morals, whatever they be — whence also, in the letter of the Magnificent Lord Jacob Curtius of best memory, afterwards appended by me at Wandsbek to the book on the Mechanics of Astronomy, I omitted his name, although the said Lord Jacob Curtius expressly named him and moreover entitled him my plagiarist. But it happened that, when I had entrusted to certain of my students the labour of correcting the printer's proofs in that book of Letters, and had handed them the originals as they were in manuscript, they thought nothing should be changed, nor dared to interrupt me while I was taken up with more serious matters, and so allowed everything to be printed as they had it in the autographs; and thus by a certain carelessness it came about that his name, with the appended epithets, was retained there. But whether this be a sufficient and just enough cause for raging with such measureless mad malice against me and mine — and indeed against my fatherland itself — we shall shortly see. He could have complained of the wrong, if any had been done him, and removed it by lawful means; not raged with so ungoverned a mind, out of an unbridled lust for revenge, and made everything far worse. Yet there is no reason for you to suppose that I am more stirred against that trifler than my condition allows — as you infer (with candid mind, however) — for I do not judge him worthy of the Emperor's wrath (as someone also wrote to me). But since, against right and divine law, and against the most praiseworthy Constitutions of the Empire, he has with brazen face attempted to dishonour me by a more-than-libellous writing, beyond all truth and my desert, I will see to it that he be proceeded against at law, and repressed and chastised according to the laws. He withdrew from Prague into Silesia a little after my arrival there, but lately, as I learned, he returned secretly — perhaps because he understood that I was no longer there; but how long my hands are, even if I be not at hand, he will shortly feel: detained under arrest, and afterwards dragged into court. And since I understand from your letter that you have not seen those filthy and scurrilous papers, I send a copy, that you may both more readily make out your own letter there, and judge the rest, of what kind they are. You will assuredly see how insane, slanderous, abusive, and fetid the compilation is, and how thoroughly it reeks of its author; more impudent calumnies you have perhaps never read. The ears of all sensible and upright men shrink from them.

But enough, and more than enough, of that savage Ditmarsh Ursus; for he is not worthy of so many words uttered by you or me; and if you knew him "inside and under the skin" (as they say) — I do not now speak of the French scab, with which he teems on account of the impurity of his life — you would testify the same with me, nay, would long since have testified it. Let me add just one thing for the sake of asserting our Hypotheses, though this be the least of it, and I have far more and stronger arguments at hand. Who would believe that those Hypotheses, first devised by me in the year 1584, which he does not fear to arrogate to himself, are really his invention — knowing that he was never practised in any celestial observations, from which new Hypotheses are to be built up? Nay, if he cannot demonstrate, according to all particulars, the apparent place of even one planet — especially of the three superior — by this newfangled supposition (as he scarcely will be able), to what intelligent man, I ask, will he prove that the invention is his own? But not he alone — others too, while I keep my own work back and delay to bring it to light, have dared to attribute this device to themselves. Among whom is a certain Scot, Duncan by name, Professor of Medicine and of the Mathematics at Helmstedt, who, having received this method from me in Denmark, first dictated it privately to his students at Rostock, and did not deny it was mine, but of his own accord called it "Tychonic" — because I was nearby, and he feared that, if he did otherwise, I could easily find it out. Yet secretly, to those students he most trusted, he whispered in the ear that he ascribed that hypothesis to me only for honour's sake, that he might have more favour and authority with his hearers, but that it was really his own; which afterwards, having become more remote from me at Helmstedt, he did more boldly and shamelessly, partly in his public lectures, partly in certain printed notices. But this fraud and imposture has been detected — both by others and especially by Doctor Daniel Cramer, minister of the Word among the people of Stettin, a singularly learned man, who was once one of the chief auditors of that Scot at Rostock; and in its own time it will perhaps be more fully exposed. If leisure remains, I will see to it that a copy of his letter — written about this matter to the most noble Holger Rosenkrantz, my kinsman (whose tutor the said Cramer then was), and sent into Denmark after my departure — be appended to these. So too Roeslin, though more cunningly and cautiously, presumed to vend my [hypotheses] as his own — yet in such a way that he did not deprive me of this speculation, but confesses that he found it, after he had himself devised similar things, in my book on the Comet of the year [15]77. A fine bit of juice indeed, if it could earn belief — for that book of mine which he mentions had been printed nine years before, and the arrangement of the celestial revolutions introduced by me had already become known to very many. But if nothing else, this above all betrays the fraud: that while he tried to form this Hypothesis somewhat differently, so that it might become his own offspring, and to "emend" it (if it please the gods), he ran into enormous absurdities — disturbing the very symmetry and proportion of the Revolutions, and setting it up most alien to the appearances themselves; so that it is thence sufficiently clear that he not only by no means understands these hypotheses (let alone is their author), but not even any others — nay, has not yet looked into the inner sanctum of Astronomy.

I should never have thought that the Italian Magini affected any such thing. But if he did, he admitted the contrary in a letter given to me in his own hand, in which he confessed that he had first perceived that Hypothesis from our second book on the Aetherial Phenomena, and that it greatly pleased him — provided the orb of Mars could cross the Solar [orb] (for he thought, on the common opinion, that the orbs in heaven are real). And from Magini, if any others are privy to this speculation, they had it on this occasion — among whom too is Francesco Patrizi, who makes mention of it in his otherwise learned volume on the New Philosophy. He indeed expressly asserts it to be mine, but so perverts it, and through ignorance misapplies it, and against my own postulates and assumptions defiles and worries it, that I do not acknowledge as mine very many things there set down on his report — which I shall also publicly protest. But about this Hypothesis or another we shall not be much exercised; the question is: who can find that method — both in continuous quantities and motions, and in discrete ones — such that the celestial phenomena answer to it in every respect.

This is the work, this the toil — and so let us be judged by our doing.

Further, since at the outset you hinted that you first received from Maestlin those [letters] addressed by me to you, who also shared with you those I had sent to him, I marvel that he was willing to answer nothing in so long a time — neither to those nor to others which I had sent earlier, indeed some years ago. Whether this be [the act] of an honest man, skilfully imbued with the mathematical disciplines, judge for yourself. It is not otherwise my custom to anticipate strangers with letters, but for the most part I am wont to be anticipated by them — let alone that I should receive no reply to mine over so long a stretch of time. But henceforth I will not trouble with my letters — ungratefully received, as I see — him whom I have always esteemed most highly, as my published writings can testify. You are pleased with what I wrote to him and to you on astronomical matters. And indeed I hold it for certain that these things are altogether so, and that many more and greater than these still lie hidden, which eluded the diligence of our predecessors, both ancient and modern.

You urge — nay, with many reasons, which I do not disapprove, you try to persuade — that I make my celestial Observations public, which in its own time I will not refuse to do, both for the reasons you abundantly adduce and for others. But to do it so soon, before most of what has been restored by me in astronomy, and founded on the choicer of those observations, has come to light, I do not think advisable; for, besides other things, this very fact which you report — about my hypotheses snatched from me, and about Cyprian's Ephemerides, in part attributed to others and corrupted — frightens and dissuades me. Yet you shall obtain at length, by the favour of the heavenly Deity, what I have noted in the heavens over very many years — not indeed so defective and mutilated, not to say uncertain, as are those which Werner (or rather Johannes Schöner) committed to a few pages — left by Regiomontanus and Walther (for I have seen no observations of Werner; you, if you have any, share them) — but exact and self-consistent, and at the same time in such abundance that they could scarcely be confined in one great volume. Besides the book of Letters, of which above, I will add our Mechanics, pertinent here, which comprise the instruments I have used for over 20 years, if they can conveniently be brought to you from Prague. From the Letters, besides other things hinted before, you will perceive whether they are of such small moment and useless, as that censor — blinded by impotence of mind — would have it, and whether they derogate anything from the Landgrave Wilhelm of most praiseworthy memory (as the same man impudently fabricated, to render me hateful to his Son). From the Mechanics you will gather with how great an effort I undertook the renewal of astronomy, and with how great diligence and how great expense I employed in these things. You will also find there, toward the latter part, a record of what I have hitherto drained off in this kind of studies, and what remains hereafter to be woven through, with the Maker of the heavens favouring; and likewise those letters of the Lord Jacob Curtius of praised memory and of Magini, which I mentioned before. I would have sent an "illuminated" (as they call it) copy, that you might more readily discern everything, but it is not now to hand.

Of your book, as it deserves along with its author, I have always thought honourably, ever since I looked into it; nor have I ever deprived — nor will I ever deprive — you or others who in any way deserve nobly of these sublime disciplines, remote from the grasp of the crowd, and who seriously aim at the kernel of truth, of their due praise; but rather I have exalted them, and shall exalt them hereafter. This is the chief thing which, in that ingenious writing of yours — though you admit it with most others by a common error — I do not approve: that you lay down a certain reality to be in the celestial orbs, so as the more readily to build a way to the Copernican imaginations and the more easily to subscribe to them. That the celestial motions observe a certain symmetry, and that there are reasons underlying why they revolve about these or other centres differently distant from the earth or the Sun, I would not deny; but the harmony and the analogy of the arrangements is to be sought a posteriori, once the motions and the occasions of the motions have been exactly established — not a priori, as both you and Maestlin wished; nay, even so it is most difficult to find. And if anyone should add this labour, I should say that he surpasses the ancient Pythagoras, who divined that a fitting harmony was present in the heavens — nay, in the whole world. And although the circular motions in heaven can sometimes seem, to one who weighs each thing curiously, to produce by their composite motion certain angular and various figures, and for the most part oblong ones, yet this happens per accidens, and reason shrinks from this supposition. For the circuits of the stars must be composed entirely of circular [motions]; otherwise they will not perpetually return uniformly and equably upon themselves, and will frustrate perpetuity — besides that they would be less simple and more anomalous, and not suited to doctrine and use either. Concerning Mercury, there is no reason for you to grieve that its star has not been observed by me, or that I would pretend the same as Copernicus in displaying its Phenomena. For although it very rarely departs from the Sun's rays, and the sphere is more inclined with us in Denmark than at Frauenburg in Prussia, where Copernicus lived, and the sea around the island of our dwelling sends up more vapours than the Vistula, yet on very many occasions — nay, almost every year, while I kept watch over the stars at our Uraniborg — it was accurately noted by us in the heavens, both as morning and as evening star; so that the path of this star, however rarely it lie open and however subject to many windings, cannot escape us — by no means to be borrowed from the doubtful observations of others, as Copernicus was forced to do. Concerning Venus, so far as it has been possible to investigate hitherto, it is certain that its apogee is not much moved forward from the Solar one — otherwise than Copernicus thought — yet not entirely united with it, as the Alphonsine [Tables], in imitation of Albategnius, would have it. In its eccentricity, however, the ratio is not equal, for I found it much less than the Solar. Whether it is mutable, I would not yet say; but your reasonings, ingenious in themselves, will not greatly prove or disprove it. To find out subtly, from the Pole-star or any other, whether the annual orb of the Earth devised by Copernicus, if it existed, would insinuate some little parallax into the stars of the eighth sphere, I have before now often and diligently tried to test; but, careful observation being made, I have hitherto found nothing of the kind. For I have observed that the Pole-star — observed precisely above or below the pole at any time of year, with its true distance from the pole applied (which this year is 2° 51' 10'', and it crosses the meridian above and below the pole at 6° 13' 26'' of Aries and Libra) — gave the same elevation of the pole within half a minute [scruple], nay, within a quarter or sixth part of it (finer things the sight does not discern); which I long ago indicated to Christoph Rothmann too, who, indulging the Copernican speculation, supposed that some discrepancy lay hidden here — so that hence there is no consolation left for defending your Copernicus (whose elegant and ingenious speculation I am otherwise wont to admire and extol). Seek it elsewhere, if it shall be given.

But of these and other things I will deal with you more fully, pleasantly and most gladly, and will share more of my own, if you, as you promise, shall some time visit me — which will now be less troublesome to you than before, since I have now fixed Urania's new seat in Bohemia, not very far removed from you, and dwell in the imperial castle Benátky, five miles distant from Prague. For that I was most graciously called hither from Germany by the Imperial Majesty, and most kindly and liberally received, I think you have already learned from others (since I have now stayed here half a year). Yet I should not wish that the harshness of fortune compel you to come to us, but rather your own choice and your love and affection toward our common studies. Whatever it shall be, you will find me not fortune's friend — whatever that be — but yours, who even in adverse lot will by no means fail you with his counsel and aid, but rather will promote you to all that is best. And if you come in good time, we shall perhaps find ways by which it will be better provided for you and yours hereafter than before. Farewell. When you write back, send your letters to Doctor Blotius at Vienna, who is in charge of the Emperor's Library there; he will easily forward them to Prague, whence they will soon reach me.

Again, fare most happily.
Given at Benátky, or the Venice of the Bohemians, the 9th day of December, New Style, 1599.
Your most loving
Tycho Brahe;
I wrote [this] with my own hand.

[Postscript:] POSTSCRIPT. If a convenient occasion offer itself, I ask that you remind Maestlin in my name about the Eclipses observed by him, to be compared with mine, for deriving the difference of meridians. For while I re-read the copy of my letter to Maestlin, I see that I made mention there of the Solar Eclipse which fell on the 25th of February of the year [15]99, where, by some carelessness or other, the eclipse-digits are noted as 10¾ — perhaps because they were thus wrongly found by one of my students, who carried out the calculation too hastily. Whereas the truer reckoning from my Tables (as I myself afterwards investigated) yields 9½ digits — which is also the quantity by which the Sun was found to be deficient by one of my [men], a student left at Uraniborg (so that he might have charge of the four remaining instruments, which, on account of their size, could not be carried away so quickly); and the times, observed from the heaven by means of the Equatorial armilla 18 feet in Diameter, from beginning to end (for it was clear there the whole time), agreed exactly with my restitution, and were of this kind: that the eclipse began there at 10h 10m, had its end at 12h 19m, and so the middle fell at 11h 12m. These things you may report to Maestlin, when you write to him, and ask and bring it about that I may obtain from him — through you, if he be unwilling to write back to me — the Eclipses, both Lunar and Solar, as many as he has hitherto observed. I have found among my papers a copy of Cramer's letter, of which I made mention before, transcribed long ago for I know not what use, which I add to these; and at the same time a copy of a letter of a certain Master Johannes Christiernus, a Dane, now living at Leiden for the sake of his studies, who was a member of my household when our book on the Comet of the year [15]77 was being printed at Uraniborg, and who arrived a little after that Ursus had been with me, and therefore learned from my other students how fraudulently he had acted. I have added also the testimony of the secretary of the most noble Erik Lange, whom Ursus then served, and who was likewise at hand when that man, attending his master, was with me. This testification Erik himself afterwards confirmed at Magdeburg in the presence of a public Notary, and testified that whatever else Ursus has in that libellous writing — so far as anything of the matter was known to him — was utterly false, a public Instrument (as they call it) having been drawn up concerning twelve such points laid before him, excerpted from that libellous book, which you will see elsewhere; from this you will judge the rest. T. B. / That the next page's surface might not be empty, I had three Epigrams inscribed there, from those composed by me after I came to Benátky. More poems, fashioned by various hands after I migrated from Denmark into Germany, I shared with Doctor Blotius, joined to me in old friendship, and asked him to make you a sharer of them too; if you receive them, you will gather more therefrom, and if there are any keen Poets among you (as without doubt there are), see that they increase the number.

Apparatus

  • Edition of record. Dreyer, Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia vol. VIII (Epistolae). MS witness: A = Vienna, Österreichische Nationalbibliothek, cod. Vindob. lat. 10702 (the Tycho–Kepler correspondence codex).
  • Date / place. Dateline "die 9 Decembris Stylo renovato 1599" — explicitly New Style (Gregorian), from "Benachia sive Venetijs Bohemorum" — Benátky nad Jizerou, the imperial castle c. 5 (German) miles from Prague, glossed with the pun "the Venice of the Bohemians" (Benátky is the Czech word for Venice). The catalogue label confirms n.s.
  • Historic significance. This is Tycho's substantive reply to Kepler's first approach and the letter in which Tycho invites Kepler to Benátky — the contact that brought Kepler into Tycho's household in 1600, gave him access to the Mars observations, and led (after Tycho's death in Oct 1601) to Kepler's elliptical-orbit discovery and the Rudolphine Tables. One of the cornerstone documents of the Scientific Revolution. It belongs with the rest of the Tycho–Kepler arc in this corpus (Kepler's Ursus-apologia 15990200001; the 1600 quarrel pair 16000408001 / 16000000001; the employment letters 16000828001 / 16001017001; Herwart's quote of Kepler-on-Ursus 16000911001).
  • The Ursus affair. Ursus = Nicolaus Reimers Bär (Raimarus Ursus Dithmarsus, "the Ditmarsh Bear"), imperial mathematician at Prague, who had published a geo-heliocentric world-system as his own (Fundamentum astronomicum, 1588) and savagely attacked Tycho — incorporating a mutilated copy of Kepler's flattering 1595 letter — in De astronomicis hypothesibus (1597). Kepler's long apology (for the unauthorised use of his letter) is what Tycho here graciously waves aside. Tycho recounts: Ursus's own preface admits Tycho "deserved well of him"; Tycho holds a thank-you poem in Ursus's own hand for hospitality and money at Uraniborg; Ursus took offence at Rothmann's letter (printed in Tycho's Epistolae astronomicae, 1596) calling him a nebulo ("scoundrel"), and at Tycho's own report of Ursus's theft of papers. Tycho disclaims responsibility for the printed epithets (left in "by a certain carelessness" of his student proof-correctors), and announces a libel suit under the Imperial Constitutions — Ursus had fled to Silesia and returned secretly ("how long my hands are … he will feel, under arrest and dragged into court"). (Ursus in fact died in August 1600, before judgment.)
  • The priority dispute over the Tychonic system. Tycho dates his invention of the hypotheses to 1584 and rebuts four rival claimants/transmitters:
  • Ursus — denied competence (no observational basis).
  • Duncan Liddel ("a certain Scot, Duncan"), Professor of Medicine and Mathematics at Helmstedt: got the method from Tycho in Denmark, taught it at Rostock as "Tychonic" (out of fear), but privately claimed it as his own, and at Helmstedt did so openly in lectures and printed intimationes. The fraud "detected especially by Doctor Daniel Cramer, minister at Stettin," once Liddel's chief auditor at Rostock and then tutor (Paedagogus) to Holger Rosenkrantz, who wrote about it to Rosenkrantz after Tycho left Denmark. — This is the same Liddel plagiarism dossier as brahe-t_15990926001, brahe-t_15991204001 (Krag's testimony-origin), and brahe-t_16000324002 (where Tycho presses Krag as eyewitness).
  • Helisaeus Roeslin — claimed to have found the system in Tycho's De cometa (1577) only after independently devising similar ideas; Tycho notes the book was printed nine years before and that Roeslin's attempted "emendation" wrecked the symmetry of the revolutions.
  • Giovanni Antonio Magini of Bologna — exculpated: Magini admitted in an autograph letter that he first saw the hypothesis in Tycho's De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis (the "second book on the Aetherial Phenomena"), objecting only that the orb of Mars would have to cross the Solar orb (Magini holding the real-orbs view). Francesco Patrizi got it via Magini and mentioned it in his Nova de universis philosophia (1591), attributing it to Tycho but garbling it against Tycho's own postulates — which Tycho says he will publicly disown.
  • The methodological core (historically poignant). Against Kepler's Mysterium Cosmographicum (1596), Tycho objects to two things: (1) Kepler's positing of real (solid) celestial orbs as a bridge to Copernicanism — Tycho's comet work had abolished solid spheres; (2) Kepler's a priori harmonic method — Tycho insists the harmony of the heavens be sought a posteriori, "once the motions and their occasions are exactly established," and would rank one who succeeds above Pythagoras. Crucially, Tycho here defends strictly circular motion ("the circuits of the stars must be composed entirely of circulars … reason shrinks from" oblong/angular composite figures) — the very assumption Kepler would overturn, with Tycho's own data, in deriving the elliptical orbit of Mars.
  • The anti-Copernican parallax argument. Tycho rehearses his decisive empirical objection to the heliocentric system: repeated precise observations of the Pole-star (its true polar distance "this year 2° 51' 10''," meridian transits at 6° 13' 26'' of Aries and Libra) show no annual stellar parallax — the pole-altitude constant to within a half-, quarter-, or sixth-minute (the limit of the eye). He had reported this to Rothmann, who had expected a discrepancy. Hence "no consolation remains for defending your Copernicus" (whose ingenuity Tycho nonetheless praises). Also: observational claims on Mercury (☿ — observed at Uraniborg "almost every year," not borrowed as Copernicus had to) and Venus (♀ — apogee not much advanced beyond the Solar, eccentricity much less than the Solar). (Planetary/zodiac glyphs are kept in the source block and named in English by this chapter's convention: ☿ = Mercury, ♀ = Venus, ♈ = Aries, ♎ = Libra.)
  • What Tycho promises to send. A copy of Ursus's libel (so Kepler can see his own mutilated letter); the Epistolae astronomicae (with the Curtius and Magini letters, and the vindication of Landgrave Wilhelm IV against Ursus's slander aimed at turning Wilhelm's son Moritz against Tycho); and the Astronomiae instauratae mechanica (the instruments "used for over 20 years," and the closing record of work done and to do) — "if they can conveniently be brought from Prague." He cites the meagre observational remains of Werner / Schöner / Regiomontanus / Walther as the foil to his own exact and abundant store.
  • Other persons & references. Michael Maestlin (Kepler's Tübingen teacher and the conduit for Tycho's earlier letters), reproached for never replying; Cyprian = Cyprian Leovitius (Cyprianus Leovicius), whose Ephemerides Kepler had reported as misattributed/corrupted — cited by Tycho as a further reason to delay publishing; Hugo Blotius (Blotius), imperial librarian at Vienna, named as the forwarding address for Kepler's reply.
