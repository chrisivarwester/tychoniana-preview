The original

NOBILISSIMO, ET VIRTVTE, SAPIENTIA, INGENIOSA ERVDITAQVE DOCTRINA
CLARISSIMO VIRO, DOMINO TYCHONI BRAHE, DOMINO DE KNUDSTRUP, EQUITI
DANO, MATHEMATICORUM NOSTRO SECULO OMNIUM PRÆSTANTISSIMO, ETC.
DOMINO SUO CUM OBSERUANTIA COLENDO. S. P.

ASI, vt OVIDIVS canit, Carmina proueniunt animo deducta sereno, Nobilissime Excellentissimeque Domine TYCHO, multo magis profundissimæ et subtilissimæ Mathematicæ Disputationes, quæ cum Carminum exaratione non sunt comparandæ, animum a perturbationibus liberrimum requirent. Non igitur rem aut a nostra amicitia alienam, aut excusatione omni, vt tu arguere videris, indignam commisissem, si responsione supersedissem. Qui enim maiores cruciatus aut dolores excogitari possunt, quam ego in Podagra iam vltra biennium, et in Calculo, hâc Hyeme, grauissimis omnium morbis, sustineo? Qui morbi hâc Hyeme alternatim me inuadentes, corpus meum tam debilitarunt, mentemque pene de statu suo præcipitarunt, vt ne literam quidem potuerim fereA exarare. Superiore autem æstate, quanquam præter Podagram, languores continuos, et ab omni labore horrores in me depræhenderem, nesciebam tamen, qualem morbum mihi essent apportaturæ et inflicturæ insolitæ illæ lassitudines: Quod mihi iam acerbissimis doloribus et cruciatibus satis innotuit. Spiritus enim Podagricus (ita enim iam libet eum appellare) in tantam malitiam in me excreuit, vt non tantum vias vrinarias, et renes, verum etiam venas mesaraicasA arenâ rubeâ, et tartaro arctissime constiparit, adeo vt quamprimum comederim aut biberim, propter nutrimenti a naturâ attracti inhibitionem, et vrinæ expulsionem, vehementissimos cruciatus et in mesaraicis, et in vijs vrinarijs, instar dolorum cholicorum sentiam, cibumque assumtum retinere in ventriculo propter nauseam et vomitum nequeamA. Et extant in me non obscura signa, quæ eundem Spiritum in alijs quoque membris idem tentare non obscure arguunt. Tentaui Medicinam Galenicam, me curante eruditissimo Dn. Doct. BVTTERO, Medico Illustrissimi Principis nostri; verum sine omni successu. Cum ea vrinam pellere voluit, obstante tartaro, in vijs vrinarijs ante vesicam, vehementissimos cruciatus sustinui. Quid balnea quoque et insessiones possint, facile intelligis, cum propter Podagram pedes ipsis immittere prohibear; extremis autem membris non calefactis, intermedia vix debito modo calefiant. Quanquam herbarum virtutes licet tartarum fortassisA frangere possint, in aquam tamen eumA dissoluere non possuntA; quod hîc omnino necessarium est, si aut in intestina regurgitare, aut per vesicam abire transudando debeat, morbusque radicitus curari. Num etiam hæc tanta spiritus Podagrici malitia possit ex fecibus Salem Petræ in me generare, eumque ad extremum gradum subtilitatis continuâ sublimatione perducere, adeoque mihi in momento vitam eripere, tibi expendendum relinquo, qui facile intelligis, quid notem. Hic enim Spiritus, cum fixæ, vt scis, sit Naturæ; reliqui autem Spiritus meliores, qui huius malitiam retundere possunt, sint volatiles, et per cogitationum intentiones, quibus me exuere nequeo, quotidie absumantur, an non in immensum virtutem suam augere, et tandem sibi solum totum imperiumA assumere, a nullo impeditus, poterit? Proinde ad sola Diuina merito me totum conuerterem, omissis Philosophicis, quanquam et hæc Diuina sint. Attamen cum iam qualemqualem cruciatuum remissionem quodammodo sentiam, pro Amore in te meo, qui certe vulgaris non est, et pro nostra Amicitia intermittere non potui, quin aliquid saltem, quod mihi in his meis ærumnis in mentem venire possit, ad tuas Disputationes iam seorsim in Charta ad me missas, responderem.

Quod igitur COPERNICVM de triplici motu Terræ impugnando, priore loco de diurno motu affers; si Terra 24. horis circumuolui fingatur, fieri non posse, vt globus plumbeus ex altissima turri demissus, punctum Terræ infra se positum, perpendiculariter ad amussim contingat; cum illud propter velocissimum Terræ motum, sese necessario interea subducatA, globumque plumbeum post se relinquat: Hoc satis a COPERNICO enodatum et dissolutum est. Cadentium enim, et ascendentium, vt ipse ait, duplicem fateamur esse motum oportet Mundi comparatione, et omnino compositum ex recto, et circulari: Quandoquidem quæ pondere suo deprimuntur, cum sint maxime terrea, non dubium, quin eandem seruent partes Naturam, quam suum totum. Id quod tibi multo minus dubium esse debet, cum tu ex meliore Philosophia scias, quod Natura Naturâ delectetur, Naturaque Naturam retineat. Si corporibus illis sui motus scientia est naturalis, vt tu vis, erit ea etiam partibus. Quemadmodum enim in alijs rebus naturalibus, verbi causa, partes Auri retinent Naturam totius, nec eo modo separatæ a toto id, quod a Natura ipsis peculiariter et specifice insitumA erat, abijciunt: Ita et in his corporibus, quæ motum, vt tu non inepteA vis, naturalem habent, partes primariam hanc insitam sibi prærogatiuam, et præcipuam ac specificam dignitatem non abijcient. Sed quid de casu rerum grauium solicitus esA, cum omnia, quæ in superficie Terræ libera et a toto separata iacent, quinimo ipsa Turris, ex qua Globus plumbeus dimitteretur, ipsaque ædificia ruerent, atque a Terræ motu relinquerentur necesse esset, si partes non retinerent motum totius: quod quam sit contra Naturæ Sapientiam, nemo non videt. Quod autem de Supputationibus affers, Terram ea ratione in vno secundo temporis scrupuloA reuolui debere in borealioribus ibi plagis sesquicentum passus maiores proxime: Quæso vtrum censebitur credibilius et sapientiæ NaturaeA, quæ semper per pauciora agit, conuenientius: an vt sesquicentum passus, an vero vt tot millia non passuum, sed milliarium Germanicorum in vno secundo temporis scrupulo conficiat? et quidem sub eodem effectu et fine? An non id vehementissime cum dicta Naturæ sapientia, qua semper pauciora et breuiora eligit, pugnabit, si assumto cœli motu per omnium Stellarum et Planetarum diuersos, vehementissimos, menteque humana incomprehensibiles atque etiam innumerabiles cursus efficere cogetur, quod poterat in vno facilime præstare? An non et hoc, quod ea ratione Planetis duplex et Ubi ipsi contrarius inesset centri motus? Nescio etiam, an fixa Sidera in tanta motus pernicitate, tam constanter suas ab inuicem distantias custodire possent. Sed sexcenta eiuscemodi proferriA possent, quæ satis conuincunt, motum diurnum in nullo alio corpore quam in terra quærendum esseA.

Ad annuum autem motum quod attinet, cur mihi non verisimile videatur, spatium a Sole ad Saturnum tot vicibus contineri intra Saturnum et affixarum Stellarum remotionem? aut quid absurdi sequitur, si Stella tertiæ Magnitudinis æquat totum orbem annuum? An id aut cum voluntate diuina pugnat, aut diuinæ Naturæ impossibile est, aut infinitæ Naturæ non competit? Hæc demonstranda omnino tibi sunt, si absurdi quid hinc colligere volueris. Non tam facile absurditatis argui possunt, quæ vulgo absurda primâ fronte videntur, sed longe maior est diuina Sapientia et Maiestas. Et quantumcunque etiam Mundi Vastitatem, et Magnitudinem concedas, nullam tamen proportionem ad infinitum Creatorem habebit. Quo maior Rex, eo maius et amplius palatium decere putat suam MaiestatemA. Quid cogitabis de DEO?

In tertio vero terræ motu, cur axis terræ non annuatim reflecteretur, vt quiescere nihilominus appareret? aut cur non axem et centrum duplici diuersoque motu agitari detur? Si terra impacta esset duro aliquo orbi et ab eo circumferretur, hæc fortassis locum habere possent. At cum terra liberrime in aëre pendeat, cur non, si centrum promoueatur, axis nihilominus super eodem centro possit nutare eo, quo Natura velit? Centrum enim semper in axe est, et est quasi Polus, vt ita dicam, reflexionis axis, nec vna reflectitur, sed respectu reflexionis est immotum. At hæc axis reflexio facilius intelligitur, si per idem terræ centrum etiam ducatur axis Zodiaci, vero Zodiaci axi parallelus, qui in immensitateA Sphæræ cum vero axe idem videbitur, circa quem videbitur axis terræ reflecti, non quidem ad Angulos rectos, sed ad tantum Angulum, quanta est Zodiaci obliquitas. Scio hoc in loco COPERNICVM esse admodum obscurum, nec facile perceptibilem. Sed hæc alio modo longe facilius possunt explicari, nec opus est triplici terræ motu, sed sufficiunt diurnus, et annuus. Nec axis terræ reuera reflectitur, siquidem manet semperA idem Angulus intersectionis planorum Eclipticæ et Æquatoris, axisque terræ motu annuo ita circumducitur, vt semper paralleliter versus eandem mundi partem spectet, adeoque propter euanescentiam orbis annui in immensitate Sphæræ ad vnum et idem punctum videatur semper collimare: Ex quibus, superueniente motu diurno, reflexio, tanquam effectus resultare videtur. Sed hæc fusius et enucleatius DEO dante, inuenies in secundo libro nostrarum Obseruationum, vbi de præcessione æquinoctiorum agemus. Librationem quoque licet in cursu Mercurij non probem, tamen eiusmodi esse video, vt in planis circulorum sese variantibus, naturalior motus excogitari non possit. Sed horum omnium explicatio nimis obscure a COPERNICO proposita est, et effectus cum causa sine distinctione ita confusus, vt quid velit, non facile a quoquam intelligatur. Sed plus intendo mentem, quam valetudo mea patitur. Alibi hæc fusius habebis.

Cometam eadem die, quâ tu, vidi, et Illustrissimo Principi nostro ostendi, qui apud me commorabatur, donec Horizontem subiret. Obseruaui eum, quantum in hac mea valetudine potui: quarum qualiumcunque obseruationum copiam seu exemplar ad te mitto, vt ipse inde motum et congruentiam cum tuis eruas.

Hæc igitur hac vice tibi sufficiant in his meis ærumnis, quæ quantæ sint, facili potes assequi coniectura ex eo, quod iam in flore ætatis constitutus, eiusmodi morbos sustineam. Desidero Oleum Vitrioli rubicundissimum illud et fragrantissimum, itemque Oleum Tartari, non quidem communis, sed singulariter per singulare mysterium indagati, vt te sine dubio non fugit, aliasque insuper extractiones et solutiones. Pro quibus si fortassis nuncium ad te ablegauero (Hîc enim, vt et vix in alijs Germaniæ locis, iustam eorum præparationem, nancisci nequeo) non dubito, quin pro nostrâ Amicitiâ mihi ea sis communicaturus, salutique meæ pro virili consulturus: Aut occasione oblatâ, ipse in posterum, pro Disputationibus Medicamenta mitte, quorum ratio iam merito potior est. Sed de tua in me beneuolentia non dubito.

Vale mi Nobiliss. Dn. TYCHO.
Cassellis ex lecto meo, 18. Aprilis, Anno 1590.
T. T. CHRISTOPHORVS ROTHMANNVS.

English translation machine translation, Probable

To the most noble man, most renowned for virtue, wisdom, and ingenious and erudite learning, Master Tycho Brahe, Lord of Knudstrup, Danish knight, of all the mathematicians of our age the most pre-eminent, etc., his lord to be honoured with all observance — heartiest greeting.

If, as Ovid sings, "songs come forth drawn from a serene mind," most noble and most excellent Master Tycho, then much more do the most profound and most subtle mathematical disputations — which are not to be compared with the composing of verses — require a mind utterly free from disturbances. I should not, therefore, have committed an act either alien to our friendship, or undeserving of any excuse (as you seem to charge), had I forgone a reply. For what greater torments or pains can be conceived than those I endure in gout [podagra], now for over two years, and in the stone [calculus], this winter — the gravest of all diseases? These diseases, assailing me by turns this winter, so weakened my body and all but cast my mind from its seat, that I could scarcely [barely] even trace a letter. Last summer, moreover, although besides the gout I detected in myself continual languors and a horror of all labour, yet I did not know what disease those unwonted weaknesses would bring upon me and inflict; which has now become known to me well enough through the bitterest pains and torments. For the gouty spirit [spiritus podagricus] (for so I now please to call it) has grown to such malignancy in me that it has most tightly clogged not only the urinary passages and the kidneys, but also the mesaraic [mesenteric] veins, with red gravel and with tartar — so that, as soon as I have eaten or drunk, owing to the inhibition of the nourishment drawn in by nature and to the expulsion of urine, I feel the most violent torments both in the mesaraic [veins] and in the urinary passages, after the manner of colic pains, and I cannot retain the food I have taken in my stomach, on account of nausea and vomiting. And there are in me not obscure signs which argue, not obscurely, that the same spirit is attempting the same thing in my other members too. I tried Galenic medicine, being treated by the most learned Doctor Butterus, physician to our Most Illustrious Prince — but without any success. When it sought to drive out the urine, with the tartar blocking [the way], I endured the most violent torments in the urinary passages before the bladder. What baths too and sitz-baths can do, you easily understand, since on account of the gout I am forbidden to put my feet into them; and the outer members not being warmed, the parts between are scarcely warmed in due measure. And although the virtues of herbs may perhaps be able to break up the tartar, yet they cannot dissolve it into water — which is here altogether necessary, if it must either flow back into the intestines, or pass off through the bladder by sweating out, and the disease be cured from the roots. Whether this so great malignancy of the gouty spirit can also generate saltpetre [salt of stone, sal petrae] in me out of the dregs, and bring it by continual sublimation to the extreme degree of subtlety, and so snatch away my life in a moment, I leave to you to weigh — you who easily understand what I mean. For since this spirit, as you know, is of a fixed nature, while the other and better spirits, which can blunt its malignancy, are volatile, and are daily consumed through the strainings of thought, of which I cannot rid myself — will it not be able to increase its power immeasurably, and at last to assume to itself alone the whole dominion, hindered by none? Therefore I should rightly turn myself wholly to divine matters alone, setting aside philosophical ones — though these too are divine. Nevertheless, since I now in some manner feel some remission, such as it is, of the torments, for my love toward you, which is surely no common love, and for our friendship, I could not refrain from at least replying — with whatever might be able to come into my mind amid these afflictions of mine — to your disputations, now sent to me separately on a sheet.

As, then, to what you bring forward in assailing Copernicus on the threefold motion of the Earth: in the first place, concerning the diurnal motion — that, if the Earth be supposed to revolve in 24 hours, it cannot be that a leaden ball let down from a very high tower should strike exactly perpendicular upon the point of the Earth set beneath it, since that point, on account of the Earth's most rapid motion, must necessarily withdraw itself meanwhile and leave the leaden ball behind it: this has been sufficiently unravelled and resolved by Copernicus. For of things falling and rising, as he himself says, we must confess the motion to be twofold in relation to the World, and altogether compounded of the rectilinear and the circular — seeing that the things which are pressed down by their own weight, being most earthy, [leave] no doubt that the parts preserve the same nature as their whole. Which ought to be far less doubtful to you, since you know from a better philosophy that Nature delights in Nature, and Nature retains Nature. If to those bodies the knowledge of their own motion is natural, as you will have it, it will belong to the parts also. For just as in other natural things — for example, the parts of gold retain the nature of the whole, nor when thus separated from the whole do they cast off that which had been peculiarly and specifically implanted in them by Nature — so too in these bodies, which have motion natural [to them], as you not ineptly will have it, the parts will not cast off this primary prerogative implanted in them, and their chief and specific dignity. But why are you anxious about the fall of heavy things, when all things which lie on the surface of the Earth free and separated from the whole — nay, the very tower from which the leaden ball would be released, and the buildings themselves — would necessarily collapse and be left behind by the Earth's motion, if the parts did not retain the motion of the whole: which, how contrary it is to the Wisdom of Nature, no one fails to see. But as to what you bring forward concerning the computations — that on that reckoning the Earth must in one second of time revolve, in the more northerly regions there, about a hundred and fifty paces or more: I ask, which will be reckoned the more credible, and the more conformable to the wisdom of Nature (which always acts through fewer means) — that it should accomplish a hundred and fifty paces, or that it should accomplish so many thousands, not of paces, but of German miles, in one second of time? and that to the same effect and end? Will it not most vehemently clash with the said wisdom of Nature, which always chooses fewer and shorter [ways], if, the motion of the heaven being assumed, it must produce, through the diverse courses of all the stars and planets — most violent, incomprehensible to the human mind, and indeed innumerable — what it could most easily have effected in one [body]? And is there not also this: that on that reckoning there would be in the planets a twofold motion of the centre, and one contrary to it [in its place / Ubi]? I do not know, either, whether the fixed stars, in so great a swiftness of motion, could so constantly keep their distances from one another. But six hundred things of the kind could be brought forward, which sufficiently prove that the diurnal motion is to be sought in no other body than in the Earth.

But as concerns the annual motion: why should it not seem probable to me that the space from the Sun to Saturn is contained so many times over within [the interval] between Saturn and the remoteness of the fixed stars? Or what absurdity follows, if a star of the third magnitude equals the whole annual orb? Does this either clash with the divine will, or is it impossible to the divine Nature, or does it not befit the infinite Nature? These things you must by all means demonstrate, if you would gather any absurdity from this. Those things are not so easily to be convicted of absurdity which to the common run seem absurd at first sight; rather, far greater is the divine Wisdom and Majesty. And however great a vastness and magnitude of the World you grant, yet it will have no proportion to the infinite Creator. The greater the King, the greater and more ample a palace he thinks befits his Majesty. What will you think of GOD?

But in the third motion of the Earth: why should not the axis of the Earth be turned back [reflected] annually, so that it should nonetheless appear to be at rest? Or why should it not be granted that the axis and the centre are agitated by a twofold and diverse motion? If the Earth were fixed into some hard orb and carried round by it, these [objections] perhaps might have place. But since the Earth hangs most freely in the air, why not — if the centre be advanced — should the axis nonetheless be able to nod upon the same centre, in whatever way Nature wills? For the centre is always in the axis, and is, so to speak, the Pole of the reflection of the axis; nor is it reflected together [with it], but in respect of the reflection is unmoved. But this reflection of the axis is more easily understood if through the same centre of the Earth there be also drawn an axis of the Zodiac, parallel to the true axis of the Zodiac, which in the immensity of the Sphere will appear the same as the true axis, about which the axis of the Earth will appear to be reflected — not indeed at right angles, but at as great an angle as is the obliquity of the Zodiac. I know that in this place Copernicus is exceedingly obscure, and not easily intelligible. But these things can be explained far more easily in another way, nor is there need of a threefold motion of the Earth, but the diurnal and the annual suffice. Nor is the axis of the Earth really reflected, since there remains always the same angle of intersection of the planes of the Ecliptic and the Equator, and the axis of the Earth is so carried round by the annual motion that it always looks parallel-wise toward the same part of the World, and so, on account of the vanishing of the annual orb in the immensity of the Sphere, it appears always to aim at one and the same point: from which, the diurnal motion supervening, the reflection seems to result, as it were an effect. But these things, God granting, you will find more fully and more clearly set out in the second book of our Observations, where we shall treat of the precession of the equinoxes. The libration too, although in the course of Mercury I do not approve it, yet I see it to be of such a kind that, in the planes of the circles varying themselves, a more natural motion cannot be devised. But the explanation of all these things has been propounded by Copernicus too obscurely, and the effect so confounded with the cause without distinction, that what he means is not easily understood by anyone. But I am straining my mind more than my health allows. You shall have these things more fully elsewhere.

The comet I saw on the same day as you, and showed it to our Most Illustrious Prince, who was staying with me, until it sank below the Horizon. I observed it, as far as in this state of health I could: of which observations, such as they are, I send you a copy or specimen, that you may yourself draw out from it the motion and the agreement with your own.

Let these things, then, suffice you for this time, amid these afflictions of mine — how great they are, you can easily reach by conjecture from this, that, set as I now am in the flower of my age, I endure diseases of this kind. I long for that most ruddy and most fragrant Oil of Vitriol, and likewise Oil of Tartar — not indeed of the common sort, but of that singularly sought out through a singular mystery, as without doubt does not escape you — and besides other extractions and solutions. For which, if perhaps I shall dispatch a messenger to you (for here, as also scarcely in other places of Germany, I cannot obtain a proper preparation of them), I do not doubt that, for our Friendship's sake, you will impart them to me, and will look to my health to the best of your power. Or, when occasion offers, do you yourself in future send Medicaments in return for the Disputations, the consideration of which now deservedly weighs more with me. But of your goodwill toward me I do not doubt.

Farewell, my most noble Master Tycho.
At Kassel, from my bed, 18 April, the year 1590.
Most devoted to you,
Christoph Rothmann.

Apparatus

  • Correspondent. Christophorus Rothmannus = Christoph Rothmann (c. 1550 – c. 1600), mathematician and astronomer at the court of Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel. His correspondence with Tycho on cosmology, instruments, and the comet of 1585 is among the most substantial of the period; this letter is one of those Tycho selected for his printed Epistolae astronomicae (1596). Rothmann here defends the Copernican explanations against Tycho's objections — though he is no doctrinaire Copernican — while pleading grave illness.
  • Edition of record. Tychonis Brahe Dani epistolarum astronomicarum libri (Uraniborg, 1596), Liber I — the volume Tycho printed himself; reprinted in Dreyer, Opera Omnia VI. No manuscript witness is recorded in the source header.
  • Salutation / titulature. DOMINO DE KNUDSTRUP, EQUITI DANO = "Lord of Knudstrup, Danish knight" — Tycho's ancestral seat and his noble rank; MATHEMATICORUM NOSTRO SECULO OMNIUM PRAeSTANTISSIMO = "of all the mathematicians of our age the most pre-eminent." S. P. = salutem plurimam (dicit), "heartiest greeting". Subscription T. T. = Tuus Totus / Tibi Toto, "Wholly yours / Most devoted to you."
  • Ovid allusion. The opening vt OVIDIVS canit, Carmina proueniunt animo deducta sereno paraphrases Ovid, Tristia I.1.39 (carmina proveniunt animo deducta sereno) — verses come from a serene mind; rendered as a quotation. The printed text begins "ASI, vt…" where the engrossed initial capital S of Si has dropped from the extraction (the decorative initial letter); the sense is Si … Carmina proueniunt …, multo magis … Disputationes … requirent ("If … songs come forth …, much more do … disputations … require [a serene mind]"). Reading adopted: [S]i; flagged.
  • Medicine / alchemy. Podagra = gout (here gout of the feet); Calculus = the stone (, lithiasis); venae mesaraicae = the mesaraic (mesenteric) veins; tartarum = tartar (Paracelsian morbid concretion); dolores cholici = colic pains; Medicina Galenica = Galenic medicine; Salem Petrae / Sal Petrae = saltpetre (here imagined generated in the body by sublimation). The Spiritus Podagricus ("gouty spirit") of "fixed" vs. "volatile" nature is Rothmann's own Paracelsian-iatrochemical coinage — kept literal and flagged, not modernised. Oleum Vitrioli = Oil of Vitriol (concentrated sulphuric acid); Oleum Tartari = Oil of Tartar (deliquesced potassium carbonate) — alchemical preparations Rothmann requests of Tycho, who ran a laboratory at Uraniborg.
  • Physician. Dn. Doct. Butterus, Medico Illustrissimi Principis nostri = Doctor Butterus (Butter), physician to the Landgrave Wilhelm IV ("our Most Illustrious Prince").
  • Astronomy / cosmology. The body is a point-by-point reply to Tycho's objections against the threefold motion of the Earth attributed to Copernicus (diurnal rotation, annual revolution, and the "third motion" of the axis — the conical motion Copernicus posited to keep the axis fixed in direction). Technical renderings by this chapter's convention: motus diurnus / annuus = diurnal / annual motion; praecessio aequinoctiorum = precession of the equinoxes; obliquitas Zodiaci = obliquity of the Zodiac (ecliptic); libratio (in cursu Mercurij) = libration (of Mercury's course); orbis annuus = the annual orb (Earth's orbit); Stella tertiae Magnitudinis = a star of the third magnitude. axis … reflecti / reflexio is Copernicus's term for the conical sweep of the axis — rendered "reflected / reflection" (Rothmann argues the apparent "reflection" is a derived effect, not a third real motion).
  • The falling-ball argument. globus plumbeus ex altissima turri demissus = the leaden ball dropped from a high tower (the standard anti-rotation objection); Rothmann answers with the doctrine that the parts share the nature and motion of the whole (partes retinent Naturam / motum totius).
  • Measures. sesquicentum passus = "a hundred and fifty paces" (one and a half hundred); milliaria Germanica = German miles. secundum temporis scrupulum = "a second of time" (the scrupulum being the sixtieth subdivision) — rendered "one second of time."
  • Ubi ipsi contrarius — An non et hoc, quod ea ratione Planetis duplex et Ubi ipsi contrarius inesset centri motus? The clause is compressed; Ubi (the scholastic category "Where / place") is here used substantively, so "a twofold motion of the centre, and one contrary to it [in its place]." The exact construction is elliptical in the printed text; rendered closely and flagged.
  • Theology. Quo maior Rex, eo maius et amplius palatium decere putat suam Maiestatem. Quid cogitabis de DEO? — the analogy that a greater King requires a greater palace, applied to God and the immensity of the heavens, is Rothmann's signature defence of a vast (Copernican) cosmos; kept as written.
  • The comet. Cometam eadem die, quâ tu, vidi — Rothmann reports observing a comet the same day Tycho did, shown to the Landgrave, and encloses his observations. (The comet of early 1590; the reference is to the body Tycho had asked about.)
  • Source-text artifacts. The source as extracted carries stray capital "A" characters fused to the end of certain words at edition line-breaks and at the very end of the letter (e.g. fereA, mesaraicasA, nequeamA, fortassisA, eumA, possuntA, subducatA, insitumA, inepteA, esA, scrupuloA, NaturaeA, proferriA, esseA, imperiumA, MaiestatemA, immensitateA, semperA, ROTHMANNVS.A). These are apparatus/footnote anchor markers of the printed edition (variant-note sigla), not part of Rothmann's Latin; they are reproduced verbatim in the Source block by this chapter's convention but carry no lexical value and are disregarded in the translation. The decorative dropped initial [S]i (see Ovid note) is the only place an editorial reading was adopted.
  • Negation check. The dense conditional/negative argumentation was verified clause by clause: fieri non posse ("it cannot be"), non dubium quin ("no doubt that"), nec … abijciunt ("nor do they cast off"), non abijcient ("will not cast off"), nemo non videt ("no one fails to see" = everyone sees), An non … pugnabit? ("Will it not clash?"), Nec axis … reuera reflectitur ("Nor is the axis really reflected"), nancisci nequeo ("I cannot obtain"), non dubito ("I do not doubt"). Each negation in the source is reproduced; none added or dropped.
  • Calendar / place. Cassellis … 18. Aprilis, Anno 1590 = at Kassel, 18 April 1590. The Hesse-Kassel court used the Julian calendar (Old Style) at this date; so dated [O.S.]. The edition label ("1590. 18. april. Kassel") agrees with the dateline; no discrepancy. ex lecto meo = "from my bed," consistent with the body's account of illness.
