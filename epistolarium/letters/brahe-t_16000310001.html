The original

NOBILISSIMO VIRO TYCHONI BRAHE IOSEPHUS SCALIGER IULIJ CÆSARIS F. S.

QUONAM fato meo accidisse dicam, Vir nobilissime, ut qui amicis meis diligentiam, studium et officium meum omnibus in rebus, omni tempore probare paratus sim, non solum omnibus modis, quibus id præstare possim, destituar, sed omnia penitus adversa mihi experiar? Ecce ex quo literas tuas accepi, quas Witebergâ discedens ad me dedisti, nunquam conquievi, quin de te et tuarum rerum statu solicite quæsiverim. Nam postremæ tuæ, quæ mihi omnem scrupulum eximere debebant, eæ vero incertiorem me tuarum rerum reddiderunt. Nanque ex illis neque quando constiteris neque ubi lares posueris, colligere potui. Nunquam tamen ab eo tempore destiti percontandi de te, præsertim quum quidam te in Silesia esse et ibi sedem fortunarum tuarum statuisse, tam constanter affirmarent, ut soli tua negotia scire et curare viderentur. Quamdiu M. CHRISTIANUS Danus, vir optimus et tui studiosissimus, hîc fuit, aliqua spes relinquebatur aut meas literas ad te tuto dandi, aut tuas eliciendi. Sed ille prius discessit, quam postremas tuas acciperem. Nunc eæ, quas hodie accepi, centum triginta diebus postquam datæ sunt, omnino bearunt me, quod me omni cura de te, quæ me anxium habebat, liberarunt, quum hactenus penderem animo, quo terrarum meas ad te mitterem, qui ubi consisteres nesciebam. Quanquam vero virtus tua et eruditio eximia non sinit te latere, neque tuas res ignorari, tamen tanta est locorum intercapedo, quæ te a nobis dividit, ut quod de Sole necessarium est, eum prius apud Eoas nationes, quam Hesperias oriri, id omnino tibi conveniat, cuius lucem nobis serius sentire continget quam ijs, qui te fruuntur hospite et a te præsente hauriunt, quod nos ipsis, ne quid dissimulem, invidemus.

Quanta vero lætitia istæ postremæ nos pertuderint, uno verbo non dixerim. Hoc unum credas velim, nihil mihi gratius esse potuisse, quam tibi otium contigisse, quod et tuis studijs et dignitati tuæ convenientissimum esset. Hoc enim unum semper spirarunt vota mea. Felicem te, mi TYCHO, qui non solum universam Europam testem eruditionis tuæ, sed etiam Cæsarem fautorem, adiutorem, et, quod cœlum ipsum invidere possit, studiorum socium nactus sis. Patriam, quam natales dederant, deseruisti, ut eam, quam doctrina tua meruit, adipiscereris. Regem suum Danis Natura dedit: uni Dano TYCHONI virtus Imperatorem adoptavit. Ab altero te abesse dolendum erat, nisi maiorem, in quo vota tua conquiescerent, haberes. Te vero, magne amice, per ius amicitiæ nostræ oro, ut me de statu, instituto et ratione rerum tuarum frequentius certiorem facias, ne qua in posterum mihi de te dubitatio relinquatur. Nam quæ videri posset cessatio aut negligentia in officio literarum, ea potius fuerit ignoratio status rerum tuarum.

Multa vero erant, de quibus te convenire per literas vellem, siquidem per properantiam istius liceret, qui mihi tuas reddidit: qui, ut ad tuas statim responderem, a me exegit, et ita in ipso articulo oppressit, ut vix spatium reliquerit his exarandis. Diatribam enim meam de præcessione æquinoctiorum et confutatione motus octavæ sphæræ ad te mittere cogito, in qua et innumera veterum testimonia a nemine hactenus animadversa studiose collegimus, et stellam illam, quam Polarem vocant, ante mille nongentos sexaginta sex annos in eo loco hæsisse, ubi hodie est, quinque luculentis vetustissimorum scriptorum testimonijs demonstramus. Præterea tot ἀτοπήματα et absurditates, quæ ex illa hypothesi motus octavæ sphæræ sequuntur, adducimus, ut nemo sit nisi imperitus aut invidus, qui contra hiscere audeat. Addo etiam, quod hactenus omnes latuit, præcessionem æquinoctiorum a THALETE usque et ANAXIMANDRO ad HIPPARCHUM admissam fuisse, primumque omnium HIPPARCHUM eam reiecisse, inducto sphæræ octavæ motu in consequentia, adeo ut contra fidem oculorum extremam in cauda Cynosuræ sive Polarem, ut vulgus vocat, esse omnium septem, quæ in asterismo illo sunt, australissimam prodiderit, quæ tamen fuit ante illum, et est hodie, eritque semper borealissima omnium. En quo posteritatem perduxit tanti viri autoritas. Nam ab illo usque hactenus vos ita creditis, et non alia re quam quod αὐτός ἔφα, quod in Mathesi nunquam locum habere debet. Nulli hominum generi hodie magis nocuit antiquitatis imperitia quam nationi Astrologorum. Nihil magis mirari mihi succurrit, quam nemini Astrologo de vitio illius hypotheseos suboluisse, neque quot et quanta absurda ex illa propagari necesse sit, si unum excipias COPERNICUM, qui et præcessionem æquinoctiorum et obliquitatem circuli æquinoctialis animadvertit, sed ignoratione rerum vetustarum ad absurdæ hypotheses confugit. Sane regularem et æquabilem Solis maximæ declinationis decrescentiam ab unica anticipatione æquinoctiorum pendere necesse est, quod a nobis optime demonstratum est. Aliter non potest, nisi per falsas hypotheses. Hinc sequitur polum mundi alium esse a polo æquinoctialis, et lineas meridianas moveri, neque in eadem sede semper hærere. Id nos egregie ex veteribus scriptoribus demonstramus. Denique tota disputatio est historia mathematica, prius testimonijs ab ultima memoria petitis collecta, deinde geometrice demonstrata. Ea adhuc in litura est. Quum per occupationes licebit in mundo describere, mittam ad te. Unum totam rem transigit, si stella polaris semper eidem loco hæsit, nullum locum esse pertinaciæ: omnia necessario, quæ dixi, consequi debere, atque adeo nullum esse motum octavæ sphæræ.

Quod de nobili viro GEORGIO HERWARTO ab HOËNBURG scribis, is iam dudum mihi de literis suis ad me notus est. Sed de stella Veneris quod scribit, videndum ne illi falsum aliquod Chronicon imposuerit homini eruditissimo, sed pro illo munere, quod gerit, occupatissimo, ut illi meliores auctores consulere non licuerit. Nam ut taceam absurditatem, quis, amabo, est iste Romanus Poëta sine prænomine et nomine? Anno quarto ante Christum natum hoc accidisse unde habet? Quis veterum hoc prodidit? Nam nullus eorum quidem, qui hodie supersunt, eius meminit, et quidem pauci supersunt, qui meminisse potuerint, OVIDIUS et MANILIUS, et nescio an alius præterea. Nam HORATIUS anno quinto ante primum Dionysiacum Christi obijt. Et PROPERTIUS nihil tale. Denique decepit hominem eruditum aut memoria aut scriptor malæ fidei.

De ALBATEGNIO utinam tibi satisfacere possem. Tantum abest, ut exemplar Arabicum nancisci possim, ut ne Latini quidem copia mihi sit impræsentia. Equidem mihi constat multa apud illum auctorem partim obscure, partim imperite reddita, quæ fidelius et lucidius explicari poterant. Mitto tibi MANILIUM nostrum, quem per otium ut tibi liceat legere, opto. Nam ex eius gemmis poteritis aliquando astrologiæ vestræ emblemata instruere. Nullus scriptorum neque apud Græcos neque apud Latinos tam male acceptus fuit, vel a mendis, vel a traiectionibus, vel ab ignoratione priscæ Astrologiæ.

Tuas literas rectius Augustam Vindelicorum mittere possis ad nobiliss. virum MARCUM VELSERUM, patritium et Reip. Augustanæ Septemvirum. Is Amstelradamum recte curabit eas. Nam profecto via Hamburgensis longinquior est: nisi aliter tibi videtur; hoc enim arbitrij tui facio. Tantum opto, ut literæ tuæ non solum tuto mihi, sed etiam cito reddantur.

Vale unice Astronomiæ vindex.

English translation machine translation, Secure

To the most noble man Tycho Brahe, Joseph Scaliger, son of Julius Caesar [Scaliger], [sends] greeting.

By what fate of mine shall I say it happened, most noble Man, that I — who am ready to prove my diligence, zeal, and service to my friends in all things, at all times — am not only deprived of all the means by which I might do it, but experience all things utterly adverse to me? Behold: from the time I received your letter, which you sent me on leaving Wittenberg, I never rested but anxiously inquired about you and the state of your affairs. For your last [letter], which ought to have removed every scruple from me, made me the more uncertain of your affairs; for from it I could gather neither when you had settled, nor where you had set your household. Yet from that time I never ceased asking about you — especially since some so constantly affirmed that you were in Silesia and had set the seat of your fortunes there, that they seemed alone to know and tend your business. As long as M. Christianus the Dane [Longomontanus], an excellent man and most devoted to you, was here, some hope was left of sending my letters to you safely, or of eliciting yours. But he departed before I received your last. Now those [letters], which I received today, a hundred and thirty days after they were sent, have wholly blessed me, in that they freed me of all the care about you that held me anxious — since hitherto I hung in mind as to where on earth I should send mine to you, not knowing where you stayed. But although your virtue and eminent erudition do not let you lie hidden, nor your affairs be unknown, yet so great is the distance of places dividing you from us, that what is necessary of the Sun — that it rises among the Eastern nations before the Western — is altogether fitting for you: whose light it falls to us to feel later than to those who enjoy you as a host, and drink from you present — which (not to dissemble) we envy them.

With how great joy those last [letters] struck me through, I could not say in one word. This one thing I would have you believe: that nothing could have been more pleasing to me than that leisure has befallen you, most fitting both for your studies and for your dignity. For this one thing my prayers always breathed. Happy you, my Tycho, who have got not only all Europe as witness of your erudition, but also the Emperor as patron, helper, and — what heaven itself might envy — companion of your studies. You deserted the fatherland which your birth had given, to gain the one your learning earned. Nature gave the Danes their King; to one Dane, Tycho, virtue adopted an Emperor. It would have been to be grieved that you were absent from the one [fatherland], had you not a greater, in which your prayers might rest. But I beg you, great friend, by the right of our friendship, to inform me more frequently of the state, plan, and method of your affairs, lest any doubt about you be left me hereafter; for what might seem a cessation or negligence in the duty of letters, has rather been ignorance of the state of your affairs.

Many things there were of which I would meet you by letter, if the haste of the man who delivered yours to me allowed — who exacted of me that I answer yours at once, and so pressed me in the very moment that he scarce left space for scratching these. For I purpose to send you my Diatriba on the precession of the equinoxes and the confutation of the motion of the eighth sphere — in which I have studiously collected innumerable testimonies of the ancients, hitherto noticed by no one, and demonstrate, by five clear testimonies of the most ancient writers, that the star they call the Pole-star stuck, 1966 years ago, in the same place where it is today. Besides, I adduce so many absurdities [ἀτοπήματα] that follow from that hypothesis of the eighth sphere's motion, that no one but the unskilled or the envious would dare to open his mouth against [it]. I add also — what hitherto escaped all — that the precession of the equinoxes was admitted from Thales and Anaximander down to Hipparchus, and that Hipparchus, first of all, rejected it, introducing the eighth sphere's motion in consequentia — so that, against the evidence of the eyes, he reported the star at the tip of Cynosura's tail (or the Pole-star, as the vulgar call it) to be, of all the seven in that asterism, the most southern — which yet was before him, is today, and will always be the most northern of all. See whither the authority of so great a man led posterity! For from him until now you [astronomers] so believe — and for no other reason than that "he himself said it" [αὐτός ἔφα], which ought never to have place in Mathematics. To no class of men today has ignorance of antiquity done more harm than to the nation of the Astrologers. Nothing comes to me to wonder at more than that no Astrologer got wind of the fault of that hypothesis — nor how many and how great absurdities must be propagated from it — if you except Copernicus alone, who noticed both the precession of the equinoxes and the obliquity of the equinoctial circle, but, from ignorance of ancient things, fled to absurd hypotheses. For the regular and even decrease of the Sun's maximum declination must depend on the single anticipation of the equinoxes — which has been most well demonstrated by us. Otherwise it cannot be, except by false hypotheses. Hence it follows that the pole of the world is other than the pole of the equinoctial, and that the meridian lines move, and do not always stick in the same seat. This we demonstrate excellently from the ancient writers. In short, the whole disputation is a mathematical history — first collected from testimonies sought from remotest memory, then demonstrated geometrically. It is still in draft. When my occupations allow it to be written out clean, I will send it to you. One thing settles the whole matter: if the Pole-star always stuck in the same place, there is no room for obstinacy; all I have said must necessarily follow — and so there is no motion of the eighth sphere.

As to what you write of the noble man Georg Herwart von Hohenburg, he has long been known to me by his letters to me. But as to what he writes about the star of Venus, it must be seen lest some false Chronicle imposed on that most learned man — but, for the office he bears, most busy, so that he could not consult better authors. For — to be silent of the absurdity — who, pray, is that Roman Poet without praenomen and nomen? Whence does he have that this happened in the fourth year before Christ was born? Which of the ancients reported this? For none indeed of those surviving today mentions it — and few survive who could have mentioned it: Ovid and Manilius, and I know not whether any other besides. For Horace died in the fifth year before the first Dionysian [year] of Christ; and Propertius [says] nothing such. In short, either his memory or a writer of bad faith deceived the learned man.

Of Al-Battani [Albategnius], would that I could satisfy you. So far am I from being able to get an Arabic copy, that I have not even a Latin one to hand at present. For my part I am sure that many things in that author are rendered partly obscurely, partly unskilfully, which could be explained more faithfully and lucidly. I send you our Manilius, which I wish you may read at leisure; for from its gems you [astronomers] may sometime furnish the emblems of your astrology. No writer, neither among the Greeks nor among the Latins, was so badly handled — whether by corruptions, or by transpositions, or by ignorance of ancient Astrology.

Your letters you might more rightly send to Augsburg, to the most noble man Marcus Welser, patrician and Septemvir of the Augsburg Republic. He will properly forward them to Amsterdam. For truly the Hamburg route is longer — unless it seem otherwise to you; for this I leave to your judgement. I only wish that your letters be delivered to me not only safely, but also quickly.

Farewell, singular champion of Astronomy.
Leiden, the 6th day before the Ides of March [10 March], New Style, 1600.

I have not yet had occasion to read your treatise on the letters of the most noble Georg Herwart, except cursorily, since immediately upon reading your letter I betook myself to writing this one. Now I shall read everything carefully. Farewell again.

Apparatus

  • Direction & correspondent. Incoming, Joseph Justus Scaliger (1540–1609, son of Julius Caesar Scaliger) at Leiden → Tycho. The letter Tycho answers in brahe-t_16000723001 / 16000623001 (23 July 1600) — read the two as a pair (Tycho there rejects Scaliger's "moving meridians" and offers his own solar theory of precession).
  • Significance — Scaliger's full precession theory and his Diatriba against the eighth-sphere motion (a major scientific statement).
  • The Diatriba. Scaliger announces his (still-draft) Diatriba de praecessione aequinoctiorum — "a mathematical history," collecting ancient testimonies and then demonstrating geometrically that the Pole-star held its place 1966 years ago as today (by "five clear testimonies"), so that the "eighth sphere's motion" is a fiction generating only absurdities (ἀτοπήματα).
  • The Hipparchus thesis. His striking historical claim: precession was accepted "from Thales and Anaximander to Hipparchus," and Hipparchus first rejected it, introducing the eighth-sphere motion in consequentia — even misreporting Polaris as the southernmost of its asterism against plain sight. Posterity, Scaliger charges, followed merely on "ipse dixit" (αὐτός ἔφα), which has no place in mathematics.
  • Copernicus and the conclusion. Only Copernicus noticed precession and the obliquity-change, "but fled to absurd hypotheses." Scaliger derives the even decrease of the Sun's maximum declination from "the single anticipation of the equinoxes," and concludes — the point Tycho will reject — that "the pole of the world is other than the pole of the equinoctial, and the meridian lines move."
  • The Herwart "Venus-star" critique. On a chronological datum Herwart von Hohenburg had circulated about a "star of Venus" in the 4th year before Christ (a Star-of-Bethlehem-adjacent chronology question), Scaliger is scathing: it rests on "a false Chronicle" and an unidentifiable "Roman Poet without praenomen or nomen"; the only poets who could have recorded it are Ovid and Manilius (Horace died too early, Propertius is silent). (Cf. the Herwart letter, brahe-t_16000911001.)
  • Al-Battani & Manilius. Scaliger cannot supply al-Battani (no Arabic or Latin copy to hand) but criticises its existing renderings; and sends Tycho his own edition of Manilius ("from its gems furnish the emblems of your astrology") — the most corruption-marred of all ancient authors. (Tycho thanks him for it in the reply of 23 July 1600.)
  • The courier route. Scaliger advises routing letters via Marcus Welser at Augsburg → Amsterdam, rather than the longer Hamburg route.
  • The encomium. The famous compliment: "Nature gave the Danes their King; to one Dane, Tycho, virtue adopted an Emperor" — and the Sun-rises-in-the-East conceit for the distance between them.
  • Cross-references. The other half of the Scaliger precession exchange (with the reply of 23 July 1600; and the earlier letter of 17 April 1599, where Tycho requested Hipparchus's equinoxes, the southern asterisms, and al-Battani); ties to the Herwart thread (Herwart's letter of 11 September 1600) and the chronology disputes (the letters to Wolfgang Franz of 27 March 1600 and to Bartholomäus Scultetus of 7 January 1600).
  • Persons & works named. Joseph Justus Scaliger; Longomontanus (M. Christianus Danus); Georg Herwart von Hohenburg; Thales, Anaximander, Hipparchus, Copernicus; Ovid, Manilius, Horace, Propertius; al-Battani (Albategnius); Marcus Welser (Augsburg). Places: Leiden, Silesia (the false rumour of Tycho's seat), Augsburg, Amsterdam, Hamburg.
  • Calendar. Lugduni Batavorum VI Eid. Martias, stylo novo CIↃIↃC → Leiden, 10 March 1600 [N.S.] (VI Ides of March = 10 March; CIↃIↃC = 1600); header and label agree. Tycho received it "130 days after"? — no: Scaliger received Tycho's after 130 days; Tycho received this one "six weeks before" his July reply.
  • Greek terms reproduced. ἀτοπήματα ("absurdities"), αὐτός ἔφα ("he himself said it"), Eoas/Hesperias (Eastern/Western).
  • What this is. A closing paragraph, preceding the letter's actual farewell (given in the published tail), in which Scaliger acknowledges receipt of a piece of Tycho's own writing — a "diatriba" addressed to Georg Herwart — promising a fuller reading once this reply is off his desk.
  • "Diatriba" — a false friend. In this Neo-Latin usage "diatriba" means a learned discourse, dissertation, or treatise, carrying none of the pejorative "angry tirade" sense the English cognate "diatribe" has since acquired; rendered here as "treatise" to avoid the false friend.
  • Georg Herwart identified. Johann Georg Herwart von Hohenburg (1553–1622), Chancellor of Bavaria, a learned correspondent of both Tycho and (later, more famously) Kepler, with whom he corresponded on both scientific and religious matters and whom he patronised [Mosley, Bearing the Heavens (Cambridge, 2007); Gingerich, An Annotated Census of Copernicus' De revolutionibus; Martens, Kepler's Philosophy and the New Astronomy]. The published tail of this same letter separately shows Scaliger discussing ancient astrology's mistreatment by copyists and editors — a topic entirely consistent with a Tycho–Herwart exchange on the correct interpretation of "prisca Astrologia."
  • The sender. Joseph Justus Scaliger (1540–1609), the preeminent classical philologist and chronologist of the age, then at Leiden; his letter as a whole (published tail) discusses postal routes for reaching him via Marcus Welser at Augsburg.
