5The manuscript witnesses

What survives of the letters as objects, by the edition’s own witness lines. tekstnet gives each document up to three witnesses with the sigla A, B, C — a city, an institution, a shelfmark, sometimes a word saying what the manuscript is. This section keeps those lines as they stand, classes each by the edition’s wording, gathers them by repository and by shelfmark, and links the catalogue record where one is verified. Where the edition marks nothing, the line is unmarked: most unmarked Vienna and Basel lines are originals or drafts, but the edition does not say so and this section does not guess.

How the archives formed

Tycho kept his letters at Uraniborg, probably in a letter-room of their own together with his drafts, and planned to continue in print the collection he had begun with the Kassel letters in 1596; the itinerant years after 1597 — Rostock, Wandsbek, Wittenberg, Benátky — gave him no chance to keep the archive together, and much of it was probably lost then (Norlind 1970, p. 172). The largest part of what remained is in the Österreichische Nationalbibliothek in Vienna, where it arrived, so far as is known, at about the time the Fugger library came from Augsburg in 1656; it holds letters to and from Tycho in original and in draft, and the originals of his own letters to Hagecius, which Hagecius sent back at Tycho’s request for the planned continuation (pp. 172–173).

The Universitätsbibliothek Basel holds two folio volumes from the eighteenth-century collection of Werner Huber, catalogued in 1789, sold after his death and bought by the university in 1806 from the bookseller Johannes Schweighauser: one with forty original letters to Tycho (one of them to his sister Sophie), the other with eighty-four drafts of his own letters, most from his last two years (p. 173). The originals of his letters to Giovanni Antonio Magini came to the private archive of the Marchesi Malvezzi de’ Medici in Bologna, bought on a piazza there by the marchese’s great-grandfather, and were edited by Favaro in 1886 (p. 173). The Universitätsbibliothek München has, among the papers of the Bavarian chancellor Herwart von Hohenburg, originals from Tycho and Herwart’s drafts in reply (p. 173).

The Rosenkrantz family archive at Rosenholm held Tycho’s letters to Holger Rosenkrantz; Jacob Langebek had them copied in the mid-eighteenth century, and the copies are in Rigsarkivet in Copenhagen. The archive itself passed by inheritance in 1802 to the foreign minister Niels Rosenkrantz and then to his brother at Fredrikshald in Norway, where it burned in 1826 (pp. 173–174). Twenty-two letters from Tycho and three drafts from Rosenkrantz survive in Langebek’s copies, some of them in cipher (p. 179); that is why every Rosenkrantz witness in this edition is a copy. Other collections are gone: Bishop Christen Worm’s fifty or so letters from Tycho to Vedel, burned in the Copenhagen fire of 1728; the letters Søren Lintrup owned, probably lost in the Bergen fire of 1702; and the Tycho letters once in the Trew collection at Erlangen, which the catalogue lists but the collection no longer holds (p. 174). Few original letters by Tycho exist at all; most survive only in copies, some were written by an assistant and only signed by him, some were sent in duplicate by different routes (p. 176).
