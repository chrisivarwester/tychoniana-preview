1Introduction

This page is the apparatus that the twelve editions of Tychoniana share: Observationes, Typographica, Epistolarium, Bibliotheca, Instrumenta, Loca, Familia, Iconographia, Horoscopi, Diarium, Receptio and Scripta. It holds no record of its own. Every row here is a dated, placed or missing thing whose full record lives in one of those editions, and every row links to it.

Three things are done here that no single edition can do. The general chronology (§2) puts the printed editions, the instruments, the sites, the observation volumes, the diary, the likenesses, the letters and the reception on one axis, in the eight periods of the itinerary and then the afterlife, with a year index for the fifty-six years of the life. The holdings (§3) give, kind by kind and then city by city, what survives of Tycho’s world and where it is kept today — the copies of his books, the volumes of his library, the likenesses, the observation codices, the specimens of his hand and the two surviving instruments in one table. The desiderata (§4) gather what each edition records as lost, destroyed, doubtful or unresolved, and hold the 30 questions of the manuscript archive and the presses until the editions they belong to carry them. The method for reading Tycho’s hand is a separate edition, Manus.

Each row prints the edition it belongs to and the key of its record. Each figure on this page is defined once, in the table below, and recurs with the same definition in the section that uses it.

What the numbers are not. No figure on this page is a sum across kinds: an instrument that was destroyed, a copy whose attribution is doubtful and a library title not yet traced are three different facts and are counted apart. No ratio here is a survival rate; a thing enters the located tier because a copy was found, so a located count measures the search as much as the survival. The copies of §3 come from 24 censuses that are still provisional — each names, beside its row, the union catalogues it has not yet reached, and its counts will rise — and 5 later printings recorded in Typographica (Nuremberg 1602 (Hülsius), Prague 1603 (Schumann re-issue), Frankfurt 1610 (Tampach), Frankfurt 1610 (Tampach), Frankfurt 1610 (Tampach)) have no census, so no copy of them can appear here. The 509 letters are those in the Epistolarium’s edition, not the whole correspondence. Nothing is drawn on the chronology that no edition records: the life is Loca’s 8 periods and 21 journeys, not a list of anecdotes, and the afterlife is Receptio’s 28 episodes, the instruments’ 21 dated fates and the 7 printings of 1602–1610. Two editions have as yet no per-record anchors, the Horoscopi and the Epistolarium, so their rows link to the edition and not to the record.

2General chronology Chronologia

The same year across every kind of thing. The first figure is the life, 1546 to 1601: the bands are the eight periods of the itinerary as Loca divides it, the diamonds its documented journeys, and the lanes below carry, year by year, the editions printed, the instruments in use, the sites standing, the observation volumes in progress, the years of the Uraniborg diary, the likenesses made, the letters written and the episodes of reception that began in his lifetime. The second figure is the afterlife, from 1601 to the present, drawn to scale: the reception episodes, the dated fates of the instruments and the printings of 1602 to 1610. Every mark links to its record. The tables that follow are the figures’ twins: the afterlife by date, and the year index of the life, one row per year, in which every cell is a link.

3Holdings Dispersio

What survives of Tycho’s world, kind by kind, and where it is kept today. Each count in the first table is the owning edition’s own. Of the printed works, 24 censuses record 227 copies (233 registry rows less 6 whole-edition placeholders): 107 located — 106 extant and 1 damaged — in a present-day collection; 35 untraced — 26 last seen on the market, 9 recorded lost — listed at their last documented place and not counted as holdings; 85 attested or inferred with no exemplar examined, listed by edition. 16 of the 24 editions have at least one located copy, 3 have documented copies none of which is located, and 5 are known only by attestation. Every census is provisional; its row names the catalogues not yet swept.

The city table joins the census copies with the library volumes, the likenesses, the observation codices, the specimens of the hand and the two surviving instruments — the one projection no single edition can make. Institutions are printed as each edition recorded them; the same library can appear under more than one spelling, and a count of institutions is therefore not printed. The table comes first; the map beneath it plots the located copies, with the untraced tier as a second layer that can be shown, and a copy whose city the census does not give is listed as not plottable rather than pinned by guess.

Which apographa are in Longomontanus's hand vs other assistants?

openposition as of 22 June 2026q-apographa-scribes

The 19 fair-copies are attributed wholesale to Longomontanus, but the copying spanned 1582–1601 and the familia rotated; Dreyer notes Tycho's own hand appears often in the earlier volumes and rarely in the later, implying multiple scribes.

Position
The wholesale attribution of the 19 Vienna fair-copies to Longomontanus is an inference, not a documented fact, and it has been replaced by a per-volume, graded attribution. Dreyer's only direct statement (TBOO X prolegomena, p.XVIII) is an aggregate gradient — 'In prioribus tomis satis multa inveniuntur Tychonis manu scripta, in posterioribus pauca' (Tycho's own hand frequent in the earlier volumes, rare in the later) — and he names no scribe; no published source gives a per-volume hand attribution. Overlaying the familia's documented service dates on each volume's observation year (contemporaneous copying — a model Dreyer's gradient itself supports, since a single retrospective copyist would have left a uniform hand and no gradient) yields the graded candidate set now shown on each observation volume: the seven 1582–88 volumes (Cod. 10657–10663) exclude Longomontanus, who reached Hven only in summer 1589/90, and belong to the early familia (Flemløse, Sascerides, Morsing, Hans Crol) with Tycho's own frequent hand; 1590–95 (Cod. 10665–10669, less the lost 1593) have Longomontanus as the strongly-favoured hand, 1592 and 1594 the cleanest (sole senior assistant); 1589, 1596–97 and 1600–01 are genuine splits (Morsing/Flemløse/Crol at the 1589 turnover; Johannes Eriksen 1596–97; Longomontanus returned, Eriksen and Tengnagel at Prague 1600–01); and 1598–99 (Cod. 10672–10673) are unattributed exile volumes. Confirmation needs a securely-attributed Longomontanus autograph as comparand. Two are now identified, neither digitised: his letter to Kepler in Vienna (Cod. lat. 10703, f.236v) and his album-amicorum leaf of 13 September 1598, signed at Wandsbek the same day as Tycho's own, among the Boetius album leaves of the Noodt collection at the Royal Library, Copenhagen (Thott 500,2° / Thott 1059,2°; Helk, Fund og Forskning 21, 1974). What would settle it: an image of either autograph, compared first against the strongly-favoured volumes (Cod. 10665–10669), then a volume-by-volume examination of the whole series.

GKS 1832 'Planetary tables' — is it in Erasmus Bartholin's hand?

openposition as of 2 June 2026q-gks1832

KB tentatively attributes the Tychonic-Keplerian planetary tables in GKS 1832 to Erasmus Bartholin, whose hand is well attested in the adjacent editorial volumes (GKS 308–310), so this is testable.

Position
The attribution is the Royal Library's own hedge, and the test that would settle it is one image short. The KB catalogue titles the volume 'Tabulæ Planetarum ex observationibus Tychonico-Keplerianis forte Er. Bartholini' — '…perhaps of Erasmus Bartholin' — and no published study of the hand exists. The volume's profile argues for caution: it is a quarto of Tychonic-Keplerian planetary tables, a working document of the 1660s rather than a primary observation log; it is absent from Picard's 1672 receipt of the five folio volumes taken to Paris; and it entered KB only by 1787 — a profile matching the later, separately-acquired Tychonica (cf. GKS 320, ‘GKS 320: how Frobenius's manuscript reached Copenhagen’) rather than the documented route of the Bartholin editorial archive. On that evidence a hand from Bartholin's circle (a student, or the young Ole Rømer) or a later Danish computational copy is at least as consistent with the record as Bartholin's own hand. A secure comparison basis exists: three signed Erasmus Bartholin Latin autographs of 1672–75 are digitised among the Royal Society's Early Letters — a fluent, lightly right-sloped humanist italic; one of the letters itself discusses Tycho's Hven observations, Picard, and the edition 'ex mea recensione', the very corpus GKS 1832 derives from. Bartholin's securely-attributed Copenhagen volumes (GKS 308–310) remain undigitised, as does GKS 1832 itself. What would settle it: a digitised image of GKS 1832, compared against the Royal Society autographs — ideally three-way, with a Rømer autograph as the principal alternative hand.

When and how was the 1593 Vienna fair-copy lost?

settled to the limit of the cataloguesposition as of 1 June 2026q-1593-loss

The 1593 apographum is the one gap in the Cod. 10657–10675 run, so the Historia Coelestis (printed from Vienna) lacks 1593 while it survives KB-only (Codex K / GKS 1828). The moment of loss is unfixed: before Tengnagel's 1604 handover to Kepler, between then and the 1621 imperial reclamation, or in Curtz's custody.

Position
The terminus ante quem is Kepler's death, 15 November 1630. The prolegomena to the observation volumes of Tychonis Brahe Opera Omnia (Dreyer, TBOO X) state the 1593 fair-copy 'had already been completely lost before Kepler's death'; the Copenhagen Meddelelser (2006) commentary restates this and adds that whether the Vienna apographa reached Curtz via Kepler's 1621 surrender or via his son-in-law Jakob Bartsch's widow (Susanna Kepler) c.1634 is unknown. The archive entered Kepler's custody through the 1604 Tengnagel–Kepler contract (recited in Ferdinand II's 1621 letter), so the loss falls in 1604×1630 and cannot be narrowed further, nor the mechanism fixed. The 'sent to Cassel and there lost' story Albert Curtz (Lucius Barrettus) put in the Historia Coelestis (1666) prolegomena is a fabrication papering over a gap that already existed when he received the nineteen volumes — Dreyer (Tycho Brahe, 1890, Appendix; and TBOO) debunks it. 1593 survives KB-only: Codex K (GKS 1828 Fasc. I, ending Venus 25 Sep 1593) + four appended leaves in Codex E (GKS 313). Residual: only a 1604–1630 inventory — Kepler's note on the Codex G front board (GKS 316), or Curtz's memorandum to Count Martinitz printed in Kästner, Geschichte der Mathematik II (1797) p.651ff — could fix whether 1593 was already gone at the 1604 handover.

GKS 320: how Frobenius's manuscript reached Copenhagen

settledposition as of 1 June 2026q-gks320-provenance

Now re-attributed to Georg Ludwig Frobenius (Hamburg mathematician/bookseller, 1566–1645): a 17th-c. autograph comparing Ptolemy, Copernicus, Tycho and Longomontanus, drawing on the Astronomia Danica (1622). How a Hamburg autograph reached the Danish Royal Library, and whether the treatise survives in other copies or print, is unknown.

Position
Both sub-questions answered. Georg Ludwig Frobenius (Iphofen 1566 – Hamburg 1645) was a Hamburg printer-bookseller who spent ~6 weeks in Tycho's familia on Hven in 1591 and in 1627 printed Longomontanus's Cyclometria (Christianson, On Tycho's Island pp.285–86) — not of the Basel Froben dynasty though he believed he was; GKS 320 draws on Longomontanus's Astronomia Danica (1622) so it is a Hamburg-period work. ACQUISITION (Brøndsted, Fund og Forskning 9, 1962, p.77 + n.10): GKS 320 is one of a 3-volume set of Frobenius printer's-manuscripts (Druckmanuskripte for books he never issued) — GKS 320–322 2°, the 'Astronomia hypothetica quadriformis' (this four-systems treatise) + an 'Astrologia Ovidiana'. On Frobenius's death at Hamburg in 1645 his literary remains passed to the Hamburg cathedral chapter (Domkapitel); when the Dombibliothek was dissolved and auctioned in the 18th c., the Royal Library Copenhagen bought them 'perhaps with a view to their Tycho-Brahe material' — exactly fitting the KB catalogue's 'Indlemmet i KB senest 1787'. So the route is Frobenius → Hamburg Dombibliothek → 18th-c. dissolution auction → KB, NOT the Gottorp channel earlier hypothesised. OTHERWISE-KNOWN: no printed version and no sibling MS (EEB/VD17/USTC negative) — a unique abandoned-publication manuscript. Residual: only the exact pre-1787 auction/accession entry would date the KB purchase precisely (archival nicety). A by-product of the same article: a previously unrecorded 1584 Tycho elegy on Ptolemy, surviving in Frobenius's 1644 Almagest proof-sheet at the Royal Library, Stockholm.

Identify the engraver of the 1602 Mechanica title-portrait

openposition as of 22 June 2026q-mech1602-engraver

The 1602 Nuremberg/Hülsius Mechanica carries a printed engraved title-portrait that is an anonymous, SAME-DIRECTION (not mirror-reversed) copy after De Gheyn's hat-portrait (New Hollstein 239) — cut by a Nuremberg workshop hand in Hülsius's circle. Position
Everything except the engraver's personal name is settled. The 1602 title-portrait is an unsigned, same-direction copy after De Gheyn's beret-portrait of Tycho (New Hollstein 239; the genealogy runs Gemperlin's 1586 painting → De Gheyn's engraving, existing by December 1590 → the Nuremberg copies). It was cut from — or shares an exemplar with — the smaller plate Hülsius had already used in his 1601 Nuremberg Epistolarum reissue: both captions carry the same idiosyncratic engraver's errors (INSTVMENTORVM, ASTONOMICORVM, INVETORIS), which fixes the derivation, and both books were printed by Christoph Lochner for Levinus Hulsius. Neither Hülsius plate is recorded in the De Gheyn New Hollstein copy-list, and no signature or attribution exists in any catalogue, auction record or portrait index (VD17; the Digitaler Portraitindex, which records the plate 'nach Jaques de Gheyn … Unbez.' [unsigned]; Hollstein LX). The documentary evidence converges on the workshop of Johann Sibmacher — the only documented Hülsius engraver in Nuremberg, cutting plates for the same press in the same years and signing a comparable nobleman portrait engraving one year later (Adolf von Schwarzenberg, 1603) — but the Brahe plate's quality sits below Sibmacher's signed prime work, and Hollstein LX (Seelig 2002) does not list it, so the evidence cannot separate Sibmacher's own quick trade-plate from a hand in his large workshop. The most defensible attribution: an anonymous copperplate cut in the Sibmacher workshop (Hülsius's Nuremberg house engraving operation, Lochner's press), c.1601–02, after De Gheyn NH 239. Cod. 10718 (digitised) bundles the meteorological diary with the Notae historicae — Tycho's running note of who visited Uraniborg: a first-rate prosopographical source for the Hven network, never indexed as structured data.

Position
Location confirmed: ÖNB Vienna, Cod. Vindob. lat. 10718, digitised (viewer: https://viewer.onb.ac.at/131EA75D — catalogue: https://search.onb.ac.at/permalink/f/sb7jht/ONB_alma21303501260003338). Bibliotheca 1 question · the edition’s own Desiderata

Tycho's own library, reconstructed

the register now existsposition as of 31 May 2026q-bibliotheca-tycho

Tycho's working library — the books he OWNED and read, distinct from the works he created (the works recorded in Typographica and Scripta) — was largely dispersed; a core (~110 vols) reached the Prague Clementinum and was studied by Kleinschnitzová (1933) and Šolc (2002). It had not been reconstructed as a structured whole — until now.

Position
Built as the Bibliotheca edition of this portal, which carries the current totals (attested entries, located works and volumes, titles not yet found). No snapshot counts are quoted here: the reconstruction moves as missing titles are located. Keyed on Tycho's ownership marks (gilt supralibros portrait-and-arms binding, 'T.B.O.' / 'T.B.O. 1576' stamps, autograph inscriptions). Five dispersal channels modelled — Clementinum/Jesuit core (~110 vols, 'Ex bibliotheca Tychoniana' 1642), heirs' sale, 1648 Swedish war-loot, Rudolfine-imperial absorption, attrition. The located and digitised Clementinum survivors are also recorded among the objects of this register, each linking back to its library entry — the sibling reconstruction to the works and to the Mechanica census. Residual: the remaining ghost entries to locate, and dispersal-channel assignment for the unassigned entries.
