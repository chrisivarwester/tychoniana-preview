Typographica Tychonis — The printed works of Tycho Brahe and of his press, 1572–1610

The books Tycho printed — not the books he owned, which are the Bibliotheca, nor what they say, which is Scripta: every edition and issue of his own works and of his press's output that Lauritz Nielsen records, from a Copenhagen broadsheet of 1572 and a book of 1573, through the output of his own press on Hven (1584–97) and at Wandsbek (1598), to the seven posthumous completions, re-issues and reprints of 1602–1610 — 29 editions and issues of 19 printed works, two of them by other authors from his press. Five of the printings are known only from documents, one elegy is attributed to the press by conjecture, and one poem was never printed at all. For each: Nielsen's bibliographical record, the edition, re-issue and reprint distinguished as he distinguishes them, the shop and the printer with the basis of the attribution, the typographic evidence by which a Uraniborg imprint is recognised, and — for the 24 editions that have been censused — the copies located today, read from each census at every build. The letters are read in the Epistolarium; the instruments the plates engrave are given in Instrumenta.

1Introduction

This is a bibliography of the printed works of Tycho Brahe and of the press he owned, arranged after the two works of Lauritz Nielsen that remain the authority for it: the Brahe entries of the Dansk Bibliografi 1551–1600 (1931–1933), whose numbers are the canonical reference for any Tycho imprint and are cited here as LN, and his history of the press, Tycho Brahes Bogtrykkeri paa Hveen (1921), which alone describes the printings after 1600. Nielsen's records are transcribed in full in the register (§3) — the diplomatic title, the collation, the contents leaf by leaf, his own list of holdings — and his account of the press is given in §6, §7 and §11. What Nielsen could not have had is added from the census stores: for every edition that has been censused, the copies located today, with their status and grade, are read from the census at each build and never typed here.

The edition holds three kinds of thing, kept apart throughout. A work is what Tycho (or Morsing, or Flemløs) wrote. An edition or issue is a printing event as Nielsen distinguishes them — an edition, a re-issue, a reprint, or the posthumous completion of an unfinished printing; the catalogue (§2) has one row for each. A copy is a physical exemplar in a library, in the trade, or recorded and untraced; the copies belong to the censuses, which this edition counts and links but does not duplicate.

Work, edition, state, copy

Nielsen's numbers name printings, not works and not states. A work with a single printing has one number; the two Icones and the Orthographia are title entries (975, 976, 1220), cross-referenced from the Brahe section; the Icones has two (976 for the broadside pair of 1585, 975 for the album of c. 1596); the Progymnasmata has one (433) for the unfinished printing of 1592 and none for its completion at Prague in 1602, because the Dansk Bibliografi ends at 1600. Variant settings inside a printing — the rejected title-quire of the De mundi, the two settings of quires LL and MM in the Epistolae — are recorded inside the entry and are called states here; they are not rows. The seven posthumous printings are described only in the 1921 monograph and carry no Nielsen number.

The copy vocabulary

The copy figures use the census vocabulary and no other. A copy is located when a record-level catalogue entry, a live surrogate, a first-hand sighting or a recent market event places it at a named holder today (the census statuses extant and damaged); in the trade when it was last seen with a dealer or at auction and is not otherwise located; recorded, untraced when a prior census or catalogue records it but no live evidence has yet been found (the census status attested); lost when its destruction or disappearance is itself recorded; inferred when a recipient copy is deduced from a letter and no exemplar is known. A whole edition of which no copy is known carries a single placeholder record in its census; those placeholders are counted here as editions without a surviving copy, never as copies. Each located copy carries the census's grade for the identification, A secure, B probable, C possible; no grade is rounded.

How to read an entry

Each register record (§3) opens with its number in this edition, its short title, its Nielsen chip and its stable key, followed by Nielsen's diplomatic title — a vertical bar marks a line-break on the leaf, the red and black of a two-colour title are noted, and a bracketed exclamation mark is Nielsen's flag on a misprint — a one-sentence gloss, and a generated line that states the work's editions and their copy records as the censuses count them today. The editions and issues follow as anchored sub-records, each with its kind, its shop and printer, Nielsen's description and, for the censused editions, the copies line and the link to the census. Then Nielsen's record — collation, contents, production, his list of copies, references — the presentation copies, the texts, the letters that mention the work, the record's place elsewhere in Tychoniana, the editorial note, and the sources. A record is cited by its section number and key, which are stable across editions.

What the figures are not

No figure here is a survival rate: the located count measures the reach of the censuses, not the fate of the edition, and the five uncensused posthumous issues contribute nothing to it although Tampach's stocktake of 1610 counted 1,197 copies of the Progymnasmata alone. "Located" is a statement about evidence, not about existence: a copy Nielsen saw in 1931 that no live catalogue record now reaches is "recorded, untraced", not lost. The Nielsen numbers are Nielsen's and are not renumbered here; the twelve editions of Tycho's own that survive in print are twelve printings of eleven works, the two Icones being two stages of one enterprise. The identification of the trigonometry manuscript with Book II of the programme (§8) is the editor's and is so marked.

A verse epistle (Ovidian heroide) written in Sophie Brahe's voice to her absent beloved Erik Lange, with Tycho himself appearing under the pseudonym Apollo. Urania = Sophie Brahe; Titan = Erik Lange; Apollo = Tycho Brahe. Composed at Uraniborg in winter 1594 when Lange had been abroad for years in pursuit of the philosopher's stone. The poem is layered with alchemical and astrological imagery and closes with four embedded horoscopes (for Titan/Lange, Urania/Sophie Brahe, Tage Thott, and Apollo/Tycho) reconstructed by Ove von Späth in Zeeberg's 1994 edition.
Production
Composed at Uraniborg in winter 1594, before 26 July 1594 — the date of Tycho's covering letter to the Scottish jurist Thomas Craig (TBOO VII.366), which encloses the poem as ‘sororis nomine a me lusam’. Tycho's authorship has been the standard scholarly view since Zeeberg 1994, grounded in Tycho's own claim in that letter, though a minority attribution to Jon Jacobsen Venusin — Gellius Sascerides's brother-in-law, not Gellius himself — is argued in Andersen-Vinilandicus (2009), pp. 225–276, and remains an open question. The Vienna MS (V) carries two layers of autograph correction — hand T (identified as Tycho's) and hand X (unknown) — establishing Tycho's direct involvement with the text regardless of primary authorship.
Copies, as described
Two manuscript witnesses: V = Cod. Vindob. Lat. 10686/12 (ÖNB Vienna; item 12 per Friis's 1868 inventory); R = Resen, Inscriptiones Hafnienses (Copenhagen, 1668). V is primary, with corrections in two hands — T (Tycho's autograph) and X (unknown). No independent printed edition in Tycho's lifetime.

Editorial note
Tycho's literary magnum opus. At 608 lines in elegiac couplets, Urania Titani is his longest poem — a sustained Ovidian heroide written in Sophie Brahe's voice to her absent beloved Erik Lange, with Tycho appearing as the god Apollo. Written in winter 1594 at Uraniborg, it is the richest surviving window onto the intellectual interior of the island: alchemical longing, astrological symbolism, and the intimate pseudonym-world of the Brahe circle all run through it. Its four embedded horoscopes — of Erik Lange, of Sophie Brahe, of the young Tage Thott, and of Tycho himself, who casts his own nativity in his sister's voice — were reconstructed from the verse by Ove von Späth for Zeeberg's 1994 edition; all four are in Horoscopi. Tycho claims the poem as his own: sending it to the Scottish jurist Thomas Craig on 26 July 1594 he encloses an Epistola Elegiaca ad imitationem Ovidianarum … sororis nomine a me lusam — an elegiac epistle in Ovid's manner, composed by me in my sister's name (TBOO VII.366) — and it is this first-person attestation on which Zeeberg's attribution rests. A minority ascription to Jon Jacobsen Venusin (Andersen-Vinilandicus 2009) remains open. First published in full by Dreyer (TBOO IX, 1922); first critically edited and translated into Danish by Peter Zeeberg (Museum Tusculanum, 1994) — the monograph that opened Tycho Brahe studies as a branch of Danish literary history (Receptio, 1994).

7.2 The typographic fingerprint

Nielsen's analysis of the type and ornament stock is the means of telling a genuine Uraniborg imprint from later re-issues and imitations:
  • Roman (Antikva) — a Mittel (20 lines ≈ 114 mm), the commonest body-type; an exceptionally large Tekst-Antikva (10 lines ≈ 66 mm), the largest roman body-type used anywhere in 16th-century Denmark; and a Cicero (20 lines ≈ 94 mm) in two cuts (an older, fuller and a younger, slimmer).
  • Italic (Kursiv) — a large lille Tekst (10 lines ≈ 64 mm) and an old-style lille Cicero (20 lines ≈ 84 mm) with roman capitals, used for notes and small matter.
  • Fraktur — only two (a Cicero body ≈ 93 mm and a Tekst display ≈ 74 mm), and used only from 1591; no Schwabacher at all.
Twelve type-sets in all — the eight founts above, together with the capitals-only roman display founts Nielsen counts in with them. For ornament Tycho had just two sets of roman initials (shaded-foliage contour letters in the 'Spanish' style, ≈ 18 mm and ≈ 25 mm) and four vignettes. He never used title-frames, friezes, or flower-borders (Røskener) — which is why the rich flower-border decoration on every page of the 1598 Mechanica betrays it as Hamburg work, not Hven.

7.3 Paper

Paper was a constant difficulty. Tycho bought it at the Frankfurt fairs and sought it from princely mills (Henrik Rantzau's, and the Duke of Mecklenburg's, approached through his kinsman Heinrich v. Below in December 1587). To free himself of the shortage he built his own paper-mill on Hven, working by 1589 or 1590; its paper is recognisable by its two watermarks and its visibly lower quality — where a book passes from imported to island paper, the impression is markedly poorer. Nordstrand's study of the moulds identifies the mark itself: the Brahe arms — shield and helm, the crest of buffalo horns (Danish vesselhorn) set with peacock feathers, the letters T and B at the sides — recognised, among other sheets, in the observation protocol now KB GKS 317 2°. The watermarked island paper runs c. 1591–1597, which makes it a positive authentication mark for the Uraniborg books: the 1588 De mundi predates the mill, and the 1598 Wandsbek Mechanica — printed on the mainland with Hven paper fetched from store — falls outside the series. The oldest of the marks appears in sheets of the unfinished Progymnasmata (1592, completed 1602 from the 1592 stock).

7.4 The printer's emblems

Two woodcut marks carved at Uraniborg recur in the books: 'Astronomy', with the motto SVSPICIENDO DESPICIO ('looking up, I look down'), and 'Chemistry', with DESPICIENDO SVSPICIO ('looking down, I look up') — the allegorical figures that stood on the castle façade.

8The programme Theatri Astronomici Opus

Tycho conceived his output as a single restoration of astronomy culminating in a work he named, in print and in his own hand, by the genitive formula Theatri Astronomici Opus. It was never assembled. This section gives the plan as the documents preserve it — the two tiers, the seven books and what each became — the imperial privilege that protected it, the attestations of the name, the three states of the architecture, Tycho's own timetable, and where the pieces went after his death.

The printings gathered in this edition were never meant to stand alone. Tycho conceived his entire output as one branded restoration of astronomy (instauratio astronomiae) culminating in a single great work he named, in print and in his own hand, with the genitive formula “Theatri Astronomici Opus” — the Work of the Astronomical Theatre. It was never assembled. What survives are the realized fragments — and the visible scars of the parts that never came.

The plan had two tiers: an introductory series on “recent phenomena” (the 1572 nova, the comets), explicitly a prodromus — the forerunner that “prepares the way” — and the systematic great work, which Tycho divided in his letter to Caspar Peucer of 13 September 1588 into seven books. The geo-heliocentric cosmology is not a topic inside the opus; it is the organizing principle of the book-division.

8.2 The privilege

Tycho did not only plan the opus privately — he advertised it in print and secured imperial protection for it. The 1592 Quaternio Primus — the collective title-leaf and prospectus for the Progymnasmata — is the documentary keystone joining the two tiers: it declares the whole Progymnasmata to stand Prodromi loco (“in the place of a forerunner”) to the greater opus, and bound into it is Rudolf II's printing privilege. Nielsen (§433) knew three witnesses to the 1592 advance issue, and the census of it grades them: Berlin, Staatsbibliothek — a partial copy, extant, though its WW2 survival is still to be confirmed; Marburg, Hessisches Staatsarchiv — the title-leaf alone; and the Königsberg working copy — Tycho's own, corrected throughout in his hand, with twelve blank leaves bound in where the Moon section was still pending — which the census grades lost: the Königsberg collections were dispersed or destroyed in 1944–45 and no post-war trace has been found. It is the witness that mattered most, the one object that could have fixed the boundary between what was printed on Hveen and what was completed in Prague.

The Diploma Caesareum · 13 June 1590

Rudolf II granted a 30-year Empire-wide printing privilege. Its language defines the planned opus, by imperial authority, as the third world-system after Ptolemy and Copernicus:

new hypotheses of the celestial motions, agreeing exactly with the observations of all ages — which was achieved neither by the earlier hypotheses of Ptolemy nor by the later ones of Copernicus
Rudolf II to Tycho Brahe · signed by the Emperor, countersigned Curtius and Barvitius — The opus was officially conceived as the restoration of astronomy that would supersede both predecessors — Tycho's own claim, ratified by the Emperor.

Jacobus Curtius to Tycho · 28 June 1590

Curtius drafted the privilege himself and later hosted Tycho in Prague. Fifteen days after the diploma he wrote:

…ubi majus illud, quod moliris, Opus, in lucem prodierit. Neque enim dubito, quin … indeque novam hanc mundi Machinam, divino sane ingenio, extruxeris.
“…when that greater Work which you are building shall have come to light. Nor do I doubt that … from it you will have built up this new Machine of the world, by a truly divine genius.”

Not to be confused with Longomontanus's Introductio in Theatrum Astronomicum (Copenhagen, 1639) — a different book by Tycho's former assistant, in which theatrum means the literal new Copenhagen observatory (the Round Tower). A 47-years-later coincidence of idiom.

8.4 A moving target

The architecture was never fixed. The record preserves three successive states of it — 1588, 1592, 1598 — and across them Tycho twice re-cut the boundary between the “recent phenomena” volumes and the systematic books.

1588Peucer letter: a three-tome comet trilogy plus a separate seven-book systematic opus.
1592Quaternio Primus: the Progymnasmata is now “in three books” that fold planetary content into the introductory frame (superior planets in Book II, inferior in Book III).
1598Mechanica: re-cut again — all comets into volume II, all five planets into volume III.

The 1592 witnesses of the plan are the copies of the unfinished Progymnasmata, censused as row 18.

8.5 Tycho’s timetable

intra quinquennium vel sexennium
Peucer letter · Uraniborg, 13 September 1588 · TBOO VII, p. 132 — “within five or six years” — completion estimated c. 1593–94

Two bottlenecks Tycho named:

  • Computational — New mean-motion and prosthaphaeresis tables had to be built afresh for each planet — precisely the labour the manuscript Triangulorum praxis (Book II) was meant to mechanize.
  • Observational — More acronychal observations of the superior planets were needed — “above all in Saturn, because of the slowness of its motion.”

He set a sequencing rule: issue first the material needing no long delay — “what is contained in the first four or five books” — and leave the planetary Books VI–VII, needing the most new observation, for last.

Beyond reach in principle

The five-year estimate was overtaken by events: loss of royal favour, departure from Hven on 29 April 1597, years in exile, and death at Prague on 24 October 1601 — seven years past the deadline he had set himself, the opus still unassembled.

Modern scholarship judges the failure not merely biographical. Kepler found the Mars orbit irreducible to either Ptolemaic theory or Tycho’s proposed geo-heliocentric replacement: an eight-arcminute residual remained that Tycho’s framework could not account for. Completing the opus required a physical, causal approach to planetary motion.

8.6 The dispersal

The pieces scattered after Tycho's death (Prague, 24 October 1601). Kepler completed the Progymnasmata from Tycho's unfinished sheets (Prague, 1602); Tengnagel sold the Mechanica woodblocks to Hülsius for a Nuremberg reprint (1602). The observation logs — the empirical core that would have filled the planetary Books III–VII — passed to Kepler under the Tengnagel–Kepler agreement of 8 July 1604 and fed the Astronomia Nova (1609) and the Rudolphine Tables (1627): Tycho's own title, but recast on the heliocentric-elliptical principles his whole geo-heliocentric programme existed to refute.

1602Kepler completed the unfinished Progymnasmata from the 388 leaves printed at Uraniborg, adding 58 printed at Prague — 446 in all — and a closing appendix explaining the staged printing (Schumann press, Prague). Tycho's son-in-law Frans Gansneb Tengnagel sold the Mechanica woodblocks to Levinus Hülsius (Nuremberg), who had Christoph Lochner print the only commercial edition.
1604The Tengnagel–Kepler agreement (8 July 1604, mediated by Johannes Pistorius): Kepler receives all of Tycho's observation logs in exchange for completing the Rudolphine Tables “to Tengnagel's satisfaction.” The empirical core — everything that would have filled the planetary Books III–VII — now in Kepler's hands.
1609–27The logs yield the Astronomia Nova (1609) — the elliptical orbit and the equal-areas law. The Tabulae Rudolphinae follow (1627): Tycho's own title, honouring Rudolf II, but built on heliocentric-elliptical principles. Tycho himself had “negotiated a deal whereby he would publish his planetary theories under the name of the Rudolphine Tables and Kepler would help him” (Thoren) — the title he planned, the physics he rejected.
1655–66Ludwig Kepler sells the original observation autographs to Frederick III of Denmark (1655; now KB Copenhagen). The Vienna fair-copy transcripts pass to the Jesuit Albert Curtz (“Lucius Barrettus”), who prints the unreliable Historia Coelestis (Augsburg 1666; some sheets reissued Regensburg 1672). Erasmus Bartholin’s Specimen (1668) catalogued its errors.
1913–29Dreyer’s Opera Omnia (TBOO X–XIII, Copenhagen, 15 vols) publishes the observations in authoritative critical form — the data Tycho intended for Books III–VII, definitively edited 325 years after his death.

11The press, 1584–1610

Nielsen's account of the press, in his own order and corrected where the data pass found him misquoted: why Tycho built it, how he equipped and staffed it, its exile, and its end. The chapters on the type stock, the paper and the emblems stand in §7, where a scholar looks for a method.

11.1 Why he built it

Tycho's plan was a vast work, a Theatrum astronomicum embracing the whole new astronomy. A press of his own answered several needs at once: the Copenhagen printers were busy and the city was plague-ridden; Hven's isolation made constant traffic with the mainland awkward; and he wished to guard his discoveries from those who might publish them as their own, and to oversee the setting and correction of his books sheet by sheet. The first notice of the press is his letter to Anders Sørensen Vedel of 16 February 1584, announcing that he would set up a small printing house on Uraniborg and procure type at the coming Frankfurt fair.

11.2 Equipping it, and the printers

Tycho engaged a printer named Joachim and sent him to Wittenberg for presses and type; further type was brokered through his old Rostock teacher Heinrich Brucæus and the Rostock printer Stephan Möllmann ('Myliander') in 1584–85. The press was housed in a small building beside the castle — Tycho's own bird's-eye woodcut of Uraniborg marks the printing-house in the left-hand pavilion. Three printers are known by name: Joachim, then Christopher Weida (named in the 1588 colophon), then Hans Gaschitz (named in 1591). Nielsen records no nationality for Joachim; Weida and Gaschitz he judged probably Germans — all three otherwise unattested, and each apparently in Tycho's service only briefly.

11.3 The press in exile

Tycho left Hven on 29 March 1597 (Nielsen gives 29 April; the date adopted here is the Loca register's, from Tycho's own statement), taking press and instruments with him. The Mechanica was printed at Wandsbek near Hamburg in 1598 on a hybrid press (his own, but with the Hamburg printer Philip de Ohr's type and ornament); an attempt to print the Progymnasmata Moon-section at Hamburg was so botched that the whole run was scrapped; a further attempt at Wittenberg (winter 1598–99, on Hven paper fetched from the Wandsbek store) was abandoned for Prague. The press reached Prague in 1600.

11.4 The end of the press

After Tycho's death Kepler saw the Progymnasmata through the Prague Schumann press (1602); the remaining stock passed to Levinus Hülsius at Nuremberg (1602; the Mechanica reprinted by Christoph Lochner) and then to Gottfried Tampach at Frankfurt (1610). Only with the 1610 Tampach re-issues was the original Uraniborg stock finally exhausted; when a wholly new edition of the works appeared at Frankfurt in 1648, it was new printing from first to last.

After Nielsen 1921, pp. 175–214; the departure date from the Loca register.
