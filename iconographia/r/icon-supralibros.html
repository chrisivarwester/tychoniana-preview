The gilt portrait supralibros — two dies

lifetimeextantAicon-supralibros
die by 20 March 1588
Tycho's most personal device: a gilt-stamped portrait of himself, bust-length in a cartwheel ruff, wearing the loaded chain scheme of his royal favour — the Frederik II medallion with the initials F.S., an elephant pendant (Danske Magazin 1746 — the earliest eyewitness — reports only 'en Elefant', seen 'tydeligen' in the second chain, and names no order; reading it as the Order of the Elephant insignia is this edition's gloss and Mortensen's 1946 gloss on the model engraving, and it sits badly against the fact that Tycho held no such membership; the photographs alone cannot resolve the object), and a third chain whose ornament no witness has made out — Danske Magazin 1746, p. 322, is the source of that negative: 'om der har været noget Smykke i den tredie, kand man ikke see'. Impressed in gold on the front covers of deluxe presentation bindings (the arms stamp on the back), it branded his gifts with his likeness: on De mundi (1588), the Epistolae (1596), the Mechanica (1598), and on Prague bindings to c.1601–03. Around the portrait runs the motto 'Hic patet exterior Tychonis forma Brahei / pulchrius eniteat, quae latet interior' ('here is Tycho's outward form; may the inner, hidden, shine more beautifully'). The stamp exists in TWO sizes, but the published metrology is shaky throughout: Mortensen 1931 prints the figures as '7,6 X 5,8 og 5,9 X 4,3 mm' — millimetres where centimetres must be meant, and with the small die's width drifting between 5.9 and 5.8 across his 1931 and 1966 articles; Friis gives 8 × 6 cm, on the authority of the earliest published rendering of the large die — the plate at Danske Magazin II (1746), p. 321, engraved by O. H. de Lode from the Royal Library's blue-satin Mechanica (LN 432 2°, eks. 2, the Petr Vok presentation copy) and printed, the accompanying text insists, at the die's own size ('Billedet paa Bogen er i selv samme Størrelse, som dette Koberstykke'); that plate is a witness in its own right, not merely Friis's authority, and it carries the only 1:1 claim in the whole literature; a scaled estimate from the Stockholm RAR: 106 A digitisation (c. 6.3–6.5 × 5.9 cm) matches none of them; and every figure is taken from impressions on compressible supports, never from tools; the smaller die is far the rarer — its documented carrier is the KB Calvin/Theocritus volume given to Hans Lindenov — and it differs tellingly from the large: the small die's ruff edge carries serrated notches, rendering the lobed cartwheel ruff of the engraving family faithfully, where the large die smooths them away (Mortensen noticed the notches; object photographs confirm them). Two facts anchor the die pair's place in the story. First, the large die is the earliest witness of the multi-chain-and-medallion costume — it predates de Gheyn's first engraving, and on the leading reconstruction the costume scheme travelled from Denmark to the Amsterdam workshop, plausibly with the De mundi presentation copies in the hands of Heinrich Rantzau, broker of the engraving commission. Second, the dies were never revised: after the second engraving corrected the costume (c.1595), the binding stamps went on impressing the old loaded scheme for the rest of Tycho's life and posthumously — the correction was aimed at the print's public circulation, not at the livery of his bindings.
Maker
Cutter unrecorded. With the die now dated before late March 1588, Gemperlin (dead by 1587) is possible as designer and Hans Crol, Tycho's goldsmith on Hven (d. 30 November 1591), as cutter — but these are names of convenience, neither documented. The die pair's relationship is genuinely open: 1597 is the small die's first documented use, not a cutting date. If it is a later cutting it needs another hand; but Friis (c. 1890) held the small die the older of the pair — undefended, and the shared design cannot by itself discriminate one commission from two cuttings off one pattern.
Original or copy
The DIE is untraced — no source places either physical tool in any collection, and the two published sizes (7.6 × 5.8 cm and 5.8 × 4.3 cm) are Mortensen's measurements of impressions, not of tools. What survives is the stamped bindings, which are originals.
Dated late 1586 → 20 March 1588
The upper bound is an object: the Royal Library's Sascerides copy of De mundi aetherei (LN 428 4°, eks. 1) carries both gilt ovals — portrait on the front cover, arms on the back — on its original parchment presentation binding, with Tycho's autograph dedication to Gellius Sascerides dated 20 March 1588 (verified on the EEB surrogate), so both dies existed by that date. The British Library's Maestlin copy, dispatched from Uraniborg on 14 May 1588, remains the second keystone — nobody rebinds a book into a Tycho presentation binding. The lower bound is the costume it carries: the multi-chain-and-medallion scheme belongs to the likeness layer begun with the 1586 sitting. This dating overturns the older literature's assumption (Mortensen; Christianson) that the stamp copies de Gheyn's engraving — the engraving was cut only in autumn 1590, two years after the die was in use. The small die's own first anchor is much later: its documented carrier is the Calvin/Theocritus volume Tycho gave Hans Lindenov, registered at Uraniborg on 21 March 1597.
Whereabouts

Neither die survives. The impressions survive on presentation and library bindings across Europe.

The die's survival is a documented negative, not an unchecked gap: Gigas 1900 describes twelve KB bindings tool by tool and mentions no tool; Mortensen 1931/1966, Studnička 1901, Kleinschnitzová 1933 and Voit's Encyklopedie knihy all describe stamped covers only. Sixteenth-century binder's tools essentially never survive.

Copy-level carriers

Portrait supralibros documented on: the KB Sascerides copy (LN 428 4°, eks. 1, autograph dedication 20 March 1588 — now the earliest dated carrier), the BL Maestlin De mundi (dispatched 14 May 1588 — the second dating keystone), the Magini De mundi, KB LN 428 copy 2, the Stockholm KB De mundi (RAR: 106 A), the Strahov Mechanica (green satin), the Wolfenbüttel Epistolae (HAB Astronomica 8), the St Petersburg Otto Brahe Mechanica (dedicated 5 October 1598), the Cambrai Lobkowitz Mechanica (dedicated 18 August 1600), and posthumous bindings to c.1603. Not the Smithsonian Epistolae, whose cover carries the arms stamp.

Settled
  1. The Petr Vok dedication date — settled at 18 September 1600. It was never a three-way: the September reading is the object itself, Tycho's autograph 'Anno 1600 Septembris Die 18' on leaf LN_432_2_003 of the Royal Library's digitisation (Digitale Samlinger object11914), read at full resolution in July 2026 (see the Mechanica watercolours record). Langebek's 'd. 18. Decemb. 1600' (Danske Magazin II, 1746, p. 321) is a misreading of the same flyleaf 146 years later, and the '1599?' sometimes attached to the copy has no stated basis.
Open questions 5
  1. Does either physical die survive? Unchecked channels are Nationalmuseet's object database and Frederiksborg's iconographic register, neither of which is likely to hold a binding tool. The documentary silence now runs back past Gigas to Resen 1668 and Danske Magazin 1746 — stamped covers described, never a tool.
  2. Per-volume die-size assignment for Kleinschnitzová's six Clementinum shelfmarks (8 H 39, 12 H 10, 14 G 30, 14 J 176, 19 C 92, 9 B 135) — the decisive test of the format hypothesis, with predictions on record (octavos small, quartos large).
  3. Reconciliation of the Bodleian SOLO full-livery description of the Savile copy against the photographed 4° T 2 Art.Seld. Sammelband, and recovery of the Weston Savilian shelfmark — one object with a misattached description, or a seventh De mundi carrier?
  4. A first-hand raking-light reading of both gilt legends on the Sascerides copy (LN 428 4°, eks. 1), illegible in the digitised surrogate.
  5. A caliper reading of the de Lode plate. Danske Magazin II (1746) claims to render the portrait die at 1:1 and the arms die at 'selv samme Form og Størrelse'; measuring the oval on a physical 1746 sheet — or on a scan with a known scale — is the cheapest independent test of the large die's dimensions anywhere in this edition, and the only one that does not measure a compressed impression on leather or vellum.
