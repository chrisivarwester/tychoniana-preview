4The objects, by holding institution

Where the thing itself is. This section re-projects the register along the axis of custody: every physical object or exemplar the edition has located or attested — the primary object of each record and every version and exemplar its record enumerates — grouped by holding institution, with inventory number, support and size where known, the verdict on it (original, copy, cast, reproduction, lost), and the institution’s own record where one is online. Objects that no longer exist are listed under the place where they perished. The question the table answers and the catalogue cannot is what does this institution hold: Frederiksborg, for instance, holds a candidate for the Steensgaard panel, a small copy of the burned oil, a cast of the Prague effigy, Heinrich Hansen’s history painting and, on one attestation, an Eckersberg sheet.

Recorded by Peder Hansen Resen, Inscriptiones Hafnienses (1668), pp. 335–336, whence its inscription is now read in full: first person ('restitutus fui'), without the 'divino provisu' clause, placed and dated VANDESBECHI AN. MDXCVII, and signed TYCHO BRAHE OT. — where the surviving canvases' caption is third-person, unplaced and undated. Mortensen's list gives its two recorded descendants as the Bodleian and Gavnø copies, but that is his list annotation only: his own prose (p. 71) asserts 'samme Inskription' across the Copenhagen and Edinburgh pictures — one chain, not branches, and wrong in detail, since the texts differ — and the shared pyramid/cone emblem connects the Copenhagen picture to the surviving canvases; the separate-branch stemma should be dropped.

In print: Danske Magazin II (Copenhagen 1746)

1 object
RecordWhatInventorySupport and sizeVerdictLink
The Týn church epitaph, Prague — the effigy that closes the seriesversion / exemplarcopper engraving, printed on the text pagethe earliest printed record of the monument, and the witness to its pre-1901 state
Attributed to Johann Martin Bernigeroth by the 2006 volume; the plate as printed carries no engraver's signature legible at 600 dpi and the 1746 text names none, so the attribution is not hardened here. The 1746 text documents the production chain: drawn in 1743 in Prague for Tycho de Hofman, the Danish Chancery secretary there for Maria Theresa's Bohemian coronation, and engraved for his Portraits historiques (The Hague, 1746), the plate then lent to Danske Magazin (bind 2, pp. 356–57) — naming the commissioner but no engraver. The Bernigeroth attribution traces to Vellev's fig.-3 caption alone, and his 'efter Danske Magazin' could even describe a later derivative. A collation trap: it is printed on the text page at the head of p. 353, not on an inserted leaf.
Grinder-Hansen ed. 2006, fig. 3; autopsy of the 1746 volume
