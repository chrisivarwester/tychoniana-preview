7Identification: the key, and the rejected identifications

How the likenesses are told apart, and how an identification is made or refused. The engraved family is the practical problem: a reader with a portrait in a book, a print in a cabinet or a painting on a wall needs to know which of the two de Gheyn states it derives from, and whether an inscribed date means anything. The discriminators below are the ones the records actually use; each is stated with the records it separates. The second part of the section keeps the identifications that have been proposed and rejected, with the ground of the rejection.

The discriminators

DiscriminatorReads one wayReads the otherCaution
The head: bare or in the plumed beretBare: the woodcut, the first engraving (beret on the parapet), the Frederiksborg oil, the Prague panel, the exile type, the Mechanica watercoloursBeret worn: the Copenhagen drawing, the second engraving, Cornelisz, the Gassendi frontispiece, Kilian, the 1594 globe miniature; the tapestry figure, if it is TychoThe beret is a return to how he dressed, not an invention of 1595, if the tapestry identification holds.
The Frederik II medal (F.S.) and the stacked chainsPresent: the portrait die, the first engraving, the Steensgaard panel (medal without Elephant)Absent: the second engraving and everything after it; the exile type and the watercolours carry other schemesThe die kept the loaded scheme for the rest of his life; a binding does not date by its costume.
The Order of the ElephantWorn: the Frederiksborg oil, the 1595 medal, the exile type; an elephant form on both dies and both engravingsAbsent: the mural effigy (no insignia at all), the tapestry figure, the Steensgaard panel, the Copenhagen watercolour (a plain knot)Christianson dates the grant to 3 May 1580, so the device gives a 1580 floor only; the usual ‘c. 1585/86’ is itself inferred from portraits, and dating portraits by it is circular. The 1746 eyewitness saw only ‘en Elefant’ on the die and named no order.
The ancestral quarteringsError state: BELKER where the Vasa sheaf belongs and HØNNOR for Rønnow — the woodcut and the first engravingCorrected: STORMVASE and RØNNOR — the second engraving and its copies (Gassendi)The woodcut has no corrected state; the error was still circulating on the 1592 prospectus.
The tablet’s vocabularyMachinarumque … dispositarum — the first engravingInstrumentorumque … dispositorum — the second engraving, Gassendi, KilianLegible on low-resolution digitisations, so it settles which engraving a given book carries.
The hands—Transposed left-for-right on the second engraving while the head is not mirrored: the fingerprint of a freshly drawn plate transferred from the Copenhagen drawingThe drawing is the mirror of the print with the head at 1:1; a copyist reproduces his source’s costume, and the drawing’s differs from both plates.
The inscribed ‘aetatis 40 … anno 1586’Carried by the woodcut, both engravings, the Gassendi frontispiece, the Weimar watercolour and Kilian’s standing figure of 1666It names the origin of the likeness and travels with the design. Wellcome’s catalogue dates Kilian’s plate 1586 from it.
The nose prosthesisShown: the Frederiksborg oil (reddened), the woodcut (as a plate on the bridge), the Týn effigy (plainly), the Copenhagen watercolour (per Biela, 1824)Absent from both de Gheyn states and from the exile type; Prandtl’s reading of it on Kilian’s plate is unconfirmedMortensen’s claim that the Prague panel indicates it is not visible on the only reproduction at any resolution.
Age and sense on the copiesCornelisz 1595: reversed, over two hemispheres, PRINCEPS in the ovalThe globe legends: ÆTATIS 47 computed fresh for 1594 and frozen on the 1613 Veen/Hondius globe; Blaeu’s miniature a reversed bustA frozen age on a later object points back to the model’s date, not the object’s.
Which engraving a book carriesEvery inserted portrait examined in Epistolae and Mechanica copies is the second engravingThe only physically verified book use of the first engraving is a 1602 Progymnasmata title versoInsertion was loose; copies with a watercolour substitute or no portrait at all are documented, and unexamined copies may differ.

Identifications considered and rejected 4

Things that have been called portraits of Tycho and are not, kept with the ground of each rejection so that a reader can judge whether the rejection is demonstrated or merely asserted. One of them is rejected on an argument its author never showed, and the edition records it as a rejection rather than as a demonstration.

The Næsbyholm painted ceiling — border medallions

rejectedrej-the-n-sbyholm-painted-ceiling-border-medallions
The claim
Implied to contain a portrait of Tycho by inclusion in a Tycho volume.
Verdict
Rejected. No source has ever proposed any head as Tycho.
Ground
Karl Madsen, Kunstens Historie i Danmark, pp. 62–64: 'Medaillonportræterne er sikkert for største Delen Portræter af Herremanden og hans Familie' — the squire being Steen Brahe, Tycho's brother. Absent from Mortensen, Dreyer, Friis, Norlind, Christianson, Beckett & Christensen and Studnička alike. Its presence in the 2006 book is a social-history illustration choice. The ceiling itself is at Nationalmuseet.

The Kunstkammer ivory box with portrait medallions

rejectedrej-the-kunstkammer-ivory-box-with-portrait-medallions
The claim
An 18th-century Kunstkammer tradition held that the two medallions showed Tycho Brahe and his wife.
Verdict
Rejected — but on an undefended argument.
Ground
Grinder-Hansen 2006: 'det hele er en skrøne' — the whole thing is a fable — because the man's head 'har ikke den fjerneste lighed med kendte portrætter af Tycho Brahe'. That is iconographic comparison without stated working: he names no compared portraits and shows no reasoning. The rejection is very probably right, and is recorded here as a rejection rather than as a demonstration. The object itself is untraced.

Langhans's photographs of the skull and upper body, Prague 1901

rejectedrej-langhans-s-photographs-of-the-skull-and-upper-body
The claim
Sometimes treated as images of Tycho.
Verdict
Out of scope, not false. These are documentary photographs of physical remains, not made likenesses.
Ground
Mortensen's census correctly ends at the Týn effigy, and the 2006 volume files them in a burial chapter, not a portrait chapter. Two things worth carrying: they falsify the claim, still circulating, that no photographic record survives from 1901; and the 'Det Kongelige Bibliotek' line under them is a reproduction credit, not a statement of ownership — no holding record for either photograph has been found.

'Tycho Brahe's chair' and the other Kunstkammer relics

rejectedrej-tycho-brahe-s-chair-and-the-other-kunstkammer-reli
The claim
Objects associated with Tycho by 18th-century tradition.
Verdict
Rejected. The chair is a Norwegian peasant chair.
Ground
Grinder-Hansen 2006, recording the Kunstkammer protocol of 1737 which enters a hollowed-trunk 'knubstol' as Tycho's chair from Uraniborg. Included here because the same appetite that produced the chair produced the ivory box: an 18th century that wanted Tycho relics and found them.
