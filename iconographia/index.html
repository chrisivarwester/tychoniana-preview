How each portrait is dated. Every lifetime portrait carries a lower and an upper bound, each fixed by an argument — a dated letter, a dated dispatch, an inscribed age, a death, an object that had to exist for something documented to happen. The bounds are only as strong as their arguments, so the argument is printed beside each pair; the grade (A–D) is the record's overall evidence grade. The portraits on the equatorial armillary's pedestalc. 1584December 1596 (the Icones instrumentorum issue)Name-labels N·C·T·G / T·BR·D sit on the entablatures above the niches; the year-tablets on the plinths beneath the figures read '…520' (leading digit obscured by the block's own carved bracket in every impression examined) and '1580' ('I580' is the font's flag-serif numeral 1). The years are floruit labels, settled by direct inspection of Nathan Chytraeus, Variorum in Europa itinerum deliciae (Herborn 1594), pp. 596–598 (2026-08-03): the Stjerneborg hypocaustum cycle — Tycho's own sibling lineage programme, on material his preface says came largely from Tycho himself — gives every figure an 'Anno X' floruit line, with COPERNICUS 'Anno 1520 post Christum' and TYCHO BRAHE 'Anno 1580': the identical pair of years the pedestal tablets carry. The pedestal therefore uses the same convention (and the obscured '…520' is confirmed as 1520); '1580' cannot in any case date the execution, since no armillary existed then. Christianson 2000's reading of 1580 as a construction year (against his own 2020 chronology) is thereby retired; what 1580 commemorates within Tycho's career (Uraniborg's completion is Dreyer's candidate) remains open, and Zeeberg 1995 ('The Inscriptions at Tycho Brahe's Uraniborg', in Skafte Jensen ed., Odense 1995, pp. 251–66 — undigitized; online retrieval exhausted 2026-08-03) is now corroborative rather than load-bearing. If Christianson's hedged Gemperlin attribution were adopted, the upper bound would fall to 1587; it stays 1598 (publication of the recording plate). The 1585 Icones instrumentorum (Nielsen 976, Uraniborg broadsides, Marburg unicum - enclosed with Tycho's 1 March 1586 letter to Wilhelm IV) was checked as a possible earlier attestation (2026-08-04): its armillary woodcut depicts the STEEL armilla (Fig. 13 type; 'pes...ex solido metallo'), not this instrument - a diagnostic null that leaves the 1598 plate the earliest witness to the portraits. MOVED 2026-08-04: the hard upper bound advances 1598 -> December 1596 - the Icones instrumentorum suite (Nielsen 975, Uraniborg 1596, 18 letterpress-free woodcut pulls issued with some Epistolae copies; presentation copies to Christian IV and Chancellor Friis in December 1596) contains the armillary printed from what is almost certainly the same woodblock (Stockholm KB exemplar examined leaf-by-leaf: identical tablets, V-medallion, and the full compositor letter-key in identical positions; print-flaw-level confirmation pending an uncoloured 1598 impression). The painting window is therefore [summer 1584 -> Dec 1596], preferred sub-window 1584-87 unchanged. The block itself was cut somewhere in [1584 -> 1596]; the documented production waves (two blocks 1585, two 1586-87 for De mundi, several 1592-93 for the Progymnasmata, boxwood-only from April 1590) make the post-1590 campaign the likeliest home for it, stated as preference.A
The Kronborg tapestry figure — the conjectured earliest likeness15811584The dating is the one certainty: the royal genealogy tapestry series for Kronborg was woven 1581–1584, so the figure — whoever he is — falls in that window on hard documentary grounds. Everything else is conditional: the identification as Tycho, first proposed by Wanscher (Kronborgs Historie, 1939; Mortensen's 'Kbh. 1930' is a probable citation error — Wanscher's 1930 book is Rosenborgs Historie), rests on facial resemblance in a small background figure and has never been proven ('possibly correct' was Mortensen's verdict). If the identification holds, this is the earliest surviving likeness of Tycho, made when he was 35–38.C
The Steensgaard panel — a lifetime portrait or an early copy, never testedc. 1584c. 1588These bounds date the LIKENESS the panel carries, not the panel itself, whose status is genuinely open. The costume places the likeness in the mid-1580s: Tycho wears the Frederik II medal and not the Order of the Elephant. That argument is weaker than it looks and should not be leaned on hard — the year Tycho received the Elephant is nowhere documented (Friis: he must have had it from Frederik II, 'but when, or on what occasion, is not known'), and the usual 'c.1585/86' is itself inferred from portraits, so dating portraits by it is circular. What the panel plainly is not is a picture of the later Tycho: it shows neither the Elephant of the 1586–87 oil nor the stacked chains of the engravings.C
The Stjerneborg astronomer cycle — Tycho as the terminus of his own science15841594Stjerneborg was built in 1584, so nothing could be painted in its warming room before that. The completed eight-figure cycle, including the Tychonides panel, is described as painted — icons with gold-lettered distichs in the hypocaustum — in Nathan Chytraeus, Variorum in Europa itinerum deliciae (Herborn 1594), pp.596–598, on material Chytraeus's preface says came largely from Tycho himself; Tycho's own fuller printed description follows in the Epistolae astronomicae (1596). The painting campaign in the meteorological diary falls in spring 1591, inside the window but tied to this cycle by no source.A
The Frederiksborg oil (attributed to Gemperlin) — known through C. A. Jensen's 1838 copylate 15861587The lower bound rests on Gassendi's report (1655 ed., p. 137) that toward the end of 1586 Tycho 'wished to be depicted' by Gemperlin — voluit, a volitional construction, not a flat report of a completed sitting; the 'fortieth birthday' precision sometimes attached to this is not in Gassendi's narrative but belongs to the caption of the volume's unrelated frontispiece print. The burned picture's own inscription, as reported by Friis (Bidrag til dansk Kunsthistorie, p. 229), 'Anno Domini 1587 aetatis suae 40', independently corroborates the 1586/87 window; a sweep of the TBOO correspondence for 1586–88 is negative. The Order of the Elephant the sitter wears supplies only a floor: Christianson (2020, p. 97) dates Tycho's grant to 3 May 1580 at Odense (quote verified; his footnote's archival source not yet traced), so the device gives a hard 1580 terminus post quem rather than a mid-1580s one. The upper bound is firmer: the painter's death. Tycho's own De mundi aetherei II (printed at Uraniborg 1587) records Gemperlin dead of the plague — so if the attribution holds, the panel cannot postdate 1587.B
The woodcut 'Effigies' (Westergaard 1376) — the praedelineatum's recordlate 1586February 1592The lower bound is the inscribed design date — 'aetatis 40 … 1586' names the sitting/design year, tied to Tycho's 40th birthday in December 1586. The upper bound is documentary: the block was in use on the title verso of the Progymnasmata prospectus distributed with letters of 20 February – 22 March 1592. The physical cutting date is open anywhere between those bounds. The 1588–90 wood-cutter recruitment letters concern instrument figures, never the portrait, and cannot anchor the block; the 25 Aug 1585 Hagecius letter shows cutting capacity intermittent and partly off-island. Both de Gheyn engravings retain the 1586 tablet years later — in this family the inscribed date travels with the design, not the object.B
The Prague panel — Mortensen's Clementinum portrait1586openThe panel, painted on wood and held in the astronomical museum in the Clementinum at Prague, is unsigned and carries the same composition and the same inscription as the 1586 woodcut, so the two are certainly connected. Which came first is the question. Mortensen argued the panel is the woodcut's MODEL rather than a copy of it, resting chiefly on a detail of the hands: the painting shows two rings that the woodcut leaves out, and a copyist adds detail rather than removing it. If he is right the panel dates from about 1586 and is the closest surviving thing to the painted stage behind the whole print campaign; if he is wrong the panel could be considerably later. He was himself tentative, and the record follows him without asserting him. Two further limits now stand on that question. The ring count — the sole load-bearing evidence for the model thesis — is untestable at the surviving reproduction's confirmed native maximum of 2200 × 3190 px: two independent attempts left no countable ring bands on either hand, so Mortensen's evidence is currently unverifiable in either direction. And since the panel's existence is itself attested by Mortensen alone, the existence question is logically prior to the dating question.C
The mural quadrant portrait (Uraniborg)15821587The great mural quadrant itself was built in 1582 — nothing could be painted in its arc before that. The date 1587 is not merely conventional: it is inscribed on the plate's own tablet ('A° DOMINI 1587 ÆTATIS SVÆ 40', confirmed letter-for-letter at 600 DPI in two independent KB Copenhagen copies), and Tycho's text endorses the tablet ('prout vides'). The cap on Gemperlin's share of the work comes from his death — dead by 1587, the die-window running 1586 to May 1588 — not from Tycho's testimony. For the effigy and the architecture the attribution rests on Tycho's own word: he named Gemperlin and Stenwinkel outright, and praised the likeness in the Mechanica ('vix similior dari possit' — scarcely could a more faithful one be made); the landscape hand he named only as 'Johannes of Antwerp, royal painter at Kronborg', the surname Knieper being inherited, uncontested scholarship. A genuine late-1586 strand — Gassendi (via Resen 1668, attached to this mural effigy specifically), Thoren 1990, and Friis's earlier essay ('omtrent 1586') — dates the execution to late 1586; under aetatis-40 reckoning (Tycho's 40th year ran 14 December 1586 – 13 December 1587) both dates are consistent with a sitting late in 1586 completed by 1587.A
The gilt portrait supralibros — two dieslate 158620 March 1588The upper bound is an object: the Royal Library's Sascerides copy of De mundi aetherei (LN 428 4°, eks. 1) carries BOTH gilt ovals — portrait on the front cover, arms on the back — on its original parchment presentation binding, with Tycho's autograph dedication to Gellius Sascerides dated 20 March 1588 (verified on the EEB surrogate), so both dies existed by that date. The British Library's Maestlin copy, dispatched from Uraniborg on 14 May 1588, remains the second keystone — nobody rebinds a book INTO a Tycho presentation binding. The lower bound is the costume it carries: the multi-chain-and-medallion scheme belongs to the likeness layer begun with the 1586 sitting. This dating overturns the older literature's assumption (Mortensen; Christianson) that the stamp copies de Gheyn's engraving — the engraving was cut only in autumn 1590, two years after the die was in use. The small die's own first anchor is much later: its documented carrier is the Calvin/Theocritus volume Tycho gave Hans Lindenov, registered at Uraniborg on 21 March 1597.A
Frederik II's funeral procession, 1588 — Tycho among the coffin-bearers5 June 15881589Frederik II died on 4 April 1588 and the procession to Roskilde took place on 5 June; the engraved suite is dated 1589.C
De Gheyn's first engraving (Westergaard 1377) — the 'overloaded' portraitautumn 15901 December 1590The tightest-dated object in the whole series, on Tycho's own letters: 'nuper Amsterodami cupro insculptae' — recently engraved at Amsterdam — he wrote on 1 December 1590, dispatching impressions to Thomas Savile, with further sendings to Rantzau (7 December) and Scultetus (8 December). The letters name no artist; the plate is signed by de Gheyn. The inscribed 'aetatis 40 … 1586' names the likeness's origin, not the plate's date.A
The Copenhagen pen drawing (SMK KKSgb5254) — the second engraving's production drawingafter 7 December 1590c. mid-1595The drawing is the working model for the second engraving — mirror-reversed against the print, needle-traced for transfer, the head at 1:1 with the engraved head (measured) — so it postdates the 7 December 1590 order for that engraving and predates the plate's completion, which Lambert Cornelisz's dated 1595 copy places by mid-1595. The 'ca. 1586?' sometimes attached to it is back-dating driven by the untenable Gemperlin attribution; the sheet's design CONTENT (the 1586 likeness of the 40-year-old) is older than the sheet. A residual alternative — one c. 1590 sheet serving both plates, v1's head also transferred from it — remains live and unexcluded: both engravings carry the identical AETATIS-40/1586 caption, and the documented v1→v2 manner shift (dense/tonal → lighter/linear) could make a single model read smoother in v1 and more furrowed in v2. The drawing's v2-matching age signature and worn cap are supporting but confounded observations (low–medium); the discriminating test — calibrated 1:1 scale registration of the head against NH 238 — has not been run.A
De Gheyn's second engraving (Westergaard 1394) — the corrected portrait7 December 1590 (ordered)mid-1595 (design attested in Tycho's hands via the Cornelisz copy); plate completion by end 1596 (Altena's outer bound)The lower bound is Tycho's own re-order in the complaint letter of 7 December 1590 ('aliam illic confieri curem' — I am having another made there). The upper bound (revised July 2026): Lambert Cornelisz's reversed copy of this portrait is dated 1595 in its plate and was in Tycho's hands by 5 September 1595 (his letter to Pontanus) — so the design existed by mid-1595. Whether Cornelisz copied the printed plate or the Vorlage drawing KKSgb5254 is contested (Mortensen 1946 p. 21: 'Forlæg: Pennetegningen'); the 1595 date therefore pins the design with high confidence and the plate's completion only probably — the hard ceiling for the finished plate is Altena's 'before the end of 1596'. This still retires the older account that dated the model-delivery to May 1596 (the painter in Tycho's diary on 4 May and Blaeu's departure on 24 May 1596 cannot explain a design that was already circulating in Cornelisz's copy the year before). The corrected heraldry gives an internal lower bound as well: the error-state was still circulating on the February–March 1592 prospectus, so the correction falls between 1592 and the plate's completion (by end 1596).A
Lambert Cornelisz's print (Westergaard 1409) — the copy Tycho disliked1595 (dated in the plate)5 September 1595 (in Tycho's hands)The plate carries its own date, 1595, and Tycho's letter to his former assistant Pontanus, written at Uraniborg on 5 September 1595, shows he had already received impressions — one had come earlier via Cort Aslaksson, who wrongly believed it engraved at Cologne. The letter is also the print's significance for the whole chronology: being a reversed copy of the second de Gheyn portrait, its date proves the v2 image existed in copyable form by mid-1595; it dates the finished v2 plate only on the leading (unproven) reading that Cornelisz copied a printed impression rather than the design — the diagnostic zones are unusable because Cornelisz replaced the background apparatus, the mirror evidence is mixed, and CERL records de Gheyn II as his teacher (undated), a possible access route to pre-plate material. Under the design-model reading the plate's own terminus loosens and the May-1596 Blaeu/Pictor channel reopens.A
The 1595 portrait medal — the only likeness in metal15951595The medal dates itself: the obverse legend reads 'EFFIGIES TYCHONIS BRAHE O F ... AETATIS 49' and the reverse carries the date 1595 beneath the Brahe arms — Tycho was 48 turning 49 that year. Whether it was struck in his lifetime is a separate and softer question, and the hedges have three distinct roots: Dreyer's 'it has been suggested' derives from — and truncates — Danske Magazin II (1746), pp. 166–168, whose editors offered an explicitly unprovable twin conjecture (the completion of the star catalogue and the great brass globe reaching usable form in 1595); Beckett's suggestion that Tycho gave the medal as a princely favour is independent of that, from a 1946 private letter to Mortensen, not derived from the 1746 discussion; and the 1746 editors also floated Tycho's nephew Tage Ottesen Tott, then 15–16, as a possible commissioner. No contemporary document of 1594–97 attesting the medal has been found in the material searched. So the object and its date are documented; the lifetime striking is a reasonable inference, not a documented fact.B
The aetatis-50 portrait (Skokloster / Edinburgh) — the exile likeness14 December 15961597The dating rests on two different texts, not one. The surviving Skokloster and Edinburgh canvases carry a third-person caption — 'ÆTATIS SVÆ ANNO 50 COMPLETO … QVO POST DIVTINVM IN PATRIA EXILIVM LIBERTATI DESIDERATÆ DIVINO PROVISV RESTITVTVS EST' — with no place and no year, verified by direct examination of high-resolution photographs of both canvases and by SHM's own catalogue transcription. The dated text is attested only on the lost Copenhagen picture, via Resen, Inscriptiones Hafnienses (1668), pp. 335–336: first person ('restitutus fui'), placed and dated VANDESBECHI AN. MDXCVII, and signed TYCHO BRAHE OT. That exemplar's year is settled at 1597 — Resen's apostrophus numeral; Dreyer 1890, p. 264, verified against the printed page image; TBOO IX.211, sourced in its own apparatus to Cod. Vindob. lat. 10686; DSL's '1598' is a transcription slip in the modern digital edition. The lower bound of 14 December 1596, the day Tycho completed his fiftieth year, follows from 'aetatis suae anno 50 completo' and stands; but the 1597 anchor transfers to the surviving canvases by type-identity inference only, since their own captions are undated.B
The painted portraits in the finest Mechanica copies — Tycho between Ptolemy and CopernicusJanuary 1598 (production of the Mechanica)24 October 1601The Mechanica was produced from January 1598, which sets the campaign's lower bound; Tycho's death on 24 October 1601 caps it, and the bulk of the presentation copies had gone out by about 1600. The Copenhagen copy's autograph dedication of 18 September 1600 is that exemplar's terminus ante quem. The former lower-bound basis — an 'Aet: LII' age inscription on the watercolours — is withdrawn: full-resolution inspection of the Copenhagen leaf (July 2026) found no age inscription of any kind, Mortensen's actual printed reading is 'Aet: LI', type-generic and anchored to Andersen's 1886 etching rather than to any leaf, and Vellev's 'LII' remains unlocated.B
The Týn church epitaph, Prague — the effigy that closes the series1604 (Kirstine's death — the only sourced terminus post quem for the erection; the 1601 burial dates the grave, not the monument)after 1604 (no sourced completion date)Tycho died in Prague on 24 October 1601 and was buried in the Týn church, but the monument's erection is sourced only as after his widow Kirstine's death in 1604 (Friis 1871 p. 298, Mortensen 1946 p. 74, Norlind 1970 p. 318); no source attests an earlier figure-stone phase. It therefore stands past the lifetime boundary — but it was made in Prague by people who had seen him, and it closes the series.B
Where every one of them is today — and whether it is the thing itself. Two questions are kept apart here, because the literature routinely runs them together. Where is it? is about survival and custody: an object can be lost, destroyed, untraced, or sitting in a named room under an inventory number. Is it an original? is a separate question with its own evidence, and for Tycho the honest answer is often no — several types survive only through copies, and for one whole type the original is gone and every located painting is a copy of a lost picture rather than of any surviving one. The evidence tier states how firmly the present location is held: located means a live record-level catalogue entry or an institution naming the object; attested means somebody once said so, sometimes a century ago. The full-figure watercolour at Strahov — the standing Tycho with a sextantc. 1598 – 1666; the drawing's date is openuntracedAt Strahov, in a presentation copy of the Progymnasmata — a different book from the Strahov Mechanica. Friis observes that the photolithograph agrees with Philip Kilian's engraving in Historia Coelestis (ed. Albert Curtz as 'Lucius Barrettus', Augsburg 1666) in both form and size so closely that the drawing may be the very model for the plate — but equally it may be after it, or by Kilian himself. Friis's own view is that the ultimate original behind both is de Gheyn's engraving. Šafařík's 1881 description of it as 'the only original portrait of Tycho in Prague' should not be relied on. A first comparison against Kilian's plate (made here, at matched figure height) confirms beyond doubt that the two are the same composition, in the same direction, with the same pose, instrument, beret, ruff, fur-lined cloak and stance. The question stays open. The pair has since been run through the house vorlage protocol, which adds one datum: the two are same-sense. Under the house mirror rule — six of six calibrated Netherlandish painting-to-engraving pairs reverse — a same-sense pair leans against the watercolour having served as a face-up-traced production model for the plate. Identifications considered and rejected

Things that have been called portraits of Tycho and are not, kept here with the ground of the rejection. One of them is rejected on an argument its author never showed — that is recorded too.

  • The Næsbyholm painted ceiling — border medallions
    Claim: Implied to contain a portrait of Tycho by inclusion in a Tycho volume.
    Verdict: Rejected. No source has ever proposed any head as Tycho.
    Karl Madsen, Kunstens Historie i Danmark, pp. 62–64: 'Medaillonportræterne er sikkert for største Delen Portræter af Herremanden og hans Familie' — the squire being Steen Brahe, Tycho's brother. Absent from Mortensen, Dreyer, Friis, Norlind, Christianson, Beckett & Christensen and Studnička alike. Its presence in the 2006 book is a social-history illustration choice. The ceiling itself is at Nationalmuseet.
  • The Kunstkammer ivory box with portrait medallions
    Claim: An 18th-century Kunstkammer tradition held that the two medallions showed Tycho Brahe and his wife.
    Verdict: Rejected — but on an undefended argument.
    Grinder-Hansen 2006: 'det hele er en skrøne' — the whole thing is a fable — because the man's head 'har ikke den fjerneste lighed med kendte portrætter af Tycho Brahe'. That is iconographic comparison without stated working: he names no compared portraits and shows no reasoning. The rejection is very probably right, and is recorded here as a rejection rather than as a demonstration. The object itself is untraced.
  • Langhans's photographs of the skull and upper body, Prague 1901
    Claim: Sometimes treated as images of Tycho.
    Verdict: Out of scope, not false. These are documentary photographs of physical remains, not made likenesses.
    Mortensen's census correctly ends at the Týn effigy, and the 2006 volume files them in a burial chapter, not a portrait chapter. Two things worth carrying: they falsify the claim, still circulating, that no photographic record survives from 1901; and the 'Det Kongelige Bibliotek' line under them is a reproduction credit, not a statement of ownership — no holding record for either photograph has been found.
  • 'Tycho Brahe's chair' and the other Kunstkammer relics
    Claim: Objects associated with Tycho by 18th-century tradition.
    Verdict: Rejected. The chair is a Norwegian peasant chair.
    Grinder-Hansen 2006, recording the Kunstkammer protocol of 1737 which enters a hollowed-trunk 'knubstol' as Tycho's chair from Uraniborg. Included here because the same appetite that produced the chair produced the ivory box: an 18th century that wanted Tycho relics and found them.
One face, engineered once, corrected once, and propagated. The likeness layer of 1586 (the sittings behind the Frederiksborg oil and the woodcut) fed a print campaign that Tycho edited like a text: the first Amsterdam engraving of 1590 arrived overloaded with chains against his instructions, he ordered a corrected replacement within days, and that second engraving — produced from the surviving Copenhagen drawing and completed by mid-1595 — became the image Europe copied, in his lifetime (the Cornelisz print he grumbled about in 1595) and after it. The gilt binding die ran on a separate track: in use by May 1588, carrying the loaded costume before the first engraving existed, and never re-cut afterwards.
