Iconographia — The Images of Tycho Brahe

This is the catalogue of the images of Tycho Brahe. It holds three series. The lifetime series is every documented likeness made while he lived or from the memory of people who had seen him: the paintings on wood, wall and instrument pedestal, the woodcut, the two Amsterdam engravings and the drawing behind the second, the gilt binding die, the medal, the exile portrait, the watercolours in the finest Mechanica copies, and the Prague effigy that closes the series. The image programme is the wider set of images he controlled: the arms stamp that partners the portrait die, the engraved portrait inserted loose in his books, the emblem-quadrant, the printed views of his buildings, and his portrait on the celestial globes. The afterlife follows the face from the first posthumous plates to the nineteenth-century history painting and the children’s memorial sheet.

The baseline is Harald Mortensen’s census of the portraits (1946), read against F. R. Friis’s descriptions of the 1890s, the exhibition volume Tycho Brahes Verden (2006), the print catalogues (Westergaard, the New Hollstein, van Regteren Altena), and Tycho’s own letters and printed words about his likeness. Every record has then been checked against the object or its institutional digitisation wherever one is public, and the plates in this edition are taken from those digitisations. Mortensen’s census is treated as a finding-list of what he located, not as an authority on what the objects are: several of his verdicts are stated without a basis, and three of them are corrected here on the evidence of the objects themselves.

Each record states what the image is, who made it and when; on what evidence the dates rest; where the object is today and how firmly that is known; whether it is an original or a copy; which versions and exemplars are known and where; where the sources disagree; what has been settled; and what remains open. The apparatus is the point of the edition, and none of it is withheld.

The unit is the image, not the object. A record is one likeness or one image type. The second engraving is one record although impressions of it sit in a dozen print rooms; the Mechanica watercolours are one record although five leaves survive and each is unique; the portrait die is one record although its impressions are on bindings across Europe. Where a record has several physical carriers, they are listed under it as versions and exemplars, and again by holding institution in §4.

Three questions are kept apart because the literature routinely runs them together. Does the thing survive? is the record’s status: extant, lost, untraced (last seen at a stated date, not found since) or attested (reported by one witness, never confirmed). Where is it? is the whereabouts tier, which states how firmly the present location is held: located means a live record-level catalogue entry or an institution naming the object; attested means somebody once said so, sometimes a century ago; for an edition multiple it says what survives and where the reproduced impression is. Is it the original? is the record’s nature, answered separately: for one whole type the original is gone and every located painting is a copy of a lost picture rather than of any surviving one.

Dates are bounds with arguments. A record that can be dated carries a lower and an upper bound, each fixed by a stated argument — a dated letter, a dedication, an inscribed age, a painter’s death, an object that had to exist for something documented to happen. The bounds are printed with their arguments in §5. One convention recurs: in this family the inscribed ‘aetatis 40 … 1586’ names the origin of the likeness and travels with the design; it never dates the object that carries it.

Record keys. Every record prints its key (icon-degheyn-v2) and is addressable: #icon-degheyn-v2 as an anchor, or ?record=icon-degheyn-v2, which highlights it. Sources are cited by short form; every short form links to its full entry in §9.

What the figures are not. The eighteen records of the lifetime series are a count of records, not of likenesses taken from life. Two of the eighteen stand in the series on other grounds and the edition says so in each: the Týn effigy was erected after 1604, from the memory of people who had seen him, and the funeral plate of 1589 is a positional claim — Tycho among the canopy-bearers — not a likeness at all. A third, the Kronborg tapestry figure, is there on a conjectural identification that has never been established. The count of extant records is not a count of surviving objects: edition multiples survive in numbers nobody has counted, and the versions tables list only the exemplars actually located. ‘Illustrated’ counts records with a plate; two records share one plate, since the inserted book portrait is the second engraving. No grade is a statement about survival, and no status is a statement about location.
