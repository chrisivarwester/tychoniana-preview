1Introduction

This is a catalogue of the astronomical instruments of Tycho Brahe: the twenty-one he engraved, one to a plate, in his Astronomiae instauratae mechanica (Wandsbek, 1598), and five further instruments that the sources document but the book does not illustrate — the cross-staff of his student years, the observatory clocks, the wooden triquetrum reputed to be Copernicus’s own, and the two iron sextants in Prague that have long been shown as his. Every entry rests on Tycho’s own description in the Mechanica, read in the 1946 English translation of Raeder and the Strömgrens and, for the Latin with a Danish translation, in Scripta; on Arne Wennberg’s exposition of the measuring geometry; on Thoren’s production history of the instruments; and, for the survivors and the fate of the apparatus, on Wolfschmidt, Šíma, Fučíková and Mortensen. The sources are listed in full in §12, each with what it contributes.

The edition is one document. The catalogue is a table for finding and comparing; the register gives each instrument in full, with its plate, its measuring principle, its dimensions in Tycho’s own units and their conversion, where it stood, what became of it, and the sources for each statement. The sections that follow re-project the same twenty-six records along axes the table cannot serve: the surviving objects, the concordance to the book and to the earlier scholarly catalogues, the rooms and crypts, the measurement problems and techniques, the reading resolution of the scales, the dispersal after 1601, and the chronology.

How to read an entry

Each register entry carries a stable key (inst-…), printed in the entry and usable in a link (?instrument=<key>) or a citation (“Instrumenta, §3, no. 5, the mural quadrant”). The entries follow the order of the plates in the Mechanica, Figures 1–22 in the numbering of the 1946 translation, and then the five instruments the book does not illustrate, by date.

The plate reproduced at the head of an entry is the engraving from the 1598 edition, cropped from the Royal Library’s second copy; its Latin caption is given as engraved. The quotation in Tycho’s own words is the 1946 translation, cited by page. How it works states the measuring principle in one paragraph, with a diagram where the geometry is the point. The specification gives the dimensions as Tycho states them (in cubits and feet, converted by the editors’ key of 388 mm to the cubit and 259 mm to the foot), the material, the resolution to which the scale could be read, and the room or crypt in which the instrument stood, linked to the site record in Loca. Relations resolve the record’s links to the other pillars: the chapter of the Mechanica that describes it, the observations it made, the people who copied or recovered it, the portraits it carries. The concordance line places the instrument in the book and in the earlier catalogues (§5). Where Blaeu re-engraved the instrument in colour in his atlas of 1664, that plate and Blaeu’s account follow.

What the figures are not

Reading resolution is not accuracy. The figure given as reads to is how finely the graduation could be read — ten arcseconds on the transversal scales of the great quadrants, about an arcminute on the simpler arcs. It is not how accurately a star’s place came out: refraction, the naked-eye image and systematic error left the achieved accuracy far coarser, a median of about 3.01′ against the modern sky for the star catalogue as a whole. The comparison is drawn in §8; the achieved figures are Observationes’.

Dates. A building date follows Wennberg, the 1946 translation and Thoren’s biography. Where Thoren’s genealogical chart of the instruments (1990, Table 5.1) gives a different year, the concordance says so; his 1973 redating from the observation logs has not yet been applied (§11). In use until is the end of Tycho’s use, 1601 unless the sources say otherwise.

Dimensions. Where Wolfschmidt and Thoren disagreed on a radius, Tycho’s own figure in the 1946 text settles it: the mural quadrant and the revolving steel quadrant are “nearly five cubits”, 194 cm; the revolving quadrant “four cubits”, 155 cm; the Augsburg quadrant “at least fourteen cubits”, about 543 cm.

Destruction. “Destroyed in the aftermath of the Bohemian war of 1619” is a collective statement about the instruments carried to Prague, from Gassendi through Dreyer and Wolfschmidt; no single instrument’s end is dated except the Augsburg quadrant (a storm, December 1574) and the great globe (the Copenhagen fire of 1728). The chronology (§10) draws each undated bar to the end of use and says so.

Survival. Not one instrument of Tycho’s own design or completion survives. The two sextants in Prague were made by other men, Habermel and (probably) Bürgi, and whether Tycho owned or used either is contested; they are graded accordingly and kept in the catalogue as “attributed”. The engravings, and the copies of the book that carry them, are the surviving witnesses (§4).

1

Quadrans minor — the smallest (gilt-brass) quadrant

Quadrant Tycho's own design destroyed Binst-smallest-quadrant

Tycho's earliest and smallest quadrant (radius ≈39 cm), of gilt brass, for altitudes. It is best remembered for a small allegorical painting on it: a tree green and living on the left, dead and withered on the right; beneath the living branches a youth with laurel, book and star-globe, beneath the dead a skeleton reaching for a table of riches — with a Latin verse that only study and knowledge confer immortality. Later kept at Stjerneborg. Not extant.

“The use of this quadrant is for finding the altitudes of the stars and of the sun and moon in such cases when complete accuracy is not necessary, a value with an error less than two or three minutes being sufficient.”
Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 15

How it works

A small altitude quadrant — his first — fitted with the Nonius/Nunes auxiliary-arc scale, which Tycho soon abandoned for his own transversal division.

Quadrans mediocris azimuthalis — the medium brass quadrant

Quadrant Tycho's own design destroyed Binst-medium-quadrant

A solid-brass altitude-azimuth quadrant of 58 cm radius with a horizontal circle, in the larger southern observatory of Uraniborg. It is the instrument on which Tycho carried both fine-reading systems side by side — the Nonius/Nunes arcs and his own transversals — and concluded that the transversal was the practical one. Not extant.

“The use of the instrument is very comprehensive and includes all cases where observed altitudes and azimuths of a planet or a fixed star can by trigonometry yield a numerical solution conforming to the requirements of observational astronomy.”
Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 18

How it works

A solid-brass altitude-azimuth quadrant — the one instrument carrying both reading systems, the Portuguese Nonius/Nunes auxiliary arcs and Tycho's own transversal division, by which he compared the two and settled on the transversal.

4

Astronomical sextant with parallel-slit sights

Sextant Tycho's own design destroyed Binst-astronomical-sextant

The astronomical sextant Tycho first devised at Augsburg (c.1569, with Petrus Ramus in mind) and refined at Hven with parallel-slit sights. Its priority — and the parallel-slit sights — are the matter of the Wittich dispute aired in the long 1587 letter to Rothmann: Paul Wittich, after his 1580 visit to Hven, reproduced Tycho's instruments at the Landgrave's observatory in Kassel. Not extant.

“The astronomical sextant instrument, which is particularly convenient in use, was first invented by myself, about twenty years ago.”
Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 25

How it works

A 60° star-distance sextant on a ball-and-socket copper globe so it can be turned into the plane of any two stars. One observer holds the fixed sight on one star, a second moves the alidade onto the other, both aiming at a central cylinder; the arc between reads the distance. For very small gaps a detached parallel sight keeps the two observers from colliding. Fitted with Tycho's pinnacidia (parallel-slit sights).

The geometry is drawn at §7, problem 2.

5

Quadrans muralis sive Tichonicus — the great mural quadrant

Quadrant Tycho's own design destroyed Ainst-mural-quadrant

A brass arc fixed to a wall set precisely in the meridian (north–south) at Uraniborg — Tycho's most celebrated instrument. Radius 194 cm — Tycho's own 'nearly five cubits' (Wolfschmidt 2002, p. 207; Thoren's 'two metres' is a rounding). Planned from 1576 and emerging from his Hven shop in June 1582 (Thoren, p. 163); Tycho was still adjusting it on the wall in 1583 (Thoren, p. 164 fn 43). Limb five inches broad, two inches thick — Tycho's own figures (Raeder 1946, p. 28), roughly 8 × 3 cm by Thoren's own conversion (arc breadth = 1/24 of the five-cubit radius; p. 163 n. 42). Thoren's main-text 'thirteen millimeters broad, and five millimeters thick' (p. 163) contradicts his own footnote and is an error. The wall within the arc bore the famous triple mural painting of Tycho himself amid views of Uraniborg's chambers. Its transversal scale was graduated to sixths of a minute; only after 1585, when the great armillary and the revolving quadrant became available to check its results, did Tycho regard its positions as confirmed (Thoren, p. 190). Known from the Mechanica engraving.

“It is called Mural, or Tichonicus, after the wall on which it is fixed. … every single minute can be divided again into six subdivisions; thus ten seconds of arc are plainly distinguishable and even half this, or five seconds of arc, can be read without difficulty.”
Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 29

How it works

A quarter-circle fixed flat on a wall set exactly in the meridian, its centre at a sight-cylinder through the south wall, graduated 0° at the top down to 90° on the plumb-line. Because the altitude and the arc above the sight-line are equal vertical angles, and the scale starts from the top, the reading is the meridian altitude directly.

The geometry is drawn at §7, problem 1.

Revolving quadrant (quadrans azimuthalis volubilis)

Quadrant Tycho's own design destroyed Binst-revolving-quadrant

A large revolving wood quadrant — 'one-and-a-half-meter' per Thoren (p. 177), 155 cm per Wolfschmidt 2002 — available at the turn of 1585–6 (Thoren, p. 177). Probably an experimental analogue to the mural quadrant, kept rigid by lightweight struts instead of anchors to a wall, so it could be turned to any azimuth. Great equatorial armillary sphere

Armillary Tycho's own design destroyed Ainst-great-equatorial-armillary

The great equatorial armillary of the Stjerneborg crypt, 1.36 m radius (Wolfschmidt 2002; Thoren: 'one-and-a-third metre'), completed by the summer solstice of 1585 — the single most important instrument on Hven (Thoren, ch. 5, p. 174). Foundation laid December 1584. Its single declination ring — made of wood to minimise flexure, except for brass alidades and graduation strips — was pivoted on a hollow steel axis tilted to the Hven latitude. In simplicity it rivalled the mural quadrant; right ascension and declination are read directly, in the equatorial coordinate system astronomers still use. Tycho had tried a zodiacal (ecliptic) armillary first but abandoned it almost immediately; the converted zodiacal armillary was the first equatorial armillary (c. 1583–84), the two smaller equatorial armillaries in the Uraniborg castle itself (north and south, 1584) were the second step, and this great Stjerneborg instrument was the third and culminating form: 'a third, which was to become the single most important instrument on Hven' (Thoren, p. 174). After 1585, the great armillary and the revolving quadrant together enabled Tycho to cross-check the mural quadrant systematically, and it was by this comet-of-1585 season that he achieved his 1′ accuracy target (Thoren, p. 190). Arcus bipartitus — the bipartite arc (1583)

Arc Tycho's own design lost Binst-bipartite-arc

A distance-measuring arc Tycho invented in 1583, while determining the precise positions of the stars in Cassiopeia for the new-star discussion of the Progymnasmata. On the ordinary sextant two observers had to sight the same small interstellar gap and got in each other's way; the bipartite arc solved this by splitting the axis of the measured angle into two parallel 'zero points'. Constructing it, Tycho realised the same split-axis principle could be adapted to the sextant itself — so the instrument is as much a step in the evolution of his sextant design as an instrument in its own right (Thoren, Lord of Uraniborg, pp. 169–170, Fig. 5.13; TBOO V, 68–71). Enumerated among the Mechanica instruments. Not extant.

“… an instrument which can measure smaller distances up to one-twelfth of the circumference of the circle, or, as it is called, one sign of the zodiac.”
Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 69

How it works

A bar carrying two 30° arcs whose zero-points sit mid-scale, and a cross-piece of two cylinders at the arcs' centres. With both sights at zero the two sight-lines are parallel, so two observers see the same (effectively infinitely distant) star; swinging the right sight onto a nearby star reads the small angle — up to 15°, or up to 30° by also shifting the left sight.

Globus magnus orichalcicus — the great brass globe

Globe Finished by Tycho on another's shell destroyed Ainst-globe

A celestial globe of almost six Tychonic feet (c. 149 cm) in diameter. Its wooden shell was commissioned in 1570 from Christopher Schissler the Elder, the leading Augsburg instrument-maker, who built it of many fir staves and rings fitted together like a great puzzle (Christianson, On Tycho's Island, p. 358; Dreyer, p. 112). On Tycho's return to Augsburg in 1575 it was found not perfectly spherical and slightly cracked; once received in Denmark in 1576 the cracks were stopped and the sphericity restored, after which the globe was observed over a two-year period to be sure it would hold its shape through the changeable Danish climate before being sheathed in brass, marked with ecliptic and equator divided to a minute of arc, and mounted in divided meridian and horizon rings. An inscription on the horizon dated its completion to 1584; only then were star positions engraved — Tycho's own measured places, added down to 1595 — making it a precision computing instrument as well as a showpiece (Mosley, Bearing the Heavens, ch. 4). The maker of the shell is normally identified as Christopher Schissler the Elder, though Mosley flags the identification as not beyond doubt. The longest-surviving of all Tycho's instruments, with a circuitous return: listed in the 6 Dec 1621 Belvedere inventory ('Ein sehr grosser globus coelestis vom metall'), it was acquired c.1625 by the Jesuit Christoph Scheiner for the Jesuit College at Neisse (Nysa) in Silesia, whence in 1632 it was carried as war-booty to Copenhagen by Prince Ulrik, son of Christian IV; there it stood in the University's observatory tower until that tower and all its contents burned in 1728 (Mortensen, Fra Tycho Brahes Bogsamling).

“A globe of this size, so solidly and finely worked, and correct in every respect, has never I think been constructed up to now by any man anywhere in the world.”
Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 104

How it works

A precision celestial globe used as an analogue computer: set the pole-altitude on the meridian at the horizon, turn a star to the meridian to read its declination there and its right ascension on the equator; a movable vertical circle gives altitude and the horizon ring azimuth — all four coordinates, and the conversions between the three systems.

Copernicus's triquetrum — the relic from Frauenburg

Parallactic rule Owned relic — made by another lost Binst-copernicus-triquetrum

A wooden triquetrum (parallactic rulers) reputed to have been made and used by Copernicus himself — the single most celebrated relic in Tycho's collection. When Elias Olsen Morsing returned from his 1584 expedition to Frauenburg (Frombork), sent to re-measure the polar altitude that Copernicus had gotten wrong, the cathedral canons — impressed by the expedition and the attention it drew to their illustrious predecessor — sent the instrument back with him as a gift to Tycho. Genuinely awed, Tycho placed it on permanent display in the North Observatory at Uraniborg and composed an impromptu Latin poem to commemorate the occasion (TBOO V, 44–47; partly trans. Edward Rosen in Dobrzycki, ed., Nicholas Copernicus on the Revolutions, 1978, p. 412). His reverence later gave way to objectivity: rulers only eight feet long, made of pine and scaled by pen and ink, could not yield decent observations, and Tycho — though he had already built a larger brass version and found it wanting — duplicated Copernicus's model in his own shop just to test it. Because he circulated the verdict so widely in the Mechanica, Thoren notes the replica 'may well have been the last such instrument built.' Distinct from Tycho's own working parallactic instrument, the brass triquetrum of the north observatory; not extant. Five years on, Tycho summed up Copernicus's instruments: 'by means of these puny cudgels he surmounted the lofty Olympus' (TBOO VI, 253).

The older Weinek 1901 / Mortensen reading instead held it 'probably made by Pater Johann Klein', director of Prague's Mathematical Chamber 1732–82 — i.e. an 18th-century instrument, not Tychonic at all (the c.1780 inventory's claim that it was 'a smaller sextant of Tycho Brahe, which he brought to Prague' notwithstanding). A sextant of the same size and very similar form survives at the Kremsmünster observatory in Austria, described by Rankl 1946 as 'Der Tychonische Sextant (von Bürgi)'. That Tycho's own Hven instruments are all lost makes any Prague-period survivor precious; the Bürgi attribution is the current consensus but graded B for the open dispute. For the shared Belvedere → Kunstkammer → 1648-Armoury → Herget (1782) → NTM Prague provenance of both surviving sextants, see Fučíková 2002 (detailed under the Habermel sextant).

7Measurement and technique

In the Mechanica Tycho introduces each instrument by the coordinates it yields — one, he writes, determines “not only declinations and right ascensions of the stars … but … their longitudes and latitudes … [and] altitude and azimuth … [and it is] suitable for measuring stellar distances”. The catalogue groups the instruments by the measurement each was built to make, and five recurring problems appear.

The grouping is editorial; Tycho never published a numbered list of five problems. It rests on two things that are his: his own functional descriptions in the Mechanica, and the three coordinate frames — horizon, equatorial, ecliptic — as Wennberg sets them out. Three of the five problems are those frames; the other two are the two frame-independent methods Tycho used, the direct angular distance between two stars and the ancient zenith-distance method of Ptolemy’s rulers. The techniques that follow are not instruments but devices and methods applied across them; Tycho describes the transversal division and the slit-sights himself in the book’s supplement.

The five measurement problems

1. Altitude — the height of a body above the horizon

A quarter-circle graduated from 0° at the top to 90° on a plumb-line reads a star's height above the horizon directly.

Instruments: Quadrans minor; Quadrans mediocris azimuthalis; Brass azimuthal quadrant; Quadrans muralis sive Tichonicus; Revolving quadrant (quadrans azimuthalis volubilis); Revolving steel quadrant; Azimuth semicircle (the doubled-scale half-circle); Quadrans maximus.

2. Angular distance — the angle between two bodies

Two arms sighting from a single vertex over a graduated arc give the angle between two points — with no horizon needed.

Instruments: Astronomical sextant with parallel-slit sights; Arcus bipartitus; Triangular sextant (sextans trigonicus); Steel sextant for a single observer; Half-sextant for small angles; Sextant of Herrevadskloster; Smaller iron sextant (Bürgi c.1600 / disputed); Sextant by Erasmus Habermel (1600).

3. Celestial coordinates — right ascension and declination, in the sky's own frame

A ring set parallel to the celestial equator hands you declination directly; a second gives right ascension — no computation.

Instruments: Northern equatorial armillary sphere; Southern equatorial armillary sphere; Great equatorial armillary sphere.

4. The ecliptic frame — longitude and latitude on the ecliptic

Rings aligned to the zodiac read a body's longitude and latitude in the ecliptic coordinates the ancients used.

Instruments: Zodiacal (ecliptic) armillary sphere.

5. Zenith distance — the angular distance of a body from the zenith

Two hinged rulers — the ancient Ptolemaic method Copernicus still used; Tycho built his own in brass, then moved beyond it.

Instruments: Parallactic instrument (triquetrum / regulae); Parallactic instrument with an azimuth circle; Copernicus's triquetrum.

Outside the five — the globe, the clocks, the cross-staff

The great celestial globe: not a measuring instrument at all, but a physical model of the whole sky on which Tycho plotted the 1000-plus stars his instruments had fixed. He did not design it — the brass-covered wooden shell was made by Christopher Schissler of Augsburg (1570) — but he completed and engraved it, so it belongs in the Mechanica and here.

Instruments: Globus magnus orichalcicus; Radius astronomicus (cross-staff); Horologia.

The techniques

The transversal (diagonal) scale (precision)

Tycho's signature precision device — a band of diagonal lines crossing a ladder of parallel rows that turns a coarse degree-scale into a reading good to a single arcminute, and to c. 10″ on his largest arcs, by interpolating diagonally. He first saw the idea in Leipzig as a youth and perfected it; it is the chief reason his instruments out-resolved every predecessor.

Used on: Quadrans muralis sive Tichonicus; Revolving steel quadrant; Great equatorial armillary sphere; Zodiacal (ecliptic) armillary sphere; Globus magnus orichalcicus; Astronomical sextant with parallel-slit sights; Quadrans mediocris azimuthalis. Tycho's own account: ch. 33 (TBOO V, p. 153).

Pinnacidia — parallel-slit sights (sighting)

Tycho's own sighting device: two equal parallel squares with adjustable brass slits, so the eye lines the star up through four slits at once — removing the parallax of the eye and letting the slit be matched to a star's brightness. It is the 'sight a central cylinder' instruction that recurs across the armillae and sextants.

Used on: Astronomical sextant with parallel-slit sights; Great equatorial armillary sphere; Southern equatorial armillary sphere; Revolving steel quadrant. Tycho's own account: ch. 33 (TBOO V, p. 153).

The Nonius (Nunes) scale — and why Tycho dropped it (precision)

Before the transversal, fine readings were attempted with the auxiliary-arc system of the Portuguese mathematician Pedro Nunes (Latin Nonius). Tycho fitted it to his smallest and medium quadrants and one sextant, but found the many nested arcs near-impossible to engrave accurately, and abandoned it for his transversal division.

Used on: Quadrans minor; Quadrans mediocris azimuthalis. Tycho's own account: ch. 4 (TBOO V, p. 12), ch. 5 (TBOO V, p. 16).

Reading the sky in equatorial coordinates (method)

Tycho's conceptual innovation as much as a mechanical one: orienting an armillary's axis parallel to the Earth's axis so a single ring reads declination and hour angle (and right-ascension differences) directly — the equatorial system astronomers still use — instead of the traditional ecliptic (zodiacal) armillary's latitude and longitude.

Used on: Great equatorial armillary sphere; Northern equatorial armillary sphere; Southern equatorial armillary sphere. Tycho's own account: ch. 17 (TBOO V, p. 64).

Mounting in the meridian (method)

Fixing a great arc exactly in the north–south meridian plane turns 'measure an altitude' into 'measure the meridian altitude', from which declination and latitude follow by simple addition or subtraction. Combined with rigidity — stone piers, steel frames, the underground Stjerneborg crypts — this is what made the precision real over long observing runs.

Used on: Quadrans muralis sive Tichonicus; Great equatorial armillary sphere. Tycho's own account: ch. 8 (TBOO V, p. 28).

8Reading resolution

Tycho drove the reading resolution of his naked-eye instruments down about sixfold, from roughly an arcminute on the simpler early arcs to about ten arcseconds on the great mural quadrant and the revolving steel quadrant, by larger radii, rigid mountings and his transversal scale. The table gives, for each instrument whose sources state a figure, the finest reading the scale allowed and the technique that allowed it; the figure plots those readings against the year of building. That is how finely the graduation could be read. What the sky yielded was coarser: for Tycho’s catalogue of 777 stars, the median separation from the modern place is 3.01′, and the median residual after the systematic offset is removed 2.62′ — the dashed line in the figure, from Observationes. Thoren’s reading of the logs agrees: about one arcminute for the best several instruments, and meridian altitudes of the sun good to 21″ by 1587–90.

9After Tycho

The apparatus left Hven with Tycho in 1597 and reached Bohemia in 1599–1600, twenty-eight instruments by the count of the time. After his death in October 1601 the Emperor bought the instruments and the observation journals from the heirs for 20,000 thalers, of which a fraction was ever paid; Kepler was denied their use and observed with a quadrant and a sextant lent by Baron Hoffmann. An imperial order of 1604 to pack the instruments for Vienna was apparently not carried out, and a rescript of 1609 kept them in Bohemia. Gassendi, the source of every later account, places their destruction in the disturbances of 1619; the legend that attaches it to the battle of the White Mountain in 1620 is a misreading of him. The inventory of the Prague treasury of December 1621 still lists the great globe and “a quadrant with many appurtenant instruments” at the Belvedere, and in 1672 an English envoy reported that the instruments had long since been sold to brass-founders. The globe alone travelled on, to the Jesuit college at Neisse and in 1632 as war booty to Copenhagen, where it burned in 1728. The two Prague sextants passed from Rudolf’s Kunstkammer through the Armoury and the auction of 1782 to the museum that holds them. The table dates each step and names its source.

11Desiderata

What this edition does not settle, and what would settle it. The items are the remainder of the compilation, stated so that a reader can act on them.

The instruments Tycho did not engrave

Chapter 25 of the Mechanica ('on certain other instruments of ours not yet engraved', TBOO V, 96–101) names a fork-shaped sextant with 155 cm arms of Brazil wood, a large semicircle for distances beyond a sixth of the circle, an astronomical radius, an astronomical ring, a portable armilla of 60 cm radius and an astrolabe, and promises more. Thoren's chart adds an Icelandic quadrant (1589) and a camera-obscura 'canal' (1591), and separates a 'semicirculus' of 1588 that he suspects is the azimuth semicircle under another pivot. None of these has a record here; the edition models the engraved instruments and five documented others.

Sources: Mechanica (1598; TBOO V), Thoren, The Lord of Uraniborg (1990).

The instruments of the student and Augsburg years

Thoren's chart opens with a half-sextant of 1569; the sextant Tycho left with Paul Hainzel, the Basel quadrants of 1568 and the gilt quadrant by Steffen Brenner used at the eclipse of 8 December 1573 are recorded, as objects of a stay, in Loca. Only the great Augsburg quadrant and the student cross-staff have records here.

Sources: Thoren, The Lord of Uraniborg (1990).

One sextant or two

The Herrevad sextant that measured the new star and the 'half-sextant for small angles' of the Mechanica (Fig. 18, whose plate is dated 1572) may be one physical instrument, engraved in its distance form as Fig. 18 and in its altitude mounting as Fig. 19. The two entries are kept and counted as one object; the merge is the owner's decision.

Sources: Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren (1946).

Dates not yet reconciled with Thoren 1973

The dates follow Wennberg 2007, the 1946 translation and Thoren 1990. Thoren's chart differs for the steel sextant (1576 against 1574 here), the brass azimuthal quadrant (1583 against 1576), the medium quadrant (1577 against 1580, though his own figure caption says 1580) and the zodiacal armillary (1581 against c. 1583–85). His 1973 article, which redates several instruments from the observation logs, has not been read for this edition.

Sources: Thoren, 'New Light on Tycho's Instruments' (1973), Thoren, The Lord of Uraniborg (1990).

The maker of the smaller Prague sextant

Bürgi (Šíma 1993, Wolfschmidt 2002) or Johann Klein, the eighteenth-century director of the Prague mathematical chamber (Weinek 1901, Mortensen 1942). The Kremsmünster sextant of the same size and form is the comparandum. Šíma's article has not been read in full.

Sources: Šíma, 'Prague Sextants of Tycho Brahe' (1993), Weinek, Die Tychonischen Instrumente (1901), Mortensen, 'Tycho Brahe Minder i Prag' (1942), Rankl, 'Der Tychonische Sextant … Kremsmünster' (1946).

Whether Tycho owned either surviving sextant

Mortensen thought it 'hardly probable' that Tycho owned the Habermel sextant and proposed a Hoffmann–Kepler provenance instead; Wolfschmidt calls both 'so-called Tychonic'. The grades record the existence and maker as secure and the link to Tycho as probable at best.

Sources: Mortensen, 'Tycho Brahe Minder i Prag' (1942), Wolfschmidt, 'Observatories and Instruments' (2002), Fučíková, 'The Belvedere in Prague' (2002).

No museum object records located

The National Technical Museum's own object records for the two sextants have not been found online; the inventory numbers here (Nos. 4 and 5) are those of the observatory's c. 1780 inventory as reported by Mortensen.

Sources: Mortensen, 'Tycho Brahe Minder i Prag' (1942).

A folio locus for every plate

The plates are reproduced from the Royal Library's copy 2; the copy online is copy 1. No page-level concordance between the two has been made, and the plates carry no signature or folio reference into the unpaginated 1598 book (Mortensen cites it by folio: 'Fol. H. 2').

Sources: Mechanica, 1598 — Royal Library copy 1, digitised.

The clocks

Tycho names four clocks; they are one record. Their placement is collective (the mural-quadrant room at Uraniborg, the warming room at Stjerneborg) and no individual clock is described beyond the largest, with its 1200-tooth wheel.

Sources: Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren (1946).

The destruction of the Hven instruments

'Destroyed in the aftermath of the Bohemian war of 1619' is a collective statement (Gassendi, followed by Dreyer and Wolfschmidt); Mortensen shows the 1621 inventory still listing a quadrant 'with many appurtenant instruments' at the Belvedere, and a 1672 report that the instruments had 'long since been sold to brass-founders'. No individual instrument's end is dated except the Augsburg quadrant (1574) and the globe (1728).

Sources: Mortensen, 'Tycho Brahe Minder i Prag' (1942), Wolfschmidt, 'Observatories and Instruments' (2002), Dreyer, Tycho Brahe (1890).
