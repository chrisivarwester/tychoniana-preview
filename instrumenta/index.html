Instrumenta — The Instruments

The finest measurements of the sky before the telescope. With the great instruments shown here, Tycho pushed naked-eye astronomy to about one arcminute — the limit of the unaided human eye.

He reached that precision with the great quadrants, sextants and armillaries here — the 21 instruments he designed or completed and engraved, plate by plate, in his Astronomiae instauratae mechanica (1598), built ever larger and steadier at Uraniborg and Stjerneborg. Every one is now lost or destroyed; the book is all that remains — and the observations it enabled, which Kepler would inherit.
What this catalogue shows — and leaves out. Left out, by design: his student cross-staff and the observatory clocks (never engraved); the triquetrum Copernicus himself used, which Tycho owned and rebuilt (a relic, not his design); and the two iron sextants in Prague long linked to him — one signed by Habermel, the other attributed to Bürgi (the attribution disputed) — absent from the book, their link to Tycho disputed. All five are catalogued in Beyond the Mechanica, at the end of the catalogue. Grades A–C show how securely each is attested.

Overview

What all these instruments were for

Tycho built dozens of instruments, but they pursue only a handful of measurements. Every point in the sky is fixed by a small set of coordinates, and in the Mechanica Tycho names them himself — describing a single instrument as one that can determine “not only declinations and right ascensions of the stars … but … their longitudes and latitudes … [and] altitude and azimuth … [and it is] suitable for measuring stellar distances.” Group the instruments by which of these each was built to read, and the five recurring problems beside appear. That is how the catalogue below is arranged.

How we know these were the questions — and what is ours, not Tycho’s

Tycho never published a numbered list of “five problems.” The grouping is ours, but it rests on two things that are his. First, his own functional descriptions in the Mechanica (1598; here in Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren’s 1946 translation), where he introduces each instrument by the coordinates it yields. Second, the structure of positional astronomy itself: every celestial coordinate belongs to the horizon frame, the equatorial frame, or the ecliptic frame — the three systems Arne Wennberg sets out in his study of Tycho’s instruments.

Three of the five problems are exactly those coordinate frames; the other two are the two frame-independent methods Tycho actually used — the direct angular distance between two stars, and the old zenith-distance method inherited from Ptolemy. So the five are a teaching lens over a sourced taxonomy, not a list we have put in his mouth. Each problem’s geometry is drawn out at its heading below — open Show the geometry to see it.

A quadrant of c.5.5 m radius (at least 14 cubits in Tycho's own figure), so large it needed some twenty men to raise, built 1569–70 in Paul Hainzel's garden at Göggingen near Augsburg — the earliest of Tycho's monumental instruments. Destroyed in a storm in December 1574. In his own words: “I think that I will also add a delineation and description of the great quadrant which we had constructed 26 years ago in Master Paul Hainzel's garden on his property half a mile from Augsburg, although it does not exist any more.” (Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 89).
How it works
A 90° arc hung from its centre with a plumb-line. Turned in azimuth and its edge brought onto the star, the plumb-line cuts the arc and splits the right angle into the zenith distance (above) and the altitude (below): h = 90°−z.
Size: radius at least 14 cubits — Tycho's own figure, ≈543 cm (≈5.5 m) (Mechanica 1946, Fig. 20, p. 89)Material: oak, iron-bound, with a brass-faced limbLocation: Paul Hainzel's garden, Göggingen, near AugsburgPrecision: scale divided to 1′ (≈1.6 mm per arcminute on the 5.5 m arc)

Quadrans muralis sive Tichonicus — the great mural quadrant
1582 (planned from 1576)
A brass arc fixed to a wall set precisely in the meridian (north–south) at Uraniborg — Tycho's most celebrated instrument. Radius 194 cm — Tycho's own 'nearly five cubits' (Wolfschmidt 2002, p.207; Th…
A brass arc fixed to a wall set precisely in the meridian (north–south) at Uraniborg — Tycho's most celebrated instrument. Radius 194 cm — Tycho's own 'nearly five cubits' (Wolfschmidt 2002, p.207; Thoren's 'two metres' is a rounding). Planned from 1576 and emerging from his Hven shop in June 1582 (Thoren, p.163); Tycho was still adjusting it on the wall in 1583 (Thoren, p.164 fn 43). Limb five inches broad, two inches thick — Tycho's own figures (Raeder 1946, p. 28), roughly 8 × 3 cm by Thoren's own conversion (arc breadth = 1/24 of the five-cubit radius; p. 163 n. 42). Thoren's main-text 'thirteen millimeters broad, and five millimeters thick' (p. 163) contradicts his own footnote and is an error. The wall within the arc bore the famous triple mural painting of Tycho himself amid views of Uraniborg's chambers. Its transversal scale was graduated to sixths of a minute; only after 1585, when the great armillary and the revolving quadrant became available to check its results, did Tycho regard its positions as confirmed (Thoren, p. 190). Known from the Mechanica engraving. In his own words: “It is called Mural, or Tichonicus, after the wall on which it is fixed. … every single minute can be divided again into six subdivisions; thus ten seconds of arc are plainly distinguishable and even half this, or five seconds of arc, can be read without difficulty.” (Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 29).
How it works
A quarter-circle fixed flat on a wall set exactly in the meridian, its centre at a sight-cylinder through the south wall, graduated 0° at the top down to 90° on the plumb-line. Because the altitude and the arc above the sight-line are equal vertical angles, and the scale starts from the top, the reading is the meridian altitude directly.
Size: radius nearly five cubits — Tycho's own figure, ≈194 cm (Mechanica 1946, Fig. 5, p. 29, in the editors' conversion). This resolves the prior split: it confirms Wolfschmidt 2002 (194 cm); Thoren's 'two metres' is a rounding.Material: brass arc on a meridian wallLocation: south-west ground-floor room, Uraniborg (fixed on the meridian wall)Precision: transversal scale read to ⅙′ (≈10″)

Revolving quadrant (quadrans azimuthalis volubilis)
1585–86
A large revolving wood quadrant — 'one-and-a-half-meter' per Thoren (p.177; radius_cm here from Wolfschmidt, 155) — available at the turn of 1585–6 (Thoren, p.177). Probably an experimental analogue t…
A large revolving wood quadrant — 'one-and-a-half-meter' per Thoren (p.177; radius_cm here from Wolfschmidt, 155) — available at the turn of 1585–6 (Thoren, p.177). Probably an experimental analogue to the mural quadrant, kept rigid by lightweight struts instead of anchors to a wall, so it could be turned to any azimuth. Together with the great equatorial armillary it gave Tycho the redundancy to verify his mural quadrant systematically, enabling the 1′ accuracy milestone achieved by the 1585 comet season. The second large quadrant (the revolving steel quadrant) entered service two years later. Not extant. In his own words: “A revolving quadrant, which can be used for determining not only altitudes, like the previous sextant, but also, most accurately, azimuths, has been constructed by us.” (Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 33).
How it works
A quadrant revolving about a vertical pillar carrying an alidade and sight; swung in azimuth onto the star, the altitude reads directly on the arc and the bearing on a surrounding azimuth ring. With it Tycho fixed the meridian (true north) by averaging Polaris's east- and west-most positions.
Size: radius four cubits — Tycho's own figure, ≈155 cm (Mechanica 1946, Fig. 6, p. 33). Confirms 155 cm; Thoren's 'one-and-a-half-metre' (150 cm) is a rounding.Location: north-east crypt, StjerneborgPrecision: minute divided in four → read to 15″

Revolving steel quadrant
1588
A revolving quadrant built in steel (1588) for rigidity and precision, and the physically largest of Tycho's quadrants (radius_cm=194 per Wolfschmidt; Thoren gives 'one and three-quarters metres'). It…
A revolving quadrant built in steel (1588) for rigidity and precision, and the physically largest of Tycho's quadrants (radius_cm=194 per Wolfschmidt; Thoren gives 'one and three-quarters metres'). It was actually the old Q. max — the 5-cubit steel quadrant inaugurated 21 March 1581, Tycho's first Hven-shop instrument, which had to be abandoned due to unstable pillars — recycled into useful form after being given a completely stable base (Thoren, p.177). Braced inside a rigid steel square and turning on a vertical iron axle, it was so solidly built that — Tycho records — a man could hang on it and ride it round several turns without disturbing it. Among the latest of the Stjerneborg battery. Not extant. The Mechanica delineates this quadrant twice: as Fig. 7 (p. 37), revolving in a brick crypt about a steel axis, and again as Fig. 21 (p. 93) in its earlier mounting on a steel azimuth-horizon carried by five columns — Tycho noting it had already been 'described … among the instruments delineated under No. 7'. In his own words: “This quadrant, which is entirely made of solid steel, and further surrounded with a steel square, serves the same purpose as the previous one, with even greater accuracy, since it is considerably larger.” (Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 37).
How it works
A circle-quadrant braced inside a steel square, turnable about a vertical iron axle; the alidade reads altitude on the arc while rules carry the bearing to a ~350 cm azimuth ring. So solidly built that — Tycho records — a man could hang on it and ride it round several turns without disturbing it.
Size: radius nearly five cubits — Tycho's own figure, ≈194 cm (Mechanica 1946, Fig. 7, p. 37), with a surrounding solid-steel square and a nine-cubit (≈350 cm) steel azimuth horizon. Confirms 194 cm and supersedes Thoren's estimate of 175 cm.Material: a circle-quadrant braced inside a rigid steel squareLocation: south-west crypt, StjerneborgPrecision: transversal scale read to ⅙′ (≈10″)

Armillary destroyed A
Great equatorial armillary sphere
1585
The great equatorial armillary of the Stjerneborg crypt, 1.36 m radius (Wolfschmidt 2002; Thoren: 'one-and-a-third metre'), completed by the summer solstice of 1585 — the single most important instrum…
The great equatorial armillary of the Stjerneborg crypt, 1.36 m radius (Wolfschmidt 2002; Thoren: 'one-and-a-third metre'), completed by the summer solstice of 1585 — the single most important instrument on Hven (Thoren, ch.5, p.174). Foundation laid December 1584. Its single declination ring — made of wood to minimise flexure, except for brass alidades and graduation strips — was pivoted on a hollow steel axis tilted to the Hven latitude. In simplicity it rivalled the mural quadrant; right ascension and declination are read directly, in the equatorial coordinate system astronomers still use. Tycho had tried a zodiacal (ecliptic) armillary first but abandoned it almost immediately; the converted zodiacal armillary was the first equatorial armillary (~1583–84), the two smaller equatorial armillaries in the Uraniborg castle itself (north and south, 1584) were the second step, and this great Stjerneborg instrument was the third and culminating form: 'a third, which was to become the single most important instrument on Hven' (Thoren, p.174). After 1585, the great armillary and the revolving quadrant together enabled Tycho to cross-check the mural quadrant systematically, and it was by this comet-of-1585 season that he achieved his 1′ accuracy target (Thoren, p.190). Not extant. In his own words: “It has one complete armilla [ring] only which can revolve, and another semicircular one, which is fixed.” (Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 65).
How it works
A single great declination ring whose axle is set parallel to the Earth's axis (tilted to Stjerneborg's latitude). Because that axis is the polar axis, a sight swung onto a star reads its declination directly on the ring and its hour angle on the equatorial arc below; right-ascension differences between a known and an unknown star are read on the same arc.
Size: one complete revolving armilla seven cubits in diameter — Tycho's own figure, ≈272 cm (radius ≈136 cm); a fixed nine-cubit (≈350 cm) semicircular armilla (Mechanica 1946, Fig. 14, p. 65)Location: south (largest) crypt, StjerneborgPrecision: ≈¼′ — one of his most accurate instruments

Zodiacal (ecliptic) armillary sphere
ca. 1583–85
A zodiacal (ecliptic) armillary, measuring celestial longitudes and latitudes in the traditional Ptolemaic system of ecliptic coordinates. Tycho's initial zodiacal armillary (ca. 1583) was tried and a…
A zodiacal (ecliptic) armillary, measuring celestial longitudes and latitudes in the traditional Ptolemaic system of ecliptic coordinates. Tycho's initial zodiacal armillary (ca. 1583) was tried and abandoned almost immediately — 'he seems to have decided so quickly that the zodiacal form of the armillary would not work that he did not bother to record any observations made with it until after he had converted it to equatorial form' (Thoren, p.174) — and the conversion to equatorial form constituted his invention of the equatorial armillary. The Mechanica plate (V, 72–87) shows a later, mature zodiacal instrument, grouped by Thoren under 1585. Tycho kept it for its traditional role (solar-longitude measurements) but regarded the equatorial type as his real instrument. Not extant. In his own words: “This instrument consists of four armillae [rings] only (while my predecessors used five or six).” (Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 53).
How it works
Four rings representing ecliptic great circles, the outer a steel meridian carrying the polar axis. The ecliptic ring is turned onto a star of known longitude to orient the sphere, after which celestial latitude and longitude are read directly — the traditional Ptolemaic type Tycho kept but judged cumbersome (the rings, he complained, were too heavy).
Size: four armillae; steel meridian ring three cubits in diameter — Tycho's own figure, ≈117 cm (Mechanica 1946, Fig. 11, p. 53)Location: north-west crypt, StjerneborgPrecision: transversal reading; latitude/longitude to under 1′

from Augsburg, c.1569
The astronomical sextant Tycho first devised at Augsburg (c.1569, with Petrus Ramus in mind) and refined at Hven with parallel-slit sights. Its priority — and the parallel-slit sights — are the matter…
The astronomical sextant Tycho first devised at Augsburg (c.1569, with Petrus Ramus in mind) and refined at Hven with parallel-slit sights. Its priority — and the parallel-slit sights — are the matter of the Wittich dispute aired in the long 1587 letter to Rothmann: Paul Wittich, after his 1580 visit to Hven, reproduced Tycho's instruments at the Landgrave's observatory in Kassel. Not extant. In his own words: “The astronomical sextant instrument, which is particularly convenient in use, was first invented by myself, about twenty years ago.” (Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 25).
How it works
A 60° star-distance sextant on a ball-and-socket copper globe so it can be turned into the plane of any two stars. One observer holds the fixed sight on one star, a second moves the alidade onto the other, both aiming at a central cylinder; the arc between reads the distance. For very small gaps a detached parallel sight keeps the two observers from colliding. Fitted with Tycho's pinnacidia (parallel-slit sights).
Size: radius four cubits — Tycho's own figure, ≈155 cm (Mechanica 1946, Fig. 4, p. 25)Material: wood clad in brass; brass limb, alidade and sightsLocation: south-east crypt, Stjerneborg (ball-and-socket globe mount)Precision: transversal scale, to ≈½′

Azimuth semicircle (the doubled-scale half-circle)
1588
An altitude-azimuth half-circle of 233 cm diameter on a 155 cm horizon ring, raised on five stone piers in the larger southern observatory of Uraniborg, surmounted by three figures (Urania, Geometry, …
An altitude-azimuth half-circle of 233 cm diameter on a 155 cm horizon ring, raised on five stone piers in the larger southern observatory of Uraniborg, surmounted by three figures (Urania, Geometry, Arithmetic). Its sight-rule pivots at one end of the diameter, on the rim; by Euclid's inscribed-angle theorem the angle subtended there is twice the true altitude, so the scale is engraved at double spacing and reads altitude directly, with azimuth on the horizon ring. One of the principal instruments engraved in the Mechanica. Not extant. In his own words: “We have also devised another instrument suitable for accurate measures of altitudes and azimuths, the appearance of which can be seen from the figure.” (Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 41).
How it works
An altitude-azimuth half-circle whose sight-rule pivots at the RIM, not the centre. By Euclid's inscribed-angle theorem the rim-subtended angle is twice the central angle, so Tycho engraved the altitude scale at DOUBLE spacing and the rim-pivoted rule then reads true altitude directly; azimuth is read on the north-set horizon ring. Wennberg's aside — 'Tycho knew his Euclid' — is the whole point.
Size: diameter six cubits — Tycho's own figure, ≈233 cm (radius ≈116 cm), on a four-cubit (≈155 cm) azimuth horizon (Mechanica 1946, Fig. 8, p. 41)Material: iron faced with brassLocation: larger southern observatory, Uraniborg (on five stone piers)Precision: altitude + azimuth; doubled (inscribed-angle) scale

Arc lost B
Arcus bipartitus — the bipartite arc (1583)
1583
A distance-measuring arc Tycho invented in 1583, while determining the precise positions of the stars in Cassiopeia for the new-star discussion of the Progymnasmata. On the ordinary sextant two observ…
A distance-measuring arc Tycho invented in 1583, while determining the precise positions of the stars in Cassiopeia for the new-star discussion of the Progymnasmata. On the ordinary sextant two observers had to sight the same small interstellar gap and got in each other's way; the bipartite arc solved this by splitting the axis of the measured angle into two parallel 'zero points'. Constructing it, Tycho realised the same split-axis principle could be adapted to the sextant itself — so the instrument is as much a step in the evolution of his sextant design as an instrument in its own right (Thoren, Lord of Uraniborg, pp.169–170, Fig. 5.13; TBOO V, 68–71). Enumerated among the Mechanica instruments. Not extant. In his own words: “… an instrument which can measure smaller distances up to one-twelfth of the circumference of the circle, or, as it is called, one sign of the zodiac.” (Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 69).
How it works
A bar carrying two 30° arcs whose zero-points sit mid-scale, and a cross-piece of two cylinders at the arcs' centres. With both sights at zero the two sight-lines are parallel, so two observers see the same (effectively infinitely distant) star; swinging the right sight onto a nearby star reads the small angle — up to 15°, or up to 30° by also shifting the left sight.
Size: alidade four cubits long — Tycho's own figure, ≈155 cm (Mechanica 1946, Fig. 15, p. 69); measures up to one-twelfth of the circle (one zodiac sign)Location: larger northern observatory, UraniborgPrecision: for very small angles, to ≈½′

Parallactic instrument (triquetrum / regulae)
Hven
Tycho's parallactic instrument (two triquetra), of the ancient Greek and Copernican type, for measuring zenith distances. In the Mechanica Tycho sets his brass instruments against Copernicus's own fir…
Tycho's parallactic instrument (two triquetra), of the ancient Greek and Copernican type, for measuring zenith distances. In the Mechanica Tycho sets his brass instruments against Copernicus's own fir-wood triquetrum. Not extant. In his own words: “This parallatic instrument, which is also called the Ptolemaic rulers, I have wished to construct, since it requires very few accessories, namely, three rulers, in order to measure in a moment the zenith distance of a star, and hence its altitude.” (Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 45).
How it works
The ancient 'Ptolemaic rules': two equal hinged rules raised by a cord over a pulley, the whole turnable in azimuth on a central pillar. Turned onto the star and the rules raised onto it, the zenith distance is read at the plumb-line on a specially graduated sine scale; altitude = 90°−z.

Globus magnus orichalcicus — the great brass globe
1570 shell; completed at Hven 1584 (horizon inscription)
A celestial globe of almost six Tychonic feet (c. 149 cm) in diameter. Its wooden shell was commissioned in 1570 from Christopher Schissler the Elder, the leading Augsburg instrument-maker, who built …
A celestial globe of almost six Tychonic feet (c. 149 cm) in diameter. Its wooden shell was commissioned in 1570 from Christopher Schissler the Elder, the leading Augsburg instrument-maker, who built it of many fir staves and rings fitted together like a great puzzle (Christianson, On Tycho's Island, p.358; Dreyer, p.112). On Tycho's return to Augsburg in 1575 it was found not perfectly spherical and slightly cracked; once received in Denmark in 1576 the cracks were stopped and the sphericity restored, after which the globe was observed over a two-year period to be sure it would hold its shape through the changeable Danish climate before being sheathed in brass, marked with ecliptic and equator divided to a minute of arc, and mounted in divided meridian and horizon rings. An inscription on the horizon dated its completion to 1584; only then were star positions engraved — Tycho's own measured places, added down to 1595 — making it a precision computing instrument as well as a showpiece (Mosley, Bearing the Heavens, ch.4). The maker of the shell is normally identified as Christopher Schissler the Elder, though Mosley flags the identification as not beyond doubt. The longest-surviving of all Tycho's instruments, with a circuitous return: listed in the 6 Dec 1621 Belvedere inventory ('Ein sehr grosser globus coelestis vom metall'), it was acquired c.1625 by the Jesuit Christoph Scheiner for the Jesuit College at Neisse (Nysa) in Silesia, whence in 1632 it was carried as war-booty to Copenhagen by Prince Ulrik, son of Christian IV; there it stood in the University's observatory tower until that tower and all its contents burned in 1728 (Mortensen, Fra Tycho Brahes Bogsamling). In his own words: “A globe of this size, so solidly and finely worked, and correct in every respect, has never I think been constructed up to now by any man anywhere in the world.” (Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 104).
How it works
A precision celestial globe used as an analogue computer: set the pole-altitude on the meridian at the horizon, turn a star to the meridian to read its declination there and its right ascension on the equator; a movable vertical circle gives altitude and the horizon ring azimuth — all four coordinates, and the conversions between the three systems.
Size: diameter almost six feet — Tycho's own figure, ≈149 cm; on a stand about five feet (≈130 cm) high (Mechanica 1946, Fig. 22, p. 103)Material: fir-wood shell (commissioned Augsburg 1570 from Christopher Schissler) sheathed in brassLocation: library, UraniborgPrecision: ecliptic & equator divided by transversals to 1′; >1000 stars

Beyond the Mechanica
Five further documented instruments that Tycho did not engrave in the Mechanica — his student cross-staff, the observatory clocks, the triquetrum Copernicus himself used, and the two iron sextants in Prague whose link to Tycho is disputed. Radius astronomicus (cross-staff) — the student instrument
1564
The cross-staff (radius) of Tycho's student years at Leipzig and Rostock. With it, observing the 1563 great conjunction of Saturn and Jupiter, he found both the Alphonsine and Copernican tables badly in error — the observation that set his life's programme. He later moved decisively beyond such medieval portable instruments (astrolabe, torquetum, cross-staff) toward the large fixed quadrant, sextant and armillary of the ancients. Not extant.

Horologia — the observatory clocks
Hven
The mechanical clocks kept for timing observations — unreliable in the period, which is why Tycho cross-checked time by his Mechanical-Equator method rather than trusting the clocks alone. They went with the rest of the apparatus to Bohemia and were among the instruments the Emperor purchased after Tycho's death: Kepler used one of Tycho's clocks to observe the lunar eclipses of December 1601 and May 1603 (Dreyer, p.365). Not extant. In his own words: “We have at our disposal four such clocks. One of these, the largest, manages the whole business with the aid of three wheels, of which the largest, cast from solid pure brass, has 1200 teeth. The diameter of this wheel is two cubits [≈78 cm]. The three other clocks are smaller, and need more wheels.” (Mechanica, trans. Raeder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 30).

Parallactic lost B Owned relic — made by another
Copernicus's triquetrum — the relic from Frauenburg
1584 (received on Hven)
A wooden triquetrum (parallactic rulers) reputed to have been made and used by Copernicus himself — the single most celebrated relic in Tycho's collection. When Elias Olsen Morsing returned from his 1584 expedition to Frauenburg (Frombork), sent to re-measure the polar altitude that Copernicus had gotten wrong, the cathedral canons — impressed by the expedition and the attention it drew to their illustrious predecessor — sent the instrument back with him as a gift to Tycho. Genuinely awed, Tycho placed it on permanent display in the North Observatory at Uraniborg and composed an impromptu Latin poem to commemorate the occasion (TBOO V, 44–47; partly trans. Edward Rosen in Dobrzycki, ed., Nicholas Copernicus on the Revolutions, 1978, p.412). His reverence later gave way to objectivity: rulers only eight feet long, made of pine and scaled by pen and ink, could not yield decent observations, and Tycho — though he had already built a larger brass version and found it wanting — duplicated Copernicus's model in his own shop just to test it. Because he circulated the verdict so widely in the Mechanica, Thoren notes the replica 'may well have been the last such instrument built.' Distinct from inst-triquetrum (Tycho's own working parallactic instrument); not extant. Five years on, Tycho summed up Copernicus's instruments: 'by means of these puny cudgels he surmounted the lofty Olympus' (TBOO VI, 253).
Location: North Observatory, Uraniborg (on permanent display)

Smaller iron sextant (Bürgi c.1600 / disputed) — surviving
c.1600 (attribution disputed)
The smaller ('Nr. 5') of the two iron sextants in the Národní technické muzeum, Prague — radius 1.11 m. Its maker is genuinely disputed. Modern scholarship (Šíma 1993; Wolfschmidt 2002) attributes it to the celebrated clockmaker and instrument-maker Jost Bürgi (1552–1632) of the Prague years, grouping it with the Habermel sextant as the only surviving instruments associated with Tycho. The older Weinek 1901 / Mortensen reading instead held it 'probably made by Pater Johann Klein', director of Prague's Mathematical Chamber 1732–82 — i.e. an 18th-century instrument, not Tychonic at all (the c.1780 inventory's claim that it was 'a smaller sextant of Tycho Brahe, which he brought to Prague' notwithstanding). A sextant of the same size and very similar form survives at the Kremsmünster observatory in Austria, described by Rankl 1946 as 'Der Tychonische Sextant (von Bürgi)'. That Tycho's own Hven instruments are all lost makes any Prague-period survivor precious; the Bürgi attribution is the current consensus but graded B for the open dispute. For the shared Belvedere → Kunstkammer → 1648-Armoury → Herget (1782) → NTM Prague provenance of both surviving sextants, see Fučíková 2002 (detailed under inst-sextant-habermel).

Sextant by Erasmus Habermel (1600) — surviving
1600
An iron sextant of 1.295 m radius (measured to the middle of the graduated arc), its alidade signed 'Pragae fecit Erasm. Habermel 1600', made by Erasmus Habermel (b. Nuremberg c.1538, d. Prague 1606), the leading instrument-maker at Rudolf II's court. It survives in the Národní technické muzeum, Prague, the larger ('Nr. 4') of the two iron sextants the Prague observatory has long shown as Tycho's (the c.1780 inventory: 'Ein grosser Sextant von Tycho Brahe, der im Jahre 1600 von Erasmus Habermel verfertigt wurde'). Modern scholarship (Šíma 1993; Wolfschmidt 2002) groups it among the only surviving instruments associated with Tycho's working life — everything from Hven is lost. Whether Tycho himself owned or used it is, however, contested: Wolfschmidt calls the two survivors 'so-called Tychonic sextants (but actually made by Bürgi and Habermel)', and the older Weinek 1901 / Mortensen reading thought it 'hardly probable' that Tycho owned this sextant — its construction stands well behind the Mechanica instruments — suggesting instead a Hoffmann→Kepler provenance (one of the instruments Baron Hoffmann lent Kepler when use of the Tychonic instruments was denied him). Hence: existence and maker A (inscription-certain); the attribution to Tycho's own observatory graded B. Later provenance is now traceable (Fučíková 2002): both sextants stood among Rudolf II's instruments at the Prague Belvedere / Kunstkammer (a large instrument there was valued at 600 in the 1619 catalogue, vs 40–80 × three-score groschen for the rest); they proved too large for the Swedish removal of 1648 and were left in the Castle Armoury (1650 inventory), sold at Joseph II's 1782 auction to Franz Leonard Herget (public teacher of mechanics/hydraulics), and eventually passed to the Národní technické muzeum — Fučíková argues they never left Prague.
Location: Národní technické muzeum (National Technical Museum), Prague
