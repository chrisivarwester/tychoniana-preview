A prosopography of the documented circle: everyone recorded as having worked for Tycho Brahe, built for him, printed him, paid for him, written to him or been written to, and the later hands through which his books and papers passed. Every entry is a documented relation, not an acquaintance: a person is here because a source names the relation, and each record says which source.

1Introduction

This is the register of the people around Tycho Brahe (1546–1601): the assistants and craftsmen of Uraniborg, the printers and publishers of his works, the patrons and the dedicatees of his presentation copies, his kin and friends, every party to the surviving correspondence, and the owners, editors and scholars of the four centuries after his death through whom the books and papers passed. It is a directory in the sense of Christianson’s Biographical Directory, and it rests on it, but it is narrower in one respect and wider in another: narrower because a person enters only through a relation a source records, wider because the correspondence brings in a hundred and thirty-nine parties whom no household roster names.

It was assembled from three layers. The core is the agent register of the Tychoniana thesaurus — the thirty-three persons recorded as author, printer, publisher, dedicatee, scribe, owner or editor of a Tychonic work, manifestation or object. Around it stand the parties to the letters, taken from the Epistolarium, whose canonical spellings this register follows. The biographies were then written from the standard literature — Christianson, Thoren, Dreyer, Nielsen, the chancery register, the Danske Magazin documents, Ellefsen for the Brahe women, Jern and Beckett for the builders — and every biography names its sources in parentheses, each linked to the sources section.

What the figures are not. This is not a count of the people Tycho knew, nor of the familia as Christianson defines it, nor of the visitors to Hven: it is the number of persons a Tychoniana record names in a documented relation to him, and it grows as records are added. A correspondent’s letter count is the number of letters that survive in the edition, not the number written; the Hven–Kassel exchange survives because Tycho printed it, and the letters to his bailiffs mostly do not. A person with no biography is not a person about whom nothing is known: the register has not yet reached them. The household is under-represented in a way the itinerary makes measurable — the persons Loca names at Tycho’s stays who have no record here are listed in §9, his wife and children among them.

Conventions

Names. Each entry is headed by the English scholarly form the Epistolarium displays (Christoph Rothmann, Thaddaeus Hagecius, Ulrich III, Duke of Mecklenburg); the Latin form, the Danish-form key the letters register uses, and every other spelling under which the person appears in the itinerary or the diary are printed in the record, so that a search under any of them finds it.

Dates. Years only. A contested year is printed as the sources give it. A person known only by a death year is plotted as a point, never as an invented lifespan.

Letters. Sent and received are counted from the person’s side. A letter between two third parties counts once for each; a joint letter counts for each addressee; so the sum over the register exceeds the 509 letters.

Seats. The documented place of operation — court, university, press, island — with its basis; never a birthplace, never a dateline.

Record keys. Every record prints its key (LONGO, COR_HENRIK_RANTZAU) and is addressable: #LONGO as an anchor, or ?person=LONGO, which highlights it. The keys are those the other pillars deep-link to and are stable.

Willem Janszoon Blaeu

1571–1638BLAEU
Guilielmus Janssonius Blaeu
assistant contemporary
No documented seat recorded.
A Dutch student, about twenty-five, who wintered on Hven in 1595–96, learning Tycho's instruments and observing methods at first hand. He returned to Amsterdam to found the Blaeu cartographic house: his celestial globes (from c. 1600) were mapped on Tycho's star positions, and he discovered the variable star P Cygni in 1600. His son Joan's Grooten Atlas (1664) reproduced Tycho's instruments in colour. (Thoren; Christianson, On Tycho's Island, 2000)

Sophie Brahe

1556 or 1559 – 1643COR_SOPHIE_BRAHE
Sophia Brahea
correspondentfamily contemporary
Seat: Hven — Tycho's sister, helped at Uraniborg
Tycho's younger sister. Her birth year is contested — Gassendi's account gives 1556, Tycho's own record 1559, and the arguments for the two dates are nearly evenly weighted (Ellefsen). After widowhood from Otto Thott (died 1588), she progressed from garden-making at Eriksholm in Scania to Paracelsian alchemy to judicial astrology, teaching herself from Latin sources translated into Danish at her own expense and from German texts. Tycho celebrated her under the sobriquet Urania — comparing her favourably to Fulvia Olympia Morata — in his verse preface Urania Titani and in the prose introduction ('Occasio et argumentum', c. 1594) he wrote for her long letter on alchemy and astrology, intended for the never-published second volume of the Epistolae astronomicae. She later remarried Erik Lange (1602). (Christianson, On Tycho's Island; DSL critical edition, Urania Titani.) In her long widowhood she became a serious genealogist of the Danish nobility, whose manuscript lineage-books and heraldic copybooks survive at the Landsarkiv in Lund, and she stood at the centre of a circle of learned Danish noblewomen (Ellefsen, Søsteren og astronomen, 2023).

Alchemist and long-standing friend of Tycho who became his brother-in-law. After a twelve-year engagement (betrothed 1590) he married Sophie Brahe in 1602. Netherlandish royal master-builder. Because Uraniborg was legally the King's building — Hven was crown property and the 400-daler building grant was royal — Beckett argues that the royal architect Hans von Paschen, not Tycho's own man, drew the original design and oversaw the first two building seasons (1576–77); he had already built for Frederik II at Kronborg (the new wing on the medieval Krogen) and the 1574 Communitet hall in Copenhagen, besides fortress work at Bohus, Akershus and the new Oslo. He transferred to Bohus late in 1577 — later falling briefly from favour, the King fearing he would betray military secrets — leaving Stenwinkel to complete the house. Beckett and Gregor Paulsson attribute to his stone-and-image workshop the Lund Cathedral high-altar retable (1577), the manors Lystrup and Vester-Vallø, and the Krognos/Hardenberg and Podebusk/Oxe tombs at Ringsted and Aarhus; the rival attribution of the sculpture to Herkules Mida keeps the carver's identity open. (Beckett & Christensen, Uraniborg, 1921; Jern, Uraniborg, 1976.)

7The circle and the objects

Parties who are not persons

Four entries are corporate bodies (the University of Copenhagen, the Chapter of Magdeburg Cathedral, the Faculty of Medicine at Wittenberg, the Council of Hamburg) and four are unnamed (the seven letters the edition groups under Anonymus, an unnamed lady, an unknown Danish student and an unknown doctor of medicine). They are counted in the entries and never in the persons. Three further keys of the letters register belong to no entry at all: the open deed of 10 August 1594, the open testimonial for Longomontanus of 1 June 1597, and the null recipient of Johannes Müller’s quittance of 29 May 1601 — documents, not addressees.

Conflicts and questions left open

  • The Pruskovský dedication copy. The biography places the Mechanica with Tycho’s autograph of 12 March 1601 in the National Museum, Prague; the agent register places the Pruskovský dedication copy in the Křivoklát castle library (ZK KT 01693). One location is wrong, or there are two copies.
  • “Hans the Younger”. Frederik II’s third son Hans (1583–1602), the subject of the 1583 nativity, was styled “Hans the Younger” in this register and, until its rebuild, in the horoscope register; that epithet belongs to his great-uncle Hans den Yngre of Sønderborg (1545–1622). Neither register now gives him the epithet.
  • Sophie Brahe’s birth year is printed as the sources give it, 1556 or 1559; the arguments are nearly evenly weighted (Ellefsen).
  • The 33 agent-register entries against Christianson. The core of this register is the thesaurus agent register, whose thirty-three persons were chosen for their relation to a work or object; it has not been reconciled against the whole of Christianson’s Directory, and the itinerary table above shows how much of the resident community that leaves out.
