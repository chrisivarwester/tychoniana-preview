1572
Sky B SN 1572 — Tycho's Supernova and its remnant
The new star of 1572 that Tycho recorded in De nova stella is now known as SN 1572 ('Tycho's Supernova'), a Type Ia whose expanding remnant (3C 10) is studied today in X-rays and through light echoes — a rare case where the founding sixteenth-century observation and the living twenty-first-century object are the same thing. A bridge to Tycho's data as still-working science.

1572
Science A The 1572 new star in contemporary eyes — Busch & Reravius (1572–73)
Tycho's De nova stella (1573) was only one of many contemporary responses to the new star — and, in Denmark, not the one that reached the public first. The German Lutheran preacher Georg Busch read the 1572 phenomenon as a comet, advancing an unusual Christian comet-theory in which it was not merely a warning from God but was physically condensed out of 'evaporated' earthly sin (a notion found only in the Lutheran north). The Danish priest Rasmus Hansen Reravius translated Busch into Danish (Copenhagen, in at least two editions; it also seeded a Swedish version), drawn to the book for its message of a warning sent by God to a sinful humanity — the comet-or-star question being, to him, secondary. Reravius enlarged Busch's title from the German 'On the comet' to the Danish 'On the new star and comet', adding the word 'star' under the influence of Tycho, whose Latin treatise had by then established that it was a star, not a comet. Reravius's vernacular book reached far more readers than Tycho's small-run, slowly-distributed Latin treatise, and so became the channel through which Tycho's discovery first reached a broad public (Fink-Jensen).

1572
Sky B The apocalyptic sky — the 1572 nova and 1577 comet read as signs
To Tycho and many of his contemporaries the 1572 new star and the 1577 comet were not first of all cosmological discoveries but divine signs. His German report on the comet — the language Queen Sophie read — dwells far more on its calamitous astrological meaning than on the epochal finding that comets lie beyond the Moon; and when he finished the Progymnasmata he closed it (1592) with what Håkansson calls a prophetic testament: the nova as an omen of religious upheaval, the seven great conjunctions since Creation mapped onto the biblical epochs, the conjunction of 1583 (the seventh) inaugurating a sabbath-like golden age. On this reading Tycho looked at the heavens 'with the eyes of another age' — not natural phenomena dissolving the medieval cosmos, but God's punishing hand driving history to its fulfilment. An interpretive thread (Håkansson 2006), who cautions explicitly against the Whig reading of the nova as 'the decisive step to modern cosmology'. The prophetic reading was not the only meaning the new star carried in Tycho's own pages: in the Elegy to Urania in De nova stella (1573) the muse's epiphany gives Tycho the nova as a personal calling, setting the heavenly worth of science against the emptiness of conventional noble ideals — the beginning of the Urania allegory that Zeeberg traces from the humble alumnus of 1573, through the 1584–85 Uraniborg poems in which Uraniborg is astronomy's sole refuge, to Tycho's late, complete identification of himself with astronomy (Zeeberg 1993).

1573
Biography A Tycho in contemporary verse — æredigte and support-poems (1573–1602)
The earliest printed reception of Tycho was in verse. De nova stella (1573) opens and closes with liminary poems (æredigte) by Hans Frandsen and Anders Sørensen Vedel — guarantees from university and church for a young nobleman publishing in the academic manner. Tycho came to manage the genre deliberately. For the Progymnasmata he ran a solicitation campaign through his correspondence (1590–92): to Camerarius the younger in Nürnberg he sent his engraved portrait with a request for a portrait-poem from Paulus Melissus, and he approached Oxford, Görlitz, Prague, Edinburgh and Vienna in the same terms; the poem Martinus Mylius supplied for the provisional 1592 edition Tycho edited himself so that, as he told Scultetus, it should not seem too arrogant; and King James VI of Scotland contributed two autograph epigrams, printed in the posthumous Prague edition of 1602. The Epistolae astronomicae (1596) carry six æredigte, and the Mechanica (1598) its own set — among them Holger Rosenkrantz's poem, which Tycho himself lengthened to fill the page. In the departure years 1597–1600 the current reversed: collections of support-poems from sympathisers followed Tycho into exile, surviving in Vienna and Copenhagen transcripts — probably excerpts of one larger gathering. Zeeberg reads the whole practice as deliberate image-management: to an unusual degree the contemporary poetic reception of Tycho was solicited, edited and staged by Tycho himself.

1588
Legacy B The endowment that failed — Uraniborg's promised perpetuity (1588) against the instruments' decay (1597)
Uraniborg was meant to outlast its founder. On 23 August 1588, in the regency after Frederik II's death, the Council of the Realm issued at Tycho's request an open charter undertaking that the building he had raised on Hven 'med maerkelig Bekostning' and its instruments be kept at Crown expense in the state they were then in, during and after his lifetime, endowed from canonries and other benefices, with his own kin — failing them native Danish nobility, failing them other Danes — preferred to succeed him in the work; the full Council of the Realm re-issued it verbatim on 17 July 1589 (Kancelliets Brevbøger 1588-92 pp.82-83, 213). It was the formal charter of Uraniborg as a perpetual institution — and it was not kept. Within a decade Tycho had lost the fief, and on 2 June 1597 royal commissioners Thomas Fincke and Iver Stub were sent to Hven to inspect the damage the island's tenants had done to the astronomical instruments still standing on Uraniborg (KB 1596-1602 p.163) — the first documented harm to the great instruments, more than twenty years before their final destruction in the Bohemian upheaval of 1619. The distance between the 1588 promise and the 1597 reality is the administrative backstory of the whole collapse, and of why the instruments and the observation archive, not the institution, became Tycho's true legacy.

1596
Sky A Willem Blaeu, Tycho’s pupil, maps Hven’s stars onto the celestial globe (1596–1600)
Willem Janszoon Blaeu — about twenty-five — wintered on Hven in 1595–96, learning Tycho’s instruments and observing methods at first hand. He carried that apprenticeship back to Amsterdam: the celestial globes issued from his shop from about 1600 were mapped on Tycho’s star positions, and Christianson notes that such globes ‘using Tycho’s stellar positions,’ from Blaeu, the Langrens and Jodocus Hondius, ‘set a new standard for celestial cartography.’ Blaeu had observed enough on the island to discover a new star in Cygnus — P Cygni, ‘Blaeu’s Star,’ of 1600 — which he marked on his globes. The pupil became the founder of the Blaeu cartographic house; his son Joan would later crown the line with the coloured Tycho instrument plates of the 1664 Grooten Atlas (read elsewhere in this collection). It is the most direct carrying of Hven’s sky into the European map of the heavens.

1598
Biography B A contemporary Danish verdict — Tycho's pomp at Wandsbek (1598)
The earliest sustained contemporary character-portrait of Tycho is a hostile one. In a Latin letter of 1598 — written just after Tycho left Denmark, by the Danish nobleman Johan Stygge (so identified in the modern edition; the editor C. F. Bricka had cautiously left it as 'Hans or Johan Stygge') and addressed to the Copenhagen bookseller Hans Aalborg, 'the most erudite Master Johannes Alburgiacus' — the writer reports the talk circulating abroad about the astronomer's departure and rebuts it from the Danish-establishment side. He records the partisans' lament that 'Denmark, deprived of both eyes, is now made blind' (Daniam utroqve orbatam oculo … coecam nunc redditam), that the loss was a relapse into 'a new barbarism from which the Muses are exiled' (novam barbariem, ex qva exulavére Musæ), and that others blamed Machiavellism, envy or avarice at court — only to insist how slight a loss it truly was 'that our country lost this Tycho and was unwilling to keep him.' The portrait that follows is the document's real value: Tycho, he writes, 'is still in a certain Rantzau castle not far off' (in arce Ranzoviana qvadam non longe hinc remota — i.e. Wandsbek), but 'very rarely enters the town; and when he comes, he arrives with so many carriages and horses, and so pompously, as if he were more than noble.' He 'no longer acknowledges his own countrymen and scarcely wishes to be held a Dane' (suos conterraneos non amplius agnoscit vixqve Danus haberi vult) — refusing even to speak once with his own cousin Jost Høg (consobrino Jodoco Hogio) — and rides through the streets behind six horses with a great crowd running to see him 'as if he were a prince' (ac si princeps esset), with silk cloth and cushions laid on his reserved seat when, rarely, he enters church. Whatever its malice, the letter is the contemporary documentary ancestor of the question J. A. Fridericia would argue at the 1901 tercentenary — that Tycho's own conduct, not merely a hostile court, drove his fall from royal favour. The same document survives in the Epistolarium as Johan Stygge's letter to Hans Aalborg of 10 July 1598 (brahe-t_15980710001; Dreyer, Opera Omnia VIII, from the Rigsarkivet copy) — the character-portrait and its critical text one cross-reference apart. A single partisan voice, to be read against the friendlier evidence; not a balanced verdict.

Data A Kepler inherits the observations (1601)
On Tycho's death in October 1601 his full set of observations, though claimed by his heirs, fell into the hands of Johannes Kepler, his last assistant — the single most consequential transfer of data in the history of astronomy. Everything that follows in the new astronomy is built on it.

1601
Science B The afterlife of the Tychonic world-system
Tycho's geo-heliocentric system — the planets circling the Sun, the Sun circling a fixed Earth — lived on through the seventeenth century as the leading compromise between Ptolemy and Copernicus, congenial to those (notably the Jesuit astronomers) who could not accept a moving Earth. Its chief textbook vehicle was Longomontanus's Astronomia Danica (1622; see the Astronomia Danica episode); among the Jesuits it found its great champion in G. B. Riccioli, whose Almagestum Novum (1651) — the same work that fixed the name of the lunar crater Tycho — argued at length for a semi-Tychonic variant. It faded only as heliocentrism and Newtonian physics prevailed.

1601
Science B The naked-eye precision tradition — Hevelius and Verbiest
Beyond his world-system, Tycho founded a method: high-precision positional astronomy with large graduated naked-eye instruments, read by paired-slit (rimula) and transversal sights to suppress parallax — the protocol the Mechanica documents. That instrumental tradition outlived him by the better part of a century. Its last great practitioner was Johannes Hevelius (1611–1687) of Danzig, who as late as the 1660s compiled a star catalogue of high accuracy using 'Tychonic' instruments WITHOUT telescopic sights (Wolfschmidt, Crossroads, p.211). When Robert Hooke insisted that only telescopic sights and micrometer eyepieces could now give adequate accuracy, Hevelius defended the naked-eye method — a defence descending directly from Tycho's rimula-and-transversal protocol — and to adjudicate the dispute the Royal Society sent the young Edmond Halley to Danzig in 1679; Halley was impressed by Hevelius's accuracy, though later retrospective comparison favoured the telescopic instruments (Biographical Encyclopedia, p.540). The tradition reached even to China, where the Jesuit Ferdinand Verbiest rebuilt the bronze instruments of the Peking Imperial Observatory in the 1670s as direct descendants of Tycho's designs. The era of naked-eye precision closed only as telescopic sights prevailed (Flamsteed at Greenwich) — Tycho's instruments having defined the achievable accuracy of an entire pre-telescopic epoch.

Legacy B The grave in the Týn Church, Prague
Tycho was buried in 1601 in the Church of Our Lady before Týn on Prague's Old Town Square, his tomb marked by a relief effigy in armour. Far from Denmark, the grave became the focus of a long contest of memory between the Danish nation that formed him and the Bohemian city that received him.

Legacy B The unpaid 20,000 daler — the heirs' thirty-year claim on the imperial treasury (1601–1630)
Rudolf II's glittering promises to provide for Tycho and his family 'seem to have gone to the grave with him' (Friis). The heirs recovered his salary arrears only with great difficulty — and, on the Emperor's order, computed only to the day of death, 24 October 1601. For the instruments the purchase price was fixed by agreement at 20,000 daler, but payment barely came: about 5,000 daler at once, then instalments with interest totalling some 2,300 more up to 1613 — and after that, apparently, nothing. A dispute between the Emperor and the Bohemian estates over who should pay (the estates arguing the Emperor had bought the instruments 'for his own pleasure') left the balance hanging. After the Battle of the White Mountain (November 1620) the Protestant family had to leave Bohemia — except the son-in-law Tengnagel, by then an imperial councillor, and Joergen Brahe, who was permitted to remain expressly to press the family's money claim against Ferdinand II. On 18 December 1628 Ferdinand rescribed from Vienna to the royal Rentekammer in Prague that the matter be settled with all speed, and the file went to the Reformation Commissioners; the outcome is unknown — no receipt from the heirs has ever been found. That the balance was still unpaid on 3 May 1630 is shown by a document of that date, drawn up in German by Sophie Brahe and the Danish kin, formally recognising Tycho's children as legitimate and their mother Christine as his lawful wife, for use in their inheritance suits — the same family arvesag to which the surviving children's letter printed by Friis in Danske Magazin (5. R. II, pp.264-266) belongs. Figures partly 'according to other statements' (Friis); graded B accordingly.

1609
Science A Astronomia Nova (1609) — the elliptical orbit of Mars
Kepler's New Astronomy — its full title 'treated by commentaries on the motions of the star Mars, from the observations of Tycho Brahe' — derived the first two laws of planetary motion (the elliptical orbit and the equal-area law) directly from the precision of Tycho's Mars observations. Tycho's data made the ellipse visible where coarser figures could not.

1622
Science A Longomontanus, Astronomia Danica (1622) — the Tychonic textbook
Christian Severin Longomontanus (1562–1647), Tycho's only long-term student — who kept the lunar theory at Benátky when Kepler took over the Mars data — carried the master's astronomy into the seventeenth-century classroom. His textbook Astronomia Danica (Amsterdam: Willem Janszoon Blaeu, 1622) is built on the Tychonic geo-heliocentric world-system, and was the principal vehicle by which the Tychonic compromise reached a generation of students. Longomontanus received the Copenhagen professorship of mathematics in 1605 and later the chair of astronomy, which he held until his death.

1637
Legacy A The Round Tower (Rundetårn), Copenhagen — Tycho's catalogue continued
The Rundetårn, the university observatory tower raised in Copenhagen by order of King Christian IV in 1637, where Longomontanus inaugurated a programme to fix precise positions for the 777 stars of Tycho's printed catalogue (the select catalogue of the Progymnasmata) — the Tychonic enterprise re-rooted in Denmark a generation after Hven (it was sometimes styled the 'Royal Stjerneborg of Copenhagen'). The tower still stands; it was also where Tycho's great brass globe finally came to rest, until tower and contents burned in 1728 (see the great brass globe in Instrumenta).

1654
Biography A Gassendi, Tychonis Brahei vita (1654)
Pierre Gassendi's life of Tycho — the first full biography (first edition Paris, 1654; enlarged second edition, The Hague, ex typographia Adriani Vlacq, 1655, 'Editio Secunda auctior & correctior', to which were added the lives of Copernicus, Peurbach and Regiomontanus). Gassendi, royal professor of mathematics, drew on Longomontanus's recollections (Tycho's chief assistant), Tycho's own works and correspondence, and the manuscript Apologia; its engraved frontispiece (the 'ætatis 40, 1586' portrait in a triumphal arch of sixteen ancestral arms, motto 'Non Haberi sed Esse') belongs to the de Gheyn tradition. It shaped the image of Tycho for the next two and a half centuries, until Dreyer's archival biography of 1890 — and is best treated as the headwater of the biographical tradition (the origin of the anecdotes), superseded for facts by Dreyer/Thoren/Christianson.

1655
Data A The observation archive → Copenhagen → the Opera Omnia
The fate of Tycho's greatest manuscript treasure, his observation logs. Bought by the Emperor with the instruments, they were lent to Kepler — who built the Astronomia Nova and the Rudolphine Tables on them — and kept after his death by his son Ludwig Kepler as a pawn against the unpaid imperial debt (a 1633 bond of 12,694 gulden). With no prospect of payment, Ludwig sold them in 1655 to King Frederik III of Denmark for 600 rixdollars; they have been in Det Kongelige Bibliotek, Copenhagen, ever since, and were published by J. L. E. Dreyer as Tomes X–XIII of the Tychonis Brahe Opera Omnia (1913–1929). Had Kepler's pledge to return them to the imperial court been kept, Werlauff observed, they would have ended in Vienna, not Copenhagen.

1664
Legacy A Joan Blaeu’s Grooten Atlas — Tycho’s instruments in colour for a vernacular public (1664)
Joan Blaeu reproduced all twenty-two of Tycho Brahe’s principal instrument illustrations from the Astronomiae Instauratae Mechanica (Wandsbek, 1598) as richly coloured engravings in his monumental Grooten Atlas oft Werelt-beschryving (Amsterdam, 1664) — nine volumes in Dutch, the largest and most expensive atlas ever produced at that date. The atlas’s Denmark section spans 91 pages (pp. 136–226); its core is the 53-page treatment of Hven, which includes the Uraniborg architectural plans (garden, elevation, floor plan), the Stjerneborg bird’s-eye and floor plan, and Dutch vernacular descriptions of every major instrument — the first presentation of Tycho’s observational apparatus to a broad non-Latin reading public; the preceding pages on Denmark, Jutland, Funen, the islands and Zealand set the geographical context. The series culminates in the famous coloured interior view of Uraniborg (p. 201), showing Tycho himself with three assistants at the mural quadrant. Blaeu also reproduces the Stjerneborg inscriptions, including the salutation that closes the crypt-entrance text: ‘Have qui haec legis facisque, et vale’ (Hail, you who read and do these things — and farewell). The surviving Utrecht copy (Universiteitsbibliotheek Utrecht, AC 62 Rariora) is one of the principal sources for the coloured instrument tradition.

1666
Science A Historia Coelestis (Augsburg 1666) — the observations first in print
The editio princeps of the bulk of Tycho's reduced annual observation logs (1582–1601) — the moment his life's data finally reached print, 65 years after his death. It was edited from manuscript transcripts in the Vienna Hofbibliothek by the Jesuit Albert Curtz (Curtius) under the anagrammatic pseudonym 'Lucius Barrettus', printed at Augsburg by Simon Utzschneider on the imperial commission of Ferdinand III. The text is famously unreliable — its omissions and printer's errors were catalogued at length by Erasmus Bartholin (1668) and again by Dreyer (1890); Bartholin's own corrected exemplars survive as the KB volumes GKS 308–309. Yet for two and a half centuries the Historia Coelestis remained the ONLY published source for Tycho's observations — the same empirical foundation on which Kepler had already built the Astronomia Nova (1609) and the Rudolphine Tables (1627), now at last public. Some copies carry a cancel title 'Ratisbonae 1672' (1666 sheets reissued at Regensburg). Superseded only by Dreyer's Opera Omnia X–XIII (1913–29), which printed the observations from the Copenhagen autographs (see the Opera Omnia edition of the observations, below).

1844
Science A Tycho's comets recomputed — the 19th-century orbit reductions (1854)
Two and a half centuries after Tycho recorded them, his cometary observations were still good enough to yield modern orbits. The seven comets entered in his working brouillon (GKS 1826, 1577–1596) were re-reduced one by one by the astronomers of the mid-nineteenth century — F. W. Woldstedt took the comet of 1577, J. R. Hind those of 1582 and 1590, C. A. F. Peters that of 1585, and H. C. F. C. Schjellerup, observer at the Copenhagen University observatory, that of 1580. Schjellerup's memoir 'Tycho Brahes Original-Observationer, benyttede til Banebestemmelse af Cometen 1580' (Videnskabernes Selskabs Skrifter, 1854) worked straight from Tycho's autograph sextant observations at the Royal Library — the quarto brouillon, with the 1670 Bartholin–Rømer fair copies beside it — learning to read Tycho's notoriously disorderly hand (the numerals always clean) by collating it against Schumacher's edition of the comet of 1585. It is the nineteenth-century forerunner of the way Tycho's data are still used as working science today (see 'The data still in use', below): the same archive, reduced by each age to the precision of its own methods. This whole recomputation campaign preceded — and partly motivated — Dreyer's printing of the observation logs in the Opera Omnia (1913–29).

1890
Biography A Dreyer, Tycho Brahe (Edinburgh 1890) — the foundational biography
J. L. E. Dreyer's Tycho Brahe: A Picture of Scientific Life and Work in the Sixteenth Century (Edinburgh: Adam & Charles Black, 1890) is the foundation of all modern Tycho scholarship — the first biography to rest on systematic archival research rather than the traditions flowing from Gassendi. Dreyer had access to the Copenhagen manuscripts (the observation logs, the correspondence), Tycho's printed works, and the Hven records; his book covers the life, the instruments (with the GKS observation-volume catalogue that remains the backbone of the KB–Vienna collation), the star catalogue, the planetary theories, and the world-system with a precision no previous account had achieved. It superseded Gassendi's Vita (1654) as the authoritative life and remains the essential primary reference for Tycho's chronology, instruments, and scientific context — cited as 'Dreyer' in every subsequent scholarly publication. Its limitations: it antedates the prosopographical deepening of Christianson (2000) and the technical reassessment of Thoren (1990); the Tychonic system and its rivals are treated from a late-Victorian scientific vantage.

1913
Data A Tychonis Brahe Opera Omnia (Copenhagen 1913–29) — the complete critical edition
Dreyer (ed.), Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia (15 vols., Copenhagen: Libraria Gyldendaliana / Societas Scientiarum Danica, 1913–29) — the complete critical edition of Tycho's printed works, correspondence, and observations. Tomes I–V: the printed works (De nova stella 1573 through the Progymnasmata and the Mechanica, with the star catalogue); VI–VIII: the correspondence (509 known letters with Dreyer's apparatus, the edition of record reprinted on tekstnet.dk with English translations); IX: the diaries, poems and scripta varia; X–XIII: the observation logs in a reliable scholarly text, superseding the Curtz/Barrettus Historia Coelestis (1666) after two and a half centuries; XIV–XV: the documents and acta illustrating Tycho's life and work. The TBOO is the bibliographic backbone of all modern Tycho scholarship; every citation to Tycho's text without a TBOO reference is, by implication, unreliable. Funded by the Carlsberg Foundation. Together with Dreyer's 1890 biography, it completed the mid-twentieth-century scholarly canonisation of Tycho.

Biography A Zeeberg, Urania Titani (1994) — first critical edition of Tycho’s Latin poetry
Peter Zeeberg’s Tycho Brahes »Urania Titani«: Et digt om Sophie Brahe (Renæssancestudier 7, Museum Tusculanum, Copenhagen, 1994, 320 pp.) is the first critical edition of Tycho’s most ambitious poem and the founding document of Tycho Brahe studies as a branch of Danish literary history. Zeeberg established Tycho’s authorship via the Craig letter (TBOO VII.366), provided the first complete Danish translation, wrote a full literary commentary on the poem’s Ovidian heroide genre, its alchemical and astrological layers, and its use of Uraniborg pseudonyms (Urania / Titan / Apollo / Mercurius), and had the four embedded horoscopes reconstructed by Ove von Späth. The monograph opened a scholarly conversation about Tycho as a humanist-literary figure — distinct from, and complementary to, his reception as an astronomical instrument-maker and observer. The edition was prepared for by Zeeberg’s short 1993 study Den praktiske muse. Tycho Brahes brug af latindigtningen (Museum Tusculanum, 55 pp.), which read Tycho’s Latin poetry as social practice — the vertical address to patrons and the horizontal binding of noble and learned friends into the Uraniborg circle — tracing the arc from the Urania elegy of De nova stella (1573) through the 1584–85 separate prints of the Uraniborg press (the carmina to Lange, Gjøe, Ulfeldt, Kaas and Rantzau) to the campaign of 1590–92 in which Tycho solicited liminary poems (æredigte) for the Progymnasmata from across learned Europe, on occasion editing them himself. Study and edition together are the twin foundations of the literary approach to Tycho.

Science A The data still in use — SN 1572 and the star catalogue
Tycho's measurements are still working scientific data. His own brightness record of the 1572 new star, reconstructed as a modern light curve (Ruiz-Lapuente 2004), establishes SN 1572 as a normal Type Ia supernova (peak M_V ≈ −19.6) — a classification independently corroborated when the spectrum of the explosion was recovered in 2008 from its centuries-delayed light-echo (Krause et al., Nature), and re-examined from his records again in 2023. His catalogue of about a thousand stars has been machine-read in all three editions and set against the modern Hipparcos positions reduced to epoch 1601 (Verbunt & van Gent 2010): the bulk of his places are good to about 2′ and the best near 1′ — a 390-year astrometric baseline back to the sky he measured.

Legacy B The 2010 exhumation & the death question
Jens Vellev's Danish–Czech team re-exhumed Tycho in November 2010 and reported in 2012. Their findings bore on two old questions. First, the hair-mercury levels were found too low to be lethal, EXCLUDING the acute mercury poisoning that a popular modern theory (some versions accusing Kepler) had alleged; the exhumation thus removed the poisoning hypothesis but did not itself positively establish the cause of death — it leaves standing the traditional account of a fatal bladder ailment / uraemia after the banquet at which Tycho is said to have held his urine too long. Second, a sample associated with the false nose showed a copper–zinc (brass) alloy rather than the gold or silver of legend. The mercury analysis (Rasmussen et al., 'Was He Murdered or Was He Not? — Part I', Archaeometry, 2013; multi-element follow-up Kučera et al. 2017) put the peak at roughly 16 µg/g in the beard-hair about two months before death — far below the 200–800 µg/g of even moderate intoxication, and attributed to Tycho's own alchemical medicine rather than to poisoning. The brass nose was confirmed by the equal-parts copper-and-zinc green stain left on the nasal bones. A 2018 study of the skeleton (Velemínský et al., Clinical Rheumatology) found diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis, supplying a physiological mechanism for the urinary symptoms of the traditional account. (The µg/g peak is reported from the team's own summary of the Archaeometry data — grade B.)
