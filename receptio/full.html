Receptio — The Reception of Tycho Brahe

Charter. Receptio records how Tycho Brahe’s work and person were received, used and remembered — by his contemporaries from the new star of 1572 onward, and after his death in 1601 by the astronomers who inherited his data, the editors who printed it, the institutions that claimed him and the sciences that still use his numbers. Each record is one documented episode, with its evidence grade, its sources at the cited page, and a link to the object or the edition where one could be verified.

1Introduction

This is the edition of a register of 28 episodes in the reception of Tycho Brahe. An episode is one documented instance of his work or his person being received, used, edited, contested, commemorated or examined: a transfer of data, a book built on his observations, a tomb opened, a crater named, a poem solicited, a claim on a treasury. The register is deliberately not confined to the years after his death. Reception began in his lifetime — the vernacular reading of the 1572 star, the liminary verse he solicited for his own books, the charter for Uraniborg’s perpetuity, a Danish nobleman’s hostile verdict of 1598 — and 7 of the 28 episodes begin before 1601. The other 21 run from the inheritance of the observations in October 1601 to analyses published in this decade; 4 of them have no end, because the thing they concern still exists.

Every episode carries a stable key, a thread (§6), an evidence grade, the works it descends from (§5), the sources it rests on, and, where one could be verified, a link to the object itself or to a digitised copy of the edition (§4). The full records are in §3; the same records as one sortable table are in §2.

What the numbers are not. The 28 episodes are a selection, not a census of Tycho’s reception. They were entered where the compilation’s own sources — the biographies, the editions, the published exhumation studies, the chancery registers, the Danish journals — documented an episode well enough to grade it; the absence of an episode says nothing about whether it happened, and the number of episodes in a thread measures the compilation’s attention, not the world’s. No figure on this page measures influence. The year an episode begins is the year of its first documented event as the record states it: for the supernova remnant that is the observation of 1572, for the comet reductions the first of the four memoirs. Where a record itself spans two moments (“1655 / 1913–29”) the table prints the numeric bounds and the register prints the record’s own phrase.

The 1572 new star in contemporary eyes — Busch & Reravius (1572–73)

1572–1573ScienceArecep-busch-reravius-1572

Tycho's De nova stella (1573) was only one of many contemporary responses to the new star — and, in Denmark, not the one that reached the public first. The German Lutheran preacher Georg Busch read the 1572 phenomenon as a comet, advancing an unusual Christian comet-theory in which it was not merely a warning from God but was physically condensed out of 'evaporated' earthly sin (a notion found only in the Lutheran north). The Danish priest Rasmus Hansen Reravius translated Busch into Danish (Copenhagen, in at least two editions; it also seeded a Swedish version), drawn to the book for its message of a warning sent by God to a sinful humanity — the comet-or-star question being, to him, secondary. Reravius enlarged Busch's title from the German 'On the comet' to the Danish 'On the new star and comet', adding the word 'star' under the influence of Tycho, whose Latin treatise had by then established that it was a star, not a comet. Reravius's vernacular book reached far more readers than Tycho's small-run, slowly-distributed Latin treatise, and so became the channel through which Tycho's discovery first reached a broad public (Fink-Jensen).

The apocalyptic sky — the 1572 nova and 1577 comet read as signs

1572–1592SkyBrecep-apocalyptic-nova

To Tycho and many of his contemporaries the 1572 new star and the 1577 comet were not first of all cosmological discoveries but divine signs. His German report on the comet — the language Queen Sophie read — dwells far more on its calamitous astrological meaning than on the finding that comets lie beyond the Moon; and when he finished the Progymnasmata he closed it (1592) with what Håkansson calls a prophetic testament: the nova as an omen of religious upheaval, the seven great conjunctions since Creation mapped onto the biblical epochs, the conjunction of 1583 (the seventh) inaugurating a sabbath-like golden age. On this reading Tycho looked at the heavens 'with the eyes of another age' — not natural phenomena dissolving the medieval cosmos, but God's punishing hand driving history to its fulfilment. An interpretive thread (Håkansson 2006), who cautions explicitly against the Whig reading of the nova as 'the decisive step to modern cosmology'. The prophetic reading was not the only meaning the new star carried in Tycho's own pages: in the Elegy to Urania in De nova stella (1573) the muse's epiphany gives Tycho the nova as a personal calling, setting the heavenly worth of science against the emptiness of conventional noble ideals — the beginning of the Urania allegory that Zeeberg traces from the humble alumnus of 1573, through the 1584–85 Uraniborg poems in which Uraniborg is astronomy's sole refuge, to Tycho's late, complete identification of himself with astronomy (Zeeberg 1993).

Tycho in contemporary verse — æredigte and support-poems (1573–1602)

1573–1602BiographyArecep-aeredigte-reception

The earliest printed reception of Tycho was in verse. De nova stella (1573) opens and closes with liminary poems (æredigte) by Hans Frandsen and Anders Sørensen Vedel — guarantees from university and church for a young nobleman publishing in the academic manner. Tycho came to manage the genre deliberately. For the Progymnasmata he ran a solicitation campaign through his correspondence (1590–92): to Camerarius the younger in Nürnberg he sent his engraved portrait with a request for a portrait-poem from Paulus Melissus, and he approached Oxford, Görlitz, Prague, Edinburgh and Vienna in the same terms; the poem Martinus Mylius supplied for the provisional 1592 edition Tycho edited himself so that, as he told Scultetus, it should not seem too arrogant; and King James VI of Scotland contributed two autograph epigrams, printed in the posthumous Prague edition of 1602. The Epistolae astronomicae (1596) carry six æredigte, and the Mechanica (1598) its own set — among them Holger Rosenkrantz's poem, which Tycho himself lengthened to fill the page. In the departure years 1597–1600 the current reversed: collections of support-poems from sympathisers followed Tycho into exile, surviving in Vienna and Copenhagen transcripts — probably excerpts of one larger gathering. Zeeberg reads the whole practice as deliberate image-management: to an unusual degree the contemporary poetic reception of Tycho was solicited, edited and staged by Tycho himself.

The endowment that failed — Uraniborg's promised perpetuity (1588) against the instruments' decay (1597)

1588–1597LegacyBrecep-uraniborg-endowment

Uraniborg was meant to outlast its founder. On 23 August 1588, in the regency after Frederik II's death, the Council of the Realm issued at Tycho's request an open charter undertaking that the building he had raised on Hven 'med maerkelig Bekostning' and its instruments be kept at Crown expense in the state they were then in, during and after his lifetime, endowed from canonries and other benefices, with his own kin — failing them native Danish nobility, failing them other Danes — preferred to succeed him in the work; the full Council of the Realm re-issued it verbatim on 17 July 1589 (Kancelliets Brevbøger 1588–92, pp. 82–83, 213). It was the formal charter of Uraniborg as a perpetual institution — and it was not kept. Within a decade Tycho had lost the fief, and on 2 June 1597 royal commissioners Thomas Fincke and Iver Stub were sent to Hven to inspect the damage the island's tenants had done to the astronomical instruments still standing on Uraniborg (KB 1596–1602, p. 163) — the first documented harm to the great instruments, more than twenty years before their final destruction in the Bohemian upheaval of 1619.

Willem Blaeu, Tycho’s pupil, maps Hven’s stars onto the celestial globe (1596–1600)

1596–1600SkyArecep-blaeu-globes

Willem Janszoon Blaeu — about twenty-five — wintered on Hven in 1595–96, learning Tycho’s instruments and observing methods at first hand. He carried that apprenticeship back to Amsterdam: the celestial globes issued from his shop from about 1600 were mapped on Tycho’s star positions, and Christianson notes that such globes ‘using Tycho’s stellar positions,’ from Blaeu, the Langrens and Jodocus Hondius, ‘set a new standard for celestial cartography.’ Blaeu had observed enough on the island to discover a new star in Cygnus — P Cygni, ‘Blaeu’s Star,’ of 1600 — which he marked on his globes. The pupil became the founder of the Blaeu cartographic house; his son Joan would later crown the line with the coloured Tycho instrument plates of the 1664 Grooten Atlas (read elsewhere in this collection). It is the most direct carrying of Hven’s sky into the European map of the heavens.

A contemporary Danish verdict — Tycho's pomp at Wandsbek (1598)

1598BiographyBrecep-stygge-1598

The earliest sustained contemporary character-portrait of Tycho is a hostile one. In a Latin letter of 1598 — written just after Tycho left Denmark, by the Danish nobleman Johan Stygge (so identified in the modern edition; the editor C. F. Bricka had cautiously left it as 'Hans or Johan Stygge') and addressed to the Copenhagen bookseller Hans Aalborg, 'the most erudite Master Johannes Alburgiacus' — the writer reports the talk circulating abroad about the astronomer's departure and rebuts it from the Danish-establishment side. He records the partisans' lament that 'Denmark, deprived of both eyes, is now made blind' (Daniam utroqve orbatam oculo … coecam nunc redditam), that the loss was a relapse into 'a new barbarism from which the Muses are exiled' (novam barbariem, ex qva exulavére Musæ), and that others blamed Machiavellism, envy or avarice at court — only to insist how slight a loss it truly was 'that our country lost this Tycho and was unwilling to keep him.' The portrait that follows is the document's real value: Tycho, he writes, 'is still in a certain Rantzau castle not far off' (in arce Ranzoviana qvadam non longe hinc remota — i.e. Wandsbek), but 'very rarely enters the town; and when he comes, he arrives with so many carriages and horses, and so pompously, as if he were more than noble.' He 'no longer acknowledges his own countrymen and scarcely wishes to be held a Dane' (suos conterraneos non amplius agnoscit vixqve Danus haberi vult) — refusing even to speak once with his own cousin Jost Høg (consobrino Jodoco Hogio) — and rides through the streets behind six horses with a great crowd running to see him 'as if he were a prince' (ac si princeps esset), with silk cloth and cushions laid on his reserved seat when, rarely, he enters church. Whatever its malice, the letter is the contemporary documentary ancestor of the question J. A. Fridericia would argue at the 1901 tercentenary — that Tycho's own conduct, not merely a hostile court, drove his fall from royal favour. The same document survives in the Epistolarium as Johan Stygge's letter to Hans Aalborg of 10 July 1598 (brahe-t_15980710001; Dreyer, Opera Omnia VIII, from the Rigsarkivet copy) — the character-portrait and its critical text one cross-reference apart. A single partisan voice, to be read against the friendlier evidence; not a balanced verdict.

The naked-eye precision tradition — Hevelius and Verbiest

17th c.ScienceBrecep-naked-eye-tradition

The last practitioner of Tychonic naked-eye precision was Johannes Hevelius (1611–1687) of Danzig, who as late as the 1660s compiled a star catalogue of high accuracy using 'Tychonic' instruments without telescopic sights (Wolfschmidt, Crossroads, p. 211). When Robert Hooke insisted that only telescopic sights and micrometer eyepieces could now give adequate accuracy, Hevelius defended the naked-eye method — a defence descending directly from Tycho's rimula-and-transversal protocol — and to adjudicate the dispute the Royal Society sent the young Edmond Halley to Danzig in 1679; Halley was impressed by Hevelius's accuracy, though later retrospective comparison favoured the telescopic instruments (Biographical Encyclopedia, p. 502). The tradition reached China, where the Jesuit Ferdinand Verbiest rebuilt the bronze instruments of the Peking Imperial Observatory in the 1670s as direct descendants of Tycho's designs. Naked-eye sights gave way to telescopic sights with Flamsteed at Greenwich.

The unpaid 20,000 daler — the heirs' thirty-year claim on the imperial treasury (1601–1630)

1601–1630LegacyBrecep-heirs-unpaid-claim

Rudolf II's promises to provide for Tycho and his family 'seem to have gone to the grave with him' (Friis). The heirs recovered his salary arrears only with great difficulty — and, on the Emperor's order, computed only to the day of death, 24 October 1601. For the instruments the purchase price was fixed by agreement at 20,000 daler, but payment barely came: about 5,000 daler at once, then instalments with interest totalling some 2,300 more up to 1613 — and after that, apparently, nothing. A dispute between the Emperor and the Bohemian estates over who should pay (the estates arguing the Emperor had bought the instruments 'for his own pleasure') left the balance hanging. After the Battle of the White Mountain (November 1620) the Protestant family had to leave Bohemia — except the son-in-law Tengnagel, by then an imperial councillor, and Jørgen Brahe, who was permitted to remain expressly to press the family's money claim against Ferdinand II. On 18 December 1628 Ferdinand rescribed from Vienna to the royal Rentekammer in Prague that the matter be settled with all speed, and the file went to the Reformation Commissioners; the outcome is unknown — no receipt from the heirs has ever been found. That the balance was still unpaid on 3 May 1630 is shown by a document of that date, drawn up in German by Sophie Brahe and the Danish kin, formally recognising Tycho's children as legitimate and their mother Christine as his lawful wife, for use in their inheritance suits — the same family arvesag to which the surviving children's letter printed by Friis in Danske Magazin (5. R. II, pp. 264–266) belongs. Figures partly 'according to other statements' (Friis); graded B accordingly.

Longomontanus, Astronomia Danica (1622) — the Tychonic textbook

1622ScienceArecep-longomontanus-danica

Christian Severin Longomontanus (1562–1647), Tycho's only long-term student — who kept the lunar theory at Benátky when Kepler took over the Mars data — carried the master's astronomy into the seventeenth-century classroom. His textbook Astronomia Danica (Amsterdam: Willem Janszoon Blaeu, 1622) is built on the Tychonic geo-heliocentric world-system, and was the principal vehicle by which the Tychonic compromise reached a generation of students. Longomontanus received the Copenhagen professorship of mathematics in 1605 and later the chair of astronomy, which he held until his death.

The Round Tower (Rundetårn), Copenhagen — Tycho's catalogue continued

1637LegacyAextantrecep-rundetaarn

The Rundetårn, the university observatory tower begun in Copenhagen by order of King Christian IV in 1637 and completed in 1642, where Longomontanus inaugurated a programme to fix precise positions for the 777 stars of Tycho's printed catalogue (the select catalogue of the Progymnasmata) — the Tychonic enterprise re-rooted in Denmark a generation after Hven (it was sometimes styled the 'Royal Stjerneborg of Copenhagen'). The tower still stands; it was also where Tycho's great brass globe finally came to rest, until tower and contents burned in 1728 (see the great brass globe in Instrumenta).

The fate of the observation logs. Bought by the Emperor with the instruments, they were lent to Kepler and kept after his death by his son Ludwig Kepler as a pawn against the unpaid imperial debt (a 1633 bond of 12,694 gulden). Joan Blaeu’s Grooten Atlas — Tycho’s instruments in colour for a vernacular public (1664)

Joan Blaeu reproduced all twenty-two of Tycho Brahe’s principal instrument illustrations from the Astronomiae Instauratae Mechanica (Wandsbek, 1598) as richly coloured engravings in his Grooten Atlas oft Werelt-beschryving (Amsterdam, 1664) — nine volumes in Dutch, the largest and most expensive atlas ever produced at that date. The series includes the coloured interior view of Uraniborg (p. 201), showing Tycho himself with three assistants at the mural quadrant. Blaeu also reproduces the Stjerneborg inscriptions, including the salutation that closes the crypt-entrance text: ‘Have qui haec legis facisque, et vale’ (Hail, you who read and do these things — and farewell). The surviving Utrecht copy (Universiteitsbibliotheek Utrecht, AC 62 Rariora) is one of the principal sources for the coloured instrument tradition.

Tycho's comets recomputed — the 19th-century orbit reductions (1854)

Two and a half centuries after Tycho recorded them, his cometary observations were still good enough to yield modern orbits. The seven comets entered in his working brouillon (GKS 1826, 1577–1596) were re-reduced one by one by the astronomers of the mid-nineteenth century — F. W. Woldstedt took the comet of 1577 (Helsingfors, 1844; Dreyer 1890, p. 172 n. 3), J. R. Hind those of 1582 and 1590 (the latter in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society 7, 1846), C. A. F. Peters that of 1585 (Astronomische Nachrichten 39, 1849; Thoren 1990, p. 203 n.), and H. C. F. C. Schjellerup, observer at the Copenhagen University observatory, that of 1580 (1854) — Schjellerup's own preface (pp. 3–4) records the division of labour. Schjellerup's memoir 'Tycho Brahes Original-Observationer, benyttede til Banebestemmelse af Cometen 1580' (Videnskabernes Selskabs Skrifter, 1854) worked straight from Tycho's autograph sextant observations at the Royal Library — the quarto brouillon, with the 1670 Bartholin–Rømer fair copies beside it — learning to read Tycho's notoriously disorderly hand (the numerals always clean) by collating it against Schumacher's edition of the comet of 1585. It is the nineteenth-century forerunner of the way Tycho's data are still used as working science today (see 'The data still in use', below): the same archive, reduced by each age to the precision of its own methods. This whole recomputation campaign preceded — and partly motivated — Dreyer's printing of the observation logs in the Opera Omnia (1913–29).

Dreyer, Tycho Brahe (Edinburgh 1890) — the foundational biography

1890BiographyArecep-dreyer-biography-1890

J. L. E. Dreyer's Tycho Brahe: A Picture of Scientific Life and Work in the Sixteenth Century (Edinburgh: Adam & Charles Black, 1890) is the first biography to rest on systematic archival research rather than on the traditions flowing from Gassendi. Zeeberg, Urania Titani (1994) — first critical edition of Tycho’s Latin poetry

1994BiographyArecep-zeeberg-urania-titani

Peter Zeeberg’s Tycho Brahes »Urania Titani«: Et digt om Sophie Brahe (Renæssancestudier 7, Museum Tusculanum, Copenhagen, 1994, 320 pp.) is the first critical edition of Tycho’s most ambitious poem and the founding document of Tycho Brahe studies as a branch of Danish literary history. Zeeberg established Tycho’s authorship via the Craig letter (TBOO VII.366), provided the first complete Danish translation, wrote a full literary commentary on the poem’s Ovidian heroide genre, its alchemical and astrological layers, and its use of Uraniborg pseudonyms (Urania / Titan / Apollo / Mercurius), and had the four embedded horoscopes reconstructed by Ove von Späth. The monograph opened a scholarly conversation about Tycho as a humanist-literary figure — distinct from, and complementary to, his reception as an astronomical instrument-maker and observer. The edition was prepared for by Zeeberg’s short 1993 study Den praktiske muse. Tycho Brahes brug af latindigtningen (Museum Tusculanum, 55 pp.), which read Tycho’s Latin poetry as social practice — the vertical address to patrons and the horizontal binding of noble and learned friends into the Uraniborg circle — tracing the arc from the Urania elegy of De nova stella (1573) through the 1584–85 separate prints of the Uraniborg press (the carmina to Lange, Gjøe, Ulfeldt, Kaas and Rantzau) to the campaign of 1590–92 in which Tycho solicited liminary poems (æredigte) for the Progymnasmata from across learned Europe, on occasion editing them himself.

The data still in use — SN 1572 and the star catalogue

2004–presentScienceAextantrecep-living-data

Tycho's measurements are still working scientific data. His own brightness record of the 1572 new star, reconstructed as a modern light curve (Ruiz-Lapuente 2004), establishes SN 1572 as a normal Type Ia supernova (peak M_V ≈ −19.6) — a classification independently corroborated when the spectrum of the explosion was recovered in 2008 from its centuries-delayed light-echo (Krause et al., Nature), and re-examined from his records again in 2023 (Hinse et al.). His catalogue of about a thousand stars has been machine-read in all three editions and set against the modern Hipparcos positions reduced to epoch 1601 (Verbunt & van Gent 2010): the bulk of his places are good to about 2′ and the best near 1′.

The 2010 exhumation & the death question

2010LegacyBrecep-exhumation-2010

Jens Vellev's Danish–Czech team re-exhumed Tycho in November 2010 and reported in 2012. Their findings bore on two old questions. First, the hair-mercury levels were found too low to be lethal, excluding the acute mercury poisoning that a popular modern theory (some versions accusing Kepler) had alleged; the exhumation thus removed the poisoning hypothesis but did not itself positively establish the cause of death — it leaves standing the traditional account of a fatal bladder ailment / uraemia after the banquet at which Tycho is said to have held his urine too long. Second, a sample associated with the false nose showed a copper–zinc (brass) alloy rather than the gold or silver of legend. The mercury analysis (Rasmussen et al., 'Was He Murdered or Was He Not? — Part I', Archaeometry, 2013; multi-element follow-up Kučera et al. 2017) put the peak at roughly 16 µg/g in the beard-hair about two months before death — far below the 200–800 µg/g of even moderate intoxication, and attributed to Tycho's own alchemical medicine rather than to poisoning. The brass nose was confirmed by the equal-parts copper-and-zinc green stain left on the nasal bones. A 2018 study of the skeleton (Kacki, Velemínský et al., PLoS ONE) found diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis, supplying a physiological mechanism for the urinary symptoms of the traditional account. (The µg/g peak is reported from the team's own summary of the Archaeometry data — grade B.)

7Desiderata

What this register does not yet hold, what it holds on weaker authority than it would like, and what would settle each point. The list is part of the edition, not an apology for it.

  1. Riccioli's Almagestum novum (1651) deserves its own record: it is cited here only inside the world-system and crater episodes.
  2. The semi-Tychonic variants — Ursus, Roslin, Riccioli's own — are not modelled as a cluster; the Tychonic-system record names them in passing.
  3. The commemorative record after 1901 — the quatercentenaries of 1946 and 2001, the museum on Hven, the Prague monuments — is not entered at all. The register ends its Legacy thread with the bones.
  4. Kepler's Harmonices mundi (1619) and the third law are not recorded as a separate use of the data; the Science thread jumps from 1609 to 1627.
  5. No online record could be verified for the Týn church's own tomb, for Riccioli's 1651 volumes, for Schjellerup's 1854 memoir, for Bricka's volume of Danske Magazin, or for Zeeberg's two studies; those records carry the citation and no link.
  6. The 1901 examination is known only through the 2010 project's account; Matiegka's own report has not been located.
  7. Hind's reduction of the comet of 1582 is dated only by the 1846 paper on the comet of 1590; its own place of publication is not recorded here.
  8. The Gingerich citation was resolved by the editor to the 1971 Sky and Telescope article; the compilation's original registry named no work.
