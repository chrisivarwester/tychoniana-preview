5The sites

The places are where he was; these are what he built, and what became of it. Fourteen structures are recorded — the island establishment on Hven and the Bohemian dwellings of the exile — each with its date, its survival status, the rooms it contained and the instruments they housed, its fate, and, where one exists, a link to the thing itself. For Uraniborg and Stjerneborg the primary architectural record is Charles Christensen's measured reconstruction of 1921, printed here with its excavation evidence, its dimension comparison and the surviving relics; Blaeu's much-reproduced views are given beneath it as the inherited print tradition they are, schematic and unreliable in detail.

Hven — the island establishment 9 sites

Hven (Ven) — the island fief

extantA1576–1597 · Ven (Hven)site-hven
The island of Hven (Swedish Ven) in the Øresund, granted to Tycho as a fief by Frederik II on 23 May 1576 — rent-free for as long as he lived and continued his mathematical studies, with 400 daler from the treasury toward a house he was to build on the island (Tycho himself supplying the stone and lime; Kancelliets Brevbøger 1576–79 p. 59). Today part of Sweden (Landskrona kommun). In his own words, “The island itself is very high, as if it were a mountain which you might ascend, but on top it is flat all over”; it lies in the Sound “which divides Scania from Sealand,” with Copenhagen three miles to the south-west, and its “latitude from the equator is 55°54½′, as measured several times by us with the greatest care” (Mechanica, trans. Ræder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, pp. 138–139). Tycho's two decades on Hven were also a hard landlordship. He held the island's tenants to heavy labour-service on Uraniborg and its works, and his disputes with them — and with the Crown over their grievances — run through the record: a royal letter of 1587 and another of 1592 document the friction, the latter in the matter of his tenant Rasmus Pedersen of Gundsømagle, whom Tycho had barred from a farm and had imprisoned, the King directing that the suit be heard before the landsdommer Lauge Beck (Becker, Danske Magazin 3. R. IV–V). Even from his Wandsbek exile Tycho went on managing the island at a distance — a Danish letter of 28 May 1598 thanks the Lord High Steward Christoffer Valkendorf for backing his bailiff against the 'disobedient peasants' there (Epistolarium: Tycho to Christoffer Valkendorf, 28 May 1598). The tenants' grievances were among the grounds on which Tycho lost royal favour and, in 1597, the fief itself. The chancery records document the friction in detail: as early as 1578 the Crown forbade Hven's tenants to move off the island without Tycho's leave 'medens han bygger der paa Landet' (KB 1576–79 p. 334); on the tenants' complaint of intolerable labour-service the King sent Arild Ugerup and Axel Gyldenstierne to Hven (20 August 1580), whose report produced the formal 'Ordning og Skik mellem Tyge Brahe og Bønderne paa Hven' of 8 January 1581 — two days' labour-service a week per half-farm, the tenants' claimed freeholder (jordegne) status revoked, and Hven's court cases moved to the Skaane landsting (KB 1580–83 pp. 128–129, 200-201). Tycho's Crown income beyond Hven was itself a knot of fiefs: Kullegaarden on the Kullen peninsula in Skaane (granted 1577), which carried the duty of keeping the Kullen lighthouse 'i Stand og lade den blusse den sædvanlige Tid' and, as Tycho successfully argued in 1579, supplied the firewood for Uraniborg (KB 1576–79 pp. 230, 249, 765; 1580–83 pp. 294–295); the prebend of the Helligtrekongers Kapel in Roskilde Cathedral (held 1579–1597, with jus patronatus over Udbye parish); the Norwegian fief of Nordfjord (1578), exchanged in 1586 for 400 daler yearly from the Øresund toll; and a royal pension terminated in 1581 once the fiefs sufficed. The end came together: a fresh royal commission investigated the tenants' renewed grievances on 9 April 1597, and in June 1597 the Roskilde prebend passed to Chancellor Christen Friis 'saaledes som Tyge Brahe hidtil har haft det' (KB 1596–1602 pp. 113, 168, 170) in the same weeks Tycho gave up Hven. The island passed in 1602 to a new lensmand, Cort Barleben, on ordinary commercial terms (50 daler the first year, 100 thereafter), by which time the buildings were reported 'meget forfalden' (KB 1596–1602 pp. 739, 746).
Dimensions. highest in the middle and flat on top; latitude 55°54½′ (Tycho's own determination); circumference 8160 passus (Mechanica 1946, pp. 138–139)

Uraniborg — the castle of the heavens

ruinedA1576–1580 · Ven (Hven)site-uraniborg
Tycho's great house, built 1576–1580 ön a square rampart-enclosed plot whose four corners pointed to the compass points, with four roads running from those corners to the raised circular space at the centre where the house stood. The central building was a square 49 feet ön a side, with round towers 18 feet in diameter ön its north and south faces (ringed by lower outhouses för fuel) and narrow entrance towers east and west; it was built apparently of red brick with sandstone ornaments in the 'Gothic Renaissance' style then new to the North — epoch-making, Dreyer judged, in the history of Scandinavian architecture. A single building uniting residence, library and upper observatory galleries above än alchemical laboratory in the basement. Its artistic genesis is still debated — advisers proposed from Hans van Paeschen (first builder of Kronborg) to the royal sculptor Johan Gregor van der Schardt — but the 1587 tablet in the great mural-quadrant painting names Tycho himself 'the constructor of the buildings and the astronomical instruments', and the domed-centre-in-a-geometric-garden plan shows a strong affinity with Italian villa architecture, especially Palladio's Villa Rotonda (Johannsen, in Tycho Brahes Verden, 2006). The standard art-historical monograph ön the building — Jern's Uraniborg: herresäte och himlaborg (Lund, 1976) — locates the specific model för the gabled façade not in the villa tradition but in Venetian church fronts, comparing it directly with S. Giovanni in Bragora and S. Maria della Carità, while cautioning that Uraniborg cannot be reduced to any single Renaissance style-circle (Jern 1976). The documented hands are these: the Netherlandish master-builder Hans von Paschen led the work before transferring to Bohus in 1577; Hans van Stenwinkel the Elder completed the interior and built Stjerneborg (royal architect, 1582); the Nuremberg founder Georg Labenwolf laid the lead-pipe water system and fountain; and Tobias Gemperlin painted the great mural quadrant (Jern 1976). Over its two doors Tycho set paired mottoes that made the building's double programme explicit — Suspiciendo despicio ('by looking up I look down') över the eastern, astronomical portal, and Despiciendo suspicio ('by looking down I look up') över the western, alchemical one — so that astronomy and the laboratory's 'terrestrial astronomy' answered each other across the house (Jern 1976). In his own words, Tycho chose “the site highest ön this island … practically in its centre, where I started building from its foundations a castle which was named after the heaven itself in which it was its task to make observations, and hence called Uraniborg.” He gives the rampart-square as three hundred feet ön a side, the central house as “nearly sixty feet” per side under a forty-five-foot wall, and seventy-five feet “from the ground to the Pegasus at the very top” (Mechanica, trans. Ræder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 126). These figures are in Tycho's short foot of 259 mm, so the house is ≈15.5 m square — consistent with the 49-foot figure (in the larger Danish foot) recorded above.
Dimensions. central building a square of 49 feet per side; round towers 18 feet in diameter on the north and south sides; narrow entrance towers east and west (Dreyer, p. 96, from Tycho's own Epistolae astronomicae p. 218 + Mechanica fol. H.4)
Fate. decay documented from Tycho's departure — royal commissioners Thomas Fincke and Iver Stub were sent on 2 June 1597 to inspect the damage the Hven tenants had done to the instruments still on Uraniborg (KB 1596–1602 p. 163); by June 1602 the buildings were reported 'meget forfalden' and surveyed for the new lensmand Cort Barleben (KB p. 746). Demolished and quarried in the 17th century; only foundations survive, partly restored

Design source. The design adapts Palladio's Villa Rotonda (known via the engraving in I quattro libri dell'architettura, 1570): Uraniborg keeps the square plan and the four axial corridors but drops the central circular domed hall (impractical in a Nordic house), substitutes octagonal portal-projections for the open loggias, and adds the two round towers N and S. Vitruvian symmetria — invoked by Tycho in the Mechanica's description of the east front, after the architectural learning he had professed since his 1574 oration — governs the whole; the volute window-crowns follow Serlio, the timber cupola Walter Rivius's German Vitruvius (1548). [Jern 1976 later relocates the façade source from the Veneto villa to Venetian church fronts — a competing attribution.]

The gardens and ramparts of Uraniborg

reconstructedB1576; redesigned after 1590 · Ven (Hven)site-gardens
The square earthwork ramparts enclosing Uraniborg, their corners set to the cardinal points, with formal gardens and orchards within and around them — among the finest in Scandinavia by Tycho's own account. Christianson describes the orchard's pippin, Rambour and Gylling apples, Doyenné and blood pears, green and blue Reine Claude plums, morello cherries, apricots, figs, quince and walnuts, and knot gardens laid out as a microcosm; Tycho's sister Sophie Brahe is recorded helping lay out and replant the garden (boxwood cuttings noted in his diary). Tycho rebuilt the ramparts and redesigned and replanted the knot gardens and orchards in the renovations of c.1591. Beyond its beauty the garden carried a symbolic-alchemical programme (Michelsen, in Tycho Brahes Verden, 2006): the medicinal plants were set in precisely planned beds so as to absorb the maximum heavenly influence (a Paracelsian-medicine principle), the enclosed garden read as a microcosm of the cosmic order and a figure of the Opus Magnum — one of the main elements of Tycho's 'earthly chemistry', in harmony with the astronomy pursued in Urania's house at its centre.
Fate. lost; the geometry recovered by excavation and partly recreated on site

The alchemical laboratory

lostB1580 · Ven (Hven)site-laboratory
The alchemical laboratory in Uraniborg's basement, fitted with sixteen furnaces of nine different kinds — Tycho's 'terrestrial astronomy', where he prepared medicines (the plague elixirs) and pursued the Paracelsian correspondence of macrocosm and microcosm. Visitors were taken down to see it; at the basement's other end lay cellars, pantries and the deep well. Tycho later added a second, smaller laboratory of five furnaces in the converted winter dining room (Thoren, Lord of Uraniborg, pp. 159, 224).
Fate. lost with Uraniborg; no trace of the furnaces survives

Stjerneborg — the star castle

reconstructedA1584 · Ven (Hven)site-stjerneborg
A separate, largely subterranean observatory built in 1584 just south of Uraniborg: five crypts beneath rotating cupolas, each giving one of the largest instruments a permanent, wind-sheltered mounting on solid ground. Stjerneborg ('star castle') answered the chief defect of Uraniborg's high galleries — that wind and an unstable footing spoiled the finest measurements. In his own words, Tycho built it “about the year 1584, on a hill outside the castle … with no small difficulty and expenditure a subterranean observatory with various cellars … of solid masonry,” his purpose “partly to have placed some of the most important instruments securely and firmly in order that they should not be exposed to the disturbing influence of the wind, and should be easier to use,” and partly to separate simultaneous observers so “they should not get in the way of each other” (Mechanica, trans. Ræder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, pp. 135–137).
Dimensions. outer premises 70 feet per side; the central semicircles 24 feet in diameter — Tycho's own figures (Mechanica 1946, p. 137; in his 259 mm foot ≈ 18 m and ≈ 6.2 m)
Fate. ruined after 1597; the crypts excavated and reconstructed in the 20th century — now the Tycho Brahe Museum

The printing house (Tycho's press)

lostA1584 · Ven (Hven)site-printing-house
Tycho's own printing house, established on Hven in 1584 so that he controlled his own means of publication and distribution. Its earliest known imprint is the memorial broadsheet Epitaphium Johannis Pratensis (1584); the earliest DATED piece is the occasional pøm Carmen ad Jacobum Ulfeldium, inscribed 'Uraniburgi, 7 Kal. Decemb.' = 25 November 1584 (Nielsen, Bogtrykkeri paa Hveen, p. 176 — correcting the sometimes-cited 27 November 1584, which conflates the date and the piece). The first true book was Elias Olsen Morsing's Diarium astrologicum et meteorologicum / Calendarium (calendar set late 1585, issued 1586). The press went on to produce De mundi (1588), the Danish-language Flemløs Astrologia (1591), the Epistolae (1596) and the advance sheets of the Progymnasmata (1592) — a work only completed at Prague in 1602.
Fate. lost; the press and types dispersed after Tycho left Hven

The paper mill

lostB1589–1597 · Ven (Hven)site-paper-mill
The paper mill on Hven that fed Tycho's press — its production a recurring concern in the correspondence (the two papermakers who absconded delayed the Progymnasmata, as Tycho complained to Hájek). Royal chancery records fix the mill's economics and fate: a privilege of 2 March 1592 ordered all inhabitants of Nørrejylland to sell their old linen rags (Lumper) to Tycho before anyone else, since he 'har anlagt en Papirmølle paa Hveen' and needed them to make paper (KB 1588–92 p. 750). A demolition order of 26 June 1602 to the new lensmand Cort Barleben locates the works 'syd for Uraniborg' and calls it a 'Stampemølle eller Papirmølle,' by then wholly derelict, to be torn down and its usable timber and stone re-used (KB 1596–1602 p. 751). The mill dam was built by Valentin von Spangenberg (also written 'Valenthin'), a hydraulic engineer previously employed by Frederick II at Kronborg (1578–88), who arrived on Hven in 1589. The Meteorological Diary records on 26 June 1589 that Valentin promised to complete the mølledæmning (mill dam) within four weeks, and on 22 July 1590: 'Abijt Valentinus opere aggeris apud molendinum confecto' — Valentin departed after completing the dam work (Vellev 2017, from TBOO IX). Tycho named the paper mill's purpose explicitly in a letter to Christoph Rothmann of 24 November 1589 (TBOO VI); Christian IV ordered oak timber felled for the stamp press in a letter to Kirsten Lindenov (Steen Bille's widow, Tycho's aunt) dated 19 March 1590 (Kancelliets Brevbøger 1588–1592, 360). Its ruined site was rediscovered by the amateur astronomer N. A. Møller Nicolaisen (1874–1954), who on a 1929 visit to Hven noticed a man-made mound and identified it as the fallen paper mill; the Scandinavian Tycho Brahe Foundation funded his excavations of 1933–34, an archæological sensation of the period (Vellev 2002).
Fate. lost; derelict by 1602 and demolished for salvage on royal order (26 June 1602, to lensmand Cort Barleben; KB 1596–1602 p. 751); site rediscovered and excavated 1933–34

The Belvedere (Royal Summer Palace) — Tycho's Prague Musaeum

extantB1599–1635 · Praguesite-belvedere
The Renaissance Royal Summer Palace (Belvedere) in the Royal Garden below Prague Castle. Tycho is reported to have made Prague observations 'in the arcades of the building' (Pierre Bergeron, 1607); after his death in 1601 the Belvedere became a memorial 'Astronomical Museum' housing the astronomy and exact-science part of Rudolf II's collections — the large instruments together with portraits of astronomers (Ptolemy, Albategnius, Copernicus, and two of Tycho himself) and of their royal patrons (Alfonso X, Charles V, Rudolf II, Frederik II of Denmark). The two surviving Tycho-associated sextants by Bürgi and Habermel passed through this collection on their way to the National Technical Museum. Dreyer (1890, p. 365) corroborates the connection from the other side: the Emperor agreed to buy Tycho's own Hven instruments from the family for 20,000 thaler; Tengnagel, as scientific heir from 1602, denied Kepler access to them and had them vaulted under Curtius' house, and Kepler later recorded the 'instrumenta Braheana' as standing 'in viridario Caesaris' (the Emperor's garden, i.e. this Belvedere) in October 1604 — before they were destroyed in the 1619 Bohemian rising against the Habsburgs. Not to be confused with Tycho's principal Prague-period working sites, Benátky nad Jizerou and the Curtius house — the latter the very building under which the instruments were stored. Grade B: the 'Musaeum' reading is Fučíková's, and Tycho's own use of the Belvedere is reported, not firmly documented.
Fate. the building survives; its astronomical contents dispersed after 1612 and removed to the Gallery Building by 1635

Benátky nad Jizerou — Tycho's first Bohemian observatory

extantB1599–1600 · Benátky nad Jizerousite-benatky
The hilltop castle at Benátky nad Jizerou, about 30 km north of Prague, granted to Tycho by Rudolf II in 1599 as his first Bohemian observatory. He was given leave to build wooden observing cubicles (now vanished); the portion available to him ran to three floors of nearly 500 m² each. Here Johannes Kepler joined Tycho in February 1600, before Tycho moved to Prague proper (Thoren, Appendix 5).
Fate. the hilltop castle survives (renovated, intended to house a small Tycho museum); the wooden observing cubicles are gone

The Curtius (Kurtz) house — Tycho's Prague residence and place of death

lostB1600–1601 · Praguesite-curtius-house
The summer home built by the imperial vice-chancellor Jacob Kurtz (Curtius), a few minutes' walk west of the Imperial Castle on the Hradčany hill — Tycho's principal Prague residence for most of his Bohemian years (the 20,000-gulden edifice he described) and the house in which he died on 24 October 1601. After his death the imperial-purchased instruments were vaulted under this very house before passing to the Belvedere garden (Dreyer, p. 365; cf. site-belvedere). Only a 7×16 m foundation remnant survives by a later school (Thoren, Appendix 5).
Fate. demolished; only a 7×16 m foundation remnant survives by a later school; statues of Tycho and Kepler stand on the site (since 1984)

The Schwarzenberg (Rožmberk) palace — Tycho's last banquet

extantC1601 · Praguesite-schwarzenberg
The Renaissance palace on Hradčany square, then owned by Petr Vok z Rožmberka (who had acquired it from Rudolf II by exchange in 1600) — by Thoren's reckoning (Appendix 5) the site of the banquet of 13 October 1601 at which Tycho fell fatally ill. The contemporary story holds that, out of deference to his host Petr Vok, he held his urine too long at table; he sickened with a bladder/uraemic ailment and died eleven days later, on 24 October 1601, at the Curtius house. Grade C: the banquet's location at the Rožmberk palace follows Thoren; the death-by-retention tale is contemporary tradition, not established fact (the 2010 exhumation only excluded mercury poisoning — see the Receptio record of the 2010 exhumation).
Fate. the palace survives on Hradčany square (now a museum)

Church of Our Lady before Týn — Tycho's grave

extantA1601 · Praguesite-tyn-church
The Church of Our Lady before Týn on Prague's Old Town Square, where Tycho was buried on 4 November 1601. His tomb is marked by a relief effigy in armour with one hand on a celestial globe. The grave's afterlife — the 1901 and 2010 exhumations and the long Danish–Czech contest of memory — is modelled in the Reception pillar.
