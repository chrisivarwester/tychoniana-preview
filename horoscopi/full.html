Horoscopi — The Astrological Manuscripts of Tycho Brahe

Every astrological document of Tycho Brahe’s known to survive as a manuscript — the texts he wrote or had written for an astrological purpose and never sent to press — and the three the sources attest but no one has located: each with its date and the basis of that date, its subject, every manuscript that carries it with the library’s own record, which part is in his hand and on whose authority, where Dreyer printed it, and where the text can be read here. The manuscripts are described once, by codex, however many documents they carry. His printed astrology, the Uraniborg weather diary, the two almanacs from the Hven press and the nativities set in the poem Urania Titani have their homes in Scripta, Diarium and Typographica and are bridged from §9, never repeated.

1Introduction

Tycho Brahe cast horoscopes because his office required it and because he held the art, within limits he stated himself, to be conjecture about the elementary world. Each of the three princely nativities was written at the king’s command, and a letter of Frederik II of 24 September 1587 reminds him to send the usual almanac for the coming year. His Copenhagen oration of 1574 spends more than half its length defending astrology; the Latin preface to Prince Hans’s nativity calls prediction a physically grounded conjecture that leaves free will intact; the fragment on weather astrology denies it the certainty of astronomy. Dreyer, who edited the nativities, set little store by the predictions but judged the treatises to surpass all others of their kind in diligence and astronomical subtlety, and set them beside the twelve genitures Cardano printed (Opera Omnia I, p. 314). Section 8 gathers what Tycho himself said of the art, each statement with its text.

This register holds the astrological documents of his whose primary form is a manuscript: 10 survive, in 19 manuscript witnesses across 15 codices in Copenhagen and Vienna, and 3 more are attested and lost. A record is a document — a nativity with its judgment, a reply to a question, a judgment on a comet, a treatise or almanac of weather astrology, a table of stars keyed to a nativity — that Tycho wrote or had written and never sent to press, or that the holding library attributes to him. A witness is one manuscript that carries it. What he printed, the weather diary his assistants kept, the almanacs of the Hven press and the poem in which four nativities are set are not records here; §9 says where each lives and why.

What the numbers are not. A witness count is not a text count: the nativity of Prince Christian is one document in nine manuscripts. “In his hand” is said of a witness, not of a document, and on a named authority; the scribal copies say nothing about who composed the text. No reconstruction, no printed book and no leaf of the weather diary is counted anywhere on this page. No figure here is a survival rate: what is lost is known only where a source happens to say so. Four of the ten surviving documents have never been printed and were not read for this edition beyond their catalogue records; their grades say so.

Nativity of Prince Christian, the later Christian IV (1577)

1 July 1577NativitysurvivingAHORO_C4

The nativity Tycho cast at King Frederik II's command for the prince born on 12 April 1577, subscribed on Hven on 1 July 1577 and delivered at Kronborg the same day. A full genethliacal treatise: the astronomical section with the figure of the birth, then the judgment by topic — temperament, the course of life, honours, journeys, marriage, death — with a German interpretation for the queen, as all three princely nativities carry. It survives in more copies than any other document of the register, all but one scribal: Tycho's own first draft, never finished, is among his papers in Vienna, and the copy handed to the king is lost. The Copenhagen copies call the subject king, a title he took only in 1588, so they were written after that year.

The astrological and meteorological diary for 1573

December 1572Weather astrologysurvivingAHORO_DIARIUM_1573

The almanac Tycho drew up for 1573 at Herrevad: figures of the heavens for the solstices, equinoxes and lunations, each judged for the coming weather, opening with a hard winter forecast from the solstice of December 1572. It is the only such diary of his that survives; the annual almanacs he later sent to Frederik II are all lost. He withheld it from the printed book on the new star, whose preface discusses it, and it was never printed before Dreyer. It survives in one manuscript, the presentation booklet as Tycho assembled it — the preface, the tract on the new star, the diary, the tract on the lunar eclipse and the elegy to Urania — of which only the eclipse tract is in his hand.

Writing to Peucer in September 1588 he reports finding in it, beside the scheme of Peucer's own geniture, the note he had made some twenty-four years before — Mercury in Aquarius in the third house, the Moon in Virgo in the ninth: a sharp and profound mind, and the misfortune of exile or prison that Peucer had since suffered. 8Tycho’s own account of the art

The office. The nativities of the three princes were written at the king’s command — the title of Prince Christian’s says so, ad mandatum serenissimi regis — and delivered as presentation volumes bound in green velvet at Uraniborg (1577, 1579, 1583). An annual almanac was part of the same service: Frederik II’s letter of 24 September 1587 asks for “the usual almanac” for the coming year, and none of the series survives (the lost almanacs; Dreyer 1890, p. 156).

The conviction. The oration of 1574 is the fullest statement: more than half of it defends astrology against its theological and philosophical critics. The preface to Prince Hans’s nativity (Scripta) puts the position in a sentence: the stars incline body, life, character and fortune, the prediction rests on physical causes and on experience, and the man who lives by reason stands above his stars. The fragment on weather astrology draws the limit: the influence of the heavens on the elementary world cannot be predicted with the certainty of astronomy, and, as Cardano had said, particular predictions succeed more often than universal ones.

The practice. The preface to the 1573 diary, printed with De nova stella, attacks the almanac-makers of the day as ill-trained and sets out the experiment of forecasting a year’s weather from the figures of the heavens; the diary itself was withheld from the press and survives in one manuscript. Weather astrology was the branch he kept longest: the Uraniborg diary ran for sixteen years (Diarium), and the Hven press’s first book was an almanac (Morsing 1586).

The reservation. Writing to Caspar Peucer in September 1588, Tycho says that as a boy he had been rather too curious about astrological and especially genethliacal predictions, and that he set them aside as his years and judgment grew, above all because he saw that the planets’ motions in the tables were wrong, and gave himself wholly to observing and correcting the motions (the letter; Opera Omnia VII, p. 137). The reservation did not end the practice: in 1600 he was still promising Ludolf Riddershusen to attend to the nativities Riddershusen had sent (the reply; Opera Omnia VIII, p. 304), and in March 1600 he keyed a table of his restored star catalogue to the Emperor’s nativity (the appendix).

The editor’s verdict. Dreyer, printing the three nativities in 1913, wrote: Etsi ipse Tycho talibus praedictionibus non multum tribuebat, dubitari non poterat, quin hi tractatus integri describendi essent. Singulari enim diligentia et in rebus astronomicis subtilitate ceteros omnes, qui ejusdem generis sunt, superare videntur — although Tycho himself set little store by such predictions, there could be no doubt that these treatises had to be printed entire, for in singular diligence and astronomical subtlety they seem to surpass all others of their kind; and he set them beside the twelve genitures Cardano printed in his commentary on Ptolemy (Opera Omnia I, p. 314). This register states what the documents are and where they are; what Tycho privately believed it does not claim to know.

9Related: the astrological record outside this register

Everything astrological that has a better home than this register, with the reason. Five kinds of remainder: the texts Tycho printed, which are Scripta’s; the weather diary, which is Diarium’s; the two printed almanacs, which are Typographica’s and the censuses’; the poem Urania Titani with the four nativities modern scholarship has computed from it; and the letters in which nativities were asked for and judged. 10Desiderata

What is missing, doubtful or unread, and what would settle it. The first three concern documents that are digitised and have simply not yet been read for this edition.

  1. Cod. 10689/41–46, fol. 21r–24v: the leaves themselves. The Tabulae codicum title names a comet of 1572, Dreyer's notes place Tycho's 1585 comet drafts on the same leaves; only a reading settles which — and whether anything in the bundle concerns 1572 at all.
  2. The draft nativity of Frederik II in Cod. 10686/17: whether it is in Tycho's hand, when it was written, and what it says. The fascicle is digitised and has not been read.
  3. The reply to the Emperor's question in Cod. 10689/50: what was asked and what answered. Digitised; unread.
  4. The hand of GKS 1823: Dreyer against the facsimile autopsy. A comparison of the German section against the wholly autograph GKS 1822 in the reading room.
  5. Cod. 10689/29: the hand rests on the Hand’s own reading alone; an external palaeographic opinion is wanted, and the subject of the geniture is unidentified.
  6. The Christian IV draft in Cod. 10688/8: the fascicle number is Dreyer's; the library's title for the fascicle ('Simplices motus a. 1576 etc.') does not name the draft. The fascicle should be read to confirm the draft is there and how far it runs.
  7. Langebeks Excerpter 178–179: the horoscope copy is not itemised in the boxes' record; which box, and how much of the text, Langebek copied.
  8. The eclipse prognostication of 1566, in which Tycho is said to have foretold the death of Sultan Süleyman: an explanation is attested (Dreyer 1890 p. 26), no document is located. Entered here, not as a record.
  9. Duke Otto II of Braunschweig-Lüneburg asked Tycho for a nativity at Harburg in October 1598 (Loca, the Harburg call). Whether it was cast is not known.
  10. David Fabricius's promised 'exempla nativitatum' (Opera Omnia VIII, p. 430): the letter is not yet keyed to the Epistolarium.
  11. The German tract on the comet of 1577 has no modern edition or translation and is not in the Danish critical edition; it cannot be read here beyond Dreyer's text.
  12. No photograph of GKS 1822, of the velvet bindings or of any horoscope leaf is in Iconographia.
  13. Cod. 10688/17, 'Calculi miscelli, observationes, pericula' (1600), holds astrological directions in a hand the Graphonomicon grades possible; it has not been examined for a prose document and is not a record.
